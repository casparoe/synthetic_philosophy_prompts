As a journalist covering philosophy and public ethics, I have been trying to formalize a puzzle about collective guilt that keeps arising in debates over historical reparations and national memory. Drawing on Karl Jaspers's 1946 distinction in Die Schuldfrage between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—without treating any one category as settled—I want to test whether a nation can bear moral guilt across generations. The reasoning below seems to drive me toward an unacceptable conclusion: either every citizen is tainted by ancestry, or the concept of collective guilt collapses into mere institutional bookkeeping. I need to know which step fails, and what giving it up costs.

(1) Moral guilt, as distinct from legal liability or political responsibility, requires moral agency: the capacity to understand a wrong, experience remorse, and undergo reform.

(2) A nation-state is not a conscious agent; it lacks the unified subjectivity necessary for remorse or moral transformation. To attribute moral guilt to it is therefore either a category error or a metaphor.

(3) Nevertheless, nations persist across generations as continuous institutional entities with legal identity, accumulated capital, territorial boundaries, and bureaucratic structures that directly inherit benefits from past injustices.

(4) If moral responsibility requires only institutional continuity and causal inheritance—rather than individual consciousness—then a nation can be responsible for historical wrongs in the same structural way that a corporation inherits liability for past environmental harm, even after all original actors have died.

(5) But if the nation is guilty, and citizenship is largely non-voluntary and inherited, then every present member is implicated in that guilt, at least as a constituent part of the guilty collective.

(6) This yields an absurd result: a child born today to parents who actively resisted the wrong is morally guilty for atrocities committed before her birth, solely by inheriting citizenship and its associated benefits, while the actual perpetrators are dead and beyond moral address.

(7) To block this, one might restrict guilt to the institution alone, treating it as what I am calling intergenerational moral sedimentation—a layered structural residue that requires institutional redesign and reparative restructuring rather than personal remorse. On this view, "guilt" names only a structural obligation, not a moral stain.

(8) Yet if we make that move, we have abandoned the specifically moral dimension of guilt that Jaspers insisted upon; we are left with administrative liability dressed in moral language.

(9) Therefore, collective moral guilt across generations is either incoherent (because nations lack moral agency), unjust (because it implicates innocent members), or vacuous (because it reduces to structural bookkeeping).

Which premise should I reject? I am inclined to defend (1)—that moral guilt requires moral agency—rather than accept (6), but I want to understand whether saving (1) forces me to deny any meaningful form of collective responsibility, or whether there is a hidden slide between (4) and (5)—perhaps an illicit part-whole inference or an equivocation between institutional continuity and membership inheritance—that makes universal guilt look inevitable when it is not. If the argument is valid, then I must choose which plausible claim is least costly to surrender, and I want to know the price of each option.
