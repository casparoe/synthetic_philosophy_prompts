I'm a PhD student in bioethics working on the authority of earlier selves over later ones, particularly in progressive dementia, and I'm trying to stabilize my own judgments about when an advance directive should bind. Here are my concrete verdicts; they mostly cohere, but one pair keeps breaking the pattern.

Case 1: At 50, M writes a directive refusing artificial nutrition if she ever reaches Alzheimer's severe enough that she no longer recognizes her children. At 82 she is in exactly that stage, generally content, smiling at her daughter without recognition, and contracts pneumonia. Verdict: honor the directive and withhold the feeding tube. Her present experiential state does not cancel her earlier authoritative refusal to live in that condition.

Case 2: Same directive, but M's refusal was explicitly grounded in her belief—now known to be false—that advanced dementia always involves constant terror. In reality she is serene, enjoys music and touch, and shows no distress. Verdict: the directive does not bind. Its authority depended on an epistemic condition that failed; she was authorizing refusal of a state she fundamentally misdescribed.

Case 3: At 55, N writes that he wants no life-extending treatment if he becomes "a different person," defined as losing all memories of his career, relationships, and core values. At 80 he has lost every autobiographical memory but retains stable evaluative dispositions—he loves music, dislikes being rushed, and shows consistent preferences. He contracts an infection and asks for antibiotics. Verdict: treat him. He has not satisfied the structural-change condition specified in his own directive; mere autobiographical erasure is not enough.

Case 4: At 60, P writes: "If I develop dementia and ask to be allowed to die, do not listen; that request is not mine." At 85 she has dementia and, for six consecutive months, calmly and consistently expresses a desire to die, describing her life as empty. She never asks impulsively. Verdict: I reverse myself repeatedly. One day the directive seems protective; the next it seems like unjustified paternalism over a stable, present preference. This is the case that fractures my account.

Case 5: At 45, Q writes a directive with an institutional-vesting clause: proxy authority activates only after 30 continuous days of incapacity, and applies only to life-sustaining treatment, never to palliative comfort measures. At 78 she has fluctuating capacity—lucid for hours every other day. During a lucid window she asks not to have a feeding tube inserted; during incoherent periods she reaches for food. Her proxy wants to withhold the tube. Verdict: the proxy authority has not vested continuously, so she cannot authorize withholding; but the palliative clause requires that comfort feeding (oral, if she accepts it) continue regardless. So withhold the tube but do not deny her food if she takes it.

Is there one principle—or a small family of principles—that delivers all five verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If coherence requires dropping one verdict, which should go? Apply your principle also to this unlisted case: a patient updated her directive annually for twenty years, always reaffirming refusal of life extension in dementia, but in the final update—made two days before a stroke caused dementia—she was severely depressed and on a new psychoactive medication. Does the principle treat that final update as governing?

Please answer in no more than 400 words. State your principle precisely, identify which clause handles which case, predict the outcome for the update case, and be explicit about which of my verdicts your account cannot save.
