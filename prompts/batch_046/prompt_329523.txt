I've been attempting to apply Berkeley's master argument to contemporary philosophy of mind, and I think I've located a gap that standard expositions overlook. The argument establishes that no idea of a sensible object can exist unperceived, because conceiving it already involves mental presence. Yet Berkeley also introduces notions—active, relational modes of cognition through which we understand spirits and causal relations, rather than passive sensible qualities. If notions are genuinely distinct from ideas in kind, then it appears the master argument restricts only phenomenal ontology: it prevents us from having an idea of unperceived matter, but it seems to leave open the possibility that a non-phenomenal substrate could be grasped notionally, functioning somewhat like a Kantian noumenal ground without the accompanying scepticism. Given this, why should Berkeley's immaterialism be taken to deny all access to an underlying reality, rather than merely denying that such reality could be an object of sensory idea? Please respond in British English, and indicate whether this line of reasoning confuses the scope of the master argument with the scope of Berkeley's broader epistemology, or whether there is indeed a further argument that blocks even notional cognition of an unperceived material substrate.
