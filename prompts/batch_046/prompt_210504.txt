In philosophical interpretations of Buddhist ethics, a long-running dispute asks whether the tradition is best understood as a form of consequentialism—where the rightness of an action is determined by its contribution to the cessation of suffering and the attainment of liberation—or whether it is fundamentally deontological or virtue-based, centered on the quality of intention (cetana) and the cultivation of mental states regardless of calculable outcomes. Proponents of the consequentialist reading point to the pervasive language of karma as cause-and-effect, to texts that weigh actions by their future fruits (vipaka), and to the teleological orientation of the path toward nirvana as an optimal end-state. Their opponents emphasize that the Buddha consistently rejects outcome-based reasoning for the practitioner—moral evaluation is immediate, tied to the presence or absence of greed, hatred, and delusion in the mind at the moment of action—and that treating karma as a utility function flattens the soteriological structure of the tradition into something it never was.
