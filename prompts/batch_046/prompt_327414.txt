After a long argument last night with a friend about whether our historical society's newest exhibit is helping anyone live or merely adding to the dead weight of the past, I need a reusable framework. I chair the programming committee for a regional public-history nonprofit; we review about six proposals a month—exhibits, digital archives, walking tours, school curricula—each claiming to serve the public, but none of them tested against anything except budget and attendance projections. I want to turn Nietzsche's account of monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—especially the idea that each mode can become life-denying when it exceeds a community's capacity to assimilate the past—into a decision procedure my committee can actually run.

Please construct a step-by-step procedure, in ordered questions with sufficient-answer conditions, that a group of five non-philosophers can apply in about twenty minutes per proposal. It should ask which mode or mixture of modes a proposal employs; check whether the proposal respects what Nietzsche calls the plastic power of its intended audience—the capacity to metabolize past material without being crushed by it; and flag the specific disadvantage that threatens each mode: monumental history degenerating into false emulation and myth-making; antiquarian history degenerating into stifling preservation that paralyzes present action; critical history degenerating into destructive severance that leaves nothing constructive in its wake. Mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be signed off by a named role—say, the committee chair—rather than being resolved by the checklist. Include explicit stop points where the answer is no, explicit hand-off points where the question is one of values rather than facts, and a brief audit of what the procedure cannot do—cases where it will misfire or give answers that depend on contested premises about what counts as service to life.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals, writing out the worked reasoning for each:

First, a "Monumental Founders" gallery that presents the city's nineteenth-century industrialists as heroic models of will and risk-taking, intended to inspire local entrepreneurs. Note that our board is politically and financially inclined to approve this, and show where the procedure produces an answer they will not like—likely requiring either rejection or heavy critical framing that undermines the inspirational purpose.

Second, an antiquarian proposal to digitize and display, with exhaustive provenance, every surviving object from a defunct textile mill, with no curatorial narrative beyond chronological completeness, intended to root the neighborhood in its labor heritage.

Third, a critical proposal for an exhibit titled "The Invention of Tradition," demonstrating that the city's beloved founding narrative was largely fabricated in the 1920s, designed explicitly to free current residents from inherited nostalgic obligations. Show where the procedure permits the debunking but demands a constructive replacement, and indicate the judgment call about whether such a replacement is present.

In each case, state the recommendation the procedure yields—approve, approve with mandatory framing, reject, or defer to values judgment—and identify the exact step where the committee must make a call it cannot outsource to the framework. Keep the language practical; avoid asking us to look up editions, page numbers, or secondary literature. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The deliverable should be something we can laminate and bring to next month's meeting.
