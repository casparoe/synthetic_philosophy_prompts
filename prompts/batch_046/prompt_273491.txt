I'm currently working through a graduate seminar in moral philosophy, and during our session on moral worth and motivation two fellow students—I'll call them Mara and Leo—presented a structured disagreement about a hypothetical case they designed together. The case involves Dr. Chen, a surgeon who donates half her income to effective charities and performs regular pro-bono surgeries in underserved regions. She acts from a genuine belief that this is her moral duty, but she also consciously and strongly desires professional prestige and the appearance of saintliness; she estimates that about sixty percent of her sustained motivation comes from the prestige-desire, with roughly forty percent from duty. Her colleague Dr. Patel performs the identical actions—same donations, same surgeries—but is motivated almost entirely by duty (around ninety-five percent), with only minimal, incidental pleasure in recognition.

Mara advanced four moves. First, she claimed that full moral worth requires the right reason to be the dominant causal factor in motivation, so Dr. Patel's actions possess full worth while Dr. Chen's do not, despite identical outcomes. Second, she argued that if we reject dominance and accept mere presence of the right reason, then a corporate executive who donates millions purely for tax advantages but feels a faint flicker of compassion would also count as morally worthy, which is clearly absurd. Third, she noted that in Dr. Chen's case the prestige-desire is not merely present but structurally directing—she schedules high-visibility surgeries first—so the wrong reason actively shapes the architecture of her agency rather than sitting idle. Fourth, she concluded that moral worth should therefore be binary: either your right reason governs the structure of choice, or you receive at most partial, derivative credit.

Leo countered with four corresponding moves. First, he objected that the dominance criterion is arbitrary—why should a fifty-one versus forty-nine split determine a categorical moral difference? Moral evaluation should instead track whether the agent is responsive to the right reasons, not their quantitative share in a psychological ledger. Second, he said Mara's tax-avoidance counterexample fails because the executive's compassion plays no causal role in the donation decision, whereas Dr. Chen's duty genuinely constrains her—she would not perform these surgeries if they violated her duty, even if prestige were available elsewhere—so responsiveness, not dominance, is the proper test. Third, he argued that Mara confuses causal influence with normative authority: the fact that prestige affects scheduling reflects a practical failure of judgment, not proof that duty lacks governing authority, since Dr. Chen still treats duty as a veto-power over her actions, which is precisely how normative authority operates in imperfect agents. Fourth, he warned that insisting on binary, sovereign right-reasons demands an impossible psychological purity and would imply that almost no real human action possesses full moral worth, draining the concept of practical significance.

Please take on the role of an expert moral philosopher with specialized training in both Kantian moral theory and contemporary moral psychology. Before rendering your judgment, please take a moment to brainstorm the key conceptual distinctions at stake—specifically, the difference between causal dominance, normative authority, and reason-responsiveness—being careful not to conflate them in your analysis. Then adjudicate the disagreement move by move: for each of Mara's four arguments and Leo's four replies, rate the move on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and provide a brief justification for each rating. Afterward, determine who is more right overall, point out what both parties are missing—perhaps regarding how institutional or collaborative contexts might redistribute or complicate the attribution of moral worth when motives are mixed—and conclude with a nuanced position that avoids both excessive motivational purity and uncritical pluralism. Please be precise, avoid generic summaries, and do not provide bibliographies or citation lists.
