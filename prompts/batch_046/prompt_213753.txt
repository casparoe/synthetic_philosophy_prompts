I've been working through an argument in my philosophy of psychiatry course that I originally took to be a clever extension of Hacking's looping effects, but now it strikes me as proving far too much—possibly that psychiatric classification is necessarily self-undermining. I'd like to know exactly where the reasoning slips, and what giving up that step costs. Here is the chain, numbered for clarity.

(1) Psychiatric categories are interactive kinds: being classified with disorder D changes how individuals understand themselves and behave, thereby altering the very phenomena the category was meant to track. (2) For a classification to be valid, applying its label should not change the essential properties of the members it classifies; if it does, the classifier was never tracking a stable kind, only causing one. (3) Therefore, psychiatric diagnoses can remain valid only if they are applied in ways that avoid triggering these loops—say, covertly or to subjects who cannot comprehend them. (4) Yet clinical ethics and efficacy demand transparent disclosure, since treatment planning and informed consent depend on the patient knowing the diagnosis. (5) So every legitimate clinical application of a psychiatric diagnosis simultaneously undermines the conditions required for that diagnosis to be true. (6) Over time and across populations, widespread diagnosis therefore makes the taxonomy systematically less accurate, not more refined. (7) This creates a practical dilemma for clinicians: either they withhold diagnoses and fail in their therapeutic duty, or they apply them knowing that doing so helps falsify the very categories they rely on. (8) The only way to preserve both epistemic integrity and clinical transparency is to abandon psychiatric classification entirely, or to adopt a policy of deliberate misdiagnosis designed to stabilize categories by preventing authentic looping.

My instinct is that (1) and (4) are things I want to keep—Hacking's point about interactivity seems right, and transparency in medicine is non-negotiable. But I cannot accept (8). I have wondered whether the escape lies in treating diagnoses as dynamic reflexive trajectories that build looping into the criteria themselves, so that a diagnosis predicts its own alteration rather than being falsified by it. Even then, I worry that this collapses the distinction between describing a disorder and prescribing a self-fulfilling narrative. Where does the argument actually break? Is there an equivocation between altering a phenomenon and falsifying a description of it? Or is the argument valid, and the real cost is admitting that psychiatric kinds are not natural in the way we often pretend? If you had to choose between preserving (2) and preserving the idea that clinicians can legitimately diagnose without thereby destroying their own taxonomy, which is less damaging to surrender, and why?
