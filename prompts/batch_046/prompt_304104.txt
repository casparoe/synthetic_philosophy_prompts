I'm currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on moral psychology and the architecture of practical reason, and I'm drafting the final examination. I want a single essay question—allocated roughly ninety minutes and thirty marks, with an expected response of about eight hundred words—addressing whether genuine moral dilemmas are actually possible, but I want to move beyond the familiar classroom rehearsal of Bernard Williams's appeal to moral residue against Ruth Marcus's denial of genuine conflict. The concrete case should be this: a regional public-health officer who co-authored the ethical framework governing vaccine distribution must now allocate a single available batch between a remote rural clinic and an urban hospital. The framework she designed assigns each population an equally weighted, non-comparable claim, and she knows that whichever allocation she chooses will not only harm one group but will also undermine her standing as the impartial architect of the very system she administers—producing not ordinary regret but a kind of "role-fracture" in which her authority to govern future allocations collapses because her choice reveals the framework's internal contradiction. Students should be asked whether this scenario amounts to a genuine moral dilemma, and specifically whether role-fracture provides stronger evidence for genuine conflict than moral residue alone, given that residue can arise even when one duty clearly overrides another.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of approximately eight hundred words, and a detailed marking scheme. B-range answers should be penalized for treating residue as decisive proof of dilemma, for ignoring the role-fracture element entirely, or for conflating practical difficulty with normative conflict.
