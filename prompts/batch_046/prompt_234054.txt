In my graduate seminar on philosophy of history and social ontology, we have spent the term examining archaeological accounts of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic-to-Postclassic Maya lowlands, and the ecological and demographic shifts on Rapa Nui. For decades the literature has repeated a familiar stalemate: one camp describes these episodes as collapse—a rapid, catastrophic loss of centralized administration, monumental production, and writing—while another insists that the same evidence shows only transformation, a reorganization into smaller-scale polities with dispersed settlement and altered material registers. What strikes me is that the empirical disputes have largely evaporated; scholars now share the same stratigraphic, paleobotanical, and settlement data. Yet the interpretive deadlock is absolute, with neither side revising its classification.

My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not merely verbal, nor is it hidden behind an empirical question we have not yet settled. Instead, I suspect the deadlock reflects an unarticulated divergence in social ontology. One side treats societies as entities with essential functional thresholds and diachronic identity conditions, so that the disappearance of palaces, writing, and redistribution networks constitutes the death of that society. The other treats societies as temporary stabilizations of practices and ecological relations, for which institutional metamorphosis is precisely the mode of persistence. If this is correct, then the two camps may be answering different questions—one about organizational discontinuity, the other about cultural and demographic continuity—under the single heading of collapse. But I also wonder whether a tacit normative teleology is at work, in which complexity is treated as the default state that societies ought to maintain, rendering any reduction a failure rather than a change.

Please diagnose the structure of this disagreement with reference to what each side actually counts as evidence, what they treat as necessary for societal identity, and whether they share standards of explanation. Is this a substantive dispute about the ontology of collective entities, a verbal disagreement about classification criteria, or a case of two research programs pursuing different questions? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress: a formal model of institutional identity over time, a conceptual distinction between functional decomplexification and organizational metamorphosis, an explicit abandonment of the collapse frame, or should we expect no resolution?
