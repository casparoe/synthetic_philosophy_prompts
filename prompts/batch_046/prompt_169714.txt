I serve on the selection committee for a municipal arts grant. The rules stipulate that once applications pass an initial eligibility screen, winners are drawn by a certified random-number generator audited to give each qualified applicant an equal, objective 2% chance. I was one of three screeners, and during review I noticed that one finalist—an acquaintance of mine—received a passing score that, in retrospect, depended on a generous interpretation of a technical criterion that I applied more strictly to other applicants. The generator itself was not tampered with; the audit confirms the draw was mechanically unbiased. Nevertheless, the grant's transparency protocol asks each committee member to declare their "epistemic stance": specifically, whether they should continue to defer to the audited 2% chance as their personal credence that this particular finalist won by pure lottery, or whether knowledge about the screening stage licenses a different belief. I am unsure whether my private awareness of the inconsistent screening updates the reference class over which the objective chance is defined, or whether it merely provides separate evidence about a different stage of the process. If I treat the 2% figure as authoritative, am I ignoring relevant information? If I abandon it, am I violating a rational obligation to defer to known objective chances? How should I resolve this, and what does the resolution imply about cases where an agent has partial, unshared insight into the conditions that generate the reference class for a probabilistic process? Please explain how confident you are in your answer and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
