I'm drafting an essay for my philosophy of cognitive science seminar on whether Dennett's taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures can be extended to artificial distributed architectures without conceptual distortion. My specific case is a class of multi-agent reinforcement-learning systems in which individual nodes are purely reactive—adjusting weights only through local reward feedback—yet the collective shares a global parameter server that allows the swarm to discard underperforming behavioral policies before any single agent executes them physically. I argue that this architecture produces a hybrid that is not straightforwardly Skinnerian at the collective level, because the system performs internal selection among competing action schemas rather than testing each through embodied trial and error; at the same time, it lacks the representational richness usually associated with Popperian minds, since no node constructs an explicit model of the environment or runs counterfactual simulations in a unified cognitive workspace. I further suggest that when these systems exchange compressed gradient summaries through a protocol that functions analogously to a shared vocabulary—enabling one node's failure to inform another's policy without direct experience—the collective might exhibit a rudimentary Gregorian capacity despite the absence of individual language-users.

I'm particularly concerned with three points and would appreciate targeted feedback. First, does my argument that internal policy-selection via a shared server collapses the Skinnerian/Popperian distinction, or am I conflating distributed computation with genuine mental representation? Second, is it coherent to attribute Gregorian tool-use to a collective when the "language" involved is nothing more than vector-encoded weight updates, and if so, does this dilute the concept of Gregorian cognition beyond recognition? Third, I want to avoid positing a mysterious fifth category, yet the architecture seems to fall between the established four; is there a principled reason—perhaps drawn from the original biological motivations of the taxonomy—to resist expanding the framework, or should I treat distributed artificial minds as genuinely requiring a new kind?

Please comment on the conceptual structure of the essay rather than on style alone. I am especially interested in whether the analogy between biological learning mechanisms and distributed parameter updates holds up under scrutiny, and whether I have missed an obvious objection—perhaps from the philosophy of collective intentionality or from existing work on extended cognition—that undermines the claim that these systems instantiate any of the four kinds in a non-metaphorical sense.
