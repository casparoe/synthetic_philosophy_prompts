I'm trying to help a close friend think through a decision that has become as much about belief as about livelihood. For fifteen years she has run a small workshop restoring antique mechanical clocks and astronomical instruments—work that depends on manual skills and historical knowledge few people still possess. Last year, materials engineers published preliminary results suggesting a new additive-manufacturing process could eventually reproduce the intricate gear assemblies she repairs, at lower cost and with greater durability. The technical community is genuinely divided: some reviewers think the process will never achieve the tolerances these instruments require; others predict it will dominate within a decade. The evidence is inconclusive, and she must act now—either invest her remaining capital in expanding the workshop and training apprentices, or begin converting the space into an archive and teaching studio.

She tells me that she doesn't think the available data justify a firm belief either way. But she also believes that if she adopts the conviction that her craft is irreplaceable—not merely as a practical posture, but as a genuine belief—she will work with the intensity and creativity that might make the workshop exceptional enough to survive regardless of technological change. She asks whether it is ethically permissible for her to "choose to believe" in the durability of her craft for this reason: because the belief itself might help create the conditions that justify it, and because committing without conviction feels like a kind of bad faith.

Her brother, an engineer, argues that this is a dangerous rationalization. He says that when evidence is genuinely insufficient, the only intellectually responsible stance is agnosticism; adopting a belief because it is useful or self-fulfilling violates a basic duty not to let wishes determine what one takes to be true. He also notes that if she convinces herself on motivational grounds and then encourages apprentices or investors to share her confidence, she risks spreading a belief she knows she hasn't earned.

Can you help me understand the philosophical structure of their disagreement? In particular: is there a legitimate distinction between adopting a belief for its practical benefits and merely acting on a provisional working assumption? Does the fact that the belief might be partly self-fulfilling change the ethical calculation? And who has the stronger position—my friend, who thinks conviction is a necessary ingredient of meaningful commitment, or her brother, who thinks insufficient evidence should always constrain what we genuinely believe?
