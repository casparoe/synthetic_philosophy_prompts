I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy of medicine presenting a paper next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on clinical ethics. My thesis extends Bernard Williams's integrity objection—specifically the concern that requiring explicit deliberative weighing of constitutive commitments reproduces a practical failure—to the architecture of clinical ethics committees. I argue that when physicians are required to use explicit weighted scoring matrices that calibrate commitment to a particular patient against impartial allocation criteria, the framework structurally reproduces the flaw Williams identified: it treats a ground-project commitment as one fungible input among others, demanding precisely the kind of practical reasoning that integrity prohibits. For each: what I should concede up front, the reply that has to land in under two minutes, and the weakest point in that reply that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—likely whether my view commits me to rejecting all triage protocols in public health emergencies—and draft the two-minute defense I can deploy if it comes.
