I am a novelist revising a manuscript in which a logician and a physicist debate whether the continuous structures in their shared theory are real or merely artifacts of formalization; to make the scene philosophically credible, I have been auditing an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, and I find Skolem's paradox far more unsettling in practice than in textbooks. I want to adapt an exam question from that seminar for a closed workshop I am running with fellow writers who have no formal logic training but need to understand why ontology identification collapses when every empirical model is computationally discrete. Write one essay question that philosophy of science graduate students could answer in ninety minutes, aimed at testing whether they can do more than summarize the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem. Frame the problem around a specific but generic scenario: a first-order formalization of a physical theory that quantifies over real-valued parameters and therefore asserts an uncountable ontology of states, implemented in a digital simulation whose domain is necessarily countable. The question should require students to explain precisely why this countable model does not falsify the theory’s uncountable commitments, and then to evaluate whether the scientist is justified in maintaining a realist commitment to the continuum despite having access only to discrete representations. Provide a model answer of approximately 600 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each carries (total 20), and include a one-line note for each point on how candidates typically miss it. The entire response should be written in American English. Do not ask for a bibliography, reading list, or citations—the exam is closed-book, and I want to see reasoning rather than recall of named sources. To keep the task from being answered by rehearsed summary, embed the niche twist that the paradox is being used not merely in pure mathematics but as a live obstacle to identifying what a computationally implemented scientific theory actually commits us to ontologically.
