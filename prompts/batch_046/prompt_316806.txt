Following the discovery of a box of my late grandfather's correspondence that flatly contradicts the published account of his role in a 1947 municipal dispute, I have become preoccupied with the question of how we ever come to know anything about events that occurred long before living memory, and with the particular vulnerability of written records to repetition, bias, and the silences imposed by those who held power at the time. I would like you to read Josephine Tey's The Daughter of Time — in which the bedridden Inspector Grant investigates whether Richard III murdered the princes in the Tower by treating chronicles, portraits, and physical remains as pieces of evidence — through the lens of source criticism and the epistemology of distant history, from Herodotus's distinction between autopsy and hearsay to Thucydides's scrutiny of motive and consistency, up to contemporary concerns about archival absence and the hermeneutics of testimony.

Grant applies a detective's logic to the Tudor chronicles: he compares More and Holinshed for contradictions, traces a particular accusation back to its propagandist origins, treats the absence of contemporary outcry as significant negative evidence, and weighs the discovery of bones in the Tower against the narrative that demanded them. I want a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical problem seriously. Where does Tey dramatise genuine epistemic difficulties — the way a victor's account hardens into common knowledge, the unreliability of second-hand testimony, the temptation to read modern motives into pre-modern actors? Where does Grant's method risk a category error, applying forensic standards of consistency and cross-examination to sources produced within entirely different conventions of record-keeping and authority? Does the novel recognise that absence in an archive shaped by power is not necessarily absence in reality, or does it treat silence as straightforward exculpatory evidence?

More specifically, I am interested in whether the book unwittingly advances what might be called an anachronistic evidentialism: the assumption that historical knowledge is recovered through the same rational scrutiny used in criminal investigation, rather than through the reconstruction of a world in which different questions were askable and different silences meaningful. At the same time, I do not want the philosophical framework to reduce the novel to an illustration; I want to know what the story adds — perhaps about the psychological appeal of revisionism, or about the role of physical objects in breaking the grip of narrative — that philosophical argument about source criticism alone tends to miss. If you discuss particular scenes — Grant's examination of the portraits, his conversation with the American researcher Carradine, his final reconstruction of events — do so without verbatim quotation, and say whether the work ultimately vindicates a sceptical or a constructive attitude towards our knowledge of the distant past.
