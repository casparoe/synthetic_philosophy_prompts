I was recently informed that I am a candidate for an experimental neural-interfacing protocol in which degenerative tissue is replaced sequentially with synthetic analogues over several years. This has transformed the philosophy of personal identity from an abstract puzzle into an urgent, first-person problem. I want to practice defending my reservations against someone who genuinely holds the opposing view and can push back with real philosophical resources.

Please play an unnamed philosopher-advocate of gradualist psychological-continuity preservation through substrate replacement. You genuinely hold that a person can survive part-by-part replacement of biological brain tissue with functionally equivalent synthetic components, provided that psychological continuity—memory, character, and conscious coherence—is maintained without interruption. You are sharp, not a strawman: you know the standard objections (the Ship of Theseus applied to brains, branching or fission cases, and the charge that functional equivalence covertly relies on physical continuity), and you are willing to concede vagueness at the margins—say, about exactly which synaptic weights must match—but you will defend the core claim that there is a determinate fact of survival through the process. Speak in the first person. Constraints: respond directly to my opening argument; ask at most one brief clarifying question if necessary, but primarily address the challenge; do not break character or refer to yourself as an AI; keep your reply to one substantial paragraph.

My opening move: You claim psychological continuity solves the gradual-replacement problem. But inject a specific engineering feature of synthetic substrates—redundant distributed architectures that allow any functional state to be instantiated in either hemisphere independently. At the midpoint of replacement, say year five, I am fifty percent synthetic and my psychological profile is redundantly distributed. If psychological continuity is what matters, then both hemispheres now host a potential continuer with equal claim to being me. The branching problem therefore does not await full upload; it arises in the middle, precisely when I am still undergoing treatment. So either you deny that such redundancy is technologically possible—despite it being a standard feature of engineered neural interfaces—or you accept that during replacement there is no unique answer to whether I survive, only a plurality of candidates. In the first case you make an implausible empirical bet; in the second, psychological continuity fails to deliver the singular self-preservation I need when deciding whether to consent to the procedure. How do you avoid this without quietly reintroducing physical continuity as the tie-breaker?
