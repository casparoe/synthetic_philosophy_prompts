In several European jurisdictions, a licensing body for clinical psychologists requires trainees to complete exactly 450 supervised therapy hours, measured solely by clock-time in session, regardless of patient outcome, supervisor evaluation, or case complexity. The body publicly justifies this rule as safeguarding competent, ethically responsible practice and insists that clinical experience cannot be reduced to a number. Yet it enforces the 450-hour threshold with absolute rigidity: a trainee with 449 hours and exceptional documented outcomes is denied licensure, while a trainee with 450 hours of passive observation receives full credit. The same institution also mandates a separate, unquantified ethical reflection portfolio, but this is graded only pass or fail and is never permitted to override the hour requirement. Meanwhile, appeals that present direct evidence of therapeutic efficacy or patient harm are dismissed on the grounds that the standard protects professional integrity, not individual results.

What philosophical commitments about professional competence, moral responsibility, and the nature of value does this licensing practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that competence is essentially a quantitative, fungible commodity? Or can a non-consequentialist, rule-based conception of professional duty explain why the threshold is outcome-blind and non-negotiable? Is the separation between the quantified hour-requirement and the unquantified ethical portfolio philosophically consistent—does it imply a two-tier theory in which some professional goods are measurable and others are not—or does it conceal an incoherence? Furthermore, if the implicit view is that rules protect integrity regardless of outcomes, why does the body invoke competence, an outcome-oriented concept, rather than professional initiation or boundary maintenance? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, indicate which rival theories of value and responsibility remain compatible with it, and describe what the licensing structure would have to become if that implicit theory were replaced by one that treated demonstrated therapeutic efficacy and ethical reflection as jointly sufficient for qualification.
