I'm preparing the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop being held to honor a philosopher who recently retired after three decades teaching practical ethics; she was known for insisting that moral philosophy must leave conceptual space for genuinely irreconcilable demands, not merely for hard choices that yield to better reasoning. The audience will be split between specialists—graduate students and colleagues who will spot a reflexive denial in the Kantian mode or a routine invocation of moral remainder the instant it appears—and family members, clinicians, and policy staff who have never taken an ethics course but who live with impossible decisions every day. I want about ten minutes of continuous address that takes the question of whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible and makes it do structural work: it should determine what I can promise, what I must withhold, and why I cannot close with either a triumphalist exhortation to do the right thing or a therapeutic retreat into language about self-care. To avoid the standard framing of conflicting obligations, I want the speech built around a distinction I am introducing for this occasion—call it dilemmas of exclusion, where two values are genuinely incompatible and choosing one permanently forfeits the other, versus dilemmas of exhaustion, where the same value pulls in opposite directions until the agent's responsive capacity is depleted. Only the first kind, if it exists, threatens the coherence of moral reasoning itself; the second is a failure of resources, not logic. The address should make this distinction shape every move: why consolation must be limited, why exhortation is inappropriate, and why the retiring professor's lifelong position matters for people who will never read her books. The tone should be ceremonial without being hollow, intellectually precise without being technically alienating. Please write this as continuous prose only—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables—as a single letter-like text meant to be read aloud in a room where some people are marking the end of a career and others are wondering whether philosophy has anything to say about real life.
