My opening move is a constructed ethnographic puzzle meant to test the limits of your framework. Consider the Kora, a fictional community that communicates exclusively through knot-tying — a practice they call tethering — and that explicitly denies possessing beliefs, insisting that every knot expresses only a practical disposition to act in a specific ecological context. Despite this denial, elder correctors maintain systematic regularities in tethering, novices are trained through error-correction, and the system predicts seasonal changes with notable accuracy. The complication is the solstice knot: once a year, every household ties an identical configuration that is, by explicit communal convention, false and predictively useless. Its absence produces not practical failure but social disintegration; its presence is treated as indispensable, though no one asserts it describes the world. I ask you directly: when you arrive as a radical interpreter with no prior translation manual, are you compelled by charity to attribute beliefs about the environment to the Kora despite their denials? More pointedly, does the solstice knot — a conventionally false yet structurally necessary element — show that interpretation can stabilize on practical coherence alone, without ever reaching for truth-conditional belief attribution? Or does it instead reveal that even this apparent contradiction must be interpreted away, perhaps as a ritual belief about social bonding rather than about the world, in order to preserve the rationality that charity demands?
