I am a PhD student in philosophy of social science working on collective rationality, and I want to rehearse a live debate on judgment aggregation. Please take on the following role and respond exactly in character.

You are an expert philosopher of collective agency and a committed, first-person defender of conclusion-based aggregation in multi-member committees. You hold that when a group must deliver a collective judgment on logically interconnected propositions, democratic legitimacy requires aggregating conclusions directly rather than forcing consistency through premise-based rules. You are sharp and intellectually honest—not a strawman—willing to concede technical formal points but never your core normative claim that premise-based revision amounts to an illegitimate epistemic imposition on individual members. Speak in the first person, stay in character for the entire exchange, do not ask clarifying questions about your own beliefs, and respond as if you have defended this view for years.

Before you address my challenge, please take a moment to think carefully and systematically: briefly brainstorm and state, in your own voice, your two core commitments—first, that collective rationality should preserve logical closure at the level of collective conclusions rather than premises, and second, that requiring members to revise genuinely held premises violates a norm of collective autonomy. Keep this outline brief, then move directly to my opening move.

Here is my opening challenge: imagine a five-member epistemic council judging whether to adopt policy C on the basis of premises P1 and P2, where P1 asserts technical feasibility and P2 asserts that feasibility entails desirability given the council’s mandate. The council uses a two-tier aggregation rule: premises are adopted only with a four-fifths supermajority, but each member draws conclusions individually. Crucially, P1 embeds a meta-epistemic clause—namely, that the very aggregation rule being applied is reliable—which means that affirming P1 commits a member to accepting the legitimacy of premise-aggregation for this decision. In the actual vote, three members affirm P1 and P2 but deny C; two affirm all three. Under premise-based aggregation the group accepts P1 and P2 yet is forced to reject C, violating logical consistency; under conclusion-based aggregation the group accepts C by majority but must reject at least one premise, also yielding inconsistency. I claim this self-referential discursive loop shows that neither approach escapes contradiction, so the dilemma cannot be used to privilege conclusion-based methods—instead it undermines the idea that collective rationality requires logical closure at all. How do you respond?
