I'm drafting a seminar on free will and I'm bored of the usual determinism-vs-libertarianism ping-pong. Before we even get to the metaphysics, I want my students to spell out what an account of free will worth wanting actually has to deliver — the full desiderta, not just one intuition pump. Write me the spec: the phenomena it must cover (Frankfurt-style hierarchical identification, Strawson's reactive-attitude practices, split-brain cases where agency looks bifurcated), the distinctions it can't collapse (like between having free will and merely being exused, or between local control and diachronic authorship), and the empirical constraints it can't violate without being dead on arrival — especially anything from cognitive science about decision-making that undermines the naive ghost-in-the-machine picture.

Then there's the niche angle I'm tinkering with: call it 'scafolded authorship,' where a will is partly constituted by external precommitments — Ulysses contracts, constitutional rules, tech lockouts — that the agent designed but now can't revoke on a whim. Any adequate theory should say whether that counts as enhancing or eroding free will, and why. For each desideratum flag whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or just camp loyalty (compatibilist, libertarian, hard determinist). Point out any pair that's jointly unsatisfiable — I'm especially suspicious that scafolded authorship clashes with the demand for immediate executive control. Finally, briefly run Frankfurt's hierarchical model, Strawson's reactive-attitude view, and a basic agent-causal libertarianism through your list. Just the conceptual scorecard, no footnotes needed.
