I'm taking a seminar on free will and moral responsibility and serving this term on my university's peer review panel for non-violent academic integrity cases—plagiarism, unauthorized collaboration, minor data falsification—with about four hearings per semester, forty-five minutes each, and our recommendation (rehabilitative workshop versus punitive notation or suspension) submitted to the dean for final approval. I need a reusable decision procedure that translates the Dennett-Caruso debate on punishment—Dennett's compatibilist claim that moral competence and sufficient autonomy can ground deserved sanctions, against Caruso's claim that all punishment should be reconceived as a preventive, public-health-style quarantine with no retributive component—into ordered practical steps. The procedure should specify the questions to ask in sequence, what answers are sufficient to stop with a non-punitive recommendation, where it hands the decision to a value judgment rather than a fact, and where it is likely to misfire. To keep it from being pure theory, build in an independence check: a concrete, indicator-based screen that asks whether the agent's deliberation was decoupled enough from coercive background conditions—institutional surveillance pressure, severe economic duress, undiagnosed cognitive impairment—to make a punitive response appropriate rather than purely preventive. Run the procedure on three cases: a student who plagiarized knowing detection algorithms were active; a student who fabricated survey data to win a competitive grant; and a student with an undiagnosed executive-function disorder who violated collaboration rules they could not parse, where the independence check yields a counter-intuitive result that the panel will not want to accept. Mark explicitly what the procedure cannot settle, and include the point where it forces an answer the panel is likely to reject and must either override with reasons or defend.
