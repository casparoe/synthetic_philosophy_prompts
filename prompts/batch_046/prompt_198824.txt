I'm working through issues in democratic theory for a graduate seminar on boundary problems, and I've tried to articulate a consistent package of views about who belongs to the demos. Here is roughly where I've landed. First, I accept an all-affected-interests principle: anyone whose vital interests are substantially shaped by a collective decision should have democratic standing in that domain. Second, I reject citizenship, territorial residency, and species membership as morally arbitrary boundary conditions, so in principle the demos extends cosmopolitically and, where relevant, across species lines. Third, I hold that democratic standing requires some minimal form of agency or preference-capacity, though this may be satisfied through proxy, guardian, or institutional representation rather than direct deliberation. Fourth, I think future generations and sentient non-human animals should be included in relevant decision-making domains—especially ecological and long-range institutional design—not merely protected by present human advocates acting on their behalf. Fifth, I believe democratic autonomy requires strong self-legislation: there should be no external authority capable of overriding the collective will of the demos on matters internal to its shared life. Sixth, I maintain that practical or administrative constraints—such as the impossibility of registering preferences that do not yet exist, or communication barriers across species—should never determine membership; the boundary is a moral status, not a feasibility condition.

I want to know whether this package hangs together or whether I'm smuggling in incompatible motivations. For instance, extending standing cosmopolitically and cross-species while insisting on strong self-legislation seems to pull in different directions, and I'm unsure whether my allowance for proxy representation genuinely satisfies the agency condition in the third commitment or merely disguises a concession to practicality that the sixth rules out. I don't want to abandon any of these commitments lightly—each feels independently well-motivated—but I'd like an audit: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, and what the cheapest consistency-restoring revision would be in each case. Please be specific about which commitment bears the cost, order the tensions by severity, and don't treat every combination as problematic; I need to know where the actual fault lines lie.
