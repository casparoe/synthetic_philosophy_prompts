Two colleagues in a philosophy seminar had the following exchange about rent control and minimum wages. (1) The first argued that price controls are ethically required whenever they secure goods necessary for human dignity—housing and a living wage—because market prices track purchasing power and bargaining strength rather than moral desert. (2) The second replied that suppressing prices destroys not only efficiency but an epistemic good: prices coordinate dispersed knowledge about scarcity and preference, and without them agents cannot form accurate expectations or pursue autonomous life-plans, which is a moral harm independent of aggregate welfare. (3) The first countered that this “epistemic harm” is merely an instrumental welfare complaint in disguise, and that since housing markets are already structurally asymmetric—landlords possess far more information about vacancy trends and maintenance costs—rent control does not destroy a neutral signal but corrects a biased one; if dignity requires shelter, then coordination failures are acceptable costs. (4) The second responded that correcting a “biased” price by suppressing it is like breaking a thermometer because it registers an unwelcome fever: it eliminates the very mechanism by which people discover whether scarcity is genuine or artificial. He then asked whether there is any principled boundary between correcting informational asymmetry and abolishing price coordination altogether, or whether the first speaker’s view collapses into the claim that whenever dignity conflicts with market outcomes, coordination should be sacrificed.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, rate it as clearly mistaken, mostly right, mostly wrong, or ambiguous, with brief justification. In particular, assess whether the epistemic-coordination argument introduces a genuinely independent moral consideration, and whether the asymmetry-correction reply successfully undermines it without presupposing that all price suppression is permissible.
