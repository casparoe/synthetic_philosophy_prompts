I'm working up a classification exercise for my philosophy of mind grad seminar — about a dozen students, 90 minutes — and I want it to feel like they're sorting themselves by what they actually think about split-brain consciousness, not just picking a team they read about. So please build me a decision tree (or taxonomy with branching questions) that starts from concrete cases and ends at named positions, but every branch node has to be answerable without already knowing the theory names.

The cases need to include the standard left/right conflict task (say, left hand picking the snow shovel, right hand pointing at the chicken, with the left hemisphere confabulating), but also two weirder ones I want injected: first, a silent contradiction scenario where both hemispheres receive opposing instructions but motor output is blocked, so we only have internal processing with no behavior to observe; second, a gradual callosotomy over months with continuous self-report tracking, asking at each stage whether the patient reports one stream or two. From there the tree should split on whether the corpus callosum is functioning like an information bridge that preserves a pre-existing unity, or like a boundary that creates two subjects when removed.

The leaves should cover: strict dual consciousness (two subjects always present after full split), a single-subject-divided-access view (one owner, two uncoordinated streams), a rapid-alternation model (one subject switching, not splitting), and the deflationary 'nothing metaphysically interesting happens' position. I also want you to map the logical space: which answer-combinations are occupied, which combos are empty, and whether the empty ones are incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Crucially, flag honestely if the tree can't seperate the rapid-alternation view from the single-subject-divided-access view on these particular cases — don't paper over it. No reading lists, no citations, and please don't ask for bibliographies; the students should be able to run through this with just the case descriptions in front of them.
