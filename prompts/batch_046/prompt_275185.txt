I serve as a voting member on the governance board of our university's interdepartmental research computing cooperative, which pools departmental funds to maintain a shared GPU cluster. Demand now exceeds supply by roughly forty percent, and our first-come-first-served queue is breaking down; we must settle on an allocation mechanism by the end of this quarter.

Three proposals are before the board, each corresponding to a classic position in the socialist calculation debate, though none of our colleagues have named them that way.

Proposal One, the Lange-style option, would create an administrative allocation committee that sets monthly shadow prices for GPU hours within each research domain. The committee would gather self-reported marginal-utility estimates from labs, run a linear-programming optimization to clear supply against demand, and adjust quotas iteratively until shortages and surpluses vanish. Its defenders claim this preserves collective ownership of the cluster while still using dispersed information through an artificial price mechanism.

Proposal Two, the Hayek-style option, would issue each lab an annual budget of tradable compute credits and run a decentralized double-auction platform every week, allowing prices to emerge from bidding rather than from central computation. Its defenders stress that only this captures tacit, local knowledge—such as which graduate student faces a hard conference deadline—that no committee can aggregate, and they observe that pooled cooperative funding already separates capital ownership from use, so private property is not required for prices to signal scarcity.

Proposal Three, the Mises-style option, argues that without genuine private ownership of capital, entrepreneurial profit-and-loss accounting, and the disciplinary threat of bankruptcy, neither shadow prices nor artificial credit markets can perform real economic calculation. It urges dissolving the internal pool and distributing flat cloud-computing stipends so that labs contract with external providers whose prices reflect actual market scarcity.

Attached specifically to Proposal Two is a technical amendment: because labs might strategically inflate bids to hoard credits, the amendment proposes a zero-knowledge bidding protocol that conceals individual bids until after allocation, preventing retaliation and gaming. The board is divided over whether this solves strategic behavior or merely obscures the very information that prices are meant to reveal.

Please draft the board's written opinion recommending a concrete course of action—adopt one proposal, adopt one with conditions, or reject all three for an alternative—together with an explicit time frame for implementation, such as a pilot period and a review date. Your memo should apply the reasoning of the socialist calculation debate honestly: treat the strongest opposing argument—whether about local knowledge, strategic misrepresentation, or the impossibility of rational calculation without real markets—with serious engagement rather than dismissal. You need not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or specific textual citations, but any conceptual claims you associate with Mises, Hayek, or Lange should be accurate.

In your response, please state how confident you are in the recommendation you advance, and identify the single point about which you are least certain—whether that concerns the empirical feasibility of the bidding protocol, the fidelity of mapping these economic positions onto GPU allocation, or another issue.

Please conclude with a final line beginning exactly with Verdict: followed by the recommended option and the pilot or review timeline.
