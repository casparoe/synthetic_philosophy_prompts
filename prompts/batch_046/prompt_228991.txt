I am drafting a historical novel that traces the global aftermath of the 1815 Tambora eruption and the first cholera pandemic along imperial trade routes. In developing the narrative, I have sought advice from two consultants, and they have reached an impasse that I, as the author, am unable to resolve. I should like an impartial adjudication.

Dr. Selina Voss, an environmental historian, contends that treating the volcanic winter or Vibrio cholerae as 'historical actors' is a category error that invites environmental determinism. She maintains that agency requires intentionality, and that borrowing its vocabulary for meteorological or microbial forces obscures the human institutions—colonial grain tariffs, canal-building policies, inadequate sanitation governance—that actually mediated suffering. To present Tambora as a protagonist in the narrative, she warns, gives readers permission to interpret famine and epidemic as meteorological destiny rather than as outcomes structured by political and economic behaviour.

Marcus Chen, a narrative theorist, replies that this presupposes an anthropocentric ontology that contemporary philosophy of science and actor-network theory have rightly problematised. He distinguishes 'agent' (an intentional being) from 'actor' (any entity whose properties causally reshape the relational network in which humans operate). The eruption intended nothing, yet it reconfigured global temperature, crop yields, and migration patterns in ways no human decision could override. To omit this efficacy, he argues, is to treat nature as inert backdrop. He further suggests that one can focalise the eruption's effects without absolving human institutions—indeed, showing how the bacillus exploited specific trade routes makes imperial culpability sharper, not dimmer, because it reveals how human logistics gained lethal leverage through non-human properties.

Voss responds that even as a technical term, 'actor' will be anthropomorphised by readers. She invokes the political genealogy of climate determinism—accounts of societal collapse that allow rulers to escape responsibility—and insists that history remains partly a moral discipline concerned with assigning praise and blame. If microbes and monarchs share a dramatis personae, she says, the normative distinction between responsible agents and background conditions collapses.

Chen answers that history need not function exclusively as a moral tribunal, and that recognising non-human efficacy complicates rather than erases responsibility. The cholera outbreak of 1817–1823 was caused neither solely by Vibrio cholerae nor solely by British canal policy; it emerged from what he terms an assemblage—a coupling of distributed causal efficacy without moral equivalence. The novel, he proposes, could demonstrate that human decisions became lethal precisely because of the bacterium's properties, not despite them.

They have asked me to referee, and I honestly cannot. Please analyse the disagreement point by point: where is Voss correct about the ontology of agency and the risk of determinism; where is Chen correct about distributed causation and the narrative value of non-human efficacy; who is more right overall; and, crucially, what both parties are missing—whether they have collapsed a necessary distinction, overlooked a practical problem specific to novel-writing, or relied upon an implicit theory of historical explanation that weakens both positions.

Please write your answer in American English.
