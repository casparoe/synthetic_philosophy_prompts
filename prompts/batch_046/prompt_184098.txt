I am preparing a seminar programme on African philosophies of the self for advanced undergraduates, and I should like to open with a well-known film that the students already recognise but have not yet examined philosophically. Take Cast Away and read it through the lens of Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood — in particular the claim that personhood is not an intrinsic, self-standing property but is constituted through recognition, care, and reciprocal presence within a community. Chuck Noland’s shipwreck strips him of every social role and relational context; to survive psychologically he invents Wilson, maintaining language, moral orientation, and narrative continuity through a one-sided dialogue with an object that cannot answer back.

Treat both the film and the philosophy with equal seriousness, and do not reduce one to an illustration of the other. Ask whether Wilson represents a minimal form of relationality that preserves Chuck’s personhood in the absence of reciprocity, or whether the film ultimately shows that full personhood is only restored when he is re-embedded in actual human recognition upon rescue. Point to specific scenes — the volleyball’s creation and naming, the conversations about absence and loyalty, Chuck’s awkward re-entry into human society, and the final separation from Wilson — and consider whether Ubuntu illuminates these moments or whether the film resists the framework by privileging individual resilience and projecting relation onto something that lacks consciousness. Be precise about the tension between being-with-others as a structural feature of the self and being-recognised-by-others as an achievement requiring another mind; if the honest reading is that Cast Away ultimately defends a more individualist account of survival and moral agency, say so. Conclude by saying what this particular pairing adds to the philosophical debate that a purely conceptual argument about relational personhood might miss — especially regarding the limits of relationality when reciprocity is impossible, and whether solitude necessarily erodes personhood or merely transforms its conditions.
