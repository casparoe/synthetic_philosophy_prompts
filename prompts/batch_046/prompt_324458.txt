okay so in our grad seminar on language and logic, two people got stuck in this loop about vagueness and the sorites. Alex started with the standard heap thing -- 10,000 grains is a heap, take one away, still a heap, etc. But then he pivoted to this idea he calls tolerance drift: in practical reasoning we don't have a single sharp cutoff, but a sliding window of acceptable error that shifts with what's at stake. So negligent might have fuzzy edges in casual talk but tighten up in a courtroom, except the window itself has fuzzy edges too -- there's no hidden line, just a gradient of institutional tolerance.

Jordan said that's just contextualism with extra steps. If the window slides based on stakes, then for each context there's still a precise boundary, it's just that we don't know exactly where it is. Alex said no, that's exactly the mistake -- you're smuggling in sharpness at the meta-level. If the boundary for each context is itself vague, then you've got second-order vagueness all over again, and Jordan hasn't solved anything, just pushed it up.

Then Jordan brought up the classic epistemicist move: maybe there IS a sharp boundary, we just don't know it, like a hidden line in the sand. Alex said that's ontologically extravagant -- you're positing precise facts about where negligence begins that don't correspond to any feature of the world or our practices. Jordan countered that without sharp boundaries, logic breaks: if being-negligent is true at case n and false at case n+1, but there's no fact of the matter in between, then we've lost bivalence, and that undermines legal reasoning entirely.

They kept circling. Alex thinks Jordan is hiding behind a false precision that doesn't exist. Jordan thinks Alex is giving up on rational decision-making by accepting contradiction at the margins.
