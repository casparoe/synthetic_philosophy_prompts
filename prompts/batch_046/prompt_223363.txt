Exam question:
In Criteria of Negro Art, W. E. B. Du Bois contends that all art is propaganda and that Black artists bear a distinctive obligation to deploy beauty in service of racial truth and justice. He criticizes the pursuit of art for art's sake as an unaffordable luxury under conditions of systematic dehumanization, yet he also distinguishes propaganda that reveals reality from propaganda that obscures it. Some readers maintain that, for Du Bois, aesthetic excellence and political effectiveness are not in tension but are strictly identical in the domain of Black art, since any truthful and beautiful depiction of Black life automatically operates as counter-propaganda.

Assess this reading. In particular, address whether Du Bois's account depends solely on what we might call representational counter-propaganda—correcting false beliefs through accurate depiction—or whether it also requires ontological counter-propaganda, that is, altering the very framework through which Black humanity and beauty are recognized. Further, explain Du Bois's distinction between truthful and deceptive propaganda, discuss whether he permits any role for art that carries no explicit social message, and evaluate whether his position collapses aesthetic value into political utility.

Student answer:
The reading is largely correct. Du Bois holds that all art is propaganda, and Black artists must choose propaganda for truth over propaganda for falsehood. He distinguishes truthful propaganda—which reveals the reality of Black life and serves justice—from deceptive propaganda, which includes racist stereotypes and commercially motivated art that ignores social conditions. Because dominant culture systematically depicts Black people as ugly and inferior, any beautiful and truthful depiction automatically corrects those false beliefs, functioning as representational counter-propaganda. Du Bois does not require ontological counter-propaganda, because replacing false representations with true ones is sufficient to repair the framework for recognizing Black humanity. He rejects art for art's sake not because he denies beauty's value, but because beauty detached from reality is dishonest and fails to perform this corrective work. At the same time, he allows art without an explicit message—such as a landscape or a love poem—provided it depicts Black experience truthfully, since even such works contribute to representational correction. Therefore, in Du Bois's framework, aesthetic excellence and political effectiveness are functionally equivalent, though not literally identical: an artwork could be beautiful yet misleading, which would make it bad propaganda despite its aesthetic quality, so he does not fully collapse the two categories.
