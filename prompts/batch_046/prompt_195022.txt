I've been working through Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences in a small reading group that splits its time between philosophy of mind and AI alignment, and one member circulated a summary from an unnamed long-form essay that attempts to extend the book's thesis into contemporary machine learning. The summary runs as follows: Hofstadter and Sander claim that analogy is not a peripheral reasoning strategy but the actual substrate of cognition, which means that even basic perceptual categorization—recognizing a particular chair as a chair—operates by analogical mapping between stored mental prototypes and present sensory input, rather than by feature extraction or statistical pattern matching. From this, the essay infers that there are no meaningful modular boundaries in the mind: language, spatial reasoning, and mathematics all run on the same analogical engine, and therefore any artificial system that performs cross-domain analogical mapping at scale is, by definition, engaging in human-style cognition. The essay further claims that Hofstadter and Sander treat categorical vagueness not as a problem for cognitive science but as its central datum—categories are fluid slippages—and that critics who demand precise algorithmic mechanisms for analogical reasoning are fundamentally misunderstanding the project, since precision is antithetical to the fluid nature of thought. Finally, it concludes that because analogy operates largely below the level of conscious awareness, introspection can never reveal the analogical structure of one's own reasoning, making first-person reports about mental processes essentially useless data for a science of mind.

Something about this feels like it overreaches, but I am not sure where the line falls between a bold interpretive extension and an actual misreading. Is this a fair summary of the core claims in Surfaces and Essences? If any of the summary's conclusions about AI or scientific methodology do not actually follow from the book's premises, please say so explicitly, and distinguish between a provocative extension the authors might tolerate and a category error they would reject.
