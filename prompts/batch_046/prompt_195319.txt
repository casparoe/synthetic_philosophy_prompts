Below is the outline for an essay I am developing on the ethics of dramatizing the dead. I argue that the standard framing—whether filmmakers owe the audience historical accuracy or whether artistic license justifies deviation—misses a distinct category of wrong. I propose that we should think in terms of representational debt owed not to viewers but to the deceased themselves, mediated through the integrity of biographical practice. The essay distinguishes epistemic harm (misleading the audience about verifiable facts) from what I call dignitary harm: the corruption of the dead person's narrative autonomy. By narrative autonomy I do not mean that the dead literally suffer, but rather that living persons have an interest in the coherence and continuity of their life-story, and that this interest generates a residual claim on how that story is reconstructed after death. I inject a further, more specific claim: that one form of dignitary harm occurs not through falsehood but through the resolution of deliberate ambiguity. If a historical figure actively cultivated uncertainty about some aspect of their private life—perhaps through contradictory statements, destroyed correspondence, or strategic silence—a dramatization that selects one interpretation and renders it visually and narratively definitive does not merely risk error; it violates a kind of posthumous narrative autonomy by foreclosing the interpretive space the figure themselves preserved. This is different from lying, because the filmmaker may be selecting a plausible interpretation; it is wrong, on my view, because it treats the dead person's self-curated opacity as raw material for narrative closure rather than as a feature to be respected.

The essay then considers objections: that narrative autonomy is metaphorical and cannot ground obligations; that all biography involves selection and closure; and that respecting ambiguity would paralyze historical fiction. I sketch replies but am unsure about them.

Please comment on this outline and the arguments sketched within it. In particular, I would value your assessment of whether the distinction between epistemic and dignitary harm is genuinely sharp or collapses upon inspection; whether the concept of posthumous narrative autonomy, especially as applied to deliberately cultivated ambiguity, is philosophically coherent or merely poetic; and whether the framework is implausibly demanding—would it prohibit too much legitimate dramatization? Additionally, please evaluate whether the move from "the dead have interests in narrative coherence" to "filmmakers have obligations to preserve ambiguity" is valid, or whether it conflates respect for persons with fidelity to archival indeterminacy.

In your response, please separate what is relatively settled in philosophical and legal discussions of posthumous harm and representation from what is genuinely contested or speculative—especially the idea of narrative autonomy applied to ambiguity—so that contested claims are not presented as consensus. Do not present my framework as if it were the dominant view; identify where it diverges from standard accounts.
