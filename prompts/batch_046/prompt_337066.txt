Because the state cannot observe need directly—only behavioral proxies such as attendance logs, job-search documentation, or asset declarations—every screening rule generates false positives and false negatives that are then interpreted as moral failures rather than measurement errors. To keep this from becoming a generic welfare debate, inject a niche framing: treat the verification architecture as an “ontological filter,” analogous to a database schema that enforces entities not present in the raw domain. The activity should use roles, explicit rules, and small non-monetary stakes (reputation points or access to a shared pool resource) so participants experience the filter from both sides—applicant and administrator—before anyone names the dynamic. Include: the group size and role assignments; a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes; the rules and payoff structure; the specific interaction moment where participants should spontaneously realize the proxy has replaced the underlying need; a debrief that maps that moment and others onto performative production and epistemic asymmetry; an honest statement of which feature of the ontological-filter idea the activity cannot reproduce (likely the long-term institutional sedimentation of categories across years of policy); and a prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first run, with a fix (probably administrators over-identifying fraud because the proxy metrics reward confirmation bias). Build the scenario from first principles; do not depend on obscure empirical case studies, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The deliverable should be something I can run without philosophical jargon.
