I'm prepping a seminar on idealization and I keep running into Friedman's old billard-player move — you know, the F-twist where the expert behaves as if shes running geometry in her head but clearly isn't, used to defend unrealisitc assumptions in economics. Lately people stretch it to evolutionary biology: group-selection models treat populations as if they were rational agents competing, and since the predictions come out okay we're told not to worry about the ontology. I think there's a bait-and-switch happening with the mechanism. Can you lay out the correspondence table — hidden computation, observable shot, missing conscious access — for both the economic and evolutionary cases? Then tell me exactly where the analogy cracks, especially around the fact that billiards has real muscle memory substituting for the math, while group selection might lack any mechanistic replacement at all. Does the "who cares if it's false" conclusion survive once that gap is exposed, or was the analogy doing all the heavy lifting by smuggling in an underlying process we don't actually have?
