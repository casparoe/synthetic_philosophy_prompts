I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I'm trying to understand why our field evaluates reasoning in large language models the way it does. The standard institutional practice is this: a lab runs a model on multi-step reasoning benchmarks—mathematical proofs, logical deduction, or planning tasks—publishes the accuracy figures, and includes sample chain-of-thought traces that appear coherent. If both accuracy and trace coherence are high, the report typically concludes that the model possesses reasoning competence in that domain. What is conspicuously absent from almost every such report is a causal-dependency audit: a systematic check that perturbing or removing an intermediate logical step actually changes the model's final output, or that the model cannot reach the correct answer via a direct pattern-matching shortcut that bypasses the displayed reasoning entirely. In other words, we treat structurally correct intermediate text as sufficient evidence that the reasoning process caused the result, without testing whether the text is causally efficacious or merely epiphenomenal formatting.

This practice strikes me as philosophically loaded, especially given two famous inversions that are standard reference points in AI: Darwin's demonstration that sophisticated adaptive design can be generated by selection processes with no representational understanding of the design problem, and Turing's demonstration that correct formal symbol-manipulation can occur without any semantic comprehension of the symbols. Those cases suggest that high competence plus plausible structural output should not, by default, be read as evidence of comprehension. Yet our evaluation norms do exactly that.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of reasoning, mental representation, and the relation between behavior and understanding does this benchmark practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: must reasoning involve internal representational states that structurally mirror the logical relations being processed, or can a behaviorist or purely functionalist account license the same evaluation protocol? Is the practice strictly committed to one particular philosophy of mind, or is it philosophically underdetermined—compatible with rival views that differ precisely on whether competence implies comprehension? And if we replaced the implicit view with one that takes the Darwin/Turing separation of competence from comprehension as fundamental—treating structured outputs as potentially causally inert—what specific changes would be required in audit protocols, benchmark design, or the language of capability reports?
