Compare the "epistemic rupture" account of historical periodization—which treats labels such as the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution as tracking genuine discontinuities in the frameworks through which knowledge is produced, authorized, and transmitted—with the "institutional sedimentation" account, which holds that those same labels are retrospective artifacts generated by archival practices, disciplinary boundaries, and pedagogical needs rather than by lived historical reality. In particular, evaluate which account better accommodates the following hypothetical anomaly: suppose rigorous archival analysis demonstrated that a substantial body of texts conventionally classified as Renaissance humanist works were in fact produced by anonymous, collective scriptoria using compositional and transmission methods structurally identical to those of late-medieval monastic houses, with no distinguishable authorial voice or individualist ethos separating them from the earlier period. Would this finding compel a revision of the rupture model, or could it be assimilated as a boundary outlier without undermining the broader discontinuity? Conversely, assess whether the sedimentation model risks explaining away too much—reducing periodization entirely to a matter of later labeling—while failing to account for why certain clusters of material and intellectual practice consistently resist redistribution across the conventional chronological boundaries. Justify your answer by attending to what each theory is committed to saying about the ontology of historical change itself.
