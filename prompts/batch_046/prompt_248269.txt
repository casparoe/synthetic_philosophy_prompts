Here's an argument my study partner cooked up about Quine and values that seems to eat its own tail. (1) Quine's ontological criterion: we're only committed to entities that are values of bound varables in our best scientific theories. (2) Moral properties like wrongness never appear in physics, biology, etc., so they're not in our best overall theory. (3) You might say ethics is its own framework with its own quantifiers, but even there we quantify over actions and agents, not over wrongness as an object—the property is just predicated. (4) So moral values fail Quine's test in every framework: they're syntactic decoration, not ontology. (5) That means moral statements aren't truth-apt; they're just expressions of preference or social conditioning. (6) But Quine treats scientific rationality and evidential norms as genuinely constraining, not mere taste. (7) So the argument proves too much: it dissolves morality and also the normative backbone that supports Quine's own naturalism. (8) Therefore at least one premise is wrong. I'd like to keep (1) and (6) if possible, but I can't see how. Which step do you reject—maybe (3) gives ethics a special quantifier structure, or (4) confuses predication with non-existence? And what's the cost? If we save morality by loosening the criterion, do we open the door to every spooky abstract object?
