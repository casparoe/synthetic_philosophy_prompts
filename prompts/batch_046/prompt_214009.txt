I am an undergraduate taking a seminar on classical Chinese philosophy and philosophy of mind, and I need to draft a one-page proposal for an eight-week independent study. The topic is Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream, but I want to avoid the well-worn framing—Cartesian dream skepticism on one side, metaphysical transformation on the other—and instead investigate a narrower problem I have been trying to formulate: whether the passage supports what I will call “diachronic phenomenal self-model skepticism.” By that I mean the claim that the difficulty is not whether the external world is real, but whether a first-person subject can retain authoritative retrospective knowledge of which state—waking or dreaming—she was in, once her phenomenal self-model is discontinuous across the transition. The existing interpretations do not settle this because they either treat the “I” as a stable substance that merely mistakes its properties, or they collapse the problem into general perceptual unreliability without addressing the specific epistemic authority of autobiographical state-reporting.

Write the proposal with the following parts: (1) the precise research question stated in philosophical rather than textual-historical terms; (2) an explanation of why both the “identity/metaphysical” and the “Cartesian skeptical” readings leave this specific problem open; (3) the philosophical approach—conceptual analysis of first-person authority across state-transitions, drawing only on the conceptual resources available in the passage itself and on general arguments about phenomenal continuity—rather than an empirical or philological one, and a justification for why that approach can make progress where the alternatives stall; (4) a description of the deliverable: a 4,000-word argument that either reconstructs Zhuangzi as holding this specific form of skepticism, or shows that the text resists it and forces a different account of retrospective self-knowledge, together with a clearly stated failure condition (e.g., if the argument cannot distinguish state-transition skepticism from ordinary memory error without importing external psychological theories); (5) a realistic scope for eight weeks of solitary work; and (6) a brief statement of why this is worth doing for someone interested in philosophy of mind, even without empirical testing or historical commentary. Do not include a bibliography, literature review by citation, or requests for further reading; I will supply references myself. Keep the tone formal and suitable for a faculty sponsor.
