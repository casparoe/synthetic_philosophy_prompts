I'm a doctoral student working at the intersection of decision theory and normative ethics, and in my reading group we've been trying to make sense of expected-value fanaticism through a particular analogy that keeps surfacing in the literature and in seminar discussion. The comparison treats the EV-maximizing moral agent as structurally analogous to a gradient-descent optimizer operating over a highly non-convex loss landscape: the shallow local minima represent near-term, high-certainty goods with bounded value, while infinitely deep global minima correspond to probabilistically remote but astronomically valuable outcomes of the kind emphasized in longtermist reasoning. On this picture, the agent's willingness to neglect robust local convergence—ignoring immediate obligations or stable welfare improvements—in order to "tunnel" toward unreachable global optima is taken to reveal not just an empirical misjudgment of probabilities, but a kind of built-in structural irrationality in the decision rule itself, as though the optimizer were compelled to chase asymptotic improvements regardless of the energy cost or the unlikelihood of arrival.

Can you lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what maps onto the loss function, the parameter space, the learning rate or step size, the distinction between local and global minima, and the tunneling mechanism—then identify where the mapping breaks down in ways that matter for the normative conclusion? I'm especially interested in whether the analogy quietly imports assumptions about the agent's epistemic access to the full landscape, about the computability of intertheoretic value comparisons across radically different outcome spaces, or about the legitimacy of treating moral choice as a continuous optimization problem rather than as a discrete, structurally constrained commitment. Once those disanalogies are on the table, does the charge of fanaticism still attach to EV maximization per se, or is the critical weight being carried by the imported preference for local stability and bounded convergence? And if the analogy collapses in key respects, what conceptual model—without borrowing the machinery of gradient-based optimization—would better capture the logic of extreme expected-value tradeoffs?
