I have come to hold a strong ontological version of Braudel's longue durée thesis, not merely the methodological claim that historians should study geography and climate, but the view that slow-moving structures — continental shelf configurations, ocean current regimes, and multi-century climatic oscillations — constitute the real causal substrate of history, while events and individual agents are essentially epiphenomenal: they occur within the constraints set by these structures but never genuinely alter them. On this view, what we call political revolutions, diplomatic breakthroughs, or the decisions of particular rulers are best understood as surface disturbances, analogous to foam on a deep current, and any historical explanation that treats them as independent causal variables commits a category error. I am aware of the standard qualifications — that Braudel himself allowed for intermediate temporalities like conjoncture and événement — but I treat those as concessions to narrative convenience rather than ontologically serious categories.

What would have to be true for this position to be false, or at least seriously weakened? I am not asking for the usual historiographical reminder that people make history; I want the specific observations, formal results, or conceptual distinctions that should genuinely move me. For instance, if there are documented cases where a single event — say, a volcanic winter of unprecedented severity, or an engineered geographic intervention — permanently altered a climatic or geographic regime that had previously persisted for millennia, does that defeat the thesis or only complicate it? More generally, I want to know which defeaters a committed structuralist historian would themselves concede as counting against the view, and which they would dismiss as misunderstandings of the structural level. How exposed is the strong version? Does it risk being insulated into unfalsifiability — for example, by redefining every apparent structural break as merely the unfolding of a deeper, previously invisible long-term pattern — and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a thesis of this kind?

In particular, I am interested in whether there is a coherent middle position that preserves the explanatory priority of slow structures without reducing events and individuals to pure noise. If such a position exists, what does it cost? And if the strong version cannot accommodate even highly localized but irreversible geographic or climatic disruptions initiated by human action — such as sustained anthropogenic alterations to river basin dynamics or atmospheric circulation patterns — then does that failure reflect badly on the ontology, or merely show that the longue durée must now include human agency as one of its slow-moving variables? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether any of them have arguably already occurred.
