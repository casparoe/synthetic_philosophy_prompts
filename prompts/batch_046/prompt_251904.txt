I retired from running ecology labs about five years ago and lately I've been reading philosphy for fun. I've gotten really stuck on these superrationality experiments -- the Platonia dilemma and that Luring Lottery setup. From what I gather, in Platonia you've got two agents with identical reasoning choosing between cooperate and defect, and in the Luring Lottery the payoff structure is designed so that predicting your own choice changes what you should pick. The literature feels like it's been spinning its wheels forever. One camp, I think the decision-theorists, says superrationality is just expected utility done wrong: formalize the probabilities correctly and the paradox vanishes. The other camp, maybe causal decision theorists, says recognizing shared reasoning means your choice is evidence about the other agent, so you have to update on yourself, which breaks standard Nash reasoning.

My hunch, probably colored by my old work on population symetry, is that the stall isn't about rationality norms at all. It's about whether "identical reasoning" is even coherent -- like asking two particles to have exactly the same state without hidden variables breaking the mirror. But maybe I'm just importing physics metaphors where they don't belong.

So can you diagnose what's actually going on? Are these sides answering different questions -- one about norms, one about causal structure? Is there a hidden empirical claim about how people predict copies of themselves? Or is it bedrock intuition about whether you can be surprised by your own choice? It's occured to me that maybe the whole framing is ill-posed. Given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress -- a formal distinction between epistemic and causal symmetry, a psychology experiment, or admitting the question should be dissolved? I don't want a literature survey; I want to know what kind of disagreement this is and whether it should even resolve.
