As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of medicine, I have been studying Thomas Szasz’s argument that mental illness is a myth—not in the sense that psychological suffering is unreal, but that its framing as a literal medical disease is a metaphor that conceals moral conflicts and problems in living. I want you to write a short story—roughly five hundred to eight hundred words—that tests this idea in a context that is not the usual clinical setting. Imagine a near-future interdisciplinary research institute where an administrative panel has formalized a diagnostic category, “epistemic rigidity disorder,” applied specifically to aging scientists who persistently reject dominant theoretical paradigms despite peer consensus. The protagonist should be a retired natural scientist who receives this diagnosis not because of any demonstrable neurocognitive deficit, but because her sustained methodological dissent has been interpreted as a pathological inability to update belief. The narrative should dramatize the Szaszian boundary question: is her condition a brain disease discoverable through biological markers, or is a social and moral disagreement being disguised in the language of medicine? Avoid a simplistic anti-psychiatry fable; instead, preserve the ambiguity Szasz himself insisted upon—namely, that genuine brain diseases exist and can alter behavior, but that labeling a disagreement about evidence or values as an illness collapses a distinction that should be kept intact.

Please also include a brief reflective appendix—no more than one paragraph—stating where your story takes liberties with Szasz’s actual positions or depends on contested readings of his work (for example, his libertarian politics, his specific claims about schizophrenia, or the degree to which he allowed any biological contribution to mental distress). Finally, say explicitly what argument or clarification about the line between real brain pathology and metaphorical “mental illness” would cause you to change the story’s central framing, and identify the specific step in your reasoning that is most exposed to failure.
