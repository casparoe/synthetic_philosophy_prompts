I'm drafting the final exam for my PhD seminar in formal epistemology and decision theory, and I need one 90-minute essay question on Good's theorem on the value of information. The question must be unanswerable by simple recitation: students should not be able to earn marks merely by stating the theorem, sketching its standard proof, and listing familiar objections such as psychological costs of knowing. Instead, the crux should require them to reconstruct the theorem's conditions from within a modified formal framework.

Please design a self-contained question that introduces a decision problem with an explicit state space, action space, and utility function defined entirely within the prompt, together with a hypothetical belief-revision rule—call it convex-hull updating—under which an agent's posterior is always a convex combination of the prior's extreme points, even when observed evidence would, under standard Bayesian conditioning, fall outside that hull. The student should be asked whether Good's result—that free information never reduces expected utility—still holds under this rule, and if not, exactly which step in the proof breaks.

Provide a model answer with five required moves: (i) a precise statement of Good's theorem with its premises (Bayesian updating, fixed utility function, costless information); (ii) an explanation of why convex-hull updating violates the law of total probability in the form used in the conditioning argument; (iii) a concrete structural example in which acquiring the signal leads to an ex-post action with lower expected utility than the ex-ante optimal action; (iv) a clear verdict on whether this constitutes a counterexample to the theorem or only a violation of one of its premises; and (v) a brief normative implication for claims about the value of information in scientific reasoning.

Also provide a marking scheme out of 20 marks, with the five moves above each assigned 4 marks. For each, include a one-line description of the characteristic mistake that should cost marks—such as conflating the update-rule failure with a failure of expected-utility maximization, or misidentifying the broken step as the linearity of expectation rather than the conditioning identity.

Please do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not include verbatim quotations from real texts; if you reference any work, paraphrase without quotation marks. All formal details should be embedded in the question so that a respondent without web access can answer entirely from the prompt.
