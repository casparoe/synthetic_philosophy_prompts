I have recently found myself in the middle of a practical disagreement that has made a conceptual pattern visible to me for the first time. In discussions about whether I have reason to continue with a burdensome commitment—first a graduate research project, then a volunteer role that demands far more emotional labor than I anticipated—my interlocutors consistently respond to my expressed lack of motivation in the same distinctive way. Rather than arguing that I ought to act regardless of what moves me, or showing that the commitment actually connects to concerns I already hold, they construct a kind of idealized counterpart to me: a version that is fully informed, free of the particular biases or fatigue that color my present judgment, and consistently rational. They then observe that this counterpart would be motivated to continue, and treat that counterfactual motivation as if it resolved my original question. The move is not the straightforward claim that obligations apply to people whether they feel like it or not, because it appeals explicitly to motivation; yet it does not seem to be the ordinary attempt to show that I already care about the outcome, because the motivation belongs to a substituted subject rather than to me as I actually am. I have seen this pattern in debates about professional duty, climate obligations, and care work.

I want to know whether philosophy of practical reason has named this specific configuration. If there is an established concept—perhaps near the well-known distinction between reasons that depend on an agent's actual concerns and reasons said to apply regardless—how exactly does it relate to that distinction? Does it amount to a subtle form of one side, or is it a genuinely separate move? If there is no precise term, I would like it named and bounded: what distinguishes this proxy-substitution maneuver from ordinary rational idealization, from paternalism, and from the claim that reasons exist independently of an agent's actual motivational set? I care more about the exact boundaries of the phenomenon—what it is, what it is not, and where it collapses back into familiar positions—than about receiving a bare label.
