For a comparative ethics seminar I am drafting a position paper that integrates Jain metaphysics with contemporary moral philosophy, and I want a consistency audit before presenting. Here are my commitments: (1) I accept anekāntavāda in a strong form—reality is ontologically multi-faceted (anekānta), so every substantive judgment expresses only one naya (standpoint) and must carry the syāt qualifier, meaning it is true only in some qualified respect. (2) I apply syādvāda to ethics: conflicts such as deontological versus consequentialist verdicts in hard cases are typically not errors but complementary partial descriptions of a complex moral reality, each valid from its angle. (3) I am a moral realist—moral properties exist mind-independently, and genuinely contradictory moral judgments, as opposed to merely different emphases, cannot both be true. (4) I deny strong moral relativism: if two judgments are logically incompatible and not just perspectival variations, at least one party is in error. (5) I hold that anekāntavāda itself is not merely one philosophical option among equals but is uniquely correct about the logic of judgment and the ontology of being. (6) My methodological stance is a cross-culturally constrained reflective equilibrium: I treat persistent moral disagreement as a diagnostic for one-sidedness rather than immediate proof of falsehood, though I allow that some particular judgments must eventually be discarded if they fail to achieve coherence with the broader set.

Please go through these commitments and identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which appear tense but dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, specify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what that revision costs me—whether in metaphysics, ethics, or method. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most conflict overall, and whether adopting it forces me to abandon either anekāntavāda or moral realism. Be specific about whether the tension lies in self-application, in the treatment of contradiction, or in the interaction between standpoint pluralism and reflective equilibrium.
