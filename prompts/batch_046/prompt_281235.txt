Consider the slogan frequently invoked in defenses of tranquilism—the position that only the absence of craving and suffering carries moral weight—namely: "What is present adds nothing; only absence can count in favor." In a recent seminar discussion on whether ethical theory must include positive goods such as joy, knowledge, or aesthetic flourishing, a student deployed this line to argue that promoting such states is ethically indifferent, since they are mere presences rather than the removal of suffering. I ask you to treat the slogan as a piece of language requiring precise disambiguation, not as a settled argument. Lay out the distinct theses it could be expressing—distinguishing metaphysical claims about the ontology of value, normative claims about the boundaries of moral duty, psychological claims about human motivation, and semantic claims about the meaning of "good" in this context. For each interpretation, indicate whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or plainly false, and say specifically whether that reading actually supports the student's dismissal of obligations to foster positive flourishing. Structure your response as a menu of readings with verdicts attached, rather than advocating for a single interpretation.
