I'm part of a small interdisciplinary reading group—mostly historians of science and a couple of philosophers—trying to determine where the conceptual labor actually needs to happen regarding non-human causation in historical explanation. We've been grappling with how germs, climate shifts, and volcanic events are treated as historical actors rather than mere background conditions, and we've noticed that these discussions tend to collapse quickly into either environmental determinism (the eruption explains the revolution) or an unreflective anthropomorphism (the pathogen pursues a strategy). Rather than asking for a literature review, I want to know: if you had to advise this group on which three open conceptual problems in this space are genuinely worth sustained collaborative work over the next two years—say, for shaping how we frame arguments in joint papers—how would you rank them?

Specifically, I am interested in problems that sit at the boundary of metaphysics and historiography: whether attributing causal efficacy to climate patterns requires a distinct ontology of collective action, or whether it remains a heuristic that risks reifying correlation; whether the primary danger of environmental determinism is empirical (we systematically overestimate non-human explanatory weight) or normative (it displaces human agency in ways that carry moral and political consequences); and whether there is a defensible middle position between treating a volcanic winter as a brute physical cause and treating it as something like a co-author in a historical narrative. But I do not want a survey. I want a defended prioritization: which of these problem-types deserves our limited attention first, which is likely overrated or destined to dissolve into semantic disputes, and what governing criteria—tractability for scholars without specialized training in metaphysics, payoff for concrete historical explanation, or risk of generating conceptual confusion that spills into policy—should determine that ordering? Furthermore, if you think there is a more important line of work we are neglecting—perhaps the problem of how to weigh counterfactual dependence when both institutional choices and non-human forces are present, or the challenge of translating causal claims across vastly different scales (microbial, meteorological, civilizational)—make the case for why it should displace one of the three I have sketched.
