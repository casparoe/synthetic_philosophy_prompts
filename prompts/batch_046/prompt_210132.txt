so ive been following the epiphenomenalism vs phenomenal judgment literature and honestly it feels like its been stuck in the same loop for decades. chalmers-style paradox: if phenomenal states are causally inert then they cant cause our beliefs about them, so our phenomenal judgments are unreliable, which undermines the premise that we know we have them. standard replies are always one of three - phenomenal concepts are direct and dont need causal linkage, the judgments are actually tracking neural correlates, or introspection is just a special kind of self-modeling that works differently. but every conference talk just re-runs these and nobody budges.

heres my hunch: the stall happens because phenomenal judgment is doing double duty. one side treats it as an immediate access report - like noticing the redness in your visual field - while the other treats it as a theoretical attribution you make when you say consciousness is non-physical. those have totally different reliability conditions, and the causal-inertness objection only clearly bites the second kind if you assume all belief formation needs causal interaction. so maybe we arent disagreeing about epiphenomenalism at all; were just using one phrase for two cognitive processes.

is that right? is this a verbal disagreement masked as metaphysical? or is there a substantive split about whether direct access reports can be causally disconnected from their targets without losing warrant? maybe theres a hidden empirical question about whether introspection actually involves causal feedback loops. and what would progress even look like - drawing the access/attribution distinction sharply, running an experiment, or admitting the question is ill-posed because phenomenal judgment was never a unified kind? tell me what you think the disagreement actually is, and whether my access-vs-attribution split fixes anything or just moves the problem.
