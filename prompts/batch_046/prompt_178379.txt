I have long considered myself someone who does not stay silent when others are unfairly diminished—whether by correcting a false impression or defending a colleague's contribution. Two weeks ago, during a formal departmental review, I interrupted a senior executive who was attributing my teammate's analysis to another division; I spoke up despite the risk of being seen as insubordinate, and I took this as confirmation of my nature. Yet yesterday, during an informal team lunch, a peer casually mischaracterized that same teammate's work in a minor, socially inconsequential way, and I said nothing, reasoning that the setting was too casual to justify tension. The reversal is unsettling: I acted with apparent conviction in the high-stakes, hierarchical setting, but retreated in the low-stakes, peer-level one. Should I take this pattern as proof that I have no stable disposition toward fairness—only reactive habits shaped by audience size, institutional hierarchy, and anticipated social cost? Or can these contrasting responses still belong to a single, coherent moral outlook that distinguishes between contexts without being merely determined by them?
