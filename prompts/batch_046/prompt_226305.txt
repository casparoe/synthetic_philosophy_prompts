heres a thing that bugs me about how archaeology actually works on the ground. When heritage boards evaluate a site for protection, they have these unwritten rules about what counts as evidence for a "social institution." If you dig up a cluster of identical loom weights and spindle whorls spread across like five houses, theyll happily list "textile production institution" in the report and protect the site. But if you dig up five houses that all have the exact same weird symbolic marking on their doorposts and the same kind of broken ceramic vessel in the corner, they wont call that a "shared ritual institution" unless you also find a temple or some writing. Same pattern—standardized material stuff across households—but one gets you an economic institution and the other gets you "maybe symbolic behavior, needs more proof."

What philosophical view about beliefs and objects is baked into that difference? Does it assume that economic practices leave harder traces than religious ones, or is it really about how we think inference works—like, that you need different kinds of evidence to infer a belief-system vs a production-system? And is that consistent with the idea that beliefs are just as "real" as workshops? Also, if we actually applied the same redundancy rules to both—say, requiring three independent object categories for any institutional claim—what would have to change in how we write heritage reports?
