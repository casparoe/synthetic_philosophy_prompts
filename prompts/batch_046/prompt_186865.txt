I'm preparing a ninety-minute session for a philosophy reading group of second-year graduate students who have separately taken courses in philosophy of language and in metaethics but have never worked through their intersection. They are familiar with Frege's principle of compositionality and have read basic presentations of Blackburn's quasi-realist project and Gibbard's norm-expressivist framework, yet they treat the Frege-Geach problem as a minor technical footnote about linguistic embedding rather than a structural challenge. I want to teach them why pure expressivism faces a genuine crisis when moral sentences appear inside conditionals, not merely a puzzle that can be patched with auxiliary machinery.

Plan the explanation for me rather than delivering it. First, specify the exact dependency order: must Frege's point about the contribution of an embedded clause to the overall content come before or after the expressivist claim that unembedded moral sentences express non-cognitive attitudes, and how should I move from the unembedded case to the embedded case without allowing the students to conflate expressing an attitude with being part of a compound judgment? Second, identify the single carrying example that should bear the weight of the session—I am considering Geach's classic scenario involving the wrongness of murder and the wrongness of inducing a sibling to commit it, but I want you to decide whether that vehicle is optimal or whether a parallel construction using institutional rules or epistemic modals would serve better; in either case, state precisely what properties the example must possess (for instance, that the same clause must be available both for direct assertion and for embedding) and what it must avoid (for instance, inviting a purely psychological or belief-desire reading). Third, map the misconceptions in the order they will arise: I expect they will initially suppose that the embedded clause expresses a suppressed or background attitude; then they will propose that expressivists can treat conditionals as coherence relations among attitudes; and finally they will assimilate the difficulty to Frege's indirect-reference puzzle, assuming the standard sense-reference distinction solves it. For each misconception, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and neutralized, and characterize the conceptual error it involves. Fourth, construct one end-of-session check question that distinguishes a student who grasps the structural problem from one who has only retained the slogan that expressivism cannot handle embedding. Fifth, state openly what should be left out at this level—whether the dynamics of recent hybrid cognitivist-expressivist accounts, the formal apparatus of dynamic semantics, or the distinction between semantic and pragmatic embedding—and justify that omission.

To keep the session from feeling like a repetition of any standard survey presentation, frame both the carrying example and the misconception map around a specific, somewhat non-standard distinction between force-preservation and content-preservation in embedded contexts, so that the students must track what happens to the expressive force of a clause separately from what happens to its conceptual contribution.
