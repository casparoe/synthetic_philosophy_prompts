Compare the logic of an individual Ulysses contract—binding one's future self against akrasia or time-inconsistent preferences—with the logic of constitutional precommitment, where a polity binds future majorities against democratic erosion or fiscal profligacy. The central puzzle is meta-binding: in personal precommitment, the present agent retains authority over the binding mechanism, whereas a constitutional clause (say, an entrenched amendment rule or an independent central bank) must survive attempts by current actors to unbind it. Is constitutional precommitment therefore a direct analog to tying oneself to the mast, or is it better modeled as an equilibrium strategy in a repeated interaction among political elites? To sharpen the comparison, distinguish hard entrenchment—formal rules with high amendment costs—from soft entrenchment—normative conventions that raise the price of deviation without legal barriers. Does this hard/soft distinction have a meaningful counterpart in individual self-binding, and if not, does that imply constitutional precommitment addresses a collective coordination failure that personal Ulysses contracts never face?
