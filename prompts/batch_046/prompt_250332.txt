I am a retired field epidemiologist who spent three decades modeling infectious-disease dynamics in Southeast Asia, and since leaving active research I have been studying philosophy of medicine with particular attention to whether health and disease are natural kinds discoverable through biology and statistics alone, or whether normative judgments about which human functions matter are inseparable from the very concept of disease. I now serve on my hospital’s Diagnostic Criteria Review Board, and we must decide on a concrete proposal that forces this abstract debate into institutional policy.

A working group has submitted a revision to the diagnostic criteria for chronic post-infectious fatigue syndromes. The proposal eliminates all patient-reported quality-of-life measures and defines the condition exclusively through quantifiable biomarker deviations—specifically, deviations measured not against contemporary clinical norms but against a reconstructed “ancestral baseline” metabolic profile derived from pre-industrial population data. The proponents argue that this restores scientific objectivity by removing subjective bias, mood effects, and social-context confounds from diagnosis; opponents reply that selecting an ancestral reference class is not a value-neutral statistical move but already presupposes a particular, contested account of proper human physiological function, and that without normative judgment about which capacities genuinely matter to a patient’s life we are not identifying a disease but merely labeling statistical outliers relative to an arbitrarily chosen past.

Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s board meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve the revision, reject it, or approve it subject to clearly specified conditions—including an explicit time frame for implementation or for a further evidence review. The reasoning must treat the strongest naturalist consideration with genuine seriousness: acknowledge that patient-reported measures are indeed vulnerable to expectation and contextual bias, and that anchoring diagnosis to an external biological reference could improve inter-rater reliability and reduce diagnostic inflation. At the same time, it must engage the normativist objection that the ancestral-baseline framework smuggles in a vision of human flourishing without admitting it, and that stripping out functional-impact assessment risks classifying people as diseased on purely statistical grounds even when their lived capacities remain intact. The memo should be structured as a document the board can vote on, with an actionable bottom line rather than personal philosophical guidance, and it should include an honest account of the weightiest consideration that favors the side you ultimately reject.
