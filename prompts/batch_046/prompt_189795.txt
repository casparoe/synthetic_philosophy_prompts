I have recently been forced to think seriously about consciousness and personal identity after a family member suffered a neurological disruption that fragmented their sense of unified awareness. Reading Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater together with reports of the classic split-brain literature has made me suspect that the standard philosophical narration of those experiments quietly imports the very model Dennett wants to eliminate.

Analyze the 1960s–1970s split-brain research conducted by Sperry and Gazzaniga—particularly the episodes in which a patient's left hand, guided by the right hemisphere, selects an object linked to a stimulus shown to the left visual field, while the language-dominant left hemisphere offers a spoken explanation tied to the different stimulus shown to the right visual field—through the lens of the Cartesian theater versus Dennett's multiple drafts model.

Structure your answer in four parts:

1. The disciplined mapping (about 250 words): Identify the "subject" or subjects in each framework; assign roles to the left hemisphere's verbal report, the right hemisphere's motor response, and the experimenter's query; and specify what counts as the primary evidence and what counts as explanation in each theory.

2. The hidden Cartesian theater (about 200 words): Show exactly where the standard interpretation assumes a central screen or unified audience—especially in the tendency to treat the left hemisphere's spoken answer as the patient's authentic belief and the right-hemisphere behavior as an alien intrusion. What does this assumption commit us to about the unity of consciousness?

3. The multiple drafts account and its strains (about 250 words): Lay out the predictions of the multiple drafts model—post-hoc editing, distributed drafting, no need for a second theater in the right hemisphere. Then identify the strain: can the model explain why the left hemisphere consistently dominates narrative authority rather than producing competing drafts of equal weight? Does it require an auxiliary account of hemispheric specialization?

4. What the episode teaches the theory (about 200 words): Is this a confirmation, an anomaly, or an unanticipated case? If the left hemisphere's confabulation is precisely the kind of post-hoc drafting Dennett describes, does the case strengthen his view? Or does the patient's persistent first-person sense of being one person, despite behavioral disunity, reveal a gap in the framework—perhaps about temporal integration or ownership—that the model does not obviously fill?

Keep the mapping precise rather than metaphorical. I want no bibliography, no citation list, and no reading recommendations—only the conceptual mechanics.
