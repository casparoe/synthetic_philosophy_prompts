I serve as a senior documentation analyst for a national historical review board that produces standardized explanatory assessments for declassified local-level perpetrator files from the 1940s. Because our board must sign off on every assessment within a thirty-minute window, reviewers are forbidden from consulting external archives once a file is opened, and the same procedure must be reusable across dozens of case records with varying completeness, I need an operational protocol—not a one-time interpretation—that translates the dispute between Christopher Browning’s situational account in Ordinary Men and Daniel Goldhagen’s cultural-character account in Hitler’s Willing Executioners into an ordered decision procedure. Rather than treating situation, culture, and individual disposition as competing hypotheses to be selected among, the protocol should apply what I call a dominance-and-residue rule: first test whether documented situational coercion (explicit orders, peer dynamics, organizational sanctions, or credible threats) was sufficient to produce the observed behavior, and if so assign situational dominance without stopping; second, test whether the actor exceeded minimal situational requirements in ways that align with an available cultural-normative frame (voluntary initiative, ideological language in personal documents, selection for cruelty, or refusal of exit options); if yes, assign cultural salience; third, only assign the remaining unexplained behavioral variance to dispositional character, flagging this step explicitly as a judgment call rather than an empirical deduction. For each test, specify what counts as sufficient documentary evidence, where the answer must be “insufficient data—escalate to board judgment,” where the procedure returns “unassessable” rather than defaulting, and what sign-off is required.

Present the deliverable as a flowchart in words: the ordered evidentiary questions, the answer thresholds, the escalation points, and the reviewer constraints. Then apply it to three worked cases drawn from typical file contents: (a) a reserve police battalion member documented in a 1942 shooting operation who formally requested transfer out, was denied, and then participated in the killings without further protest; (b) a municipal bureaucrat who, without direct written orders, redesigned a property-confiscation form to accelerate processing and used explicitly antisemitic language in internal memoranda; and (c) a local party organizer who arranged deportation logistics strictly according to received instructions, never exceeded procedural requirements, but kept meticulous personal records praising the efficiency and cleanliness of each transport. One of these—likely case (a)—should yield a result that assigns situational dominance and therefore points away from individual culpability, which my board will find morally uncomfortable; you should address that discomfort explicitly but without resolving it, noting that the procedure classifies explanatory weight, not moral responsibility. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: it cannot determine whether a culture was “willing” in Goldhagen’s population-level sense using only a single file, it cannot substitute for psychological evaluation when behavioral traces are absent, and it is designed for individual file assessment only, not for writing collective historical narratives. Keep all steps feasible for a reviewer with general historical training but no specialized psychological or sociological expertise, and mark every point where human judgment is irreducible.
