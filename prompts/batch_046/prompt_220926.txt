I am preparing a graduate seminar on the ethics and epistemology of collective remembrance, and I want the students to work through a concrete institutional case rather than remain at the level of abstract principle. Take the Alamo of 1836—not merely the siege itself, but the century-long transformation of the site into a shrine of Anglo-Texan heroism, set against the archival record that complicates the standard narrative: the presence of Tejano defenders such as Juan Seguín, the fact that the Republic of Texas sought to preserve chattel slavery in defiance of Mexico’s 1829 abolition, and the subsequent erasure of both elements from popular commemoration. Analyze this episode through the lens of the conflict between collective memory and the historical record, paying particular attention to a distinction I have been using—what I call “mnemonic justice,” the claim that a community’s lived, transmitted narrative carries normative weight that should not be overridden by archival correction, versus “epistemic justice,” which holds that the record must correct memory when the two diverge, especially where the divergence serves the interests of dominant groups.

I want a disciplined mapping: identify the specific bearers of the collective memory (Anglo-Texan commemorative organizations, state curriculum boards, museum curators), state precisely which claims in the memorial narrative the archival record contradicts, and show how each of the two positions—mnemonic versus epistemic justice—would adjudicate concrete conflicts over monument design, school-text framing, and the curation of the Alamo museum itself. Then be honest about where the framework is silent or breaks: does it have resources for cases where the memory is internally contested, as with Tejano descendants whose family narratives also diverge from both the heroic myth and the critical archive? And what does the Alamo case ultimately show about the broader theory—is it a straightforward confirmation, an anomaly that forces a revision of the distinction, or a demonstration that the distinction was never really about justice but about whose institutional power controls the site? Do not offer a bibliography; work from the well-known historical contours and from the conceptual structure I have laid out.
