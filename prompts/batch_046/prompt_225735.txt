Write an essay on whether Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model of consciousness genuinely eliminates the Cartesian theater, or whether it merely redistributes theatrical properties across parallel processes without removing the need for a unified experiential audience. Begin by precisely defining the Cartesian theater as Dennett characterizes it: the conception of a central screen or audience in the brain where diverse sensory inputs converge for a singular observer. Then lay out the core mechanisms of the multiple drafts model—parallel, distributed processing streams, the absence of a canonical narrative, and the editorial processes that generate coherence retrospectively. Next, examine the disanalogy that critics often stress: in the theater model, the audience is spatially centralized, whereas in Dennett's account the editorial function that selects and binds content operates over time and across modules, producing what might be called a diachronically distributed theater. Weigh the argument that removing a spatial center is inadequate if there remains a functional center—some process playing the role of the subject to whom a given draft is presented. Consider the counter-argument that functional centralization is not true centralization, since no single module enjoys privileged access and every access is itself just another draft being edited. Reach and defend a clear verdict: does the multiple drafts model successfully banish the Cartesian theater, or does it preserve the theater's essential structure in a fragmented, temporal form? Your conclusion should specify exactly which feature of conscious experience—if any—must be abandoned for the anti-theatrical position to hold without residue.
