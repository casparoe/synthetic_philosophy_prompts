I am drafting a short philosophical essay for an internal workshop on metaphysics and philosophy of mind. The central move applies Frege's distinction between sense and reference to identity statements involving synthetic or substrate-transferred persons. I present the core argument in outline form below and would appreciate focused comments on its coherence and on whether the analogy to Frege's original puzzle holds or collapses.

Outline:

Consider a gradual-replacement scenario in which every biological neuron in a human brain is replaced by a functionally identical silicon analog, preserving all psychological continuity. At the conclusion, we ask whether the claim that the post-replacement entity is identical to the pre-replacement person is cognitively analogous to the familiar astronomical identity in which Venus is discovered to be both the Morning Star and the Evening Star—informative because the two singular terms have different senses—or whether it is closer to a tautology like asserting that the Morning Star is itself.

I argue that if we treat the pre-replacement person and the post-replacement entity as presenting the same individual under different modes—biological substrate versus synthetic substrate—then the identity statement carries genuine cognitive value: it conveys something not contained in either term alone, just as the astronomical discovery does. From this, one might infer that substrate-invariant personal identity requires an explicit identity claim that is never merely tautological.

However, I also raise an internal tension. If the mode of presentation for a person necessarily includes substrate properties, then any change in substrate, even one that preserves all functional and psychological properties, generates a new sense. Consequently, any cross-substrate identity claim would always be informative. This seems to make personal identity either implausibly fragile—no identity survives replacement—or implausibly cheap—identity becomes an open empirical question about reference rather than a metaphysical continuity. I am unsure whether Frege's framework resolves this tension or merely relocates it from metaphysics to semantics.

What I need from you:

1. Is the analogy to Frege's puzzle properly drawn, or does applying the sense-reference distinction to singular terms for persons violate assumptions in Frege's original account?
2. Does the argument conflate epistemic informativeness with metaphysical conditions for identity?
3. Can the mode of presentation of a person be characterized more precisely so as to avoid the fragility-cheapness dilemma?

Please respond as if commenting on a draft for revision: direct, rigorous, and focused on the philosophical machinery rather than on style or bibliographic recommendations.
