I'm on the strategic planning committee for the Meridian County Library Consortium and next month we're voting on whether to shutter three of our eight branches and consolidate resources into a centralized digital archive. Our chair—he came over from urban planning—has been pushing this "outside view" approach where we treat our branch network as belonging to a referance class of post-industrial municipal cultural institutions. He's citing base-rate figures suggesting roughly 60 percent consolidation within 30 years for these kinds of systems, and he wants that macro forecast to dominate our decision even though local circulation data shows two of the targeted branches holding steady or growing. There's also this philosophical problem about how you even pick the right reference class: he keeps comparing us to Roman provincial library networks and to 1970s urban renewal projects, but those feel like completely different animals with different failure modes. I think the deeper issue is whether applying empire-decline curves to a modern hybrid physical-digital institution creates a forecasting trap—where we rationally update our beliefs every year based on local signals but stay anchored to the same macro pattern, slowly draining resources through a sequence of individually reasonable cuts.

Anyway, I need you to draft a short policy memo for the committee that takes a definite position—either approve the consolidation or reject it—not just lay out options. Include clear criteria we can actually apply in future votes, and honestly treat the strongest argument for the side that loses, which is probably that ignoring base rates is just inside-view hubris that has bankrupted comparable institutions before. Write it in American English, keep it readable and formal, and please don't ask us to look up a bibliography—this has to stand on its own for people without web access.
