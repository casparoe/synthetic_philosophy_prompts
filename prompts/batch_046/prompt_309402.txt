In my graduate seminar on distributive ethics, two doctoral students—Elena and Marcus—became deadlocked over a modified rescue scenario designed for autonomous triage systems: a medical drone can rescue either one critically injured pilot, who will die without immediate aid, or five passengers with serious but non-immediately-fatal injuries, but not both, due to fuel and payload constraints. Elena opened by defending a position derived from John Taurek’s argument that moral duties run to individuals rather than to aggregates; since each of the six people generates an identical, non-additive claim to rescue, the drone should not maximize headcount but should assign each person an equal chance—effectively flipping a coin between saving the pilot and saving the five—because saving five is not five times as important as saving one.

Marcus replied that Elena misinterprets the structure of competing claims: even if duties do not aggregate linearly, they still accumulate, with the pilot possessing one claim of full weight and each passenger possessing an identical claim, yielding five reasons against one, so the drone ought to save the larger group. Elena countered that Marcus illegitimately sums obligations: if she owes rescue to you and to your identical twin, she does not owe twice as much to the pair, since the duty is fully satisfied with respect to each individual, and multiplying claimants should not invent a collective moral patient. Marcus then introduced a “fleet-consistency” objection—if one thousand identical drones each face this choice and all flip coins, the expected aggregate survival rate is catastrophically lower than under a maximize policy—while Elena responded that this smuggles in consequentialism: statistical patterns across distributed agents do not alter any single drone’s direct duty to those immediately before it, and allowing fleet-level expectations to override individual claims risks a “moral dilution” effect in which the five passengers matter less simply because analogous choices occur elsewhere.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move—rating Elena’s initial Taurek-inspired position, Marcus’s accumulation-of-claims reply, Elena’s anti-summation rejoinder, Marcus’s fleet-consistency move, and Elena’s moral-dilution response—placing each on a spectrum from clearly mistaken to clearly correct with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, identify what both miss regarding whether the autonomous-system framing introduces distinct normative considerations (such as design-level accountability or the absence of emotional moral costs in algorithmic decision-making), and determine whether the coin-flip conclusion survives once the disanalogies between human rescuers and algorithmic agents are made explicit.
