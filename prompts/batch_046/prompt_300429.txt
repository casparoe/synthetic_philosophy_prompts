Consider a transnational river-basin council that must deliver a unified, binding recommendation on whether to mandate the relocation of settlements from an expanding flood-plain that also sustains long-standing indigenous agricultural practice. The council comprises climate modelers, municipal engineers, and elected representatives from the affected watershed communities. Its charter requires that any recommendation be justified by explicit, jointly endorsed premises spanning hydrological projections, infrastructure-cost assessments, and normative claims about cultural continuity, distributive fairness, and obligations to future generations. The council discovers that, although pairwise majorities agree on each individual premise, no consistent majority exists for the overall recommendation derived by logical aggregation of those premises; conversely, aggregating directly on the recommendation and then deriving premises post hoc produces a different, also majority-supported, but logically incompatible set of justifications. This is an instance of the discursive dilemma in collective judgment formation.

Please describe the most important implications of this structural tension for how the council ought to design its deliberative protocol. In particular, address whether adopting a premise-based aggregation rule genuinely respects democratic equality when minority stakeholders reject the normative framing embedded in certain premises; whether a conclusion-based rule undermines transparency and accountability by decoupling the collective decision from its stated reasoning; and whether introducing a hierarchical ordering of premises—treating empirical hydrological and economic claims as fixed constraints within which normative disagreement is subsequently resolved—merely displaces the aggregation conflict into the prior, politically contested selection of which claims count as genuinely empirical rather than value-laden.
