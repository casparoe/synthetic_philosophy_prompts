I'm a phd student in philosophy of cognitive science and I need a short prospectus for my dissertation committee, maybe 2-3 pages. My half-baked idea involves the Aunt Hillary / ant-fugue framing — not as a cute metaphor but as an actual structural model for distributed cognition. The question I want to pin down: if an ant colony's behavior is organized like a fugue, where each ant carries a local 'voice' that only makes sense through interdependence with the others and there's no central conductor, does that open a third option between 'the colony is one big conscious subject' and 'it's just a bunch of ants with zero collective experience'? Or is the fugue analogy just dressing up the same old group-mind problem in musical language?

Existing answers split pretty cleanly between panpsychist-leaning 'colony-as-subject' views and hard eliminativist 'nothing it is like to be the colony' views, but neither camp really addresses the feature that makes the fugue interesting: global coherence without a unified point of view. I think that's the gap I can actually work on.

My approach: reconstruct the ant-fugue scenario at the level of functional architecture, not neurobiology — I'm not running experiments. Then I want to borrow the integration/unification distinction from predictive processing literature, but only as a philosophical tool, not an empirical claim. The move is showing that a system can have deep informational integration across its parts without there being 'one subject' that receives it all. If the colony achieves integration without unification in a stable, non-accidental way, then we've carved out conceptual space for distributed minds that aren't unified subjects. If not, then the fugue collapses into either standard group-mind theory or eliminativism, which is also a result worth having.

What I'd deliver: an ~8000 word chapter with three parts — the framing, applying integration-vs-unification to colony behavior, and a failure-mode analysis of what would break the analogy. Success looks like a clear conceptual distinction that survives pressure; failure looks like the distinction collapsing, meaning any integrated system necessarily has unification, which would actually be a sharp negative finding.

Scope: one semester, mostly armchair. I won't cite specific papers I can't verify, so I'll refer to 'the standard group-mind literature' and fill references in later.
