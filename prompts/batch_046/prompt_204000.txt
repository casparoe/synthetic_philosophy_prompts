In Moore scholarship there is a persistent disagreement about whether the doctrine of organic unity, developed in Principia Ethica for aesthetic value, extends to friendship, which Moore ranks among the greatest goods. One side holds that friendship is itself an organic whole of mental states—mutual affection, shared attention, emotional resonance—whose value exceeds the sum of its parts, and that Moore's silence on this is an omission. The other side holds that friendship's value is irreducibly relational and intentional, not a compositional property of mental complexes, and that importing aesthetic vocabulary into ethics confuses two domains. The exchange has stalled: proponents cite Moore's examples of beautiful wholes and his lack of explicit relational analysis; opponents cite his emphasis on conscious affection and the absence of aesthetic terminology in the ethical chapters. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is partly verbal—both sides use "organic unity" for a compositional structure in aesthetics and an axiological feature in ethics—masking a deeper disagreement about whether value supervenes on internal mental structure or on relational orientation. Diagnose the stall: is it a substantive disagreement about value, a verbal confusion over one term, a hidden empirical question about Moore's intentions, or two different questions being pursued under one heading? What specific conceptual distinction, textual observation, or shift in framing would count as genuine progress?
