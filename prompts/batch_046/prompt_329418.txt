I am writing a paper for a seminar on philosophy of action, and my advisor keeps pressing me on a tension I keep circling: the standard literature on sphexishness—the mechanical rigidity exemplified by the digger wasp’s stereotyped nesting behavior—is usually deployed to argue that perfect behavioral inflexibility undermines free will. But I want to stress-test the opposite intuition. Imagine an agent—call it a “Self-Binding Sphex”—whose decision architecture is fully deterministic and context-invariant, repeating the exact same neural-computational pathway for every choice, with no capacity for mid-course revision or stimulus-dependent override. Crucially, however, this rigidity is not externally imposed; the agent deliberately engineered it as a permanent, irrevocable commitment to a prior moral framework—for instance, hard-coding a vow of absolute non-violence into every motor-planning loop. The agent reports subjective experience of deliberation, yet functional analysis reveals zero variance in processing pathways across identical or even radically different stimuli.

Please give me your credence that this Self-Binding Sphex possesses libertarian free will, decomposed into at least three explicit terms: (a) the prior probability that libertarian free will is conceptually coherent at all; (b) the conditional probability that, if coherent, it requires causal openness or flexibility at the level of action-generation; (c) the conditional probability that self-imposed, reason-responsive rigidity can satisfy—or genuinely bypass—whatever openness condition (b) demands, rather than collapsing into mere determinism; together with any other multiplicative or disjunctive factor you think belongs in the calculation. For each term, provide a specific number between 0 and 1 and a single line of justification, then show how they combine arithmetically into the final credence. After that, tell me which single term you are least confident in, and specify exactly what kind of evidence—whether an empirical result in the neuropsychology of voluntary action, a formal result in action theory, or a conceptual argument about self-binding commitments—would most substantially shift that term and therefore move the overall number.
