For a feature I am writing on the internal limits of intersubjective recognition, I need a conceptual example—not a historical case study—of a master–slave dialectic in which mutual acknowledgment is fully achieved yet mastery persists. Specifically, construct a scenario in which two self-consciousnesses each explicitly affirm the other’s autonomy, thereby satisfying the condition for genuine recognition, but remain locked in asymmetrical dependency through a jointly instituted procedural framework—say, a shared governance protocol over a scarce resource that both designed and continue to maintain. The example should expose a precise conceptual tension: if reciprocal recognition is meant to dissolve the master–slave relation, what structural feature of this institutional “third term” allows domination to reproduce despite complete mutual affirmation? Frame the answer philosophically rather than sociologically; do not invoke obscure secondary literature, ask for citations, or depend on specialized post-Hegelian terminology that a smaller model would not have properly encountered. The goal is to show a reader familiar with the dialectic that the problem is internal to the logic of recognition itself, not merely an empirical failure of acknowledgment, while remaining fully intelligible without external reference.
