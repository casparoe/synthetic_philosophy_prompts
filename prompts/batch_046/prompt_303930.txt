I have been working through Karl Jaspers' fourfold distinction of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—in the context of post-atrocity collective responsibility, and I want to apply it to a specific historical moment rather than to abstract nations. Consider Willy Brandt's unscripted kneeling at the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising memorial on 7 December 1970: Brandt was then West German Chancellor, he had spent the war in Norwegian exile and resistance with no personal criminal involvement in the Holocaust, yet he fell to his knees in a gesture that was immediately interpreted, both internationally and within West Germany, as an act of national contrition. Analyse this episode through Jaspers' framework by identifying the relevant actors—Brandt as individual and as state representative, the West German polity, the survivors and descendants present, and the domestic critics who accused him of humiliating an innocent generation—and specifying, for each, which of Jaspers' guilt categories applies, which do not, and what form of acknowledgment or action the theory would prescribe. In particular, examine whether Brandt's gesture should be read as an expression of political guilt borne by the state he represented, as an illegitimate assumption of individual moral guilt that Jaspers reserves for personal conscience, or as an attempt to perform metaphysical guilt on behalf of a collective, despite Jaspers' insistence that metaphysical guilt is strictly individual and non-transferable.

Then assess what the episode shows about the theory itself: does Jaspers' taxonomy explain why an unimplicated chancellor might feel compelled to kneel, and does it account for the backlash from citizens who rejected any imputation of guilt for crimes they had not committed?
