At a recent meetup of an online rationalist reading group, two regulars—Jordan and Alex—got into a sustained disagreement about peer disagreement, using a concrete framing: they both initially assign a 70% credence to some claim P (for example, that a particular engineering approach will resolve a known bottleneck by a given date), but upon learning the other has arrived at 30%, they dispute what rational revision requires.

Jordan opened with the conciliationist line: absent independent reason to think one is more reliable, both should move toward the midpoint—roughly 50%—because treating one’s own reasoning as privileged without evidence is arbitrary self-favoritism. He noted that “inside access” to one’s own reasoning does not break the symmetry, since the peer presumably has equivalent inside access to theirs, and there is no neutral standpoint from which to weigh those internal experiences.

Alex pushed back with a steadfastness-style reply: he can inspect his own reasoning for identifiable errors, whereas he can observe only the conclusion of Jordan’s reasoning, not its internal structure. Therefore he should discount Jordan’s view only by his prior expectation of Jordan’s domain-specific error rate, not by splitting the difference. He argued that conciliationism implicitly assumes exchangeability of reasoning processes, which is false—expertise, track record, and transparency differ across individuals.

Jordan then raised a meta-level concern: if they disagree about whether to split the difference—Jordan says yes, Alex says no—conciliationism applies to that meta-disagreement too. Without a “termination condition,” a shared prior on rationality norms, this generates a regress that collapses all credences toward a social mean. He claimed steadfastness avoids collapse only by arbitrarily declaring one’s own framework immune to revision.

Alex responded that Jordan’s “termination condition” is not neutral; it smuggles in an egalitarian prior that assigns zero weight to private reasoning transparency. He argued that demanding a framework treaty before any belief revision forces artificial homogenization, treating domain-specific expertise as exchangeable across fields. He added that applying linear averaging at every meta-level is like accepting a Dutch book against one’s own calibrated confidence—one’s long-run belief state becomes independent of private evidence.

Jordan countered that the burden of proof lies on Alex to demonstrate non-exchangeability. Until Alex can specify the feature that makes his reasoning architecture more reliable in this domain—without circularly appealing to his current belief—applying private calibration weights is just sophisticated steadfastness. The default of equal weighting is not a hidden prior, he said; it is the absence of a demonstrated differential.

Alex closed by saying Jordan’s “default” is itself a substantive epistemic commitment—specifically, epistemic egalitarianism disguised as neutrality. Choosing the midpoint assigns value to social convergence over reasoning fidelity, which is not rational compromise but a hidden prior in favor of collective belief averaging.
