Consider this extension of Benatar's asymmetry to rescue ethics, which I built for a discussion and now find suspicious because it licenses inaction. (1) The absence of pain is good for a potential person who never comes to exist, but the absence of pleasure is not bad for them. (2) So there is no positive moral reason to bring someone into existence for the pleasure they would experience; only the prevention of suffering counts in favor. (3) Apply this now to an existing person facing death: saving them prevents immediate suffering, but it also ensures they will continue to experience both future pleasure and future suffering. (4) By (1)-(2), the future pleasure carries no compensatory weight—those pleasures are not goods that were owed to them or that they are now being deprived of if they die. (5) Only the future suffering counts negatively. (6) Therefore, unless I can guarantee that their remaining life contains less suffering than the immediate death-pain I prevent, I have net reason not to save them. (7) Generalized, this implies we should refrain from rescue whenever future harm is possible, which undermines ordinary obligations to save lives. (8) That conclusion is unacceptable. Which step should I reject? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (2) if possible, but I need to know what each alternative costs—especially whether giving up (3) requires treating existence and rescue as morally asymmetrical in a way that looks ad hoc.
