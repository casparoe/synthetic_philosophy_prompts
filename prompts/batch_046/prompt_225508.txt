I am auditing an advanced seminar on axiology, and during our session on negative-utilitarian variants we became entangled in a debate that I have not been able to shake off. One of the tutors defended a position she called tranquilism: the claim that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, whereas pleasure, love, or achievement are at best neutral by-products, never reasons in themselves to prefer one life over another. She insisted that this was not mere pessimism but a rigorous axiology, and I found myself unable to articulate exactly where the intuition broke down without sounding as though I were simply clinging to romantic attachments.

To help me think through this more clearly, I would like you to write a short philosophical story — no more than about a thousand words — set in a rehabilitation centre on an archipelago where citizens may elect to undergo a treatment known as craving-dampening. The procedure does not induce euphoria or artificial happiness; rather, it reduces all affective intensity to a flat, serene equilibrium in which suffering is absent but so too is any felt urgency to create, to love, or to remember with pain. Your protagonist should be a retired cartographer who has come to the centre not because she is miserable, but because she has begun to suspect that her lifelong passion for mapping unexplored territories was itself a form of disguised craving that kept her perpetually dissatisfied. She is interviewed by a counsellor who is a committed tranquilist and who argues, with genuine patience and without caricature, that her memories of discovery are merely the shadow of an unmet need rather than a reason to reject the treatment. The story should move between this present-tense conversation and brief, sensory fragments of her past expeditions — not as sentimental flashbacks, but as contested evidence that the counsellor attempts to reinterpret.

Please avoid resolving the narrative with a didactic twist. Instead, allow the tension to rest on whether a life emptied of all craving is still recognisably a human life, particularly when the absence of suffering also removes the conditions under which care, grief, and artistic attention seem to arise. Write in a restrained, literary register, and do not include any bibliographic references or appeals to named philosophers beyond what is already mentioned; the point is to explore the concept through the concrete logic of the characters rather than through exposition.
