I'm sitting on our university's Emerging Biotech Ethics Pannel — we meet every other Thursday in that airless room behind the philosophy dept, which probably explains why we keep making the same mistakes — and we've got a genuinely awkward case for this week's session. A consortium of neuroscientists and applied ethicists has applied for institutional approval and pilot funding for something they call 'Project Affective Baseline Reset': essentially, using in-utero neurodevelopmental modulation to engineer out not just nociception but the capacity for existential dread, grief, and what they term 'negative valence gradients' altogether. Their justification is straight from the hedonistic imperative — if we can abolish suffering through biotechnology, especially for future generations who can't consent to being born into suffering but also can't suffer if we succeed, then we have a positive moral duty to do it. The pannel is split. Some members treat it as an extreme form of preventive medicine; others argue it collapses therapy into enhancement, imposes a mandatory 'eudaimonic neutrality' on people who never asked for it, and eliminates the affective conditions that make moral growth and authentic choice possible in the first place. There's also a strange internal note about 'pre-emptive hedonic engineering' and whether autonomy survives when you restructure the neural architecture of feeling itself.

Please draft the short opinion memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve with strict conditions, reject with a resubmission pathway, or defer for further review — and include a clear time frame, for instance contingent approval pending an independent ethical audit within six months. The reasoning needs to take the hedonistic imperative seriously, not dismiss it as science-fiction utopianism, but it also needs to engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly: probably that suffering is not merely a medical problem to be eliminated but is constitutive of certain kinds of meaning, relational depth, or moral understanding, and that removing it preemptively is closer to editing the human condition without consent than to curing a disease. Write it in British English — honour, programme, centre, etc. — and keep the tone formal but readable, since half the pannel are biologists who glaze over at modal logic. Oh, and please don't quote the proposal's title page verbatim; they spelt 'affective' as 'affectve', which feels like a bad omen, and I'd rather not propogate the error. I need this by Wednesday evening.
