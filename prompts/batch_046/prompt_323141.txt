I work in distributed systems design, and I have noticed a recurring architectural tension that feels as though it ought to have a philosophical name, but I cannot tell whether I am seeing an old moral idea in new technical clothing. On one side are systems built for absolute retention: append-only event logs, immutable distributed ledgers, and versioned storage architectures in which deletion is technically impossible and every past state remains reconstructible. The justification offered is usually accountability—if every action is permanently traceable, history cannot be denied or distorted. On the other side are systems engineered for active erasure: not merely permitting users to delete data, but making recovery structurally or cryptographically infeasible by default, so that forgetting requires no further effort and remembering demands exceptional authorization. The tension is sharper than a routine privacy-versus-security tradeoff, because both architectures make a claim about the moral status of the past: one treats persistence as the default ethical condition of information, the other treats disappearance as the default. I am aware of philosophical work on a duty to remember and on the value of forgetting—Margalit's ethics of memory and Rieff's arguments for forgetting come to mind—but those discussions appear centered on human consciousness, collective ritual, or social practice. In the cases I am describing, the "rememberer" is an automated system with no awareness, and "forgetting" is not organic decay but an engineered absence that must be actively maintained. Is there an established philosophical concept or literature that addresses this specific configuration—non-conscious, automated retention versus structurally enforced erasure—or does the Margalit/Rieff distinction apply directly, with the engineering context being only implementation detail? If there is no established name for this technical variant, what exactly distinguishes it from the human case, and where should the boundary between them be drawn?
