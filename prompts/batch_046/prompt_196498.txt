I was recently covering a symposium on behavioural public policy for my column, and one of the panellists offered a summary of the libertarian-paternalist position—associated with Thaler and Sunstein—that I found both compelling and suspiciously neat. He argued that, because human cognition is bounded, people inevitably rely on heuristics and environmental defaults, which means any policy setting already steers behaviour; consequently, a government that deliberately designs choice architecture to promote welfare is not imposing foreign values but merely correcting for the cognitive biases that already distort autonomous choice. He went on to claim that critics who invoke “sludge”—excessive friction in opting out—have missed the essential point, since libertarian paternalism permits easy exit and therefore preserves full autonomy in every meaningful sense. From this, he concluded that once we genuinely accept bounded rationality, the only coherent position is to embrace engineered defaults, because the idea of a neutral choice architecture is not merely difficult but conceptually impossible.

I am not convinced that this captures the debate fairly, but I lack the philosophical training to locate the precise distortions. In particular, I wonder whether he has conflated the descriptive claim that choice architecture is unavoidable with the normative claim that welfare-promoting design is therefore automatically permissible; whether he has understated the ways in which personalised digital defaults—what some researchers are now calling “adaptive nudging”—can erode the transparency and predictability that genuine autonomy requires; and whether his dismissal of the “sludge” objection depends on an overly narrow, almost procedural conception of freedom that ignores the cumulative burden of constant opt-out. Could you audit this summary with care? Which parts would a careful defender of libertarian paternalism accept as accurate, which are misrepresentations or subtle overstatements, and does his conclusion—that bounded rationality entails embracing engineered defaults—actually follow once the premises are corrected?
