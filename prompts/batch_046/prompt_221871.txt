I've been trying to wrap my head around coherence arguments for expected utility maximization — specifically the Dutch-book style reasoning — because I'm working on a project about aligning an AI's preference ordering and I keep running into people saying any non-EU ordering is automatically exploitable. I think I've pushed the argument into a corner where it proves way too much, but I can't find the exit.

Here is the chain as I understand it:
(1) If an agent's choices violate the independence axiom (say, they show Allais-type patterns) or continuity (say, lexicografic safety priorities where preventing catastrophic harm dominates every other value), then there exists a finite sequence of trades or bets that guarantees a net loss, no matter the outcomes.
(2) Being vulnerable to a guaranteed net loss is anologous to holding contradictory beliefs — it's a form of practical incoherance.
(3) Therefore any agent with such preferences, including one with a hard lexicographic rule against causing harm, is practically irrational in the same way a belief-incoherent agent is epistemically irrational.
(4) But if that's right, then an AI with a lexicographic safety constraint is rationally defective, and we should redesign it to maximize expected utility with some very large but still finite weight on harm avoidance.
(5) Yet this feels wrong: the lexicographic rule seems rational precisely because it refuses to trade away safety for any amount of benefit, and calling that "incoherent" seems to confuse coherence with a specific quantitative representation.
(6) So either the coherence argument overreaches by treating representional flexibility (using infinite or non-Archimedean utilities) as the only rational response, or there's a hidden premise in (2) that equates bet-acceptance dispositions with rationality itself.

Which step should I reject? Is it (2), where the analogy between belief coherence and preference coherence breaks down because preferences don't have truth-values? Is it (4), because lexicographic preferences can actually be embedded in an EU framework with non-standard representations, so there's no real violation to begin with? Or is the real problem in (1) — that constructing the Dutch book for lexicographic preferences requires the agent to voluntarily enter bets they would rationally refuse? I want an honest assessment: if the coherence argument is genuinely unscathed here, then I should drop the lexicographic safety idea; but if it's just been insulated by qualifications, I want to know whether that insulation is a feature or a bug for a view meant to guide actual design choices.

Also, please push back hard if I'm mixing up synchronic and diachronic coherence, or if the premise that lexicographic preferences violate continuity is itself confused, or if the whole framing assumes a betting model that doesn't apply to artificial agents. Don't just comply politely if the setup is broken — tell me straight.
