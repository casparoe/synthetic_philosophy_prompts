I was just in a long, somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about moral luck, and I’m trying to crystallize the exact conceptual knot we kept circling. We both take seriously the standard taxonomy—result, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal luck, along the lines of Nagel and Williams—but we kept stumbling over a case that doesn’t sit neatly in any one bin and seems to generate a regress rather than a clean attribution. I want an example of a conceptual issue that emerges specifically when we apply the distinction between constitutive and circumstantial luck to a situation I’ll call structurally distributed agency: an agent whose moral character and available choices are partly constituted by institutional arrangements (say, a mid-level bureaucrat operating inside an incentive structure they inherited) that are themselves the accumulated, unintended outcomes of prior agents’ choices, each of which was subject to its own moral luck.

The example should be concrete enough to be vivid—perhaps a sharply drawn scenario or a formalized tension—showing exactly where the taxonomy buckles. Specifically, it should illustrate why attributing luck to the present agent seems to require attributing luck to the prior agents who shaped the structure, but doing so threatens to dissolve the very distinction between constitutive and circumstantial luck, because the “circumstances” that constitute the present agent’s character are themselves “constituted” by others’ luck-laden choices. I’m not asking for a resolution, a literature review, or any citations; I just want the example precise enough that someone familiar with the classic machinery would recognize it being pushed past its usual limits, without collapsing all moral assessment into an undifferentiated global structural luck or over-individualizing responsibility in a way that ignores the inherited architecture.
