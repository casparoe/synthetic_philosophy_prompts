I'm working through a conceptual problem at the intersection of action theory and medical ethics, and I want to test whether a refined distinction between killing and letting die survives institutional complexity. Rather than applying the standard doctrine of double effect in its familiar individual form, I'm using a framework I call "default-state asymmetry with institutional standing" — the claim that classifying an act as killing or allowing death requires identifying which agent has legitimately established the current care baseline, and that withdrawing an intervention instituted by that same agent differs morally from refusing an intervention proposed by an outside party, with the evaluation turning on a counterfactual stability test: whether, in nearby worlds, the patient's life continues through the same institutional causal chain or only through an external substitution.

Consider the well-known 2017 case of Charlie Gard, an infant with a severe mitochondrial DNA depletion syndrome who was mechanically ventilated at Great Ormond Street Hospital. His parents sought court permission to transfer him to the United States for an experimental nucleoside therapy that had not been tested in patients with his particular genetic condition, arguing that continued ventilation and the proposed treatment fell within their parental authority; the hospital, supported by clinical assessments that further treatment was futile and caused suffering, sought judicial authorization to withdraw ventilation, contending that disconnection would merely allow the underlying disease to take its natural course rather than actively causing death. The court ultimately assigned decision-making authority to the medical institution and authorized withdrawal, after which ventilation was stopped and the child died.

Analyze this episode through the lens described above: determine who, under the framework, holds the institutional standing to define the default care state — the parents as legal guardians, the clinicians as baseline-establishers, or the court as arbiter — and classify the physical act of disconnecting the ventilator accordingly, whether as direct withdrawal of an agent-instituted mechanism, omission to maintain an integrated baseline, or refusal of an external proposal. Examine whether the parents' demand for experimental therapy functions within the framework as an attempt to shift the default state, thereby altering the moral category of the hospital's withdrawal from "allowing" to "killing" or vice versa. Identify which features of the dispute the framework explains well — for instance, why the assignment of authority seemed morally loaded rather than merely procedural — and where it forces an artificial or silent classification. Finally, assess what the case reveals about the framework itself: does the fact that both sides invoked killing versus letting die to reach diametrically opposed conclusions indicate that the distinction is genuinely underdetermined without a prior theory of institutional standing, or does it suggest that the distinction was never carrying the normative weight and that the real conflict was always about who possesses the authority to set the baseline?
