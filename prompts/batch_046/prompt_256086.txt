Question: First, distinguish cost-bearing supererogation—where an agent knowingly accepts a definite, measurable loss such as exhaustion, injury, or foregone opportunity—from risk-bearing supererogation, where the agent accepts an uncertain but potentially severe harm. Then define supererogation generally and explain why the philosopher J.O. Urmson thought it posed a structural problem for traditional moral theories that recognize only obligations, permissions, and prohibitions. Next, apply both categories to the following case: Dr. Chen, an off-duty physician with no contractual or legal duty to intervene, spends twelve hours performing emergency surgery on strangers after a train derailment, suffering documented sleep deprivation and a minor laceration. Evaluate whether her conduct is best understood as cost-bearing, risk-bearing, both, or neither, and defend your classification. Finally, address the collapse objection: if Dr. Chen's action is genuinely the morally best option available to her, then failing to perform it should be blameworthy, which seems to turn supererogation into mere obligation. Explain whether this objection succeeds, and in doing so discuss how Urmson's framework relates to utilitarian theories that evaluate actions solely by outcome.

Student Answer: Supererogation refers to acts that are morally good and worthy of praise but not morally required, falling outside the standard division of actions into required, permitted, and forbidden. In his well-known discussion of saints and heroes, J.O. Urmson argued that traditional deontic schemes lack a category for genuinely heroic behavior, since rushing into danger to save strangers is clearly admirable yet not demanded of everyone. He therefore proposed expanding the framework to include acts that go beyond duty.

In the case, Dr. Chen's conduct is risk-bearing supererogation. Although she ended up with sleep deprivation and a minor cut, the primary moral feature of her choice was exposure to an uncertain severe harm—possible infection, structural collapse, or further injury—rather than the acceptance of a definite loss. Because she was off duty and faced no legal obligation, her intervention exceeds ordinary moral expectations, making it supererogatory rather than obligatory.

The collapse objection claims that if her act was the best available, omission is blameworthy, collapsing the category into obligation. This objection fails because it assumes morality operates like a single ranking where the top option is automatically mandatory. But Urmson showed that utilitarian theories avoid this difficulty by recognizing that several outcomes can be equally optimal, which allows heroic acts to sit in an optional zone of high value without being strictly required. Therefore, Dr. Chen deserves special praise, yet another physician who rested instead is not blameworthy, and supererogation remains coherent. Meanwhile, cost-bearing supererogation applies to cases like donating a kidney, where the loss is definite rather than probabilistic, so Dr. Chen's case should not be classified that way.
