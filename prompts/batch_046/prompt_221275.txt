Here is an argument I reconstructed from Strawson's Basic Argument, then pushed one step further, and now it seems to collapse every form of normative assessment—including rational and epistemic ones—into the same regress. I want to know whether the extension is legitimate or whether there is a hidden slide that lets me keep the moral conclusion without the self-undermining result.

(1) If an agent S is truly responsible for performing action A, then S must be responsible for the psychological features N—desires, beliefs, character traits—in virtue of which A was performed.
(2) To be responsible for N, S must either have authored N through prior responsible choices or stand in a relation of authorship to the prior conditions that produced N.
(3) Any chain of such prior conditions either extends infinitely, which is impossible for a finite agent, or terminates in conditions—genetic endowment, early environment, arbitrary luck—for which S is not responsible.
(4) Therefore, no agent is ever truly responsible for any feature of their psychology, and hence never truly responsible for any action.
(5) The same structure applies to belief-formation and judgment: if S is rationally accountable for holding belief B, then S must be responsible for the cognitive dispositions that produced B, which launches an identical regress.
(6) Consequently, no agent is ever rationally required to revise a belief, or ever properly subject to evaluation for reasoning well or poorly.
(7) But if (6) holds, then there is no normative basis for accepting or rejecting arguments—including this one—because no interlocutor can be accountable for recognizing error.
(8) Thus the argument is self-undermining: if sound, it removes the very conditions for its own rational acceptance.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I would prefer to keep (1) and (4) restricted to moral desert, and I am inclined to reject (5) as an overgeneralization, but I need to know whether that requires giving up a principle that (1)-(4) actually depend on—such as the claim that responsibility always requires diachronic authorship of one's conditions—or whether (5) commits a scope shift or an equivocation between moral responsibility and rational accountability that was already latent in (2).
