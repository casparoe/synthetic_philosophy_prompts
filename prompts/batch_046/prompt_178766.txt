I'm drafting a seminar paper on the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents, and I'd like committed critical feedback rather than a reading list. Here is the core argument.

Thesis: The obstacle arises only if we formalize self-trust as an a priori logical endorsement that is closed under modal rules K, 4, and 5—especially distribution over logical consequence. I claim this mischaracterizes rational self-trust and that the obstacle dissolves once we replace binary logical endorsement with graded, empirically calibrated confidence.

Argument sketch:
1. Löb's theorem shows that for any consistent formal system T extending basic arithmetic, if T proves Prov_T(⌜φ⌝) → φ, then T proves φ. In doxastic terms, if an agent can derive "if I believe p, then p," it can already derive p—making self-trust redundant or viciously circular.
2. This derivation requires that the agent's meta-belief in its own consistency be formalizable inside T and closed under logical consequence.
3. I propose instead that rational agents maintain confidence in their own soundness through resource-bounded self-monitoring—tracking past error rates, running partial consistency checks, and analogical reasoning—yielding a graded credence that is not derivable as a theorem within the object-language.
4. Because this meta-confidence lacks the modal closure conditions required for the Löbian proof, the agent can rationally assign high credence to "I am generally sound" without triggering the fixed-point structure.

My main worry is whether step 3 illegitimately imports empirical psychology into formal epistemology, and whether I am misrepresenting the doxastic conditions needed for the theorem.

Please give a committed assessment: take a definite position on whether this genuinely dissolves the obstacle or merely relocates it, identify the weakest link, and flag specifically where I take liberties with the standard formulation of Löb's theorem or with the concept of rational self-trust.
