I'm currently enrolled in a seminar on relational ethics and African philosophy, and I've noticed a structural tension in my university's institutional design that I can't resolve conceptually. Several humanities courses in our program have been redesigned around collective inquiry: group-authored essays are submitted and graded as unitary works, peer evaluation carries substantial weight, and the syllabus frames knowledge as co-constituted rather than individually possessed, which seems to align with Ubuntu-informed understandings of personhood. At the same time, the university's disability and accessibility office operates on an entirely different logic—accommodations must be requested by an individual student, documented through private medical verification, and granted to that specific person as an isolated bearer of rights, with no mechanism for collective or relational adjustments to group-based assessment. What theory of personhood—metaphysically individualist, genuinely relational, or an unstable hybrid—does this bifurcated practice actually presuppose? Is the accommodation framework implicitly committed to a bounded self that exists prior to and independently of social relations, and if so, is that commitment strictly necessary for the policy to function, or could a relational conception be operationalized without collapsing into incoherence? Furthermore, if the underlying view of the person were replaced with a consistently relational account, what specific structural changes would be required in both the accessibility office's procedures and the collective pedagogy they are meant to serve? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify where the institution is philosophically underdetermined.
