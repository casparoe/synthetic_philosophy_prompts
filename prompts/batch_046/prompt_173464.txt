I'm working on a project in philosophy of biology about whether multi-level selection frameworks can illuminate the dynamics of scientific controversies themselves. Take the episode of Hamilton's 1964 inclusive-fitness formulation and its immediate reception—including Maynard Smith's contemporaneous critical reply and Dawkins's later gene-centered reinterpretation in The Selfish Gene—as a case study. Map the episode onto the gene/individual/group distinction: treat the competing formalisms as potential replicators, the research programs and citation networks as interactors, and identify what selection pressures (predictive success, formal tractability, disciplinary boundary-keeping) operated at each level. Specifically, ask whether Hamilton's rule rb > c was initially resisted because it was read at the wrong unit of selection, or because its unit-neutral mathematics made it vulnerable to competing interpretations. Then report where the mapping holds and where it breaks: does treating conceptual disagreement as competitive exclusion among replicators explain patterns of acceptance and resistance that a standard sociology-of-science account misses, or does it misdescribe cooperative refinement and empirical testing as zero-sum selection? I want to know whether this episode supports the claim that the unit debate is largely interpretive framing rather than a substantive empirical disagreement, and what that implies for applying selection theory reflexively to scientific change.
