I am planning a ninety-minute session for a philosophy reading group composed of graduate students with training in metaphysics and philosophy of science but no formal physics background. The session aims to introduce the debate between spacetime substantivalism and relationism, using Newton’s bucket argument as the entry point. Rather than present the classic single-bucket demonstration, I want to center the discussion on a modified scenario: imagine two identical buckets, A and B, filled with water and suspended by twisted ropes in an otherwise completely empty universe with no distant stars or matter. Both are initially at rest relative to each other; then both ropes are released, but only one bucket is observed to develop a concave water surface as it spins, while the other remains flat—though from the perspective of an observer riding with either bucket, both appear to be rotating relative to the other.

My goal is not to resolve the debate, but to make the students feel the force of the argument for absolute space and the relationist’s need to invoke something like Mach’s principle, without letting them retreat into “it depends on the frame.”

Plan the teaching arc: in what order must the conceptual pieces be introduced so that the dependency is clear—what must be in place before the bucket can do its philosophical work, and what must come after to show why relationism is not trivial? Identify the single carrying example or visual model that should carry the whole session—specify what properties it must have and what it must lack (for instance, why the absence of distant matter is essential and why using Earth as a reference frame must be explicitly excluded). Map the misconceptions the students will likely form—such as conflating rotational motion with linear motion, treating the frame-dependence of velocity as sufficient to dissolve the problem, or assuming general relativity automatically vindicates relationism—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Suggest one concrete check or exercise at the end that would reveal whether the students have grasped the conceptual tension rather than just memorized the positions. Finally, state what technical material (for example, the mathematical formulation of inertia or specific interpretations of general covariance) should be openly left out at this level, and why.
