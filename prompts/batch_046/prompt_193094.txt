As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing an in-depth feature on the normative foundations of AI value alignment. Before speaking with researchers, I want the full conceptual specification—without a bibliography, reading list, or citation request—of what any adequate theory of whose values an aligned system ought to serve actually has to deliver.

Set out the desiderata: the phenomena such a theory must explain; the hard cases it must handle correctly; the distinctions it must preserve (e.g., between preference aggregation and liberal neutrality, between representing a value and enforcing it, between epistemic authority and democratic authorization); and the consistency conditions it must satisfy with established results in social choice theory and liberal political philosophy. Behind each requirement, provide the motivating consideration or case. Flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable—especially where aggregation conflicts with neutrality in deeply pluralistic societies, or where expert deference conflicts with popular sovereignty—and indicate which demands are non-negotiable and which are camp-specific preferences.

Keep the assessment of existing frameworks brief and subordinate: how do liberal-neutral proceduralism, preference-aggregation approaches, and epistemic-democratic hybrid models fare against your list? The conceptual work should lie in the criteria, not the scorecard.

Conclude with a concrete architectural trade-off that forces the theory to do real work: an AI system must choose between preserving the grammatical and relational norms of a minority language that embeds non-commodified, non-instrumental values, and optimizing for the expressed preferences of a global user base that treats language as a pure efficiency tool, where the two are structurally irreconcilable at the model-design level. The theory must explain what renders one resolution legitimate rather than merely efficient, and it must do so without appealing to external authority or citation.
