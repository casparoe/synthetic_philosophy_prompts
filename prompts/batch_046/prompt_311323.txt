As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy and the history of economic thought, I have been revisiting Keynes’s 1930 essay in which he projected that, within about a century, rising productivity might solve the economic problem and permit a working week of roughly fifteen hours, thereby freeing humanity for the art of living well. That prediction has not materialized: despite enormous gains in output per hour, the forty-hour norm remains entrenched, and in wealthy societies we see contradictory institutional responses—Japan’s recognition of karoshi, or death by overwork, and France’s legislated thirty-five-hour week. At the same time, the digital economy has introduced a form of leisure colonization Keynes could not have anticipated. Platform-mediated free time is increasingly structured as shadow work—attention harvested, data produced, preferences refined—so that rest itself functions as an input to production rather than as an escape from it.

I would like you to predict, as precisely as Keynes’s own framework allows, how he would diagnose this situation. Specifically:

First, would he interpret karoshi and the persistence of long hours in high-productivity economies as evidence that the love of money and the moral obligation to labor remain stronger than he hoped, or would he see them as structural failures—status competition, institutional inertia, or market imperfections—that his framework did not fully model?

Second, how would he evaluate the French thirty-five-hour week? Would he regard it as a successful institutionalization of his vision, treating leisure as a positive entitlement rather than a residual absence of labor? Or would he criticize it for failing to cultivate what we might now call autotelic leisure—activity pursued for its own sake rather than for mere physiological recovery or consumption?

Third, does his distinction between absolute needs and relative needs help him understand an economy in which leisure is not simply consumed but continuously monetized through attention extraction? Or does the colonization of rest by economic rationality reveal a gap in his conception of the good life—perhaps an unstated assumption that leisure, once freed from material necessity, would naturally become self-cultivating?

Please ground your answer in Keynes’s known commitments—his skepticism toward compound interest, his belief that the economic problem is technically soluble, his concern with the insatiability of wants—without appealing to secondary scholarship I could not verify without library access. Where his framework is genuinely silent on these modern phenomena, say so explicitly, and indicate whether the silence is a limitation of his economics or a limitation of applying a 1930 conceptual vocabulary to the attention economy.
