You are an expert philosopher of mind with clinical research experience. Before drafting anything, briefly brainstorm the core differences between a Cartesian theater framework, in which a single unified stream of consciousness is presented to an inner observer at a specific time, and a multiple drafts framework, in which distributed competing narratives have no canonical final version and no central observer. I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I need you to turn these theoretical commitments into a reusable decision procedure — a step-by-step flowchart in words — for a clinical neuropsychologist or research psychologist who evaluates first-person reports of conscious experience in patients with split-brain conditions, severe traumatic brain injury, or disorders of consciousness. The practitioner applies this repeatedly, has roughly fifteen minutes per case, lacks library access, and must sign off on one of two classifications: Theater-Consistent Interpretation or Multiple-Drafts Interpretation.

Structure the procedure as follows. First, list the ordered diagnostic questions, specifying what counts as a sufficient answer to each and which answers move the case toward one classification or the other. Second, include explicit stop points where the procedure delivers a classification. Third, mark explicit hand-off points where the issue becomes one of values or interpretive judgment rather than procedural fact, so the practitioner knows to stop and decide personally. Fourth, embed a niche metric you construct — call it the intra-session conflict density index — that estimates whether contradictory reports emerge within a single continuous testing session (indicating competing drafts) versus across separated sessions (indicating theater revision). Fifth, apply the full procedure to three worked cases: (a) a split-brain patient who reports seeing a red circle on the left and a blue square on the right yet verbally denies any left-field awareness; (b) a traumatic brain injury patient who consistently describes a unified movie-like experiential stream despite objective evidence of fragmented attention; (c) a minimally conscious patient who gives contradictory reports about pain location within one ten-minute interview. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls disguised as rules, where the procedure is likely to misfire, and what it fundamentally cannot establish — for instance, whether consciousness is actually distributed, or merely whether a particular report is best interpreted through that lens. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to texts I cannot access without searching. Write formally and concisely, and conclude with a brief note on the limits of proceduralization in this domain.
