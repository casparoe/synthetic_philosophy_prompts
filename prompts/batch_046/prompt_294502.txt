I have recently been appointed to my university’s Committee on International Scholars and Displacement, and the role feels less abstract since my cousin was evacuated from her institution during an authoritarian closure of universities abroad. Next week we must vote on whether to extend our sanctuary-scholar program—currently restricted to academics facing direct, individualized persecution—to include scholars displaced by what our draft proposal calls “systemic institutional collapse.” Under this framing, the harm is not a targeted threat against a particular person but the disappearance of the professional and legal infrastructure that made their work and physical security possible. One committee member argues that without a specific persecutor there is no asylum obligation, only discretionary charity, and that expanding the category will render the program conceptually incoherent and administratively unmanageable; another insists that the structural vulnerability is morally identical to targeted persecution, and that limiting protection to the latter abandons people whose risk profile is the same in every relevant respect except causal proximity.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—whether to adopt the extension, with precise eligibility criteria, a pilot duration, and a clear division of responsibility between our university, state immigration authorities, and international disciplinary networks—and it should treat the “targeted vulnerability” versus “systemic exposure” distinction as a serious conceptual issue rather than a rhetorical device. In particular, the memo should address who bears the primary obligation of admission in these expanded cases: the host institution as an associative body with special duties to its disciplinary peers, the state as a territorial authority with distributive obligations, or the global academic network as a community of practice. The recommendation must also engage the strongest argument for the narrower view—that expanding the category risks dissolving the moral specificity of asylum into general humanitarian aid—and explain whether that risk is decisive here. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the committee needs reasoning evaluated on its own merits, not references I have no time to verify.
