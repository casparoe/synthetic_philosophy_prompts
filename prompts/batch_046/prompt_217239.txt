Consider the following exam question and student response. The question asks students to evaluate the modal argument that a statue (David) and the lump of clay (Lump) composing it are non-identical, given that Lump can survive being squashed into a sphere while David cannot. The answer should address: (i) the structure of the modal argument and its reliance on Leibniz's law; (ii) the mereological essentialist response—often associated with Chisholm—that distinguishes their persistence conditions by treating Lump's material parts as essential to it; and (iii) whether accepting that David and Lump are genuinely distinct forces a four-dimensionalist ontology of temporal parts, or whether a three-dimensionalist can consistently accommodate co-located, non-identical enduring objects (for instance, via a constitution relation).

Student answer:
The modal argument is straightforward. By Leibniz's law, if two objects share all properties, they are identical. Here, Lump has the modal property of being able to survive squashing, while David lacks it. So they differ in a real property and are therefore not identical. This is a valid application of the law.

The essentialist responds by saying that Lump is essentially composed of exactly these clay molecules, so squashing destroys Lump by destroying its parts. David, however, is an artifact, so its identity depends on form and artistic function rather than on which specific molecules compose it. Thus David can survive the loss or rearrangement of parts that Lump cannot. This resolves the puzzle without rejecting Leibniz's law, because once we properly specify the persistence conditions of each object, there is no contradiction: they really do have different modal properties, and that is exactly why they are different things.

However, there is a deeper problem. Since David and Lump share every intrinsic physical property—same mass, shape, location—their difference cannot be a real metaphysical difference; it must be purely conceptual or sortal-relative. Therefore, applying Leibniz's law here is a mistake, because the law applies only to non-conceptual, intrinsic properties. The modal difference is just a projection of our different concepts—statue versus lump—onto the same object.

Moreover, if they really were two distinct objects, they would occupy exactly the same space at the same time, which violates the principle that material bodies are impenetrable. The only consistent way to avoid this contradiction is to adopt a four-dimensionalist ontology in which David and Lump are not enduring three-dimensional objects but different four-dimensional spacetime worms that overlap in a temporal slice. Without temporal parts, co-location of distinct enduring objects is impossible.
