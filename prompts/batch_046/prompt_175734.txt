I am preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher of social science who defends an unnamed but specific position she calls developmental-affordance cohort theory. According to this view, generational labels such as "Boomer," "Millennial," and "Gen Z" are neither natural kinds nor purely conventional demographic tags; rather, they denote modal kinds constituted by shared exposure, during a neuroplastic window roughly between ages twelve and twenty-five, to a distinctive technological and institutional environment that durably structures cognition. Birth years function only as fallible proxies, not as essential criteria, and the genuine boundaries depend on when an affordance matrix—for example, analog information scarcity versus algorithmic abundance—becomes developmentally dominant for an individual. She further maintains that these cohorts possess real predictive power regarding cognitive style and information-processing habits, while explicitly denying that they determine moral character or political orientation.

I would like you to draft eight to ten rigorous but conversational interview questions that go well beyond asking her to summarize the view or answer generic biographical prompts. Please organize the questions around four pressure points that mirror actual debates in social ontology and developmental psychology. First, address the boundary problem: ask how the theory avoids arbitrary cutoff dates without collapsing generational distinctions into a smooth demographic continuum, and anticipate her likely appeal to phase transitions in technological diffusion or probabilistic clustering; then supply a follow-up that forces her to explain why those transitions do not themselves depend on the very cohort distinctions they are meant to establish. Second, probe whether her modal-kind framework genuinely escapes essentialism, particularly in light of analogies to gender or racial categorization, anticipating her reply that cohort membership is revisable and exposure-based rather than innate; follow up by pressing whether exposure-based clustering still risks reifying socially constructed divisions under the guise of developmental science. Third, examine the epistemic circularity of identifying cohort membership when the defining technological exposure is often inferred from the behavioral patterns the cohort is invoked to explain; anticipate her defense that independent archival measures of media access or institutional design can break the circle, then ask how she handles cases where those archival measures conflict with self-identified generational belonging. Fourth, confront the demographic objection that institutional change is gradual, producing continuous gradients rather than discrete breaks; anticipate her response that critical-period plasticity amplifies small environmental differences into large cognitive divergences, and follow up by asking what magnitude of gradient would be sufficient to dissolve a cohort entirely rather than merely blur its edges.

Conclude with a single, precisely formulated question about empirical falsifiability: ask what result in a well-designed longitudinal study—for instance, tracking cognitive-style metrics across a rapid technological transition within a single birth-year span—would genuinely refute the theory rather than being absorbed by an auxiliary adjustment to the critical-window hypothesis or the affordance-matrix specification. The overall tone should suit an intellectually engaged podcast audience familiar with the basic ontology of social kinds but not assumed to have read her specific papers.
