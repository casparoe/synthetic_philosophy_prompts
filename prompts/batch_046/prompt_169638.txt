For my seminar in the philosophy of history, I have been trying to articulate exactly when a legend crosses over into being treated as history, and I want to test whether my intuitions form a coherent pattern. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, the Trojan War. Before the nineteenth-century excavations, it seemed pure myth; after Schliemann's work at Hisarlik and the identification of Wilusa in Hittite correspondence, I now treat the conflict itself as likely historical, though I regard the wooden horse and direct divine interventions as legendary accretions rather than historical events.

Second, King Arthur. Even granting the post-Roman references in Welsh texts and the archaeological interest at Tintagel, I maintain that Arthur remains essentially legendary. The Round Table, Merlin, and the sword in the stone appear structurally mythic, and the absence of contemporary documentation feels decisive to me in a way that the archaeological hints are not.

Third, the Exodus. Here I feel genuine tension. There is no direct archaeological record of a mass migration matching the biblical narrative, yet the Merneptah Stele mentions Israel, and there is evidence of Semitic presence in Egypt. I am inclined to say there is a historical kernel—perhaps a smaller migration or social transformation that was later expanded—so I classify this as "legend containing a historical core" rather than pure legend.

Fourth, Prester John. For centuries Europeans maintained belief in a powerful Christian priest-king in Asia or Africa. When travelers encountered the Christian Ethiopian emperor, some mapped the legend onto him. My verdict is that this is not a case of legend becoming history, but rather of a legend being retroactively anchored to an existing real figure. The historical figure did not validate the legend's content; the legend was relocated onto reality.

Fifth, the Black Sea deluge and the Epic of Gilgamesh. Geological evidence for a rapid flooding around 5600 BCE has led some to claim the flood narrative was "confirmed." I think this gets the relationship wrong: it may show that a natural event lies behind part of the narrative, but it does not make Gilgamesh or Utnapishtim historical figures, nor does it validate the epic as a historical record. So I distinguish sharply between "the narrative contains a verifiable event" and "the narrative is history."

My difficulty is that I seem to apply different standards across these cases. For Troy, archaeological correlation shifts the status of the core conflict; for Arthur, archaeological hints do not shift the status of the core figure; for the Exodus, absence of direct archaeology still permits a historical kernel; for Gilgamesh, presence of a verifiable event does not shift the narrative's status; and for Prester John, contact with a real figure changes the legend's geography without changing its historicity. Is there a single principle—perhaps involving the type of corroboration required, the structural role of the supernatural, or a distinction between event-corroboration and agent-corroboration—that captures all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn for the sake of coherence? And whatever principle results, what does it predict for a case I have not listed—specifically, the nineteenth-century nationalist claim that the Nibelungenlied's Siegfried was a real chieftain, based largely on the structural similarity of the legend to other heroic-age narratives?
