Read Lois Lowry's novel The Giver through the philosophical debate over equality of welfare, resources, and capabilities—particularly Amartya Sen's argument that justice requires equality in people's real freedoms to achieve valuable functionings, not merely in distributed goods or achieved happiness. The Community enforces Sameness: identical dwellings, assigned occupations, and pharmacological suppression of deep emotion, desire, and memory. Is this best understood as an extreme form of equality of welfare (uniform flat contentment), equality of resources (uniform bundles of goods and roles), or a deliberate truncation of capabilities? Focus on Jonas's reception of transmitted memories—love, grief, color, violence, and family attachment—as an awakening to capabilities the system has eliminated. Does his flight defend a Sen-style pluralism of human capabilities, or does the novel concede that some capabilities are too destructive to preserve? Take both the philosophical framework and the text seriously: point to specific scenes such as the job-assignment ceremony, Jonas's first perception of color in the apple, and the sled-and-snow memory, and say where the book illuminates the equality debate and where it resists being reduced to a philosophical illustration.
