I run a graduate seminar on self-reference and consciousness — twelve students, ninety minutes, one session per week. We have been working through Hofstadter’s Gödel, Escher, Bach, with particular attention to the move from Gödel’s arithmetic formula, which asserts its own unprovability within a formal system, to the “strange loop” claim that self-aware minds may be analogous not because they contain literal copies of themselves, but because they encode compressed, self-pointing representations that allow the system to speak about its own limits without stepping outside itself. I want an in-class activity that makes students feel the analogical pressure of this specific move, rather than merely grasping Gödel’s theorem intellectually. The intuition I want destabilised is that self-awareness is straightforwardly “the mind thinking about itself”; instead, participants should experience why direct self-reference works easily at the level of individual sentences but collapses when a system tries to evaluate its own consistency, and why indirect, model-based reference then becomes unavoidable.

Design a structured simulation or role-play — with explicit rules, assigned roles, physical or symbolic materials, and a constraint or payoff structure — that forces the problem to emerge mechanically. Specify the group breakdown, the timeline across the ninety minutes, and how the rules arrange for participants to construct direct self-reference successfully in an early round, then hit a wall in a later round when they attempt system-level self-evaluation. The mechanics should make them discover, through their own failed coordination or blocked inference, that an external representational layer (or a looped internal model) is required — before you name this as the strange loop. Include a debrief that maps precise moments in the activity onto (a) the construction of the Gödel sentence, (b) the indirect self-model of the strange loop, and (c) the analogical leap to conscious self-awareness. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for instance, one subgroup solving the second round too quickly, or participants conflating sentence-level and system-level reference — and how to handle it. Finally, be frank about which feature of Hofstadter’s analogy the activity cannot reproduce and that I, as facilitator, must supply explicitly: for example, the phenomenological “what it is like” dimension of self-awareness that formal self-reference entirely omits. Do not include a bibliography or reading list; the proposal should be answerable from the conceptual structure alone. Write in British English.
