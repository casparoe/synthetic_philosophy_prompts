Consider the well-known passage in the Zhuangzi in which Zhuang Zhou's wife dies. His friend Hui Shi comes to offer condolences and finds Zhuang Zhou sitting with legs spread, beating on a tub and singing. When Hui Shi reproaches him for failing to weep, Zhuang Zhou replies that he initially grieved, but then reflected that before her birth she had no form, and amid the transformations of qi she came to be; now she has transformed once more, like the turning of the seasons in a great house, and therefore he sees no cause for lamentation.

I am trying to understand whether this response is best interpreted as endorsing what I will call "transformational identity"—the view that the self is not a persisting substance but a temporary configuration of qi, so that mourning presupposes a false ontology of stable personhood—or whether it is better read as an "affective counter-rhythm," a deliberate performative rupture with the social script of grief that need not commit Zhuang Zhou to any particular metaphysics of the self. The two readings converge on the same outward behavior, yet they diverge on whether his calm necessarily depends on accepting a continuous ontology of transformation or can be sustained as a purely practical stance independent of such commitments.

Could you help me determine which reading the passage actually supports? Specifically, I want to know whether the text offers any internal reason to treat Zhuang Zhou's reply as a metaphysical argument about qi and identity rather than as a refusal to enact conventional sorrow, and whether a further conceptual distinction—perhaps between viewing death as a return to an undifferentiated origin versus viewing it as merely one change among innumerable others—would allow us to adjudicate between these interpretations. I am not seeking a bibliography or external references, only a careful analysis of the argument's conceptual structure as it stands within the passage itself.
