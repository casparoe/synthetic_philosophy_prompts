I'm trying to determine my actual view on political obligation and the limits of justified civil disobedience, and I want to see whether my intuitions form a coherent principle or fall apart.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. A draftee refuses deployment in a war he judges aggressively unjust and accepts imprisonment; I think his obligation to serve in this specific conflict is void, though his general obligation to obey just laws remains intact. A taxpayer withholds exactly the calculated portion of her income tax that funds that war, donates it to humanitarian relief, and publishes her reasoning; I think this selective refusal is justified—she avoids direct complicity without rejecting the fiscal system wholesale. A low-level administrator secretly alters records to block a deportation she considers cruel but that is fully legal; I think this is not justified, because her private moral judgment does not suspend her institutional duty to apply the law. A reporter publishes classified evidence of systematic violations of international humanitarian law, despite warnings that disclosure endangers sources; I think her obligation to secrecy is overridden. The same reporter publishes classified diplomatic cables revealing only embarrassing but lawful negotiations, arguing that all state secrecy is undemocratic; I think her obligation holds—without grave hidden wrong, confidentiality remains binding. Protesters occupy a public plaza peacefully, accept arrest without resistance, and do not impede emergency access, aiming to force legislative debate; I think this open, accountable disobedience is justified. The same protesters, after months without result, anonymously sabotage the legislature's voting infrastructure for one day—no injuries, but covert and destructive; I think this is not justified, since it abandons the public, accountable character that distinguishes civil disobedience from private coercion.

The case I keep revisiting is this: a private citizen breaks into a weapons facility to destroy manufacturing blueprints for the unjust war, then turns himself in and issues a public statement accepting punishment. Sometimes this looks like justified refusal of complicity; other times it looks like covert vigilantism that undermines the rule of law regardless of motive.

Is there a principle—perhaps about the modularity of obligation, the publicity and accountability of the act, or a sharp distinction between avoiding complicity and enforcing private conscience—that captures verdicts one through six and also resolves case eight? Or must I overturn one of the seven to preserve simplicity? If there is a principle, what does it predict for a case I have not listed: a physician who refuses to administer a legally required but medically unnecessary procedure that she considers degrading?
