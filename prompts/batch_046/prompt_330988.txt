Here's the student answer:

"Ian Hacking distinguishes 'interactive kinds' from 'indifferent kinds' like minerals or planets. Psychiatric diagnoses are interactive because people are self-interpreting: once they learn they fall under a category, they change their behavior and self-understanding in response. The classic case is multiple personality disorder in the 1980s, where clinicians began diagnosing it more frequently, patients started presenting with more alters, and therapists used hypnosis to bring out personalities. This shows the loop is mainly one-directional: the diagnostic label produces symptoms in vulnerable people, but the patients don't really feed back into the taxonomy itself. Hacking calls this dynamic nominalism, which means that naming something brings it into existence. From this he concludes that conditions like MPD lack biological reality and are essentially narratives co-constructed by doctors and patients, though the suffering is real. The same logic applies to depression, except the loop is weaker there because depression has stronger biological roots, so Hacking's point mostly works for dissociative disorders."
