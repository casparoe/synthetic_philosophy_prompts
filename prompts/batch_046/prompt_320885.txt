I am writing from my metaphysics seminar, where we have been working through the problem of material constitution—specifically the familiar case of a statue molded from a lump of clay. I want to push the conceptual issue into a difficulty that our standard formulations tend to gloss over. Here is the setup: macroscopic material objects are not microscopically static. At any given moment, the atoms and molecules composing the clay are being replaced, exchanged with the surrounding environment, or rearranged, so that the precise collection of particles that makes up the clay at time t1 is not identical to the collection at t2. This means that, strictly speaking, there may be no single enduring material object—call it “the Clay”—that persists through the statue’s lifetime and is coincident with it at every stage. Instead, there might only be a rapid succession of momentary clay-aggregates, each one coincident with the statue for an instant.

The conceptual issue I want you to explain is whether this microscopic turnover undermines the standard claim that the statue and the clay are two distinct objects that share the same location. If material constitution is a relation that holds between two objects existing simultaneously, then at each instant the statue is indeed constituted by some particular clay-aggregate. But if we say the statue is constituted by “the clay” (singular) across its entire existence, we seem to be treating that succession of aggregates as one object, apparently attributing to a material aggregate a persistence condition—allowing gradual part-replacement—that we might deny to other physical collections. So does this mean the statue is never constituted by a single material object, but only by a series? And if so, does that dissolve the supposed coincidence relation, since there is no one clay-object to be distinct from the statue? Or should we instead grant that the clay-object does have a special persistence condition (perhaps functional or continuous-process-based) that allows it to survive part-replacement, and if so, why should that condition apply to the clay but not to the statue, given that both seem to survive the same replacements?

Please explain this conceptual tension precisely: distinguish the persistence conditions appropriate to an artifact (defined perhaps by form, function, or artistic intention) from those appropriate to a material aggregate, and address whether microscopic turnover forces us to abandon the idea of one enduring clay-object coincident with the statue. In particular, clarify whether material constitution, on this picture, is always a relation to a “process” or flow of matter rather than to a stable substance, and what that implies for the claim that the statue and its material base are genuinely distinct. I am not asking for references, citations, or a survey of specific philosophers’ views; I want a conceptual clarification that I could work through with only the conceptual resources available in a standard metaphysics course.
