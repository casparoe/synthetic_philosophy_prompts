I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I have been trying to understand why the debate over suffering risks—s-risks—has stalled despite widespread agreement on the premises. On one side are researchers and advocates who argue that future scenarios involving misaligned artificial intelligence, stable totalitarian regimes, or space colonization could generate suffering on an astronomical scale, and that this possibility should dominate our longterm priorities; on the other side are critics who grant the logical possibility but treat it as negligibly probable, or who argue that promoting positive value is the proper aim and that suffering-reduction framing introduces an unmotivated asymmetry. Both camps use expected-value reasoning, both accept that suffering is intrinsically bad, and both acknowledge that the future could be enormous, yet over years of forums, papers, and informal discussion almost no one has changed position. I suspect the impasse is not empirical but conceptual: specifically, I wonder whether the sides implicitly differ on whether extreme suffering possesses what I will call temporal moral isolation—the property that suffering in one timeline or causal branch cannot be morally offset by happiness in another—so that the disagreement is really about the structure of moral aggregation rather than about probabilities. Is this stall a verbal dispute about the word risk, a substantive but hidden disagreement about decision-theoretic weights or about which hypothetical futures count as admissible evidence, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about the asymmetry between suffering and flourishing that neither side can argue for? Diagnose the structure using what each side actually says, distinguish what is strictly presupposed from what merely fits, and tell me what—if anything—would move the debate: a formal result, a new conceptual distinction, an empirical constraint, or an argument that the question is ill-posed. Present your response as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
