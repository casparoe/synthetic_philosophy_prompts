As a novelist who often constructs antagonists meant to evoke genuine reader resentment rather than mere aesthetic revulsion, I am struck by a conceptual tension in P. F. Strawson’s account of reactive attitudes and moral responsibility. Strawson maintains that attitudes such as resentment are essentially interpersonal responses to the quality of another’s will directed at us, and he opposes them to the “objective attitude” we adopt toward those we treat as objects of social management. Yet readers routinely experience something structurally akin to resentment toward fictional wrongdoers—consider the enduring moral anger directed at a figure like Iago—despite knowing that no actual interpersonal relation exists and that the agent in question is ontologically absent. Give an example of a conceptual issue that arises specifically at the intersection of this mediated, reader-directed reactive attitude and Strawson’s framework. Your example should isolate a precise point of tension—perhaps whether the lack of ontological reciprocity collapses the attitude into aesthetic judgment, or whether Strawson’s distinction implicitly presupposes a form of co-presence that narrative fiction deliberately suspends—without requesting citations, bibliographies, or references to obscure secondary literature. Frame the issue as one a writer of morally complex fiction might genuinely need to navigate, not merely as an academic taxonomy problem.
