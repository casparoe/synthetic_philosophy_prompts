I have recently assumed responsibility for coordinating a postgraduate philosophy workshop on rational belief and self-location, and the Sleeping Beauty problem has disturbed my own intuitions more profoundly than I expected: I remain genuinely uncertain whether my credence in a repeated event ought to shift merely because I might be one of several indistinguishable awakenings of the same subject. I should like you to design a structured, 90-minute classroom activity for ten to twelve participants that makes this epistemic tension physically palpable rather than merely notional.

The exercise should take the form of a staged game or role-play in which participants literally occupy centred worlds governed by a hidden coin toss. In the heads condition, there is a single Monday awakening; in the tails condition, there are identical Monday and Tuesday awakenings separated by complete amnesia. To prevent the session from collapsing into a familiar textbook rehearsal, please inject the following variation: in the tails branch only, the Tuesday awakening experiences a faint but genuine sensory difference—a slightly cooler ambient temperature—whereas the Monday awakening does not; both are told, however, that this cue is unreliable and may be confabulated. The group must then reach a collective decision—specifically, distributing a shared pool of points that will be paid out to every awakening—before any individual learns whether they are in heads or tails, or whether they are Monday or Tuesday. The payoff rules should be arranged so that halfers and thirders advocate radically different distributions, and the conflict should emerge from the participants' own reasoning under the amnesia and information asymmetry, not from any explanation given afterwards.

Please provide: a precise 90-minute timeline; explicit roles and rules that enforce the amnesia protocol and the restricted communication between temporal parts; the debrief that maps specific moments onto the halfing and thirder positions, with particular attention to how the unreliable sensory cue complicates the counting argument; an honest statement of which feature of self-locating belief the game cannot reproduce—above all, the purely first-person indexicality of credence—and how you will supply that deficit verbally; and a frank prediction of what will go wrong in the first run, together with practical remedies. Write in formal British English, avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations entirely, and do not use verbatim quotations; paraphrase any reference to specific thinkers without quotation marks.
