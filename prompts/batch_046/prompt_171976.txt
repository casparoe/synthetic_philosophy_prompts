Recently, after observing an adaptive routing system adjust its behavior in real time without any obvious internal revision, I found myself returning to the long-running debate about sphexishness: the sphex wasp’s rigid, stereotyped sequence of burrow-checking and egg-laying, classically used to ask whether mechanical repetition is compatible with intentional action or free will. For decades the exchange has seemed frozen. One side treats the wasp as showing that rigid, deterministic behavior can still be genuinely goal-directed; the other insists that real agency requires perturbation-sensitive flexibility—the capacity to adapt when conditions change—and that human freedom is therefore either complex sphexishness in disguise or categorically different from it. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about insect biology or even about psychology, but about a hidden conceptual split: whether counterfactual robustness is a constitutive condition of free action or merely an epistemic marker we use to distinguish agents from mechanisms. Is that diagnosis correct, or is the stall actually caused by a verbal dispute over “flexibility,” by two different questions—one about action explanation, one about moral responsibility—running under a single heading, or by unargued starting intuitions about which cases count as data? I want an analysis of the disagreement’s structure, supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest account of whether any move—an empirical finding about adaptive behavior, a formal model of decision-making, or a conceptual distinction—could genuinely resolve it or dissolve it. Please answer in American English, and clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature; do not present a contested view as the consensus.
