I hold the strong version of Wittgenstein's private language argument: not merely that a wholly private language is empirically impossible, but that first-person psychological authority is constituted entirely through mastery of public expressive practices, leaving no residual "inner" content that could be conceptually grasped without intersubjective criteria of correct application. I take this to undermine any philosophical psychology—Cartesian, phenomenological, or contemporary—that treats mental representation as fundamentally non-social, including recent suggestions that artificial systems might develop genuinely private conceptual schemes through unsupervised internal coherence or that neurodivergent cognition involves essentially incommunicable phenomenal structures. What would count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward: conceptual analyses showing that normative constraint can arise without intersubjective correction; concrete cases from developmental psychology, machine learning, or neurodivergence where meaningful conceptual differentiation occurs without shared linguistic scaffolding; and arguments that phenomenal concepts operate demonstratively rather than descriptively, allowing private grasp without public criteria. Which of these would committed defenders of the strong social-constitution view concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject or mislocating the normative structure? Has the view been qualified—say, by allowing "quasi-private" mental vocabulary, by retreating to a purely negative thesis about language-games, or by insulating itself against any counterexample—into something that risks nothing empirically? If so, is that insulation a virtue that preserves the argument's conceptual rigor, or a vice that renders it unfalsifiable in precisely the ways that weaken philosophical positions of this kind? I would find most persuasive any result that shows private normative correction is not just possible but necessary for explaining specific capacities we already recognize.
