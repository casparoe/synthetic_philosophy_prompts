I have just finished a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend about whether everyday social conventions—tipping, queuing, jaywalking—carry genuine moral weight, and in particular whether a visitor who violates local practice acts wrongly or merely displays poor manners. We kept returning to a conceptual distinction I find useful between institutionally scaffolded norms (conventions embedded in legal or economic structures that make compliance a condition of fair cooperation and create specific dependencies) and emergent coordination norms (practices that solve collective-action problems without comparable distributive stakes or institutional backing), and to whether residents and temporary visitors stand in different moral relations to each. To help me think more clearly and avoid the biases of our debate, I drafted the following exam question for my introductory moral philosophy course; I now need to grade a student answer, but I want a detached, point-by-point assessment rather than my own impression.

Here is the exam question I assigned:

Some philosophers distinguish institutionally scaffolded norms—conventions embedded in a legal or economic framework that makes one party’s compliance a condition of another party’s livelihood—from emergent coordination norms, which solve collective-action problems without creating comparable dependencies. A further distinction is sometimes drawn between resident obligations (duties owed by long-term participants in the cooperative scheme) and visitor obligations (duties owed by temporary participants). Apply these distinctions to evaluate whether a tourist who refuses to tip in a country with a subminimum wage for tipped workers, or who jaywalks in a city where pedestrians consistently wait for signals, acts morally wrongly or merely rudely. Your answer should address whether the tourist’s ignorance of the local convention excuses her, and whether the two types of norms generate obligations of different strength or kind for residents versus visitors.

Here is the student’s answer (approximately 350 words):

Everyday conventions fall into two categories: institutionally scaffolded norms and emergent coordination norms. A scaffolded norm, such as tipping in jurisdictions with a subminimum wage for tipped workers, is embedded in a legal-economic structure that makes the customer’s contribution part of the worker’s livelihood; violating it therefore deprives a specific party of expected compensation within a system designed to depend on it. An emergent norm, such as queuing or obeying pedestrian signals, solves a collective action problem—preventing chaos at bus stops or intersections—without creating comparable distributive stakes or institutional dependencies.

Because scaffolded norms involve structured harm, they generate resident obligations: locals who regularly benefit from the system owe compliance as a matter of fairness. Visitors, however, occupy a different moral position. Since they do not participate in the long-term cooperative scheme, they bear only derivative obligations—specifically, an obligation not to undermine the system when they are aware of its scaffolding. By contrast, emergent norms generate at most weak obligations of courtesy for both residents and visitors, since no party is made vulnerable by noncompliance.

Applying this framework, a tourist who refuses to tip in a tipped-wage economy commits a moral wrong if she knows about the wage structure, because she exploits a scaffolded dependency. If she is genuinely ignorant, however, she does not act wrongly—though she may display poor judgment—because the obligation is activated by awareness of the institutional frame. Meanwhile, jaywalking or cutting a queue is never more than rude, regardless of the visitor’s knowledge, since these practices are purely emergent.

This shows that the distinction between resident and visitor obligations tracks the scaffolded/emergent divide precisely: only scaffolded norms can bind visitors, and only under conditions of epistemic access.
