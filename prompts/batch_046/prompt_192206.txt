I've been getting into judgment agregration lately, specifically the discursive dilemma where a group can be consistent on every premise vote but end up with a conclusion that contradicts those premises if you apply majority rule flat-out. So I cooked up this position: the paradox only really bites if you treat collective reasoning like a one-shot vote over all propositions at once. Instead, I think groups should use a sequential closure procedure — evaluate premises in some order, and if you hit an inconsistency with what you've already locked in, you don't just overrule; you enter a suspension state where you revison the least central premise. This lets you keep collective rationality without pretending there's a "group mind" floating around. Basically I think the dilemma is an artifact of simultaneous agregration, not of democracy itself.

What would count against this? I want the real defeaters, not just "you're hiding the problem." Are there formal results showing any sequential closure rule eventually collapses into either pure premise-based or pure conclusion-based agregration? Would it kill my view if empirical studies show real committees never actually suspend and revise — they just vote and live with contradiction? Or if the ordering of premises is always going to be politically fought over, so my "closure" just moves the conflict upstream? Also, am I accidentally smuggling in an epistemic hierarchy that clashes with democratic equality? Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me what defenders of the standard dilemma view (the List/Pettit-style ones) would actually concede versus what they'd dismiss as goalpost-moving. And be honest: is this position well-insulated enough that it's almost unfalsifiable, and if so, is that a problem for a view about how groups should reason?
