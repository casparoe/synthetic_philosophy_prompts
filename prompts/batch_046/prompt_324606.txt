Write a Petrarchan sonnet (octave ABBAABBA, sestet CDECDE, with a closing rhyming couplet EE) from the first-person perspective of Avicenna's flying man at the instant of his creation in empty space, eyes veiled and limbs separated so that no sensation reaches him and no memory remains. In the octave, establish through imagery and rhyme the total absence of sensory input and bodily awareness: he has never seen light, felt air, or touched another limb, and there is no prior experience to recall. The volta at line 9 should occur with the phrase "Yet I am," marking the immediate, non-inferential affirmation of self-existence. In the sestet, develop the argument's implication: because this awareness of self occurs without any bodily mediation, the soul that knows itself is not identical to the body it might later inhabit. In the final couplet, include a precise distinction from Descartes's cogito: the flying man does not affirm himself as a thinking substance opposed to extended substance, because in his suspended state he lacks even the concept of extension or spatial body altogether; his certainty is of pure self-presence unopposed. Use iambic pentameter throughout, and let the shift in rhyme sounds between the octave (suggesting absence and separation) and the sestet (suggesting presence and identity) do structural work that mirrors the argument's move from negated sensation to affirmed self-knowledge. The poem should be accurate enough that a historian of Islamic philosophy would recognize Avicenna's specific framing—that this is presented as a direct datum of the soul's own nature in his psychological writings rather than derived as a metaphysical deduction—without requiring the poem to cite texts, use untranslated Arabic terms, or mention specific editions.
