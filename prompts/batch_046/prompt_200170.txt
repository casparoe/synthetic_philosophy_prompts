In a philosophy of history reading group I attend, two participants—let’s call them Elena and Marcus—spent an hour debating why certain moral norms collapse rather than being gradually reasoned away. Elena defended Bernard Williams’s thesis that practices like slavery and dueling did not end through moral argument but through shifts in social conditions, economic interests, and the erosion of the honor structures that made them sustainable; she argued that people usually construct post-hoc moral justifications once a practice has become structurally inconvenient. Marcus countered with Kwame Anthony Appiah’s claim that moral revolutions can occur through arguments internal to honor itself—most clearly with dueling—where participants came to see the practice as incompatible with true honor, not merely unfashionable or costly. To avoid rehearsing the familiar examples, they applied the dispute to the disappearance of formal blood-feud obligations in societies that adopted centralized legal authority.

Elena argued that blood-feuds vanished because the state monopolized violence and made private vengeance structurally impossible and socially ruinous; any subsequent moral language about the immorality of revenge was retrospective rationalization, and she noted that in groups that rejected state legitimacy, blood-feud obligations often persisted precisely because the structural pressure was absent. Marcus replied that this persistence proves his point: those resistant groups maintained an alternative honor code that explicitly valued vengeance, which shows the practice died not wherever the state was present, but wherever the honor code itself was transformed to view vengeance as barbaric rather than noble. Elena then pressed a further objection: if honor is genuinely being redefined by moral argument, then we should see isolated individuals or small communities abandoning blood-feuds on purely normative grounds even without state pressure, yet historical records rarely show such spontaneous moral conversions. Marcus conceded that pure argument rarely moves people in isolation, but insisted that the transformation of honor is a collective, socially embedded process—what Appiah describes—not an individual rational choice, so the absence of lone converts does not disprove the role of normative reasoning.

They turned to me and asked for an adjudication. Please evaluate their exchange step by step: rate the strength of Elena’s structural-interest claim, Marcus’s honor-redefinition claim, Elena’s counter-example of resistant groups, Marcus’s response about collective embeddedness, and Elena’s final challenge about the absence of spontaneous individual conversions. Who is right about which specific move, what are both missing about the interaction between structural pressure and normative change, and which account—if either—better explains why a norm like dueling or blood-feud actually dies?
