I am preparing a diagnostic instrument for a graduate programme on moral agency, and I should like your help in constructing a decision tree that sorts participants by which variety of free will they actually take to be worth wanting, rather than by the label they habitually apply to themselves. The impetus came from a recent discussion with colleagues in which we realised that self-described compatibilists often diverge sharply over three concrete scenarios: whether a democratic assembly in which no individual voter determines the outcome can bear collective moral responsibility; whether a neuroscientist who deliberately restructures her own reward circuitry over many years retains the kind of sourcehood required for praise or blame; and whether an agent who was coerced into a harmful act but later fully endorses it as expressing her true values thereby restores the moral standing of the choice. I want the tree to begin from a shared commitment—that there exists at least one conception of free will that matters for moral responsibility, regardless of whether physical determinism is true—and then proceed through branching questions that can be answered by reflecting on these and similar cases, without presupposing familiarity with the specialist literature.

Please construct the taxonomy from the logical space first: identify the principal axes along which positions genuinely diverge—such as whether responsibility requires the availability of alternative possibilities, whether it demands that the agent be the ultimate originator of her actions rather than merely responsive to reasons in the appropriate way, and whether collective or distributed agency can ever satisfy the conditions that make responsibility appropriate. Each branch node should present a specific case or claim on which the positions come apart, phrased as a question that a respondent could answer without already knowing which philosophical position it corresponds to. The tree should terminate in named leaves that must include hierarchical compatibilism, source-based libertarianism, reactive-attitude compatibilism (the view that makes responsibility depend on the appropriateness of interpersonal responses rather than on metaphysical control), and hard determinist rejection of all free-will-related moral responsibility. Where the logical combinations yield unoccupied positions, note them briefly and diagnose whether the gap arises from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. In particular, flag any pair of leaves—such as hierarchical compatibilism and reactive-attitude compatibilism in configurations where both deny the need for alternative possibilities—that your tree cannot genuinely separate on the basis of verdicts alone, and explain why the distinction collapses at the practical level.

Keep the instrument concise and usable: no more than seven branch nodes, with every path leading from the initial commitment to one of the required leaves within four or five answers. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the aim is to reveal conceptual commitments through verdicts on cases. Write the output in polished, formal prose suitable for circulation to seminar participants, employing British spelling conventions throughout.
