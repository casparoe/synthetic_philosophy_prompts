I've been chewing on the Löbian obstacle with some friends — you know, the Gödel-flavored problem where any agent that's formally consistent can't actually prove its own consistency, so a fully rational self-trusting system hits a ceiling right at the point where it tries to validate its own reasoning loop. My roommate is convinced this makes hierarchical AI architectures impossible, because if agent A can't verify its own consistency proof, it can never rationally endorse a modified version of itself. But I think people conflate the formal result with the practical dynamics: there's a difference between "can't derive consistency in system S" and "can't update on evidence that I'm consistent," and I also keep wondering about this weird asymmetry I made up — call it delegation inversion — where agent A can verify B's consistency proof even though B can't verify its own, so A might rationally trust B more than B trusts itself. Does that actually help design self-improving systems, or is it just a philosophical parlor trick?

Anyway, I want to know which open questions here are actually worth working on if you're allocating real resources — say, three philosophy PhD students for four years, or a small grant. There's the formalization of reflective consistency in bounded agents, the probabilistic self-trust literature tied to logical induction, whether probabilistic reasoning genuinely sidesteps the second incompleteness result or just hides it, and this delegation/inversion angle I mentioned. Give me a defended prioritization: which are upstream of the others, which are tractable with current logical and decision-theoretic tools versus needing breakthroughs in proof theory, and which ones wouldn't change much downstream even if solved. I'm especially interested in what evidence over the next few years — maybe a formal result about probabilistic reasoning and consistency, or a failure mode in logical induction — should make us reorder the list. Don't just survey; tell me where the effort should go and why, including whether some of these questions are basically dead ends that people keep revisiting because they sound scary.
