Consider the framework of geometric rationality that arises from Kelly betting and ergodicity economics: in sequential, multiplicative environments, rational agents maximize the geometric growth rate of capital rather than arithmetic expectation. The familiar controversies involve optimal leverage, partial Kelly strategies, and whether ergodic reasoning applies to single lifetimes or only to statistical ensembles. I want to focus on a structurally different but under-examined setting: an agent makes sequential, multiplicative wagers with private geometric-optimal targets, but must periodically justify its trajectory to an evaluator who scores performance by arithmetic mean returns over discrete evaluation windows, and whose approval determines continued access to capital. This creates a two-masters problem in which the strategy that maximizes long-run geometric growth—often involving high variance and substantial interim drawdowns—systematically conflicts with the strategy that secures evaluator approval.

Within this intersection of geometric rationality and evaluation-constrained decision-making, which open conceptual questions deserve priority? Specifically, I am weighing three clusters: first, whether a formally modified Kelly criterion can optimize geometric growth subject to an arithmetic-mean approval constraint over finite windows; second, whether such approval mechanisms induce a predictable, quantifiable welfare loss relative to unconstrained geometric maximization; and third, whether agents should adopt a meta-criterion that explicitly trades geometric growth against approval-risk. If you were directing a small theoretical research group with three years and no empirical laboratory—working only with formal models and normative arguments—which cluster should be tackled first, second, and third? Defend the ordering in terms of upstream dependencies: which results other clusters presuppose, which are tractable with existing tools from stochastic optimization and decision theory, and which, if resolved, would most change how we design institutional investment mandates or prediction-market governance structures?
