I have been working through a set of commitments about historical materialism in preparation for a graduate seminar, and I would like to know whether they form a coherent package or whether I am smuggling in contradictions that I have not yet noticed. Roughly, my position is as follows.

First, I accept the classical claim that the economic structure—the relations and forces of production—functions as the base that determines the legal, political, and ideological superstructure in the last instance, though I interpret this as a claim about long-run explanatory priority rather than about the causal determination of every particular event.

Second, I hold that the superstructure possesses relative autonomy: legal institutions, for instance, can develop internal logics that feed back upon and reshape economic relations, particularly during periods of structural crisis.

Third, I reject technological determinism—the view that the forces of production drive history independently—while maintaining that technological development is internal to those forces and can eventually generate contradictions with existing relations of production.

Fourth, I take cultural and religious phenomena to be epiphenomenal to material conditions: they do not independently cause structural change, though they may operate as ideological state apparatuses that stabilize a given mode of production.

Fifth, I want to extend this framework to contemporary digital economies by proposing that, in this domain, the traditional base-superstructure distinction should be replaced by a distinction between a protocol layer—the underlying code, infrastructure, and ownership rules governing data and computation—and an interface layer—the political, cultural, and experiential formations that users encounter. I take the protocol layer to be the functional equivalent of the economic base here.

Sixth, I maintain that Gramscian hegemony operates not only through ideas but also through embodied material practices—ritual, architecture, bodily discipline—that belong to the protocol layer rather than to the superstructure.

My central worry is that the fifth and sixth commitments may undermine the first and fourth. If the protocol-interface distinction replaces the base-superstructure model for a significant domain, have I abandoned the claim of long-run explanatory priority? And if material practices of hegemony are relocated into the protocol layer, am I collapsing culture into the base in a way that renders the epiphenomenalism of the fourth commitment either trivial or false?

Could you audit these commitments for me? I want to know which pairs are genuinely in tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and what the cheapest revision is should consistency require giving something up. Please be specific about whether the fifth commitment functions as a local extension or as a global replacement, and whether the sixth requires me to drop the autonomy claim in the second.
