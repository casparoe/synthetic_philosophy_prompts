I am composing a dialogue for my novel set in a winter seminar room where the protagonist, Dr. Elias Vore, a philosopher of mind, advances what he calls the "null-content thesis." On his reading of higher-order theories, Vore argues that consciousness is not an intrinsic feature of first-order mental states but a purely relational property constituted entirely by a concurrent meta-representational attitude. From this he infers that if a mental state—say, a sharp visceral grief or a subliminal belief—fails to attract the requisite higher-order representation, it does not merely slip into unconsciousness but undergoes a kind of ontological cancellation, leaving behind no mental residue whatsoever. He insists that unconscious mental life is therefore impossible in principle: any state lacking the second-order correlation is not hidden but void, expelled entirely from the mental economy. His interlocutor objects that this conflates being conscious with existing, yet Vore presses the point by comparing this alleged cancellation to subliminal perceptions, which he claims occur without awareness precisely because the first-order content has been nullified rather than merely overlooked.

For the sake of writing Vore's position rigorously but revealing its underlying confusion, I want to understand where the null-content thesis misinterprets the standard higher-order framework—particularly the distinction between being conscious of a state and being in a conscious state. Do defenders of these theories, who treat first-order representations as capable of existing without being targets of higher-order thoughts, genuinely maintain that an unrepresented pain or belief persists as a fully real but unconscious mental content? Or are they committed to denying its existence altogether? And does Vore's analogy to subliminal perception strengthen his case for ontological cancellation, or does it instead show that first-order representations can retain full mental reality even when awareness is absent, thereby undermining the thesis he seeks to defend?
