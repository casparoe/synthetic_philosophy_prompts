I'm preparing a position statement for a philosophy-of-mind workshop on consciousness and its neural correlates, and I want a rigorous consistency audit before I present. Here is where I have landed:

(1) I accept the conceptual distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness—Block's distinction—but I reject that they are metaphysically independent properties. Instead, I hold that phenomenal consciousness is direct, non-inferential self-access to representational content, whereas access consciousness is the global broadcasting of that same content to reasoning and action systems.

(2) I endorse Global Workspace Theory as the correct functional architecture for access consciousness: broadcasting into a fronto-parietal workspace, marked by long-range synchronization and signatures like the P3b, is both necessary and sufficient for a state to be access-conscious.

(3) I believe phenomenal consciousness can occur without global broadcasting—specifically, in localized recurrent processing confined to sensory cortices, where there is phenomenal presence that never reaches the workspace.

(4) I also believe the converse dissociation is real: global broadcasting can occur without phenomenal consciousness, as in highly automatized expert performance or subliminal priming that reaches the workspace and guides behavior without any direct self-access.

(5) Despite (3), I maintain that any state counting as genuinely phenomenal must be potentially globalizable—there must exist, in principle, a counterfactual pathway by which its content could enter the global workspace, since without this potentiality it would fail to qualify as a mental representation at all.

(6) I think the hard problem of consciousness is genuine and applies specifically to the direct self-access characterized in (1): explaining broadcasting solves access consciousness, yet it leaves untouched why direct access should carry subjective character.

(7) Methodologically, I treat first-person reports as authoritative for phenomenal presence and neural workspace signatures as authoritative for access, but I deny that any purely behavioral or third-person functional criterion can establish phenomenal consciousness absent report.

These commitments feel coherent to me, but I suspect I am smuggling in contradictions. In particular, (1) frames phenomenal consciousness as a mode of access, yet (3) and (4) treat the two as fully dissociable, which sounds like the metaphysical independence I rejected. Meanwhile, (5) seems to constrain (3) by requiring potential globalizability, yet I am unsure whether this saves consistency or merely introduces a hidden conflict with permanently isolated local loops. And (6) preserves a hard problem for direct self-access, even though (1) appears to reduce phenomenal consciousness to a functional relation that should dissolve, not preserve, an explanatory gap.
