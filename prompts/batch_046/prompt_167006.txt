I was recently discussing multi-agent commitment mechanisms with a colleague who works on cooperative AI, and I found myself stuck on a conceptual point about safe Pareto improvements. As I understand them, an SPI is a restructuring of a game's payoff rules—rather than a particular strategy profile—such that every player receives a strictly higher payoff than under the baseline, irrespective of the opponents' choices. This seems like an unambiguous gain, and I initially assumed that rational agents should therefore be able to enact such a restructuring unilaterally: if my payoff improves in every contingency, then your decision to cooperate or defect should not affect my incentive to adopt the new rules. But my colleague pointed out that the literature treats SPIs as achievements that typically require collective agreement or institutional design, and that individually incentivized SPIs are considered a special case rather than the default. This has left me puzzled about where my reasoning fails. Am I wrongly treating a game-form transformation as an individual action within the original action space? Or is the mistake deeper—perhaps involving the difference between ex post dominance in a fixed game and the ex ante conditions required to restructure the game itself—so that what looks like a unilateral improvement actually depends on shared mechanisms that one party cannot deploy alone? I would appreciate help identifying the precise conceptual confusion here, especially if it relates to how dominance reasoning applies when the very set of available moves is what needs to change.
