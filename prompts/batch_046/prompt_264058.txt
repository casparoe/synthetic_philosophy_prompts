Here is an argument I built for a seminar on mental causation, and I find it suspicious because it seems to collapse epiphenomenalism into self-refutation and then overreach to threaten related physicalist positions. (1) Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal properties have no downward causal influence on physical processes, including brain states. (2) A judgment such as "I am now experiencing pain" is a physical event—neural and behavioral. (3) Therefore the phenomenal property of being in pain makes no causal contribution to the occurrence of that judgment; the judgment is produced entirely by non-phenomenal neural mechanisms. (4) Introduce what I will call the Responsiveness Requirement: for a judgment J asserting property P to be genuinely about P and to yield knowledge of P, P must make some causal contribution to J—otherwise J is responsive only to whatever physical state actually produced it, and is at best about that substrate. (5) By (3) and (4), phenomenal judgments are not genuinely about phenomenal properties, do not yield phenomenal knowledge, and apply their concepts to the wrong objects. (6) The same reasoning applies across the board: if any mental property is denied causal efficacy, judgments about it become unanchored. (7) But epiphenomenalism is usually motivated by direct acquaintance or introspective access—precisely the phenomenal knowledge that (5) denies. So the view undermines its own foundation. (8) Worse, the argument seems to work against any position that strips causal efficacy from a mental category, including some non-reductive physicalist accounts of mental causation, which is more damage than I intended. Which step should I reject? I am inclined to keep (1) and (3), but I am unsure whether (4) smuggles in an inappropriate causal theory of mental content, or whether there is a hidden ambiguity between causing the judgment-token and explaining its accuracy. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—identify the flawed step or the least costly premise to abandon and state briefly why—then lay out the reasoning.
