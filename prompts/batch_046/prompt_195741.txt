Read Mark Twain's Adventures of Huckleberry Finn through the lens of slur semantics and pragmatics — specifically the debate over whether racial slurs carry pejorative content as part of semantic meaning (expressivist and hybrid accounts) or whether that force is purely pragmatic (conventional implicature, presupposition, or contextual association), together with the further problem of how such terms behave when embedded in fictional narrative rather than uttered in direct speech.

The novel is saturated with a particular anti-Black racial slur applied to Jim, deployed by Pap, the Duke and the Dauphin, and woven through Huck's own narration. Take both directions seriously: use philosophical tools to illuminate specific scenes and character choices, and let the novel's complexity resist or refine those tools.

First, reconstruct the implicit semantic landscape. Is there a neutral counterpart in the novel's world — a purely descriptive term with exactly the same extension — or does the slur perform descriptive work that no other available term can do? Compare moments where the word appears in direct dialogue (Pap's rant, the Duke's mockery) with moments where it appears in Huck's narrative reporting. Does Twain treat the term as picking out Jim descriptively, as expressing contempt, or as doing both simultaneously?

Second, examine pragmatic asymmetry and appropriateness conditions. Jim never applies the term to himself; white characters monopolize it, and Jim's own speech remains formally dignified. Does this dramatize a pragmatic claim about reclamation — that only members of the targeted group can neutralize or appropriate a slur — and does the narrative's refusal to let Jim speak the word back constitute a form of linguistic silencing? Or does the framing narrative, in which Huck gradually recognizes Jim's full humanity, turn every utterance of the slur into an echoic mention that condemns rather than endorses its users?

Third, confront the fictional-context problem. If a slur's pejorative force is partly conventional and context-sensitive, does embedding it in an anti-racist novel strip it of harm, or does it remain an unreclaimed echo that retains pragmatic punch regardless of framing? Point to concrete dynamics: Huck's crisis on the raft, where he decides he would rather suffer eternal damnation than betray Jim; Tom Sawyer's reduction of Jim's freedom to a game at the novel's end; and the persistent gap between Jim's moral stature and the language used to name him. Where does the philosophical reading illuminate these moments, and where does the novel resist — for instance, through Huck's unreliable narration, which makes it hard to pin down whether a given utterance expresses the speaker's attitude, the culture's convention, or Twain's ironic distance?

Finally, ask what the text adds that philosophical argument about slurs rarely captures: the experience of hearing a derogatory term applied repeatedly to someone one comes to recognize as fully human, the way a child-narrator's linguistic habits can shift without any explicit change in semantic belief, and the possibility that a single work can simultaneously deploy and condemn a term without resolving whether that deployment is use, mention, or something in between.

A strong answer will not reduce the novel to an illustration of any single semantic theory, nor will it distort the philosophical debate to make Twain into a systematic theorist. It should say clearly whether the work vindicates one side, complicates both, and what an honest critic must concede about where the reading breaks down.
