Construct a realistic, non-scientific-fictional thought experiment—situated in an ordinary workplace or clinical setting, avoiding trolleys, lotteries, and exotic technology—in which an internalist and an externalist about practical reasons disagree about whether an agent has any reason to take a specific action, with the disagreement running in the less commonly discussed direction: the agent’s actual motivational set is entirely silent about the outcome, yet there is a robust, prudential (not moral) consideration favoring action. The internalist must deny that there is literally no reason to act; the externalist must affirm a reason that does not depend on any counterfactual revision of the agent’s desires. Present the scenario in one paragraph. Then analyze it by stating: (a) the agent’s belief and desire profile; (b) the external fact alleged to generate the reason; (c) the internalist’s argument that this is at best a normative pressure, not a reason for this agent; (d) the externalist’s reply; and (e) which structural feature—specifically, the modal stability of the agent’s indifference—does the work in forcing the disagreement. Finish by explaining how an internalist could try to dissolve the case by appealing to the distinction between reasons and derivative normative considerations, and whether that move succeeds.
