As a retired physicist who has lately turned to moral and political philosophy in a local reading group, I have become preoccupied with debates over inheritance taxation, dynastic wealth, and equality of opportunity, yet I keep stumbling over a particular argumentative pattern that I cannot name. In discussions with fellow amateurs, we notice that defenders and critics of high inheritance taxes tend to invoke two distinct normative baselines—leveling the starting line for each generation, and preventing the consolidation of hereditary economic power—without clearly specifying how they interact. The pattern I want to identify arises not in the policies themselves but in the way specific transmission mechanisms are evaluated against these two baselines interchangeably, allowing the mechanism to escape full scrutiny from either direction.

Consider two concrete configurations. First, a generation-skipping trust in which liquid capital is legally held for grandchildren, bypassing the intermediate generation entirely; second, a philanthropic dynasty in which a family endows named university chairs, museum wings, or foundation board positions that confer substantial social capital and network access without transferring spendable assets. Critics attacking from the equality-of-opportunity angle observe that the descendants still inherit family-derived advantages—prestige, connections, cultural legitimacy—so the playing field remains uneven. Defenders then shift to the anti-dynastic baseline and reply that these structures actually dissolve concentrated wealth, since the descendants never control capital and the assets are either time-locked or socially dispersed. Yet when the conversation turns to whether such structures should fall under inheritance-tax regimes, the same defenders often revert to the opportunity baseline, arguing that because no direct cash passes to the child, there is no unfair head start requiring correction. The structural feature—indirect, interstitial transmission—thus functions as a normative pivot: it is treated as mitigating dynastic concentration when opportunity is the concern, and as leaving opportunity intact when dynastic concentration is the concern.

This is not, I think, a simple case of motte-and-bailey, since the two baselines are not a defensible core paired with an indefensible extension; they are genuinely independent moral commitments that happen to share a policy slogan. Nor is it plain equivocation, because the speaker is not sliding between two meanings of a single term but rather applying two separate evaluative frameworks selectively to the same object. I am looking for an established concept—perhaps in the philosophy of distributive justice or in the logic of normative argumentation—that captures this specific failure mode: a transmission mechanism being double-accounted across independent moral ledgers so that it always falls into the gap between them. If there is no such term, I would welcome a precise coinage with clear boundary conditions—distinguishing it from strategic ambiguity, from value pluralism used defensively, and from ordinary auxiliary hypothesis—so that I can recognize when an argument about inheritance is doing this particular work rather than genuinely engaging either baseline on its own terms. I do not need references or a reading list; I need the carving itself—what exactly the phenomenon is, where its edges lie, and what it is not.
