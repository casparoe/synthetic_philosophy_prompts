In my AP History class we were debating whether the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476 CE was overdetermined or genuinely contingent, and my teacher kept repeating "hindsight is 20/20" to dismiss any claim that late-antique administrators should have anticipated the crisis. She seemed to be invoking it against both hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy—the tendency to weave chaotic events into neat causal stories only after they have occurred. I suspect, however, that the slogan is doing several different jobs at once. Could you lay out, as a clear menu, the distinct precise theses that "hindsight is 20/20" could express in historical explanation: an epistemic thesis about information asymmetry between actors and later observers; a psychological thesis about systematic bias in retrospective judgment; a narratological thesis about falsely imposing coherence on contingent events; and a normative thesis about whether we are ever justified in blaming past agents for failing to foresee outcomes? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and specify which ones actually support my teacher's dismissal of "they should have seen it coming" and which ones, if taken seriously, might cut the other way by admitting that some structural patterns really were visible in real time. I need this disambiguation for office hours, not a single interpretation.
