I've been writing about political philosophy for a while and I've kinda come around to this view: republican liberty — the Pettit-style freedom-as-non-domination frame — doesn't actually carve nature at it's joints. If nobody's actively stopping you, then saying you're 'unfree' just because some authority could arbitrarily interfere feels like treating a modal possibility as an actual prison. The classic benevolent slaveowner, the employer who never uses their firing rights, the democratic state with unused emergency powers — in all these cases you're negatively free, and calling it domination seems like re-branding anxiety.

But I want to stress-test this collapse argument properly. What would actually count against the idea that republican liberty is just negative liberty with extra branding? Don't just hit me with the slaveowner intuition — I want the hard institutional cases. For instance, imagine an AI governance layer that has legal veto power over every citizen decision but is hard-coded to never invoke it except under a transparent, verifiable emergency protocol. Is that domination without actual interference, and does it matter for freedom? Or consider a direct-democracy setup where citizens have formal discursive control over policy, but the deliberation is so structurally slow that arbitrary outcomes happen constantly — is the absence of direct interference enough?

Rank the defeaters for me: fatal, awkward, or dismissible. Which ones would a serious republican theorist concede actually hurt their view, and which would they brush off as category errors? And be honest — has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability? Like, if every case of potential interference gets redescribed as 'structural domination' at some hidden systemic level, does that save the theory or just insulate it? I want an honest map of where it's actually exposed.
