I've been working through a tension between Dennett's multiple drafts model and the determinacy of first-person reference, and I've ended up with an argument that seems to prove far more than it should. Here is the chain as I understand it.

(1) The Cartesian theatre requires a single spatiotemporal point at which all conscious content is presented to one subject.
(2) The multiple drafts model denies any such point; conscious contents are distributed across parallel processes with no central unifier.
(3) The first-person pronoun "I" is an indexical whose reference is fixed by being the speaker of the present token.
(4) But in a distributed system with no central unifier, there is no unique speaker—each draft-process that produces a mental token could in principle generate its own "I".
(5) So if draft A registers seeing red and draft B registers hearing a bell, there is no fact of the matter whether the "I" in "I see red" is identical to the "I" in "I hear a bell".
(6) Therefore, under the multiple drafts model, first-person reference is radically indeterminate: there are many "I"s and no principled way to assign them.
(7) Yet I clearly know that I am the same subject across these experiences; otherwise self-knowledge and agency collapse.
(8) So if the model makes first-person reference indeterminate, and determinacy is non-negotiable, the model must be false and the Cartesian theatre restored.

Which premise or step should I reject? I'd particularly like to keep (2) and (7) intact if possible. Please explain why the targeted step looked plausible on first reading, then price the alternatives—what each major rejection costs in terms of our account of consciousness and indexical reference.
