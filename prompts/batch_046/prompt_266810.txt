As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind, I am trying to construct a classification instrument for the debate over split-brain patients and the unity of consciousness—something more granular than the familiar "one mind or two" framing found in introductory accounts. Please build a decision tree whose branches are labeled by the specific claim that divides sibling positions, sorting views according to how they answer three concrete diagnostic judgments.

The first branch asks about phenomenal consciousness without linguistic self-reference: in the standard visual-field task, the right hemisphere guides manual behavior toward objects in the left visual field yet cannot verbally report them; does this hemisphere host a conscious stream that merely lacks an "I"-index, or is consciousness absent wherever linguistic self-reference is absent?

The second branch asks about cross-cueing: if one hemisphere acquires information about its environment solely through subtle emotional or behavioral signals from the other—without direct callosal transmission—does this restore a single integrated subject, or does it leave two subjects that are merely well-coordinated?

The third branch asks about long-term autobiographical divergence: if, over years, the two hemispheres develop stable, conflicting preferences and incompatible first-person narratives, does this eventually constitute two persons regardless of present functional coordination?

Construct the tree from the logical space first: state the combinations of answers these questions allow, then place actual positions at the leaves. Include, where they fit, the unified-subject interpretation, the two-conscious-streams view, and an "unnamed" position I shall call emergent pluralism—accepting behavioral unity but denying a single conscious author. For each leaf, provide the position's name (or "unnamed") and exactly one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Identify any logical combination that remains unoccupied—such as denying consciousness to the right hemisphere while insisting that cross-cueing creates one subject—and briefly diagnose whether its absence stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

Please do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need an instrument I can apply without consulting texts. Limit the tree to no more than eight leaves, and phrase every branch as a judgment about a concrete case rather than an abstract theoretical label, so that a respondent could navigate it without prior knowledge of the position names.
