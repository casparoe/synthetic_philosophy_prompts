I am working through a problem for my political philosophy seminar on whether "Western values" denotes a coherent normative category or operates mainly as rhetorical boundary-work. I want you to compare two conceptions that are routinely conflated in public argument: the institutional-procedural conception, which treats Western values as a portable cluster of governance norms—rule of law, separation of powers, electoral accountability, protected speech—compatible with diverse cultural substrates; and the civilizational-heritage conception, which treats them as an integrated moral ontology rooted in specific historical lineages—Greco-Roman rationalism, Judeo-Christian anthropology, Enlightenment humanism—that form an inseparable package. To avoid treating this as a simple taxonomy, apply the functional distinction—drawn in some recent conceptual analyses—between values operating as boundary markers (defining in-group membership) and values operating as normative commitments (defining legitimate state action). For each conception, determine whether it primarily serves one function, both, or whether the two are systematically conflated, and identify the precise argumentative move that allows one to slide into the other. Then design a concrete policy scenario—such as a liberal democratic state restricting religious dress in public institutions—where the two conceptions yield opposite verdicts, and show exactly which structural feature of the case forces the divergence rather than merely illustrating it. Finally, evaluate whether the phrase "Western values" retains analytical coherence once this functional ambiguity is made explicit, or whether it collapses into an essentially contested concept whose primary use is to legitimate exclusion. Work without citations, bibliographies, or reference lists; I am interested in the conceptual architecture, not in scholarly attribution.
