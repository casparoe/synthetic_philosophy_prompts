Last evening my friend and I argued for several hours about Darwin’s account of morality and religion, particularly as developed in The Descent of Man and in his private reflections on the evolutionary origins of ethical feeling, and we parted with the dispute still open. She held that Darwin’s explanation of the moral sense—tracing sympathy, social instinct, and the “greatest distinction” of human moral capacity to natural selection—reduces all normative authority to contingent biological adaptation. On her reading, if moral obligations are merely evolved mechanisms that enhanced tribal survival, then they carry no binding force independent of evolutionary history; religious ethics may be false, but evolutionary ethics is equally non-authoritative, leaving us with descriptive anthropology rather than genuine morality. I replied that Darwin’s narrative is not purely reductive: he describes a progressive widening of sympathy from kin and tribe toward strangers, other races, and eventually all sentient beings, a trajectory that supplies an internal standard of improvement. This suggests, I argued, that Darwin offers a naturalistic but genuinely normative framework—one in which moral progress consists in extending sympathy beyond its original adaptive scope, even when such extension offers no immediate survival advantage. Our disagreement then narrowed to a sharper point. Darwin occasionally writes of “higher” moral development in terms that sound almost teleological, as though human ethical consciousness aims at a state that transcends mere reproductive fitness. My friend insisted that any such language is either rhetorical ornament or an unconscious residue of theistic teleology that Darwin’s materialism cannot accommodate. I maintained that one can read these passages consistently within a materialist framework if one distinguishes between the causal origin of a capacity and the normative logic of its mature exercise—much as vision evolved for survival but can be used to study astronomy. Write an essay that examines this tension. Weigh the interpretation of Darwin as a moral anti-realist who dissolves religious ethics without providing any alternative foundation, against the interpretation of him as a naturalistic normativist whose evolutionary story supplies morality with a different but real kind of authority. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: does Darwin’s view, developed consistently, leave morality without objective binding force, or does it ground such force in the progressive, sympathy-expanding dynamics of evolved human nature? Do not end on a non-committal note; justify whichever verdict you reach.
