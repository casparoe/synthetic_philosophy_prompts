I sit on our municipality's Joint Committee on Public Health and Labor Policy, and the council has asked us to prepare a formal advisory memo recommending which regulatory framework should govern sex work within city jurisdiction. The three conventional models before us are the Nordic model, which decriminalizes selling while criminalizing purchase; the state-licensed legalization regimes characteristic of Germany and the Netherlands, which regulate commercial sex work through licensing, zoning, and health-monitoring requirements; and the full decriminalization approach adopted in New Zealand, which removes criminal penalties for both parties and integrates the work into standard occupational-health and labor-law frameworks. A local worker-advocacy coalition, however, has proposed a hybrid that does not fit neatly into any of these categories: full decriminalization coupled with mandatory cooperative-governance structures, in which providers must organize into democratically managed collectives that set working conditions, pricing, and safety protocols, supported by municipal start-up grants but not subject to individual licensing.

This proposal forces a philosophical question that comparative policy reviews usually suppress—whether individual transactional consent is sufficient for legitimate labor practice in this domain, or whether meaningful autonomy requires collective, structural control over the conditions of work. Please draft the committee's written opinion—suitable for circulation before our vote—recommending either one of the three standard frameworks or the hybrid cooperative model, with a concrete implementation timeline and conditions for review. The memo should engage seriously with the strongest objection to your recommended position, especially the argument that collective governance either imposes an unsustainable organizational burden on marginalized providers or, conversely, that decriminalization without such structures fails to address economic coercion. Frame your reasoning around the conceptual distinction between individual consent and structural autonomy, without relying on bibliographic citations or references to specific empirical studies that the committee cannot independently verify.
