I'm in a graduate seminar on classical Indian philosophy, and our discussion of Nāgārjuna's catuskoti in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā has reached a frustrating standstill. One side reads the tetralemma as a logical instrument that systematically negates all four positions—affirmation, negation, both, neither—regarding svabhāva, leaving a kind of ontological void that must be approached through non-conceptual direct cognition. The other side insists that the same fourfold negation is reflexively applied to emptiness itself, so that the point is not to establish a special ontological status for emptiness but to dissolve the very framework of reification; on this reading, emptiness is purely soteriological, a tool for ending conceptual proliferation rather than a metaphysical thesis about what exists. Both camps cite the same verses, agree that Nāgārjuna rejects inherent existence, and agree that prasaṅga reasoning is central, yet neither has moved in weeks.

My working hypothesis is that the stall arises because the two sides are treating the four corners differently: one group takes them as exhaustive logical possibilities that must be eliminated to reveal a non-propositional reality, while the other takes them as exhaustive modes of conceptual grasping that collapse once recognized as empty. Given that, is this disagreement verbal—centered on what "emptiness" means—substantive—about whether Madhyamaka permits any positive characterization of reality—or are the sides answering different questions, one ontological and one about the mechanics of de-reification, under the single heading of "interpreting the tetralemma"? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—an internal formal distinction within the tetralemma's structure, a reframing of soteriological versus ontological aims, or an argument that the question is ill-posed—would actually count as progress.
