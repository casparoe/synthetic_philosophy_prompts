I am organizing the retirement dinner for my former high school history teacher, Margaret Okonkwo, who taught world history for thirty-two years at a public school where most students had never traveled outside the state. She always began her survey course with a single question—whether human history has a shape or is just one thing after another—and she refused to answer it herself, which drove some students to distraction and made others stay after class for decades. The audience will be a mixture of her family, retired colleagues, current students, and a few local museum curators who know the recent popular debates about grand narratives well enough to catch an imprecise reference.

Over the past year I have been reading, entirely on my own and without any academic background, several of the well-known books that attempt to tell the whole human story—Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens, Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, and the more recent argument by David Graeber and David Wengrow that we have been telling ourselves a recurring myth about foragers becoming farmers becoming citizens. What strikes me is not merely that these books disagree with one another, but that each seems to carry a hidden moral grading system: it praises or pities past societies according to how closely they resemble modern liberal values, as though the people living in them had signed up to be characters in a story with a predetermined ending. I want the opening remarks—perhaps seven or eight minutes, formal enough for the occasion but spoken as if by someone who has been thinking alone with these books—to honor Margaret's career by taking this observation seriously without turning the evening into a lecture.

Specifically, I would like the speech structured in three movements: first, a genuine expression of gratitude for her refusal to simplify the past; second, an accessible reflection on why the question of historical shape matters to ordinary people, using the idea that every grand narrative quietly assigns moral credit and blame to people who never asked to be judged by it; and third, a closing that returns to her actual classroom practice—asking questions rather than closing them—without framing that practice as mere compromise or failure of conviction. The philosophical tension should do real structural work: the speaker must be able to praise a teacher who taught the long view without endorsing any particular long view, and the speech should make clear what can and cannot be said in such a tribute. Please write the remarks so that they land for the curators without losing the family, and so that the hidden moral grading of narratives is the thread that ties the parts together rather than decorative ornament.
