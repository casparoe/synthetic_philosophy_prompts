I am taking a postgraduate seminar on predictive history and reference-class forecasting, in which we treat imperial dissolution not as narrative chronology but as a classification exercise against structural analogues. Our working group has reached consistent verdicts on five cases that, I believe, jointly underdetermine the taxonomy we are actually using. First, we classified the Western Roman Empire’s fifth-century collapse as fiscal-military decoupling: the centre retained symbolic legitimacy but lost extractive capacity to maintain frontier armies, producing peripheral absorption rather than direct conquest. Second, we read the Ming transition in 1644 as an agricultural-climatic shock amplified by internal rebellion, explicitly rejecting an external-conquest analogue despite the Manchu entry. Third, we placed the British withdrawal from India in 1947 under managed devolution, since democratic pressure made direct rule costlier than strategic withdrawal while military capacity remained largely intact. Fourth, we interpreted the Soviet dissolution in 1991 as institutional sclerosis without military defeat—the armed forces stayed operational, yet the ideological-administrative framework lost coherence. Fifth, we treated the Achaemenid fall in 330 BCE as rapid military decapitation by a peripheral actor with superior tactical mobility, distinguishing it sharply from gradual decay.

We are now divided over the Ottoman Empire’s territorial contraction between 1878 and 1923. One faction wants to align it with Rome—fiscal strain, frontier overextension, and the inability to extract sufficient revenue to hold Balkan and Arab provinces—while another faction argues for the Soviet analogue, noting that the Ottoman military was modernised (not degraded) but that the administrative-ideological framework, including the millet arrangements and later constitutional experiments, failed to adapt to nationalist pressures. A minority view suggests the Ming case is closer, because internal nationalist movements functioned analogously to internal rebellion, with external powers only accelerating an already inevitable fragmentation. Given our five prior verdicts, I want you to take a definite stand: should we classify the Ottoman case with Rome or with Moscow, and what is the precise principle—fiscal-military decoupling versus institutional-ideological incoherence—that forces that choice? Furthermore, identify one further historical case, not yet discussed in our seminar, that would serve as the critical test between these two rules: that is, a case in which fiscal-military capacity and institutional coherence come apart, so that our choice of principle for the Ottomans makes a clear, falsifiable prediction. Please do not merely survey the options; commit to a position, defend it with reference to the logical structure of our existing taxonomy, and explain exactly what outcome in your proposed test case would show that your principle is mistaken.
