Consider the following line of reasoning from Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776). Smith contends that colonies burden the state with enormous defense and administrative costs while yielding little direct revenue; that the monopoly of colonial trade enriches a small merchant class rather than the nation; and that Britain should therefore grant self-government and retain only free commercial intercourse. He treats political separation as a direct fiscal relief, implying that once direct rule ends, both the cost of protection and the economic asymmetry of the relationship disappear together.

I am trying to understand whether this reasoning holds if we separate two dimensions that Smith seems to run together. To frame the problem, I distinguish "jurisdictional presence distance" — the geographic and bureaucratic span of direct imperial rule and its associated defense obligations — from "fiscal extraction distance" — the net value flow from colony to metropole maintained through tariff preference, navigation laws, and monopoly pricing. Smith appears to assume that abolishing the first necessarily collapses the second. Yet one might imagine a "non-colonial preferential trade zone" in which formal independence eliminates jurisdictional presence entirely, but asymmetric commercial treaties preserve fiscal extraction. If such an arrangement is institutionally stable, then Smith's claim that independence saves public money depends on a hidden premise: that free trade and equal commercial treatment are inseparable from political self-rule. Where exactly does this premise enter his argument, and does it survive scrutiny?
