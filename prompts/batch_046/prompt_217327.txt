I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so this might sound a bit muddled. I've been reading about Amartya Sen's point that famines — like Bengal 1943, the Irish famine, and even parts of the Great Leap Foward — weren't really about food disappearing, but about people losing thier legal and economic entitlements to food that was physically present. But then I looked at how humanitarian agencies actually declare famine today: they use satellite crop-yield data and supply-chain breakdowns as triggers, not market-price spikes that make food unaffordable. Official records for the Great Leap Foward also blamed natural disasters for the deaths, not the redistribution policies.

So here's what I'm stuck on. What philosophical commitments about causation and blame does that practice — treating famine as a supply failure or natural event rather than an entitlement collapse — actually presuppose? Does it quietly assume that only physical scarcity counts as a real disaster, which lets states off the hook for distribution failures? Could a control-based view of responsibility still justify punishing policy makers differently depending on whether warehouses were empty? And is there an inconsistency with how we treat software failures — we blame the deploy, not the server, when the code is bad but the hardware works?

If Sen's entitlement analysis is right, does keeping this supply-focused framing in aid programmes commit us to seeing famine victims as just unlucky, not wronged?
