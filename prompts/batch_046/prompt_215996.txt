I've been thinking about consciousness largely on my own, and I've arrived at a particular form of panpsychism that seems to sidestep the usual combination problem, though I worry I may have just hidden the difficulty rather than solved it. Instead of picturing macro-minds as built from micro-subjects stacked together like bricks, I imagine consciousness as a continuous universal field, and a human mind as a local region of high coherence within it—something like a standing wave rather than an aggregate. The "combination" is not summation but resonance: wherever information flow is structurally isolated, a resonant boundary forms, producing what we experience as a unified subject. So there are no little minds inside me combining into a big one; there is one field with local peaks defined by coherence thresholds and information-isolation boundaries.

What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure—not just the standard combination-problem objections that my framing might already absorb, but which specific arguments, conceptual moves, or empirical findings (for instance, about split-brain cases or formal measures of integrated information) would genuinely threaten it. Which defeaters would be fatal, and which merely awkward? In particular, if the coherence-threshold idea can always be adjusted—redescribing any observed boundary as a new resonant region—has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability, and is that insulation a problem for a metaphysical claim of this kind? Please rank the counter-evidence and arguments from decisive to negligible. I also want to know what a defender of this resonance-field panpsychism would themselves concede counts against them, versus what they would dismiss as misapplying an aggregation model that the view rejects. Finally, is there any conceivable result—conceptual or empirical—that would make a committed defender abandon the field model entirely rather than just tweaking the threshold parameters?
