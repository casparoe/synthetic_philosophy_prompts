I have been working through the Walzerian problem of dirty hands in modern coalition governance, and I think I have identified a structural resolution that the standard literature overlooks. Imagine a legislative body that assigns every morally compromising decision—for instance, authorizing a rights-infringing surveillance measure deemed necessary for security—to a rotating subcommittee, so that no single representative ever participates in more than one such vote. Because the stain is distributed across the entire membership and never accumulates on any individual agent, it seems to me that dirty hands are dissolved into a collective administrative cost rather than a personal moral burden. I want a committed response: does this rotation scheme genuinely resolve the problem, or does it rest on a false presupposition about how political responsibility aggregates? Specifically, if responsibility is non-divisible and attaches to anyone who participates in the collective act, then rotation merely camouflages the dirt rather than washing it away. Take a definite position and defend it: does institutional rotation actually clean the hands, and if not, what precisely remains unclean?
