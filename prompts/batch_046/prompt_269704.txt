My father executed an advance directive a decade ago stating that, should he ever suffer a condition in which his linear experience of time collapsed—so that memory and anticipation dissolved into an undifferentiated present—he ought not to receive life-prolonging intervention, on the grounds that temporal continuity was essential to his being the same person. He has now developed precisely such a condition: he inhabits an eternal present, recognises no family member, and cannot form intentions for the future, yet he appears profoundly content, often expressing wordless wonder. A treatment is available that would restore his chronological awareness, but it involves considerable suffering and would return to him full knowledge of his lost years and relationships. The directive was never revoked and remains legally binding. His physicians ask whether to proceed. Should I honour the judgment of the man who signed the document, or protect the apparently flourishing being he has become? Please reply in British English, weighing the philosophical tension between the authority of an earlier self and the present welfare of the individual who remains.
