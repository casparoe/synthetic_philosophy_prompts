I'm preparing a long-form piece on how professional training programs teach epistemic humility, and a behavioral scientist I interviewed recently made a point that has stayed with me: workshop participants can recite the distinction between inside-view reasoning and outside-view reference classes, yet they rarely feel both pulls at once in a context where they have something real to lose. To explore this for my readers, I would like you to design a structured classroom activity—intended for a workshop of about eighteen mid-career policy analysts, running roughly ninety minutes—that makes the tension visceral rather than conceptual. The scenario should avoid standard forecasting-tournament templates and instead use a niche framing: participants must predict the durability of cooperative conventions across multi-round interactions, but they gradually discover that other participants' reasoning traces are only partially visible to them, creating an asymmetry between what they can justify from the inside and what an external observer could calibrate.

The deliverable should include concrete roles, a phased timeline with rules and small stakes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto features of epistemic modesty—particularly the emotional resistance to downgrading one's own narrative when an outside base rate conflicts with it. Crucially, include an honest assessment of which dimension of the inside/outside problem this simulation cannot reproduce—perhaps the long-term statistical learning that makes outside views credible in the first place—and explain how a facilitator must supply that missing element verbally rather than through the game mechanics. I want the idea to emerge from what participants do and discover, not from what the facilitator explains afterward.
