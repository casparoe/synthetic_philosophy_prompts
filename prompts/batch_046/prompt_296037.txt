I run a postgraduate seminar on the philosophy of translation, with twelve participants, for a single ninety-minute session. I want them to experience — before any theoretical framing — the tension central to Le Ton beau de Marot: that preserving a poem's formal architecture (rhyme, metre, sonic pattern) is not decorative but constitutive of its meaning, and that "fidelity" therefore fragments into competing obligations rather than a single standard.

Design an in-class activity with the following structure. First, with no preliminary lecture, divide the group into three pairs. Give each pair the same invented eight-line source text in modern English (compose it for this exercise; do not reproduce a copyrighted poem). The text must secretly embed three independent formal constraints: an ABABCDCD rhyme scheme, strict iambic tetrametre, and a hidden acrostic in which the first word of each line spells a short philosophical claim about language itself. Do not reveal any of these constraints to the participants.

Assign each pair a different translation brief: Pair One must maximise literal propositional accuracy; Pair Two must preserve rhyme and metre exactly; Pair Three must preserve the hidden acrostic and the emotional register, even if rhyme and metre collapse. Give them twenty minutes to produce a version. Then have each pair read their result aloud. The remaining participants (acting as judges) must vote, without knowing the briefs, on which version is the "truest" translation, awarding points on a non-monetary scale (for instance, each judge distributes three tokens).

Only after the vote should you reveal the three hidden constraints and the philosophical claim encoded in the acrostic. Then run a structured defence: each pair explains which feature they sacrificed and why that sacrifice was necessary; the judges must identify exactly which constraint each version has broken.

Please include: the full rules and token scheme; a debrief that maps each stage of the activity onto the claim that form is a meaning-carrier rather than a neutral container; an explicit note on which aspect of this idea the activity structurally cannot reproduce and that I, as facilitator, must supply verbally; and a frank prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for example, participants fixating on one constraint and ignoring others, or judges conflating beauty with fidelity) together with how to manage that failure. Write in British English, keep the tone formal and polished, and do not ask participants to produce bibliographies, reading lists, or citations they could not verify unaided.
