I do not have an academic background, but I have spent considerable time working through a conceptual puzzle that I understand falls under the topics of dispositions, finks, and masks. I have drafted some notes for a short essay and would welcome your comments on their coherence, particularly regarding a distinction I am trying to draw. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or references to specific authors; I want to know whether the reasoning itself holds together.

My central concern is whether a disposition can be genuinely present in a thing when the very feature that would manifest it is permanently neutralized by something that is not external but constitutive. The standard examples I have encountered involve a fragile vase protected by a removable sleeve, or a wire that is not live because a hidden switch is turned off. In both cases, an external or separable condition—the sleeve, the switch—seems to mask or fink the disposition. But I want to examine a different structure. Consider a ceramic object manufactured in such a way that a shock-absorbing polymer is chemically bonded into its clay matrix during firing. The polymer is not added afterward; it is part of the material constitution of the object. If dropped, the object does not shatter, not because an external blocker intercepted the impact, but because its internal composition transforms the impact energy. Is this object fragile?

I argue that it is not, but for a reason that differs from the usual mask case. In the usual case, we say the vase is still fragile, but a mask prevents the manifestation. In my case, I want to say the fragility is not merely masked; it is structurally occluded. The disposition to shatter is not present and hidden; it is absent because the causal pathway that would constitute shattering has been replaced by a different pathway that is internal to the thing itself.

I then extend this to a psychological analog. Suppose a person is raised in an environment—say, a highly structured institutional setting—where every potential source of frustration is systematically removed before it reaches them. The removal is not an occasional intervention by a caregiver (an external mask); it is built into the architecture of their daily life. Can we say this person possesses patience, or is patience, like fragility in my ceramic example, structurally occluded?

My notes propose three claims:

First, that we should distinguish between intermittent finks (temporary, external interruptions) and constitutive occlusion (permanent, internal restructuring of the manifestation conditions).

Second, that constitutive occlusion removes the disposition rather than hiding it, because the causal basis for the disposition has been replaced.

Third, that applying this to psychological traits is more difficult than applying it to physical ones, because psychological dispositions often seem to depend on counterfactual conditions that never actually occur, whereas physical dispositions seem tied to actual structural properties.

I am especially unsure about the second claim. It seems to me that if the causal basis is replaced, then attributing the original disposition becomes a mistake—similar to saying a rubber ball is fragile because it contains glass molecules that are never arranged in a brittle lattice. But I worry I am conflating the absence of a disposition with the presence of a masked one.

Could you comment on these notes? Specifically: Is the distinction between intermittent finking and constitutive occlusion conceptually stable? Does my ceramic example actually describe a masked fragility rather than an absent one? And is the extension to psychological traits like patience a legitimate analogy, or does it confuse metaphysical structure with environmental conditioning?

Please treat this as an exploratory draft rather than a finished argument. I am interested in whether the idea is worth developing further and where its weakest points lie.
