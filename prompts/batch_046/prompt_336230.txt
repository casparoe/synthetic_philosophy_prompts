For an upper-level philosophy seminar on rationality and personal identity—14 students, 75 minutes—design an in-class negotiation game, “The Intertemporal Treaty,” that makes pure time preference feel unstable from the inside rather than describing it from the outside.

Divide the class into four groups of three or four; each group represents one continuous agent across four life-stages (ages 25, 45, 65, and 85). Give every group 20 “welfare tokens” to distribute across the four stages. The payoff rule is tangible but small: at the end of class each student receives snacks or privilege cards in proportion to the tokens assigned to the stage they play, except that tokens assigned to future stages are multiplied by two—representing compound returns—provided the group writes a single “continuity justification” explaining why that future stage counts as the same agent entitled to the reward. Each student is assigned one stage-role and receives a hidden “discount card” that specifies a pure time-preference rate (for example: “solely because of temporal distance, every future token counts as 0.6 present value for you”). The group must reach unanimous agreement on a binding allocation plan without revealing their cards.

Once proposals are on the table, reveal the cards. The groups will discover that unanimous agreement is impossible under pure discounting: the 25-stage representative can veto any plan that saves significantly for 85, while the 85-stage representative can veto present-heavy plans—yet both claim to speak for one person. After this deadlock is exposed, give the groups 15 minutes to revise using either a “neutral-weighting” rule (all stages count equally) or a “fragmentation” rule (stages are separate bargainers who can make side-payments). Each group then presents its revised treaty and states the philosophical cost of its revision rule.

In the debrief, map the initial deadlock onto the dynamic-inconsistency problem for exponential discounting; map the hidden-card revelation onto the metaphysical question of whether future selves are strangers; and map the multiplicative-reward/continuity-justification mechanic onto the distinction between instrumental and pure reasons for caring about the future. Predict the failure mode: groups will initially try to split the difference by averaging discount rates, producing unstable compromises that collapse as soon as one stage threatens defection—advise the facilitator to let them fail rather than rescue them. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the actual phenomenology of temporal distance and the felt urgency of present desire—supply that yourself through a brief reflective writing prompt at the close, asking each student to record whether their negotiation position changed once they imagined being the 85-year-old holding the veto.
