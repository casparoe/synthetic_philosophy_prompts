As a retired biologist who has only recently begun studying political philosophy—mostly through a monthly reading group with former university colleagues and occasional long dinners where empirical habits collide with normative confusion—I have been trying to understand whether economic prosperity and social order can ever, by themselves, redeem authoritarian governance. I keep returning to the development models associated with China and Singapore, not because I want to cite specific statistics, but because they present a conceptual puzzle that standard textbook treatments seem to glide over: the institutional mechanisms that generate welfare and stability may be genuinely optimal, yet they may simultaneously undermine the very conditions that would allow citizens to evaluate whether they have been fairly governed. To clarify my own thinking, I would like you to construct a precisely engineered thought experiment for me.

Please design a realistic, single-paragraph scenario involving a fictionalized medium-sized post-industrial state—conceptually adjacent to the Singaporean or Chinese cases, but entirely invented—where authoritarian rule produces demonstrably high prosperity and durable social order through an institutional feature that is genuinely welfare-optimal rather than patently cruel or inefficient. Crucially, this same feature must embed an ordinary, non-science-fictional form of epistemic closure: centralized control over long-range economic and ecological modeling that shapes policy but is systematically withheld from the public, thereby preventing citizens from accessing the counterfactual information about welfare trajectories under democratic governance that would be necessary for rational retrospective consent. The scenario should contain no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, no demon agents, and no fantasy resources; the closure should arise naturally from standard practices of classified forecasting and information management.

Then demonstrate that the case delivers three distinct, non-stipulated verdicts. First, a pure performance-legitimacy theorist—who holds that prosperity and order are sufficient for justified rule—must conclude that the government is fully legitimate. Second, a pure proceduralist—who holds that democratic participation and transparent consent are necessary conditions—must conclude that the rule is illegitimate regardless of welfare outcomes. Third, a conditional-consent hybrid theorist—who holds that performance can justify authoritarianism only when fully informed citizens would retrospectively consent—must reach a verdict of suspended or partial legitimacy, precisely because the epistemic closure destroys the informational conditions required for meaningful retrospective evaluation. After stating each verdict, explain exactly which structural feature of the institutional design drives the divergence. Next, identify what happens if that information-asymmetry feature is removed: does the hybrid view collapse back into pure performance legitimacy, or can it survive through some other route? Finally, provide an honest account of how a defender of pure performance legitimacy would attack the hybrid position—particularly by challenging whether counterfactual knowledge or retrospective consent conditions carry any normative weight in justifying rule. My aim is not to settle the debate but to isolate the variable that forces these three positions apart without letting them talk past one another.
