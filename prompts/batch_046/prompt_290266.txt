I am trying to identify a recurring structural feature in canonical thought experiments used to test whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. In case after case — Sartre's student, trolley-style conflicts, medical triage scenarios — one horn of the dilemma is narrated as an active, direct infliction of harm with full agential detail, while the other horn is narrated as allowing comparable harm through inaction, rule-adherence, or systemic allocation, often with the causal chain rendered in passive terms. This creates a narrative asymmetry that is not reducible to the familiar action/omission distinction: it biases deontological intuition toward seeing an unresolvable conflict, while allowing consequentialist reasoning to dissolve the same case into an optimization problem, regardless of the actual logical form of the theories being tested. Is there an established concept for this specific staging bias in the literature on moral intuition or experimental philosophy, and if so, how is it distinguished from general framing effects? If no standard term exists, please define the phenomenon precisely, specify its boundary conditions, and describe how one might construct a dilemma example that neutralizes this asymmetry.
