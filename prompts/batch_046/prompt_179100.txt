I'm trying to understand the philosophical architecture behind South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission. The Commission granted amnesty for politically motivated offenses contingent on full disclosure, and its design was often justified by the Ubuntu principle that personhood is constituted through others—sometimes glossed as a person being a person through other people. Meanwhile, South Africa's ordinary criminal law remains largely retributive, and its corporate law treats firms as rights-bearing entities with no corresponding relational obligations to the communities they affect. What theory of moral personhood does the TRC practice strictly presuppose? Is it necessarily the view that individual identity is constitutively relational and that wrongs are primarily injuries to social bonds rather than violations of autonomous rights, or can the same institutional design be justified on purely consequentialist or contractualist grounds? And does the coexistence of restorative amnesty for political violence with punitive sentencing for common crime, plus atomic corporate personhood, reflect a coherent unified view, or does it depend on two incompatible metaphysics of the self—one relational and context-dependent, the other individualistic and rights-based? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what a legal system consistently built on this relational conception would need to change about negligence doctrine, property rights, or corporate liability.
