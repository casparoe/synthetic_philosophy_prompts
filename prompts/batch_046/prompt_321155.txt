As a novelist constructing worlds where institutions carry hidden histories, I request a satirical municipal ordinance—approximately 500 words, deadpan and legalistic, in the dry voice of an administrative manual—from the fictional city of Chesterton-on-Avon. The code should codify Chesterton’s fence as absolute law: no boundary, procedural or physical, may be altered until its original purpose is certified by a licensed epistemic historian. The target is the Burkean argument from epistemic humility—that embedded social knowledge outstrips individual reason—not any living figure. Keep the irony sustained and internal rather than winking: include specific bureaucratic artifacts such as Form 27-B (Petition to Comprehend a Fence), penalties for “premature reform,” and a dissenting councilor’s memo noting that some fences were originally built to exclude. The humor must exaggerate a real feature of the view, so that a reader recognizes the logic being pushed to its extreme. Inject one conceptual twist: treat each fence not merely as property division but as an epistemic boundary marking what a community collectively remembers versus what it has deliberately forgotten, so that removing it risks collective amnesia as much as structural change. The reader should feel the genuine philosophical tension between preservation and progress without being told it directly. Write as if this ordinance were genuinely enforceable law.
