I am preparing material for an advanced epistemology seminar focused on Nozick's tracking conditions and the problem of epistemic closure, and I need a taxonomy presented entirely in continuous prose—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, written as one might write a letter or an email. The taxonomy should classify positions not by the names philosophers give them but by the concrete commitments that separate them in logical space, beginning with whether one accepts that knowledge requires sensitivity to falsity in nearby worlds; then branching on whether that sensitivity survives known deductive entailment; then on whether it survives only for direct perceptual beliefs or also for beliefs formed through statistical inference and testimony; and finally on whether there is any special failure mode for abductive extensions of tracking beliefs. At each branch the dividing question should be phrased as something a student could answer without already knowing which named position it corresponds to, using concrete cases—for instance, whether knowing one is not a brain in a vat permits knowing one has hands, or whether tracking that one's lottery ticket will likely lose allows one to know one will not win, or whether knowing a statistical base rate allows one to know a particular prediction derived from it. One leaf must be Nozick's own anti-closure stance; another the Dretske-style view that closure fails whenever the entailment involves worlds where the tracking condition for the derived belief is violated; another a modified closure-accepting view that restricts tracking to base beliefs but allows closure for their deductive consequences; and one leaf should be reserved for an unnamed combination I want included: the view that closure holds for strict deductive entailments but systematically fails for abductive or inductive extensions of a tracking belief, which I will call selective abductive closure failure. After mapping the named and unnamed positions, note any pair of leaves your taxonomy genuinely cannot distinguish—such as if two different philosophical motivations converge on the same pattern of answers across all cases—and diagnose any region of the logical space that remains unoccupied, saying briefly whether the gap looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
