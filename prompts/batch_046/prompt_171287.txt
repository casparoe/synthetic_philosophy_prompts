I'm designing a single fifty-minute seminar for philosophy undergraduates on the parallel between Darwinian natural selection and Turing computation as instances of competence without comprehension. I want students to see that both cases invert the ordinary inference from intelligent outcome to intelligent cause, yet I want to avoid treating one as a mere metaphor for the other. Plan the teaching sequence: which conceptual dependency—say, the distinction between functional success and representational understanding—must be secured before the parallel can be drawn; identify the one concrete working example that can carry both the evolutionary and computational dimensions without reducing either to the other; list the misconceptions students are likely to form at each stage—particularly that evolutionary design implies foresight, that algorithmic success implies hidden semantic grasp, and that recognizing both cases undermines all attributions of understanding—and specify exactly where in the arc each should be intercepted; propose one concise check-for-understanding question that distinguishes grasping the inversion itself from merely repeating the two cases; and state clearly what boundary issue—perhaps the relation between functional equivalence and phenomenal consciousness—should be named but deliberately left unresolved at this level.
