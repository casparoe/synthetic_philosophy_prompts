While drafting the third section of my novel—a narrative that moves between the archaeological strata of Hisarlik and the monastic chronicles of Glastonbury—I have become preoccupied with an institutional puzzle that seems to outstrip mere academic disagreement. I was discussing this with a friend who teaches comparative mythology, and she pointed out that heritage boards and state curriculum committees do not treat the Trojan War, the Arthurian cycle, and the Exodus as variations on a single evidentiary problem; instead, they apply what looks like an unstated taxonomy of narrative legitimacy, one that assigns each story to a different epistemic category despite comparable gaps in the material record.

Consider the practice in concrete terms. Archaeological funding bodies routinely authorize excavations at Hisarlik explicitly to test correlations between material layers and the Homeric narrative, and museums label Trojan artifacts with cautious but open-ended phrasing suggesting potential correspondence to the legendary siege. By contrast, excavations at Tintagel or Glastonbury are funded only when framed as studies of medieval ecclesiastical culture; Arthurian references are relegated to display panels marked as legendary and without historical core, regardless of comparable ambiguity in the archaeological record. Meanwhile, in certain national education systems, the Exodus is taught within history textbooks either as a documented migration or as a foundational tradition exempt from archaeological verification, with museum labels shifting from biblical narrative unsupported by material evidence to the journey of the Israelites depending not on new finds but on the commissioning authority’s relationship to religious communities. The same evidentiary profile—textual transmission across centuries, absence of contemporary extrabiblical corroboration, selective material traces—yields three different institutional verdicts: open inquiry, categorical exclusion, and protected charter status.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of historical knowledge, collective identity, and the function of origin narratives must this triage presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that allows an institution to treat legend and history not as points on a single evidentiary continuum but as categories assigned by what we might call communal charter function—the role a narrative plays in constituting a present group’s self-understanding—rather than by uniform standards of material corroboration. Is the practice strictly committed to the view that history is defined by secular, intersubjective verification, and that narratives serving religious or national charter purposes therefore occupy a separate, non-historical domain? Or does it presuppose something more subtle: that negative archaeological evidence counts differently against secular legends than against sacred ones, and that a legend becomes history only when its community of belief has dissolved into purely academic interest? Distinguish what this institutional pattern strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say whether its parts are philosophically consistent, and tell me what would have to change—both in museum labeling and in textbook commissioning—if we replaced its implicit framework with a single, uniform evidential standard applied regardless of a narrative’s present communal function.
