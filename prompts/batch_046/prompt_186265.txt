As a novelist preparing a long narrative about how readers form moral character through sustained engagement with biographical lives—not through explicit precept, but through the peculiar intimacy of watching another consciousness navigate failure, ambition, and restraint over time—I find myself caught in a conceptual tension I will call the credibility-exemplarity problem. The more psychologically complex and morally ambiguous a rendered life becomes, the less it can function as a stable model for imitation; yet the more it is shaped into an exemplary arc, the more it collapses into hagiography and loses the very texture that makes moral learning feel earned rather than imposed. This tension runs through the tradition from Plutarch’s parallel comparisons through medieval hagiography to modern confessional memoir, but I am unsure which open questions within this tradition genuinely matter for understanding biography as moral pedagogy, and which are merely antiquarian.

Given that my concern is practical rather than scholarly—what a reader can actually take away from a life rendered in prose—I want you to take a definite, committed position. Among the unresolved conceptual problems about biography as a vehicle for moral learning—whether Plutarch’s method of parallel comparison resolves the credibility-exemplarity problem or merely displaces it onto the reader’s judgment; whether hagiography’s deliberate suppression of vice destroys the conditions for moral learning or instead creates a necessary aspirational distance; whether modern demands for psychological transparency have made the exemplary life narratively impossible without flattening it; and whether a reader situated outside the subject’s historical and moral world can learn virtue from such lives without reducing them to consumable spectacle—which two or three deserve the most sustained intellectual effort now, and in what precise order?

Please defend your ranking with explicit criteria: which questions are upstream of others, which carry the greatest consequence for how biography actually operates as moral instruction rather than as entertainment or edification, and which lines of inquiry you would deprioritize because resolving them would not substantially alter our understanding of how lives teach. I am writing in American English, and I need a reasoned agenda—not a survey of positions, but a clear choice about where the work should go and why.
