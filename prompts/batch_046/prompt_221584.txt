Consider the institutional practice, common across very different regimes, of commemorating a catastrophic military defeat or collective annihilation as a foundational national myth: the Confederate Lost Cause in American memorial culture, the fall of Masada in Israeli state ritual, the 1389 Battle of Kosovo in Serbian Vidovdan commemorations, and the 1940 Dunkirk evacuation in British popular memory. In each case the event was a strategic failure or total defeat, yet state-sponsored monuments, school curricula, and public rituals frame it not as cautionary failure but as proof of the nation’s essential character—emphasizing voluntary choice over survival, moral purity over success, and present continuity with a community that in some cases was physically destroyed. What philosophical commitments about collective identity, the moral weight of outcomes, and the relation between a people and its history does this practice presuppose? Must it assume that national existence is an intrinsic good independent of flourishing, or can it be reconciled with a purely instrumental account of the state? And is it philosophically consistent with these same societies’ parallel commitment, in welfare and policy domains, to judging institutions by their consequences rather than by the purity of the intentions they memorialize?
