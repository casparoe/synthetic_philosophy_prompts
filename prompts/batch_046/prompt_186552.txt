I hold that in Adam Smith's moral philosophy, the impartial spectator is not an independent, pre-existing arbiter that corrects sympathy from outside, but is constituted through the iterative failure and recalibration of sympathetic engagement—sympathy does not refer to an external standard but builds its internal corrector from within its own accumulated misalignments. What would genuinely count against this reading? I want more than the standard reply that Smith clearly separates spectator and agent; I want to know which moves in his treatment of propriety, approval, and resentment, which structural parallels or disanalogies with his account of exchange, and which internal tensions in the Theory of Moral Sentiments undermine the claim that the spectator emerges from within sympathy's dynamics rather than policing it from without. Rank the defeaters by decisiveness, distinguish evidence that a defender could absorb by qualification from evidence that would require abandoning the core claim, and assess whether the position risks being insulated by redefining sympathy so broadly that no failure can falsify it—if so, whether that insulation is a vice for an interpretive thesis.
