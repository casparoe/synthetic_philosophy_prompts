Since the 1970s, the debate between naturalists—who treat disease as deviation from species-typical biological function, exemplified by biostatistical theories that ground pathology in statistical abnormality relative to a reference class—and normativists—who treat disease as a state that is harmful, undesirable, or welfare-reducing for the bearer, exemplified by welfare-based accounts—has generated an extensive literature without convergence. Naturalists charge that normativism collapses the distinction between being diseased and being unhappy or socially disvalued; normativists reply that naturalism smuggles values into the choice of reference class, the delineation of species-typical design, and the handling of asymptomatic conditions or evolutionary mismatches like infertility or senescence. Despite repeated exchanges over function, value-ladenness, and clinical judgment, neither framework has displaced the other.

My working hypothesis is that part of the stall is verbal—turning on whether “function” means evolutionary design-function or present causal contribution—while part is substantive, with naturalists addressing a biological-classificatory question and normativists addressing a clinical-justificatory one. But I also suspect both sides share an unexamined assumption that health is a univocal, organism-level property, which prevents them from accommodating cases in which a subsystem—say, a synthetic or chimeric microbiome—operates dysfunctionally by its own local norms yet contributes positively to host welfare, or in which epigenetic drift produces statistically abnormal but adaptively neutral phenotypes.
