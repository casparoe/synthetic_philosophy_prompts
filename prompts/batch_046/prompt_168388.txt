I am a retired field ecologist who, since leaving active research, has taken up the study of epistemology and philosophy of science. For the past year I have served as the designated critical reader for a monthly interdisciplinary salon attended by scientists and philosophers; after each session I must submit a brief, signed assessment—rating the speaker's central thesis as well-supported, provisionally supported, or unsupported—within forty-five minutes of the talk's conclusion. I have no access to literature databases during that window, and I am not expected to verify every empirical citation, but my assessment must be justified with explicit reasoning and must stand as a public record. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the inside-view versus outside-view distinction and in epistemic modesty, that I can apply consistently across talks regardless of discipline. The procedure must preserve two distinctions that I regard as essential and that are often conflated: first, between structural uncertainty (doubt about the integrity of my own reasoning chain) and stochastic uncertainty (doubt arising from the inherent variability or noise of the domain itself); second, between high and low reference-class liquidity, by which I mean the ease with which I can place the present claim into a well-defined class of past claims with known base rates.

The deliverable should be a step-by-step checklist or flowchart expressed in prose, specifying the exact order of operations, the questions I must answer at each stage, and every point at which judgment is unavoidable and must be flagged explicitly rather than concealed behind pseudo-quantification. It must include explicit instructions for cases in which I lack a numerical base rate. It should also state clearly what the procedure cannot do—where it will misfire, remain silent, or produce a result that should be overridden. Finally, apply the procedure to three hypothetical salon presentations: (1) a neuroscientist arguing that a specific eight-week mindfulness protocol slows cortical thinning in adults over sixty, based on a single forty-subject pilot study with no replication; (2) a philosopher of physics claiming that current quantum-gravity research programs will converge on a single framework within two decades because the relevant mathematical structures are unusually constrained; and (3) a case in which the inside-view reasoning is technically sound, the reference class is favorable, and the conclusion is one I am personally inclined to reject—specifically, a rigorously structured argument that immediate economic-development metrics should take precedence over long-term biodiversity conservation in certain developing regions. For the third case, the procedure should yield a rating of well-supported or provisionally supported, and you should identify precisely where my personal discomfort enters the audit and how the procedure requires me to treat it. The procedure must be executable in under forty-five minutes without bibliographic lookup and should not depend on my familiarity with any particular named theory or author.
