I have come to hold that Kepler’s astrological practice is best understood not as a superstitious residue appended to an otherwise modern astronomy, but as an integrated “geometric semantics”: a constrained system in which planetary configurations function analogously to phonemes in a celestial language of influence, continuous with his harmonic cosmology and with the proportional reasoning in his optics, and in which his well-known criticisms of traditional natal astrology represent internal refinements—restricting influence-claims to measurable angular relations—rather than an abandonment of astrological reasoning altogether. Tell me what would count against this integrated reading, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I want the specific textual practices, predictive failures he himself acknowledged, unresolved tensions with his own celestial mechanics, and broader philosophical objections—say, about causal closure or about whether a “language” of angles can carry semantic content without conventional interpretation—that should move a defender. Which defeaters would such a defender concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as anachronistic or as category errors? Also assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every apparent counter-instance can be redescribed as a further “refinement”—and whether that insulation is a defect for a historical-interpretive thesis of this kind.
