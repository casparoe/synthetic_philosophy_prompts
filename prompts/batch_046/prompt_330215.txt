I am in the early stages of a novel that moves between Washington and Berlin during the 1990s, and I find myself stuck on a conceptual problem that determines how two of my central characters can misunderstand each other without either being foolish. One character, an American legislative aide, describes a proposed healthcare mandate as a liberal reform meant to correct structural economic coercion; the other, a German constitutional lawyer, objects that the same measure is illiberal because it expands discretionary state power over private economic ordering. I want to understand whether this disagreement is merely semantic or whether there are two genuinely distinct normative architectures hiding behind the same word.

Please compare the American concept of liberalism—particularly as it crystallized in the post-New Deal and Progressive traditions, where freedom is often understood as requiring state intervention to neutralize private domination and secure equal opportunity—with the European concept, especially the classical and ordoliberal traditions, where liberalism centers on constitutional limits to arbitrary power, the rule of law, and the protection of a competitive economic order from both state and private encroachment. Do not treat this as a simple dictionary problem; I want you to examine whether the two traditions share an underlying commitment, for instance to individual autonomy, to equality of status, or to a particular theory of coercion, or whether they are fundamentally incommensurable, using the same term as a homophone.

Specifically, map how each tradition identifies the primary threat to human freedom—whether it lies in unregulated economic structures or in the concentration of political authority—and whether those diagnoses can be translated into one another's vocabulary without distortion. If I translate the American concern about market coercion into European liberal terms, does it survive as a legitimate liberal grievance, or does it get recast as a demand for positive state power that the European framework treats as the problem itself? Conversely, if I restate the European insistence on constitutional and economic order in American progressive terms, does it read as a defense of privilege, or does it preserve a coherent liberal insight that the American tradition has lost? I am less interested in a historical genealogy with names and dates than in a rigorous conceptual comparison: where the two meanings overlap, where they diverge irreconcilably, and whether there is any residual core that justifies keeping one word for both.
