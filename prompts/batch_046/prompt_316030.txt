I'm a PhD student in comparative religion and philosophy, and I'm writing a chapter on institutional practices in Theravada Buddhism. I was talking with my friends over coffee about how temples in Thailand and Sri Lanka run these merit-making ceremonies that feel weirdly transactional. Basically, a layperson comes in, donates money or goods to the monks, and then there's a ritual where the monk chants in Pali and the donor explicitly dedicates the merit to a dead relative—sometimes they write both names in a ledger, and I've even seen temples issue an informal receipt that lists the donor, the amount, and the intended reciever. The language used is literally about transferring or sharing the good karma generated by the gift.

My advisor says this is all just symoblic, but I think the institutional machinery—named ledgers, specific chanting formulas, receipts—suggests something stronger is going on philosophically. At the same time, classical Buddhist ethics is often read as consequentialist in orientation, focused on reducing suffering and cultivating wholesome mental states, and the doctrine of karma is usually taken to be strictly individual. So there's a tension.

What philosophical commitments about moral value, causation, and agency does this practice actually presuppose? Does it require treating merit as a quantifiable, fungible resource that can be moved between persons like a bank transfer? Or can a non-consequentialist, individual-karma framework accommodate the ritual without contradiction? And if we replaced the implicit theory with one that takes individual mental cultivation and non-transferability seriously, which parts of the temple practice—ledgers, named recipients, the ritual language—would become incoherent or have to be abandoned?
