I'm working up a problem set for my free will seminar and I'm tired of students just parroting Frankfurt back at me, so I invented this tracking exercise with a fictional colleague, Dr. Voss, who judges whether agents have free will that's actually worth wanting — not just moral responsibility, but the kind of control you'd want engineered into a society if you could redesign human agency from scratch.

Her verdicts so far: in the classic hierarchical harmony case she says yes; in the Mele-style zygote manipulation case she says no; in Fischer's intermediate manipulation scenario where the mechanism is present but doesn't actually cause the act she says yes; in Pereboom's tax-evasion manipulation case she says no; and in the weird case where someone's second-order volitions are randomly generated but then stabilize for decades she says yes, but grudgingly.

Now here's the new case I'm throwing at her: an agent whose practical reasoning is mediated by an external decision-support system — call it an algorithim — that she can in principle disable, but only at the cost of severe cognitive dissonance. She never disables it, her higher-order preferences align with its recommendations, and she knows it's there but treats it like a trusted advisor rather than a controller. The system was designed by an anonymous committee, updates based on her feedback loops, and could be swapped for a different architecture if she insisted. Does Voss grant her free will worth wanting? I want you to map out the competing rules she might be running — sourcehood conditions, hierarchical mesh, reason-responsiveness, or maybe this hybrid I'm tentatively calling negotiable transparency — and tell me which one best explains her pattern. Then predict her verdict on the algorithim case, and identify the single further case that would actually split the remaining candidates apart. Don't just list philosophers like you're checking boxes; actually work through the logic, and if her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so and explain what principle each option commits her to.
