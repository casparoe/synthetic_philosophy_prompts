I am writing a term paper for a philosophy of technology course that examines competing definitions of the AI alignment problem. Our readings have emphasized the narrow view—alignment as intent alignment—versus the broader value-alignment view that permits overriding a user's expressed preferences when those preferences appear adaptive or misinformed. I want to push this distinction into a specific, somewhat under-discussed corner: autonomous systems that must infer user goals not from explicit commands but from extended behavioral histories subject to framing effects and adaptive preference formation.

Specifically, what is your actual credence—please give a precise number between 0 and 1—that in such cases there remains an operationally coherent, non-arbitrary distinction between aligning with the user's intent and aligning with the user's values? In other words, is there a purely procedural way to identify "what the user intends" that does not already depend on normative judgments about which revealed preferences are authentic? Decompose your answer as follows: (i) assign a rough probability to the claim that revealed-preference theory can reconstruct intent without normative filtering of adaptive or framing-distorted choices; (ii) assign a probability to the claim that any procedural account of intent-formation in autonomous systems necessarily presupposes a value-laden theory of rational agency; (iii) assign a probability to the claim that empirical studies of user acceptance could, in principle, adjudicate whether reconstructed goals count as genuinely intended rather than externally imposed; and include any other factor you think belongs. Combine them briefly to reach your overall figure, explain the combination rule, and tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to. Finally, indicate what specific conceptual argument or empirical result would most substantially move your credence in either direction.
