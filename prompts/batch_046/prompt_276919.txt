I've been thinking about why the prospect of human extinction seems to carry a weight that can't be captured just by adding up all the individual deaths it would cause. I keep noticing a particular structure in the long-term commitments people make. A researcher spends thirty years on a theoretical problem she knows she will never see solved, not because she expects personal fame, but because she acts as if there is an implicit agreement with future minds who will finish the work. A craftsperson restores an old building not for present use alone, but with the quiet assumption that strangers generations later will inhabit it. In both cases, the present effort seems to borrow part of its point from a future that is supposed to receive it. If humanity were to disappear entirely, those future people would never exist to be harmed, yet the present commitments would still feel hollowed out—not merely disappointed, but retrospectively misdirected, as if the contract they presumed with the future was void from the start.

Is there an established philosophical concept for this specific pattern: a present action that derives its meaning from an assumed future continuation, such that total extinction undermines the point of the action without harming anyone who performs it? I'm not asking about consequentialism, legacy, or the non-identity problem; I'm asking about the structure of an implicit intergenerational contract and its retrospective collapse. If there is a name, I want to know precisely what it includes and excludes—for instance, whether it applies only to collective projects or also to private ones, and whether it requires the actor to consciously imagine the future. If there is no established term, can you coin one with a clear definition and boundary cases? In either case, please don't just give me a label; show me the edges of the phenomenon and what distinguishes it from ordinary futility or from the idea that meaning requires an eternal audience.
