Here's an argument I cooked up after skimming Moore's Principa Ethica and now I'm second-guessing it. (1) Moore says friendship and beauty are both intrinsic goods, and that their value involves what he calls organic unity — the whole is worth more than the sum of parts. (2) To test if something is intrinsically good, he asks us to imagine it existing in absolute isolation, with nothing else in the universe. (3) I can imagine a beautiful sunset in isolation: just the colors and shapes relating to each other, no observers, no world around it. (4) But friendship is essentially reltaional — it needs at least two people and the care between them. So if I imagine friendship in total isolation, I'm either imagining it without the other person (in which case there's no friendship) or I'm isolating two people together (in which case I'm not testing one good, I'm testing a whole miniature society). (5) So either friendship fails the isolation test, or it passes only by treating the two friends as internal parts of one object, which makes the organic unity just the sum of each person's internal states plus the relation. (6) But Moore insists friendship has extra, irreducible value and that it clearly survives the isolation test — he calls it one of the greatest goods. (7) So there's a contradiction. (8) The only ways out seem to be: deny friendship is intrinsic (which contradicts Moore), or deny the isolation test applies to relational goods. (9) But if relational goods escape the test, then justice, loyalty, and even knowledge sneak in too, and Moore's taxonomy of intrinsic goods collapses to solo mental states and pretty objects. Where does this actually break? I'd rather keep (1) and (2), but I feel like I'm treating the other friend as outside the friendship when maybe they're inside it. Which premise should I drop, and what's the cost?
