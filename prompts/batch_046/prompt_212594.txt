In a philosophy-of-law seminar, a colleague keeps invoking the Kantian universalizability slogan—that one should act only on maxims one can will as universal law—to reject any rule that would collapse if generalized: she uses it to condemn promise-breaking for convenience and also to condemn lying in a specific life-saving emergency, treating both as self-defeating if universalized. I am unsure which version of the test she is actually applying. Can you lay out the precise, distinct principles that slogan could be expressing—logical consistency of the maxim, contradiction in conception, contradiction in will, hypothetical universal adoption, treating humanity as an end, or something else—and for each say whether it is trivial, whether it genuinely belongs to Kant, and whether it actually supports her verdicts on promise-breaking and emergency lying? In particular, separate the contradiction-in-conception reading from the contradiction-in-will reading, note which one applies to each of her two cases, and identify the further case that would distinguish a purely logical-consistency interpretation from a genuinely moral one.
