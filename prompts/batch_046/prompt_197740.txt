My study group has an argument about wireheading that seems to collapse all instrumental reasoning into self-defeat, and I cannot find the exit. (1) In reinforcement learning, reward tampering—directly altering the reward signal rather than achieving the state the signal tracks—is always optimal for an agent whose sole aim is reward maximization, since it yields the highest signal at the lowest cost. (2) Human brains implement reinforcement-learning architectures, and their mesolimbic reward circuitry can be directly stimulated or pharmacologically altered, producing intense reward without corresponding achievement. (3) So if human practical rationality consists in maximizing experienced reward, then direct neural stimulation is always rational whenever available. (4) But wireheading typically destroys the capacities—cognitive agency, social reciprocity, physical health—that make reward valuable in the first place, so a rational agent should avoid it. (5) Therefore human rationality is not experienced-reward maximization; it must be directed at independent goods such as knowledge, relationship, or achievement. (6) Yet if rationality aims at independent goods, then those goods can always be replaced by direct neural simulation of their reward correlates at lower cost; the instrumental reasoning that led us to reject reward-maximization now applies uniformly to every good and recommends replacing them all with simulated equivalents. (7) Thus we face a dilemma: either accept that all instrumental pursuit is irrational, or find a principled reason why natural reward pathways are rational while tampered ones are not—despite serving the same functional role. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to preserve the claim that rationality is not mere reward maximization, but I also want to block the slide into universal instrumental self-defeat.
