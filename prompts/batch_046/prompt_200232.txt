I'm closing a graduate seminar on value pluralism and practical reason with a session on hard choices, and I want to open with a poem that captures a distinction we kept returning to: the difference between values that are genuinely incomparable (no comparative relation holds) and values that are "on a par"—a fourth comparative relation that is not equality, not superiority, and not bare incomparability, but a kind of rough equality that still permits rational choice without dissolving into measurement. Too often this collapses into the idea that we simply "weigh" competing goods on a hidden scale. I'd like a sestina—six six-line stanzas with end-words rotating in the standard permutation pattern, followed by a three-line envoi that includes all six end-words—to dramatize the refusal of such weighing.

The six end-words must be: loyalty, justice, parity, regret, scale, world. The speaker should be someone who has left a long family tradition—say, a craft or communal obligation—to pursue institutional reform, and the poem should occupy the moment after the choice is made, not before. Structurally, "scale" should fall at line endings or in rhyme positions that frustrate quantification; "parity" should never be rendered as simple equality but as a relation that survives comparison intact; "regret" should recur not as remorse but as a rational residue that the rejected option still exerts normative pull; and "loyalty" and "justice" should rotate through positions that deny either a stable dominance. The envoi should place all six words in close proximity without ranking them, so the decision reads as a reconfiguration rather than a victory. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher familiar with Ruth Chang's account of hard choices recognizes the distinction between parity and equality, and it should leave the tension fully open—no moral that the reform was "worth it" and no shrug that the tradition was merely sentimental.
