In Nyāya, anupalabdhi (absence-cognition or non-apprehension) functions as a means of knowing the non-existence of an object. Analyze the following three scenarios, specifying in each whether anupalabdhi operates, whether the resulting judgment is valid, and whether any other pramāṇa is involved.

(i) A sighted observer stands in a well-lit, unobstructed room and judges, “There is no elephant here.”

(ii) The same observer, now positioned behind a one-way mirror that renders large animals optically invisible from her side, makes the identical judgment about the identical room, in which an elephant is in fact present on the far side.

(iii) A physicist examines a cloud-chamber image and, observing no tracks of a specific particle type, concludes that no particles of that type passed through the chamber during the observation window.

In your analysis, explain precisely what yogyānupalabdhi requires—distinguishing the fitness of conditions (yogyatā) from mere subjective capacity—and clarify why absence-cognition is not reducible to “the absence of perception.” Then address whether instrumental mediation transforms an otherwise direct absence-cognition into anumāna.

Student answer:
Anupalabdhi is recognized in Nyāya either as an independent fifth pramāṇa alongside pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, and śabda, or as a specialized mode of perceptual cognition. Its object is always absence (abhāva), and it operates when the conditions for perceiving an object are satisfied but the object is not present.

In scenario (i), the observer’s eyes function, the light is adequate, and the space is open, so yogyatā is satisfied. Because no elephant is perceived despite these fitting conditions, she directly cognizes absence through anupalabdhi, and her judgment is true.

In scenario (ii), the observer’s subjective perceptual conditions are identical—she has functioning vision and illumination—so yogyatā is still satisfied from her standpoint. The elephant is present but concealed by the mirror, which introduces an unknown obstructive condition. Nyāya would classify her cognition as an instance of anupalabdhi that happens to be erroneous: the method was correctly applied, but an hidden obstacle made the conditions appear fitting when they were not fully satisfied for that locus. Thus the judgment is false, yet the pramāṇa-type remains anupalabdhi rather than inference.

In scenario (iii), the physicist relies on an instrument (the cloud chamber) and on the interpretation of track absence. Because there is no direct sensory contact with the particle, and because instruments extend but do not replace the sensory apparatus, this case crosses into anumāna. The scientist infers absence from the absence of instrumental effects. Furthermore, Nyāya restricts direct anupalabdhi to macroscopic objects that are in principle perceptible to unaided senses; for submicroscopic entities, absence can never be directly cognized and must always be reached through inference or testimony.
