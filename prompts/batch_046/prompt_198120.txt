A popular neuroscience article I came across summarizes Frank Jackson's knowledge argument about Mary, the color scientist, in the following way. Mary spends her entire life in a black-and-white room and masters every physical and neuroscientific fact about color vision. Upon leaving and seeing a red rose, she learns what it is like to see red. The article says Jackson takes this to show that phenomenal properties—qualia—are real, non-physical features of experience that cause no physical effects but are immediately known through introspection. It then claims that contemporary physicalists generally reply by arguing Mary never actually learns anything genuinely new: her brain already contained all the relevant information, and the feeling of novelty is merely her recognizing data she already possessed, now tagged with a different representational format—propositional knowledge versus an iconic-phenomenal format. The article concludes that because physicalists must concede this format gap, Jackson's argument succeeds in proving that consciousness involves something beyond physics, even if that extra element is causally powerless.

This framing strikes me as mostly right but suspicious in a few places—particularly in how it characterizes the physicalist response and whether Jackson's conclusion is as strong as the article suggests.
