Next month I am leading a single ninety-minute seminar for a cross-disciplinary reading group—graduate students in literature and the history of science with little formal training in political theory—on the thesis that modern nations are recent constructions rather than ancient, natural communities, a line of inquiry linked to the study of invented traditions and imagined communities. Rather than surveying the literature, I intend to build the session around one precisely drawn carrying case: a state-sponsored harvest festival reconstructed in a post-colonial republic during the 1970s, publicly presented as the restoration of an ancient agrarian rite, but actually assembled from fragmented ethnographic records, newly composed civic choreography, and a standardized calendar introduced by the ministry of culture. My purpose is not to narrate the episode for its own sake, but to use it to illuminate the structural interplay between ritual repetition and administrative reach—census categories, cartographic boundaries, and synchronized public time—so that the group can see why these dimensions depend on each other rather than operating in isolation.

I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself. Please specify the order in which the conceptual components must be introduced, indicating precisely which idea must be in place before the next can land, especially the dependency between the technologies that render a population legible to itself and the invented traditions that lend that legibility emotional weight. Describe the properties the carrying example must possess—what historical textures it should include and which it should deliberately omit—so that the distinction between sincere collective feeling and actual institutional discontinuity remains visible, rather than collapsing into either cynical propaganda or romantic authenticity. Anticipate the misconceptions this audience will likely bring, such as the belief that widespread feeling of national belonging proves the nation is old, or that reconstructed rituals are necessarily elite manipulation, and identify the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused. Propose a concrete check—perhaps a brief written reflection or a targeted discussion prompt—that would reveal by the end whether the participants have grasped the structural argument about construction and continuity, rather than merely retaining the festival anecdote. Finally, state clearly what should be left out at this level—whether particular typologies of nationalism, comparative regional cases, or specific historiographical debates—and explain how these omissions protect the conceptual dependency structure you have outlined.
