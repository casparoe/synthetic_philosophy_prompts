In a seminar on moral motivation, a colleague repeatedly invoked the line 'moral worth requires acting for the right reasons' to dismiss a comparison between two physicians: one administers a life-saving drug solely to maximize billing revenue, yet applies the correct dosage with perfect technical competence and full knowledge of the patient's autonomy; the other acts from genuine concern for the patient but with noticeably less skill. She concluded that the first physician's action lacks moral worth entirely, regardless of outcome, and that this judgment needs no further defense. I think the slogan is doing hidden work, but I cannot say what. Can you separate the precise theses that sentence might be expressing, arranged as a menu? For each reading—from a trivial observation that motives sometimes color our assessments, through moderate claims about praiseworthiness, to strong Kantian or neo-Aristotelian necessary-and-sufficient conditions—state whether it is platitudinous, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which of these readings actually support her dismissal of the revenue-driven physician, which would instead require comparing him unfavorably to the less-skilled but well-motivated colleague, and which leave the comparison silent. In particular, be explicit about whether 'the right reasons' must be conscious, whether they must be the sole causal source, and whether technical competence can ever substitute for them; and identify the point at which the slogan stops being an argument and becomes an assertion.
