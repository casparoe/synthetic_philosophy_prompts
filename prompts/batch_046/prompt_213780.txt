I am revising a proposal for a university general-education history requirement, and I have become puzzled by an institutional pattern that seems to run deeper than mere tradition. In the curriculum-design process I have observed, mandatory survey courses are arranged as chronological arcs that begin with ancient political forms and are required to terminate in the modern liberal democratic state and integrated global markets; student assessments reward the identification of developmental steps linking past institutions directly to present ones, while essays that treat major transitions—such as the seventeenth-century English settlement—as contingent local equilibria among several viable alternatives, rather than as necessary preconditions for modern representation, are marked down for lacking historical significance. Proposals for required courses organized around counterfactual nodes or collapsed alternatives are rejected by committees on the explicit ground that they teach speculation rather than the forces that actually shaped current institutions. I want to understand the implicit philosophical theory behind this practice. Does the grading and funding structure necessarily presuppose a Whiggish ontology in which past events possess meaning only in proportion to their causal contribution to the present? Or can a historian who rejects progress narratives defend the same pedagogical architecture on purely pragmatic grounds—say, that tractable explanation requires selecting the actual causal chain? Furthermore, if the practice does embed Whiggish commitments, what precisely would have to change in examination rubrics, archive-funding criteria, and heritage-designation standards if we replaced that view with one that treats the present constitutional order as one contingent outcome among many equally real past possibilities? Please take a definite, committed stance: reconstruct the implicit theory of historical value and knowledge, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and argue whether the institutional pattern could survive without Whiggish assumptions. Deliver your answer as exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding remarks.
