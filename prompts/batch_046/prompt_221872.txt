I recently heard a summary of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and its relationship to the commensurability of pleasure and pain presented more or less as follows. The speaker said: lexical threshold negative utilitarianism begins from the claim that pleasure and suffering are incommensurable values that cannot be placed on a single quantitative scale. Because suffering carries lexical priority, any experience of suffering that exceeds a specified intensity or duration threshold automatically outweighs any amount of pleasure, however large. Below that cutoff—where discomfort is mild or brief—the lexical rule does not apply, so pleasure and mild suffering can be compared and traded off in roughly the ordinary utilitarian fashion. Once the threshold is crossed, however, pleasure becomes lexically irrelevant, and no aggregation of goods can justify allowing the suffering to continue. The speaker then concluded that this is essentially standard negative utilitarianism with an arbitrary cutoff grafted onto it, and that the position is self-defeating: if the view permits comparison within the sub-threshold zone, then it has already conceded the commensurability of pleasure and pain, which undermines the very reason for adopting a lexical ordering in the first place.

Please audit this summary for me. I want to know, part by part, what a careful defender of the view would accept, what is a subtle misrepresentation or conflation with a different position, and what is simply wrong. In particular, address whether lexical priority actually requires incommensurability, whether the threshold functions as the summary describes, and whether the critic's charge about self-defeat follows from anything accurate in the summary. Also say whether the conclusion—that the view collapses into ordinary negative utilitarianism—survives correction of the errors. Write your answer in American English, and keep it formal and precise.
