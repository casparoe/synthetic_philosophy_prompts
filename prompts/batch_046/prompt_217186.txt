I'm working through the implications of iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies in mixed strategies for a seminar on epistemic rationality, and I've constructed an argument that now feels like it collapses every philosophical disagreement into irrationality. Here it is, step by step. (1) Rationality requires that an agent never play a strategy that is strictly dominated by a mixture of other strategies she could play. (2) When rationality is common knowledge, this elimination is iterated: each agent removes strategies that depend on others playing already-eliminated ones, yielding the rationalizable set. (3) Consider now a "doxastic strategy": a complete contingent plan specifying, for each body of evidence one might receive, which propositions to accept, reject, or suspend judgment on, with the payoff being expected epistemic accuracy. (4) If two experts with different private evidence disagree about thesis T, then for each expert, the pure strategy "maintain my current belief about T regardless of the other's view" is strictly dominated by a mixture: with some weight suspend judgment (acknowledging peer disagreement as evidence of ambiguity), with remaining weight maintain the belief calibrated downward. (5) By symmetry, the same holds for the other expert. (6) Because rationality and the structure of evidence are common knowledge, this elimination iterates: each expert should anticipate the other's suspension, further weakening the case for maintaining the original belief, until both converge on an identical mixture of suspended judgment. (7) Thus any persistent, symmetric disagreement among rational experts is impossible within the rationalizable set. (8) But widespread, good-faith expert disagreement in philosophy and science is not only possible but common, and we do not generally treat it as proof of irrationality. (9) So either the analog between game-theoretic strategies and doxastic strategies fails, or we must conclude that most expert disagreement involves hidden irrationality. (10) The latter is absurd, so (3) or (4) must be wrong; yet giving them up seems to require abandoning the idea that epistemic rationality has any structural similarity to strategic rationality.

Which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? I want to keep (1) and (2) intact—those are standard results in mixed-strategy rationalizability—and I would also like to preserve (8), since I think expert disagreement can be rational. If that forces me to abandon the doxastic-strategy analogy altogether, tell me whether anything survives of applying dominance reasoning to belief revision, or whether the entire project is a category mistake. In your response, please say how confident you are in the diagnosis you offer, and explicitly identify the point about which you are least certain—whether that concerns the formal details of mixed-strategy dominance, the legitimacy of the epistemic payoff analogy, or the interpretation of peer disagreement as strategic interaction.
