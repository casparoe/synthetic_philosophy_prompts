I sit on the clinical ethics review panel of a university hospital, and we have been trying to maintain consistency across our assisted-dying and end-of-life rulings. Our record so far includes five decisions that we treat as binding precedents. First, we approved physician-assisted suicide for a terminally ill cancer patient with intact decision-making capacity, no family pressure, and a self-initiated request. Second, we rejected a sustained request for assisted dying from a patient with severe, treatment-resistant depression but no terminal physical illness, even after two years of psychiatric evaluation confirmed stable capacity and an absence of coercion. Third, we authorized the withdrawal of mechanical ventilation from a patient in a permanent vegetative state at the request of the family, despite an earlier, vague oral statement in which the patient expressed a general reluctance to be maintained in such a condition, which we judged too indefinite to override best-interest considerations. Fourth, we denied a request for active physician-administered lethal injection from a fully capacitated terminal patient, on the explicit ground that our institution permits patient-administered assisted suicide but draws a firm line against the physician being the direct causal agent of death, regardless of consent. Fifth, we approved assisted suicide for a patient with locked-in syndrome caused by advanced ALS, who retained full cognition and could trigger a pre-programmed delivery system solely through eye-movement commands, with the physician present only to verify capacity and set up the device.

We now face a proposal that seems to sit precisely on the fault line between these rulings. The patient has advanced ALS, intact cognition, and a clear, sustained request to die. Unlike our locked-in precedent, however, the patient does not wish to self-administer via eye-tracking; instead, they ask the physician to program an autonomous, AI-mediated infusion system that interprets the patient's neural signals—captured through a non-invasive brain-computer interface—to initiate and complete the delivery of a lethal dose without any further physical action by either party. The patient argues that, because the algorithm executes only upon a specific, verified pattern of intentional neural activity that they alone can generate, the causal chain remains an extension of their agency rather than an act of the physician. The family supports the request, there is no evidence of coercion, and the prognosis is terminal within months. The physician, for their part, would configure the hardware and load the medication, but would not touch the patient or press any switch during execution.

What verdict should our panel reach if we want to be consistent with our own precedents? Which of our earlier cases is the closest analog—the locked-in eye-tracking approval, the denial of physician injection, or perhaps the vegetative-state withdrawal—and what implicit principle or principles best explain the pattern across all five? I am particularly interested in whether there are two or more rules that fit our track record equally well but diverge on this new case, and, if so, which further hypothetical scenario would best distinguish them. Finally, if the panel chooses to approve, what principle about distributed or technologically mediated agency are we effectively adopting; and if we reject, what line about physician involvement are we hardening? I am preparing this for an ethics course essay and want to avoid both a superficial appeal to autonomy and a reductive reading of our own history.
