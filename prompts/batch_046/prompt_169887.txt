You know how in countries with strong judicial review courts can nullify statutes that got through democratic legislatures, and the usual defense is that they're just enforcing a higher law the people already locked in? But then look at what they actually do: they run proportionality tests, weigh rights against policy goals, read broad principles into old texts, and insist they're not substituting policy judgment — except that's exactly what balancing looks like. Meanwhile those same legal systems treat legislative findings of fact as authoritative in ordinary administrative law. So what view of democratic authority and instutional knowledge is this practice smuggling in? Does it presuppose legislatures are systematically blind to rights but competent everywhere else? Or that rights conflicts are a special kind of problem only courts can solve? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether it's one consistent view or a patchwork, and tell me which parts of review — invalidation, balancing tests, the whole thing — would have to change if we replaced it with a straight deliberative-democracy model where legislatures are the final word on both facts and values.
