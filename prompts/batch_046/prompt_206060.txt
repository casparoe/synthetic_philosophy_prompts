I’m working through the old-evidence problem in Bayesian confirmation theory for my philosophy of science course, and I’m stuck on a structural wrinkle that standard introductions gloss over. Most expositions say that if evidence E is already known, conditioning on T → E should not raise P(T) because P(E) is already fixed near 1. But suppose we model theories not as flat propositions but as structured frameworks with two parameter kinds: predictive parameters that govern future observations, and retrodictive parameters that govern how the framework accommodates already-observed anomalies. Intuitively, old evidence should still confirm a framework when its retrodictive parameters are tightly constrained by the framework’s core architecture rather than being freely calibrated post hoc. Can you explain, conceptually and in the weeds, why standard Bayesian conditionalization seems to erase this distinction? Specifically, is the tension mainly that Bayesians conflate logical learning—updating on the entailment relation itself—with empirical learning, or does it run deeper, into how priors over structured parameter spaces are assigned? I want an intuitive account of where the asymmetry between predictive and retrodictive confirmation actually lives, without referencing specific named formalisms or asking for sources.
