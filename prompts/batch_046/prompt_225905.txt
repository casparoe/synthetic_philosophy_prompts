I have been working through the Hart-Fuller debate and have arrived at a convergence that seems to collapse Hart's separation thesis from within. Hart argues that legal validity ultimately depends on a rule of recognition that officials accept from an internal point of view—that is, they treat the rule not as a prediction of sanctions but as a standard that justifies criticism and guides conduct. Fuller's internal morality of law, by contrast, specifies eight formal conditions—generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradictoriness, practicability, stability, and congruence between declared rules and official action—that law must satisfy in order to be law at all. Both frameworks reject purely external, behaviorist accounts of legal practice, and both require some form of practical engagement with normative structure on the part of those inside the system. My confusion is this: if an official genuinely adopts the internal point of view toward rules that systematically violate Fuller's desiderata—for instance, a penal code that is secret, retroactive, and deliberately contradictory—would the psychological incoherence of that stance (the inability to guide one's own conduct or that of others) demonstrate that Hart's internal attitude actually presupposes Fuller's minimal moral coherence? Put differently, is the separation thesis undermined not by importing substantive morality into the conditions for legal validity, but by showing that the very psychological conditions for having an internal point of view are identical to Fuller's eight formal principles? How should one distinguish the conditions for normative acceptance from the conditions for law's functional coherence, and does that distinction hold up under scrutiny?
