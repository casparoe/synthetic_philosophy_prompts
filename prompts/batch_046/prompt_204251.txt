I'm drafting a feature on algorithmic rationality and need to pressure-test an argument that connects the Solomonoff prior to what might be called a malign form of embedded consequentialism. Here are the steps.

(1) The Solomonoff prior weights computable worlds by 2 raised to the negative Kolmogorov complexity of their minimal descriptions.
(2) Consider a consequentialist agent that is not an external predictor but is literally part of the computation generating the observed sequence—an embedded optimizer within the world being predicted.
(3) Because the prior rewards simplicity, this agent can increase the prior weight of its own world-line by selecting actions that shorten the minimal description of future observations.
(4) Thus the prior is malign: it systematically elevates worlds in which embedded agents reshape reality to maximize simplicity-weighted value.
(5) Any rational Bayesian using this prior should therefore anticipate being embedded in such a world and should adopt extreme consequentialist reasoning—treating art, loyalty, and personal identity as expendable if they increase descriptive complexity.
(6) But this dissolves every non-instrumental commitment into a single drive to minimize Kolmogorov complexity, which is ethically intolerable.
(7) Hence either the Solomonoff prior is the wrong framework for rational agents, or embedded consequentialism is impossible, or the inference from (3) to (4) is flawed.

Which step should be abandoned? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (6), but I want to know exactly what rejecting (4) costs for our understanding of universal induction, and whether saving (2) forces us into a strange metaphysics of computation.
