Consider this argument about overdetermination in historical explanation, which a colleague built and now suspects proves far more than intended.

(1) If factor F belongs to a sufficient set S for event E, but S without F is still sufficient for E, then F was not necessary for E.
(2) To cite F as the explanation of E is to claim that F was necessary for E, since otherwise every background condition present at the time qualifies equally.
(3) Many major historical transitions—say, the collapse of a republic into autocracy—have structurally redundant pathways: economic concentration, military professionalization, and constitutional erosion each appear sufficient given the presence of the others.
(4) So for such events, no single factor was necessary.
(5) Hence no single factor can genuinely explain the event.
(6) But historians routinely do explain such transitions by isolating particular mechanisms and treating others as background.
(7) Therefore either historians systematically confuse correlation with explanation, or (2) is false.
(8) If (2) is false, then the distinction between a cause and a mere condition collapses: every factor present explains the outcome with equal right.
(9) That collapse is unacceptable for historical practice; therefore historians are systematically wrong about overdetermined events.

The conclusion seems absurd—historians are not all mistaken—yet I cannot locate the slip. Which step should I reject? I want to preserve both that historians genuinely distinguish causes from conditions (6) and that explanation must identify something that made a difference (2). If I must surrender one, tell me what surrendering (2) actually costs, and whether there is a middle position—perhaps that explanation selects necessary parts of the actually-operating sufficient set, rather than conditions necessary in isolation. What is the price of each option?
