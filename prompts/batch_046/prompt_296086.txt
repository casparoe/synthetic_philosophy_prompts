I am grading a graduate exam on value theory. The question asked students to address whether the phenomenal heterogeneity of pleasure—eating, solving a proof, feeling schadenfreude—undermines hedonism; to distinguish that problem from the axiological “common currency” problem about interpersonal comparison of well-being; and to evaluate whether attitudinal or representational theories supply the missing uniformity without collapsing into the paradox of hedonism.

Here is one response. It correctly notes that sensory uniformity fails and introduces both attitudinal and representational replies, but it contains subtle errors: it misdeploys Mill’s qualitative distinction as if it solved heterogeneity rather than ranking pleasures; it treats the paradox of hedonism as part of the same objection rather than a separate issue; and it mischaracterizes why representationalism might fail to unify pleasures, missing that the common feature lies in representational mode rather than in variable content. The answer also largely ignores the “common currency” axis.

Please assign a specific grade and justify it with point-by-point comments. Flag exactly where reasoning slips, where distinctions are conflated, and where a plausible-sounding claim does not follow. Do not write your own essay on hedonism; assess this answer as presented.

Student answer:
“Hedonism claims pleasure is the sole intrinsic good, yet pleasures are radically heterogeneous—bodily, intellectual, and malicious ones share no obvious feel. This is the heterogeneity problem. Bentham treated pleasure as a uniform sensation measurable by intensity and duration, but he ignored qualitative differences. Mill solved this by distinguishing higher and lower pleasures according to quality, giving hedonism a framework for comparison despite heterogeneity. The attitudinal theory improves on both: pleasure is just desiring an experience to continue, so all pleasures share the same pro-attitude regardless of content. The representational theory holds that pleasure represents something as good for the subject, but because what is good varies widely, it cannot supply a common feature. Finally, the paradox of hedonism shows that direct pursuit of pleasure is self-defeating, so hedonism faces both heterogeneity and self-defeat. Only the attitudinal view survives both problems.”
