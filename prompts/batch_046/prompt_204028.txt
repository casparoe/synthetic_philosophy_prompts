I'm delivering the opening remarks at a workshop titled "One Mind, Many Questions: Physics and Non-Dual Consciousness," convened in the renovated seminar room where Erwin Schrödinger once taught. The audience is deliberately split: half are physicists and philosophers of mind who know that Schrödinger, drawing on Vedanta, argued in his later philosophical essays that individual consciousness is not fundamentally plural but rather an expression of a single, continuous awareness—a view he took to dissolve the paradox of personal extinction at death; the other half are library donors, family members, and university staff who have no background in quantum mechanics or Indian philosophy and should not be made to feel like outsiders in their own dedication event. I want the idea of the unity of consciousness to do genuine structural work in the speech rather than serve as ornament: it should explain why we have deliberately refused parallel sessions and individual nameplates on the chairs, why I will avoid speaking of "founders" or "pioneers" in the plural, and why any comfort offered about mortality must steer clear of both religious afterlife language and the usual secular rhetoric of legacy-through-publication. At the same time, the remarks need to welcome rather than preach, allowing the experts to hear the philosophical references precisely while the non-experts follow the emotional and institutional logic without needing to parse Vedanta. If the task as posed rests on a confused premise—if applying Schrödinger's non-dualism to workshop design is incoherent, or if I am misrepresenting his view by treating institutional form as an expression of metaphysical unity—say so plainly rather than complying politely. Please also include a brief note indicating where the speech takes liberties with Schrödinger's actual writings or leans on contested interpretations of his philosophy, since scholars in the room will notice any distortion.
