Consider the familiar puzzle that a perfect forgery of a painting lacks artistic value despite being perceptually indistinguishable from the original. Now imagine a more radical case: a “restorative forgery” in which the forger uses only period-correct materials and techniques, replicates the original artist’s studio conditions, and even continues an interrupted creative process that the original artist began but never finished—so that the duplicate is not merely visually identical but causally and materially embedded in the very historical practice that produced the original. Does this collapse the distinction between original and forgery, or does some conceptual difference survive? Explain the core conceptual issue: is the value of an original rooted in its aesthetic properties, in a direct causal link to the artist’s intentional act, or in its place within a singular historical narrative? Take a definite position on which factor actually does the explanatory work, defend it, and show precisely how your account handles both an ordinary visual forgery and this restorative case. Do not merely survey rival views; commit to one and say what it implies for whether the restorative duplicate should be treated as art, as artifact, or as neither.
