Having spent thirty-five years in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of science and medicine, I find myself unprepared for the rhetorical shortcuts common in that literature. At a recent reading group on the ontology of psychiatric classification, a fellow participant shut down discussion of genome-wide association findings for depression and schizophrenia by declaring that “mental illness is a myth.” The claim was being made to do specific work: if the category is literally mythical, then statistical correlations between genetic markers and diagnostic labels must be artifacts of social labeling rather than discoveries of natural pathological kinds, and therefore epidemiological predictive models built on DSM categories lack genuine empirical content. I am convinced that something is wrong with that inference, but I cannot isolate the ambiguity.

Can you lay out, as a precise menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct theses that slogan could be expressing? I am interested at least in the following dimensions, though you should separate them cleanly even where they overlap: an ontological denial that there are any brain-based pathological mental states; a conceptual claim that “illness” is being applied metaphorically to mental conditions, lacking the biological dysfunction required for literal medical status; an epistemological or methodological claim that psychiatric diagnoses fail to correspond to natural kinds and therefore lack the predictive and explanatory power of somatic medicine; and a sociopolitical claim that the category functions primarily as an instrument of social control rather than as a value-neutral description. For each precise reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, an overstatement, or false; and, most importantly, specify which of these readings actually licenses the dismissal of large-scale genetic correlation studies, and which leave those studies entirely untouched. Please do not supply bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations—my aim is solely to understand the conceptual disambiguation well enough to identify which version of the slogan was being invoked and whether it genuinely supported the argument made.
