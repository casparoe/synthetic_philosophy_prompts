Read the 1942 film Casablanca through Bernard Williams's integrity objection and his idea of "one thought too many." Focus on Rick Blaine's arc from cynical neutrality to his decision at the airport to send Ilsa Lund away with Victor Laszlo, and in particular on the scene in which Ilsa asks Rick to decide for both of them and he refuses, insisting that she must make the choice herself.

I want you to apply Williams's framework by distinguishing between what I will call integrity-preserving refusal—in which a character declines to weigh a personal ground project against an external moral demand, thereby avoiding the alienation that Williams associates with overly impartial reasoning—and integrity-subsuming commitment, in which a character redefines the ground project so that it already encompasses the moral demand, making the sacrifice feel continuous with identity rather than a betrayal of it. Does Rick's refusal to choose for Ilsa represent an integrity-preserving refusal that protects him from counting his love as one factor among others? Or does his parting explanation—that the fate of a few individuals is insignificant beside the struggle against fascism—show that he has already subsumed his attachment to Ilsa within a larger political project, and if so, does that preserve his integrity or deepen his alienation from it? Be faithful both to the film's plot and major scenes (you need not reproduce dialogue verbatim) and to Williams's claim that moral theories demanding strict impartiality can estrange agents from the commitments that make life meaningful.

Conclude by saying whether Casablanca dramatizes something about integrity and personal attachment that Williams's argumentative essays do not fully capture—for example, whether the form of Rick's farewell, in which he frames the decision as Ilsa's rather than his own moral calculation, allows him to retain a kind of wholeness that philosophical argument about weighing lives tends to dissolve.
