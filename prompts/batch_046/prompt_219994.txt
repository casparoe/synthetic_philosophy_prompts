I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of biology seminar on how historical definitions of life handle synthetic and borderline cases, and I keep returning to Aristotle's hylomorphic framework—life understood as the presence of soul organizing matter, with nutritive, sensitive, and rational capacities arranged hierarchically. My professor noted that modern debates often assume a genetic or evolutionary criterion that Aristotle would not recognize, but we did not discuss what he might say about entities that fall outside both traditional and modern categories.

So I want to ask you to reconstruct, speculatively but rigorously, Aristotle's likely judgment on the following hypothetical. Imagine that researchers eventually identify self-sustaining chemical networks in deep anoxic subsurface environments: they maintain homeostatic boundaries, reproduce through mineral-catalyzed replication of metabolic cycles rather than nucleic acids, and persist over geological timescales—yet they contain no DNA, RNA, or proteins, and have no evolutionary lineage in the standard biological sense. They are metabolically active and self-maintaining, not inert like viruses, but they are not conventional cells either. Applying Aristotle's metaphysics and his account of living beings, do you think he would classify them as alive?

More specifically, please address these points in your reconstruction. Would he grant them the status of natural beings, or would he treat them as artificial or accidental formations? If alive, which level of soul—nutritive, sensitive, or rational—would he assign, and what features of the case would justify that assignment? How would he evaluate the absence of genetic heredity: as a failure to satisfy the reproductive aspect of the nutritive soul, or as compatible with reproduction understood through formal causation and material continuity? And if these networks were initially synthesized in a laboratory but then sustained themselves independently, how would his distinction between nature and art bear on their status?

Please ground your answer in Aristotle's conceptual vocabulary—hylomorphism, formal and material cause, the hierarchy of souls—rather than importing modern biological taxonomy. I'm not looking for citations or a literature review, but for a philosophically disciplined prediction of how his framework would digest this particular borderline case.
