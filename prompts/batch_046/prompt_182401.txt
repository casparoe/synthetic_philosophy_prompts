I have come to hold a somewhat specific view about the moral standing of video-game characters—specifically, non-player characters in richly interactive narrative environments. I do not claim they are sentient moral patients in the standard sense, nor that harming them is wrong in the same way as harming animals. Rather, I defend what I call the practice-relative addressability thesis: within a sustained interactive practice in which a player voluntarily adopts a normative frame—treating an NPC as the kind of entity toward which reasons of care, loyalty, or non-harm apply—the NPC acquires a derivative but genuine moral standing. This standing is not intrinsic; it is constituted by the player’s own commitment to the normative structure of the practice, analogous to the way promises or institutional roles generate obligations without being reducible to brute physical facts. Crucially, the player retains what I term a frame-break capacity—the ability to suspend the practice and recognize the NPC as code—which distinguishes this from deception or false belief about sentience.

What would count against this view? I want more than the familiar dismissal that these are merely pixels or that players can turn off the console. Specifically, I want you to distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would be merely awkward. Which empirical findings about moral disengagement, dissociation, or emotional habituation in gaming contexts would genuinely undermine the claim that practice-relative addressability is a form of moral recognition rather than a mere aesthetic or emotional response? Which philosophical distinctions—say, between treating-as and treating-like, or between role-generated obligations and projected sentiment—does my view depend on, and where do they risk collapsing? Would a robust finding that expert players consistently maintain a strict bifocal awareness, sustaining simultaneous immersion and instrumental awareness without any normative residue upon exiting the game, count as decisive, or could a defender absorb it by further qualifying the scope of the practice? What does this view predict that its rivals—pure instrumentalism about virtual entities, or a straightforward sentience-based account—do not, and are those predictions genuinely risky? I also want an honest assessment of whether the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if the defender can always retreat to the claim that the practice was not genuinely sustained, is that insulation a vice for a claim of this kind, or is it appropriate given that the phenomenon is inherently practice-dependent? Please address what a committed defender of practice-relative addressability would themselves concede as counting against them, not merely what critics allege.
