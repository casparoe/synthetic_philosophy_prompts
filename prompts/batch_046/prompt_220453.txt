After a long and somewhat heated discussion last evening, my friend tried to settle whether human reasoning can be mechanized by giving me what he called the definitive Lucas-Penrose summary. I want an independent audit before I reply. He said that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows any consistent formal system strong enough for elementary arithmetic contains true arithmetical sentences it cannot prove. Lucas then argued that if a human mind were identical to one such system F, there would be a Gödel sentence G_F that is true but unprovable in F; yet a human mathematician, seeing that F is consistent, can recognize G_F as true. Hence the mind is not F. Penrose, he said, turned this into a universal anti-mechanist result: because no algorithm can escape its own Gödel sentence, human mathematical insight is inherently non-computational, and this applies to any artificial system—including connectionist networks with fixed weights, since any such network implements a computable function and can therefore be axiomatized—because a human can always construct the corresponding Gödel sentence and see its truth, while the machine never can.

From this he drew two conclusions. First, he claimed the argument definitively refutes physicalism, since mental processes cannot be reduced to deterministic computational operations in any physical device. Second, he said the standard objection—that human minds are sometimes inconsistent—fails completely, because Lucas's formulation requires consistency as a premise, and if human reasoning were genuinely inconsistent we could not do mathematics at all, making the objection self-defeating.

I suspect he is conflating Lucas with Penrose and overstating what either proves, but I cannot locate the errors precisely. Could you go through his summary claim by claim? For the Gödelian premise, Lucas's consistency move, Penrose's extension to all algorithms and neural networks, the inference to physicalism, and his dismissal of the inconsistency objection, tell me what a careful defender would accept, where the account subtly misrepresents the original arguments—perhaps by treating Penrose as merely strengthening Lucas rather than offering a distinct line, or by assuming every computable system can be captured in a single consistent axiomatization—and whether the conclusions survive these corrections. In particular, does the argument show that no algorithm whatsoever could replicate human cognition, or only that the mind is not identical to any one formal system? And is his reply to the consistency objection as decisive as he presents it?
