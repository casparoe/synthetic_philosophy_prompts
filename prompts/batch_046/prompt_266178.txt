I need advice about a close friend who, over the past year, has gradually changed how she describes her own mental life. She works as an audio engineer, and she now reports that when she listens to music she no longer experiences any separate “inner richness” beyond the auditory processing, emotional tagging, and physical responses she can observe—only those reactions themselves, with no hidden qualitative layer sitting behind them. The same applies to pain: when she burns her hand, she describes withdrawal reflexes, vocalizations, and threat-evaluations, but insists she can locate no additional “raw hurt” that these functions accompany. She is not depressed or detached—she comforts friends, cries at films, and performs her job with full engagement—but she claims her earlier belief in a private, vivid inner world was an explanatory habit rather than a direct observation. She asks whether she should try to recover that earlier sense through meditation or therapy, or whether accepting the current functional picture is philosophically correct. Her therapist suggests this resembles depersonalization, yet she notes she feels fully present, just without the extra layer she used to assume was there. Some acquaintances say she has grasped an important philosophical truth about consciousness; others say she has reasoned herself out of her own humanity. I want to understand whether there is a genuine philosophical issue embedded here, and what attitude is appropriate—should one strive to restore the feeling of an inner qualitative life, or is it reasonable to regard that feeling as a kind of useful fiction? In your answer, please separate what is well established about the structure of conscious experience from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any single contested account—such as that qualitative features are identical to functional or representational states, or that they are entirely illusory—as if it were the established consensus.
