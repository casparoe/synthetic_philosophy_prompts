I'm working on a feature about how philosophy exams are getting weirder, and my editor wants me to include an example of a question that isn't just "explain Kant's categorical imperative." I was chatting with a friend who teaches a seminar on moral conflict—upper-level, mostly juniors and seniors, three-hour final, essay counts for about forty percent of the grade.

Could you write me the exam prompt itself, plus a model answer outline and a marking scheme? The topic is whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, but don't just ask them to compare the usual suspects—everyone memorizes those. Instead, build the question around a specific constructed case: a senior administrater at a public-health authority who learns that breaking patient confidentiality could stop an imminent outbreak, but keeping it lets the outbreak spread; she also has a third option, delegating the call to an ethics committee that she knows will choose to preserve confidentiality, which lets her avoid direct responsibility. The exam should ask students whether this counts as a genuine moral dilemma, and specifically whether that third option—delegation—introduces a different kind of failure, maybe moral cowardice rather than moral tragedy, and whether recognizing that distinction actually weakens the case for saying she's in a true dilemma.

The model answer needs to show the moves a top answer must make: distinguish practical difficulty from a real conflict of obligations, apply at least two theoretical frameworks correctly (say, a view that accepts moral remainders versus one that insists on principled resolution), deal with the delegation option head-on rather than ignoring it, and take a clear defended position on whether the triadic structure makes the case stronger or weaker. The marking scheme should list the specific analytical moves that earn marks—like noticing that delegation transforms rather than dissolves the conflict—and the specific mistakes that should cost points, like treating all three options as symetrical without noticing the agency problem, or summarizing the literature without applying it to the case. Aim for about 2500-3000 words, time limit three hours, and make sure the difficulty comes from the crux rather than obscure jargon. Oh, and no bibliographies or citations—this is a closed-book exam, so asking for reading lists wouldn't make sense anyway.
