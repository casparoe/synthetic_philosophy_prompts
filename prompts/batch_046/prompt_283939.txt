Consider the institutional practice—common in peer-reviewed grant funding—where proposals are evaluated across independent dimensions (methodological rigor, feasibility, significance) and funded if and only if each score meets a fixed aspiration level, even when a rejected proposal misses that level on one axis by an amount smaller than the known inter-rater variance, and even when funded proposals vastly exceed the threshold on other axes with no corresponding reward. Appeals based on these near-misses are routinely dismissed on the grounds that the threshold represents a non-negotiable minimal competence, not a comparative ranking. What conception of bounded rationality and of value does this practice presuppose? Does it commit the institution to the view that rational choice under cognitive limits should be modeled not as approximate optimization but as lexicographic satisfaction of independent constraints? Or does it instead presuppose that value is fundamentally non-aggregative—that a proposal's merit is not a scalar to be maximized but a conjunction of binary conditions? Is the practice philosophically consistent with the same bodies' auditing requirement that allocations maximize expected return per dollar, or does that auditing language reveal that the satisficing rule is merely a heuristic that the institution does not actually endorse as normative? Finally, if the aspiration levels themselves adapt downward when budgets are tight rather than being fixed by the nature of the dimensions, what does that reveal about whether the thresholds track real normative boundaries or merely institutional convenience? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what a funding system built on explicit scalar optimization would have to abandon or transform.
