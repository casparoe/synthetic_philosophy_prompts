For a philosophy-of-economics seminar, I want a decision tree that classifies where someone actually stands on Sen's liberal paradox — not by the label they prefer, but by how they answer concrete diagnostic questions. Start from the premise that minimal liberal rights (each agent decides at least one personal pair) conflict with weak Pareto optimality in the canonical two-agent case. First node: is the contradiction genuine or only apparent? If genuine, split by resolution strategy: restrict the domain of admissible preferences (e.g., exclude meddlesome preferences about others' choices), weaken liberalism to a conditional right that holds only for non-interacting choices, or reject Pareto aggregation altogether. If only apparent, split by whether the dissolution comes from representing preferences as extended orderings or from distinguishing choice-autonomy from preference-autonomy. Every leaf must carry a named or unnamed position — Arrow-style impossibility acceptance; Sen's minimal-liberty-with-restricted-domain; Gibbard's libertarian-claim reformulation; a Nozick-style rights-based rejection of aggregation; and an unnamed 'meta-liberal' view that treats the contradiction as something to institutionalize rather than solve — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Frame branch points around a concrete two-agent book-choice scenario so respondents navigate without citing literature. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate. Conclude with one sentence on which branch is least populated and why. End with a final line: \answer{...} containing the name of that least-occupied position.
