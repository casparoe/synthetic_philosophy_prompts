I was talking with a friend who studies media philosophy about whether Kierkegaard’s attack on the press in his pseudonymous review is really an ethical complaint or something closer to epistemology, and now I want to turn that conversation into a formal six-month research proposal for a small fellowship in philosophy and communication. The precise question is this: when Kierkegaard distinguishes the crowd from the public and ties the latter specifically to the press, is he describing a failure mode of belief that is structurally different from both cowardly conformity and statistical aggregation—namely, a condition in which opinions circulate anonymously, are adopted without being personally endorsed, and thereby lose the first-person structure that makes them genuinely held? Existing scholarship, as I understand it, reads this passage either as a sociological diagnosis of democratic leveling or as an ethical exhortation to individual responsibility, but neither interpretation explains why the press, rather than the crowd alone, should be the unique vehicle of this corruption; that gap is what I want to close. My approach would be conceptual rather than historical: I will analyze the account of reflection and leveling in the review to determine whether they describe an epistemic pathology—specifically, the substitution of distributed testimony for judgment—without treating the text as a document about 1840s Danish newspapers. I will then construct a taxonomy that separates crowd-belief, public-belief, and individual commitment, and apply it to two contrasting moments in the text to see if the taxonomy holds or collapses back into the ethical reading. A successful deliverable would be a single sustained essay that gives the taxonomy fully and shows exactly where in the text the epistemic reading gains independent traction; failure would be demonstrating that the epistemic vocabulary is redundant given the ethical and sociological ones. The scope is deliberately narrow—no archival work, no empirical study of modern media—because the point is to see whether a philosophical distinction can do work that historical or sociological explanations cannot. Please write this as a one-page proposal in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, arranged like a letter: state the question precisely, explain why the literature leaves it open, set out the conceptual approach and why it is preferable to the obvious historical or empirical alternatives, describe what the result and the failure conditions look like, specify the realistic six-month scope, and include a brief justification for why a philosophy fund should support this rather than leaving it to media historians.
