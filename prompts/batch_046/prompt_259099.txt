Having retired from experimental physics and turned lately to philosophy of technology, I have been trying to determine whether virtual objects and virtual harms are genuinely real or merely metaphorical. I devised the following argument for a seminar, but I now find it suspicious because, if sound, it seems to prove that no virtual event can ever be harmful—not even sustained virtual harassment that produces documented psychological trauma in real users. I would prefer to keep the claim that harm requires a numerically persisting subject of damage, and I accept the technical point that virtual objects are reconstructed rather than enduring; but I want to know whether the error lies elsewhere, and what surrendering either of those commitments would cost.

(1) For an event to constitute a real harm, there must be a specific, numerically identical subject that is damaged, deprived, or destroyed.
(2) Virtual objects are not enduring substances but functional types instantiated by data tokens that are fully replaced at every server tick, save-state serialization, or client render; causal continuity is preserved, but numerical identity is not.
(3) Therefore, the virtual artifact or avatar that a user interacts with at time t is not the same particular entity present at t+1, but a successor token bearing the same relational properties.
(4) If so, then an act that "destroys" a virtual object or "assaults" a virtual avatar damages no persisting particular: it merely prevents the reconstruction of one token-type and initiates a different state.
(5) The harm, if any, must therefore be borne either by the virtual token—which lacks persistence and so cannot be a subject of harm—or by the real human user behind the screen.
(6) But harm to the real user mediated by virtual events requires that the virtual interaction reach the user as a specific, targeted subject; yet the user's "presence" in the virtual space is itself tokenized—reconstructed with each login and update—so there is no persisting virtual subject to serve as the conduit of harm.
(7) Hence virtual actions cannot damage any persisting virtual subject, and they cannot reach a real subject except through generic physical intermediaries (light from a screen, sound from a speaker), which makes the specific virtual content causally irrelevant.
(8) This implies that severe psychological trauma induced by personalized virtual harassment is not genuinely caused by the virtual harm, but only by the physical display—an implausibly reductionist conclusion.

Where does this reasoning fail, if it does? Is the slide from (2) to (3) an illegitimate inference from physical reconstruction to metaphysical non-identity? Or is the error in (6), where I conflate the tokenization of presence with the absence of a real-world target? If I must abandon (1), what alternative account of harm—perhaps one that allows harm to functional types or to relations rather than to enduring particulars—would I be committing myself to, and does it avoid making every software update a moral injury?
