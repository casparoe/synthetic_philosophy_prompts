For my junior-year philosophy seminar I have drafted the following essay, and I would be grateful for targeted comments rather than a general summary. The prompt for the course asks whether historical explanation is fundamentally law-governed or narrative-structured, but I am trying to avoid simply repeating the standard Hempel-versus-Danto framing that every textbook covers. Instead, I propose that institutional history requires a third form—what I call syntactic narrative—which treats an institution’s internal rules of membership and legitimacy as a kind of grammar that constrains which event-sequences can count as explanations of collapse or persistence.

Here is the draft’s core argument, presented in outline form with the key moves expanded.

I begin by granting the covering-law model its strongest case: if we explain a bridge collapse by citing load-bearing physics, we seem to subsume the event under a general law and initial conditions. I then argue that this model fails for institutional collapse because institutions do not have uniform physical properties; they have constitutive rules. The League of Nations, for example, did not dissolve because of a general law of state behavior but because its own syntax of collective security required unanimous council action, and specific violations—Italy’s invasion of Ethiopia in 1935 and Japan’s withdrawal in 1933—interacted with that syntax to make further collective enforcement grammatically impossible within the institution’s rules. A pure narrative can list these events in temporal order, but it cannot explain why this particular sequence led to dissolution rather than to mere crisis, unless it appeals to the institutional syntax that defined what counted as a valid collective decision.

From this I derive my central concept: institutional syntax. By this I mean the set of internal rules that determine which actions by members have institutional force—voting rights, veto conditions, membership criteria—such that the same physical behavior (say, a delegate walking out of a chamber) can have radically different explanatory weight depending on the syntax in effect. I then propose syntactic narrative as an explanatory form that combines the sequencing of events characteristic of narrative history with the structural constraints characteristic of covering-law explanation, except that the “laws” are not universal natural laws but local grammatical rules specific to the institution.

My concrete thesis is therefore that explaining the League’s failure requires not a choice between covering-law and narrative explanation, but a recognition that the narrative is syntactically constrained: the plot only makes explanatory sense when read against the grammar of collective security. I conclude by suggesting that this framework might apply to other institutional collapses—constitutional conventions, corporate boards, international treaties—where the explanation depends on rules that are neither universal physical laws nor merely descriptive sequences.

What I need feedback on is specifically the following. First, is the distinction between institutional syntax and ordinary narrative context actually coherent, or am I just renaming the background conditions that narrative historians already include? Second, does my League example genuinely illustrate syntax at work, or does it smuggle in hidden causal assumptions about state power that I have not made explicit? Third, is syntactic narrative genuinely a distinct explanatory category, or is it ultimately reducible to one of the two traditional sides—perhaps a narrative with unusually detailed background conditions? I am particularly unsure whether I need to address the objection that institutional rules are themselves historical products, and therefore that appealing to them as explanatory constraints risks circularity. Any suggestions for sharpening the concept, for a better example, or for a way to defend syntactic narrative against reduction to either standard view would be especially welcome. I am not looking for bibliographic recommendations; I want to know whether the conceptual architecture holds together on its own terms.
