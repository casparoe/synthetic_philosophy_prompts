As a journalist writing about the philosophy of social science, I am preparing a feature on whether cultural values can operate as independent causes in historical explanation. During my reporting, a historian of economic thought offered me the following characterization of Max Weber's well-known study linking Calvinist ethics to the development of modern capitalism, together with its broader implications. She said Weber showed that the psychological anxiety generated by the doctrine of predestination led believers to seek signs of salvation in worldly economic achievement, and that this religious value system therefore served as the primary and sufficient causal driver of rationalized capitalism in Northern Europe, quite apart from technological innovation or market structures. She added that Weber's reliance on ideal-type analysis means his thesis is not an empirical historical claim but a purely conceptual model lacking factual commitments. Finally, she concluded that Weber's framework actually proves culture can never be an autonomous cause: values influence behavior only through individual psychological mediation, and because those mental states themselves depend on underlying material conditions, any explanation invoking culture is ultimately reducible to economic structures and individual incentives. Before I publish this framing, I want to know whether it holds up. Which parts accurately capture Weber's arguments about elective affinity, unintended consequences, and the causal weight of religious ethics? Where does her summary subtly conflate Weber's heuristic method with fictional modeling, overstate the sufficiency of cultural causes, or misrepresent his concept of value-rational action as causally inert? And does her reductionist conclusion—that culture is never more than an intervening variable between economic conditions and outcomes—actually follow from Weber's explicit defense of multi-causal, non-reductive explanation, or does it depend on misreading his position?
