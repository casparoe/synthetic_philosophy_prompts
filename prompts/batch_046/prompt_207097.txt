I'm trying to map my actual judgments about corruption, clientelism, and gift-giving, and whether Western legal categories apply universally or only within specific institutional frames.

Here are my verdicts. A legislator takes cash for a specific vote: corrupt. A minister in a system with no formal merit rules appoints relatives to staff posts: not clearly corruption, since no explicit institutional duty is violated, even though it looks like nepotism from outside. A private-practice doctor accepts luxury travel from a pharmaceutical firm and then prescribes more of its products: corrupt, because it subverts a professional duty owed to patients. A tribal elder distributes communal irrigation rights preferentially to his clan, where that is the traditional and acknowledged basis of authority: not corruption. But if that same elder, now elected to a modern municipal council with formal equality rules, does the identical thing: corrupt. A Japanese regulator receives an expensive seasonal gift from a firm he oversees; refusal would violate a social protocol that sustains long-term cooperation: not corrupt, though it creates reciprocal obligation. A city councilor directs public contracts to supporters who mobilized voters, because political survival requires it and voters understand the exchange: corrupt under a universalist standard, yet I hesitate to apply the label if the entire system operates on that logic and no formal rule is broken.

The hard tension is between the minister appointing relatives in a non-merit system and the regulator accepting the seasonal gift: both involve obligations outside formal rules, but one feels closer to corruption than the other. Is the principle that corruption requires violating an explicit role-obligation? That saves the minister and the regulator, but seems to miss something essential about the legislator and the doctor. Or is it about substituting particularistic interests for public or universal ones? That captures the legislator, the doctor, and the councilor, but makes the tribal elder and possibly the minister harder to place consistently.

What principle, if any, delivers all six verdicts, and which ones must I drop? And apply it to a case I haven't settled: in a "dual-track" bureaucracy where formal universalist rules exist on paper but everyone knows actual resource allocation runs through an acknowledged reciprocity network, is using the network corruption, or is corruption strictly limited to violating the formal rules?
