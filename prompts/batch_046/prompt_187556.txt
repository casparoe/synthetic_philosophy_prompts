I work as a clinical intake coordinator at a community mental-health center that uses an existential-phenomenological framework. We receive referrals in which clients explain harmful behavior, career paralysis, or relational failure by saying they “could not have acted otherwise”—not as legal defenses, but as lived descriptions of their situation. I need a reusable decision procedure my assessment team can run during a 45-minute structured interview to determine whether a client’s claim expresses Sartrean bad faith (a self-deceptive flight from the ontological structure of being-for-itself) or reflects a genuine structural constraint that should be treated as determinative for treatment planning.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions, specifying for each what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a classification of bad faith, where it stops with a classification of genuine constraint, and where it must hand the case to a senior clinician because the distinction turns on values rather than phenomenological evidence. The procedure must operationalize Sartre’s distinction between facticity (the given, including biological and social conditions) and transcendence (the for-itself’s negation and projection beyond the given), and it must include a specific test for the “spirit of seriousness”—the treatment of values, roles, or psychological traits as pre-given things rather than as chosen projects. Structure the bad-faith branch into at least two sub-types: one in which the client denies transcendence by treating themselves as pure facticity, and one in which the client denies facticity by treating transcendence as unlimited and therefore dismissing real constraints.

Include three worked applications: (1) a client who says they remained in an abusive relationship because “love is a force beyond choice”; (2) a client with a diagnosed obsessive-compulsive disorder who describes ritual behavior as “happening to me, not done by me”; and (3) a case where the procedure yields a classification that conflicts with the client’s own self-interpretation, and explain explicitly what the procedure can and cannot do about that conflict—particularly whether labeling a claim as bad faith changes the ethical obligation of the clinician.

The procedure should be usable by staff without philosophical training, should flag every step that is a judgment call rather than a mechanical rule, and should state its own limits: which kinds of cases it will systematically misclassify, and what it cannot determine about treatment obligations. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not reference obscure post-Sartrean scholarship that would not be common knowledge; and write the procedure in formal, polished language appropriate for an institutional manual.
