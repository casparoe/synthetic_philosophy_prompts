I'm trying to decide where philosophical work on luck egalitarianism is actually load-bearing for institutional design, and where it's decorative. Most overviews rehearse the leveling-down objection and the choice-circumstance distinction, but I'm interested in three pressure points that don't get the same attention: first, how to apply the brute-luck/option-luck distinction in sequential institutional lotteries — cases where an earlier brute-luck outcome (say, an initial allocation of resources or access) reshapes the option set for a later choice, making it unclear whether we should correct the initial distribution, the mechanism, or both; second, whether the distinction collapses epistemically when we lack counterfactual access to what agents would have chosen under neutral option sets, especially in algorithmically mediated matching markets; and third, whether taking preference-formation seriously as partly luck-governed pushes the view toward prioritarianism or sufficientarianism rather than equality.

Assume a small research group with about two years and no mandate to produce empirical ML results — just conceptual clarification that could guide mechanism design. Which of these open lines of work should they prioritize, and in what order? Defend the ranking explicitly: use tractability for philosophers rather than economists, upstream dependency (which answers unlock which others), and practical relevance for anyone building distributive institutions. I also want to know what evidence or argument over the next couple of years would move one of these up or down your list. In particular, if it turned out that counterfactual identification of option sets is computationally intractable at institutional scale, which parts of the agenda survive and which become decorative?
