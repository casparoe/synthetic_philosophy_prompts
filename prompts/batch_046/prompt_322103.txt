I was talking with two friends—Mira and Jonah—about a parallel they think is under-explored between Darwin and Turing, and I want an outside adjudication rather than my own bias. Mira began with Darwin's inversion in The Descent of Man: Darwin observes that his reasoning faculties evolved for practical survival rather than metaphysical truth, so he distrusts his own intuition that nature requires a designer. Mira says this is genuinely self-undermining—if evolutionary origin discredits reasoning, then the evolutionary argument itself is discredited. She contrasts this with Turing's inversion in his 1950 essay Computing Machinery and Intelligence: Turing does not claim that reasoning is broken, but that we overvalue internal comprehension. A system can display full linguistic competence without any inner understanding, and that behavioral success is sufficient for intelligence. Mira claims Turing escapes self-undermining because it is a functional redefinition, not an epistemic suicide note.

Jonah pushed back. He argued that Darwin's move is not self-undermining because it targets specifically metaphysical intuition, not empirical or logical reasoning; Darwin can still observe natural selection without claiming transcendent access. Meanwhile, Jonah said, Turing's position is exactly the kind Darwin should warn us about. Our strong intuition that machines lack real understanding is just an evolved anthropocentric bias—parallel to Darwin's design intuition. Turing asks us to suppress that bias in favor of behavioral competence, but Darwin's inversion shows we have no trustworthy reasoning faculty that can justify suppressing an evolved intuition. So Darwin undermines Turing's confidence in the very reasoning that licenses his functionalism.

Mira replied that Jonah smuggles in a false symmetry. Darwin's distrust applies to reasoning about origins and first causes—domains where selection pressures never operated—whereas Turing's functionalism operates in practical prediction, where selection does operate and where Darwin himself trusted observation. She added that if Jonah is right, Darwin's own empirical work collapses, since empirical observation also relies on evolved cognition. Jonah answered that Mira now concedes too much: if Darwin's distrust is domain-restricted, then Turing can make an exactly parallel restriction—behavioral criteria are trustworthy in practical domains, and metaphysical worries about real understanding are precisely the untestable, origin-like speculation Darwin should make us distrust. He said both thinkers separate practical competence from metaphysical comprehension, but Mira arbitrarily saves Darwin by labeling his domain empirical and Turing's speculative. Then they paused and asked whether both inversions say something about human self-knowledge. Darwin says we overestimate our reasoning about deep origins; Turing says we overestimate the importance of inner states for intelligence. Jonah said that is their real agreement, but Mira insisted they draw opposite normative conclusions—Darwin urges epistemic humility about what we can know, while Turing urges an expansion of what entities we grant intelligence to.

I want a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange above: for each round, whose claim is stronger regarding self-undermining, evolutionary bias, domain restrictions, and the normative conclusion?
