As a novelist constructing a scene, I need the opening address my protagonist—a philosopher-essayist—will deliver at a private symposium held in a converted library. Half the audience are analytic philosophers of mind; half are engineers who build reasoning systems but have no formal training in philosophy. She wishes to frame the gathering around free-floating rationales—normative considerations that hold whether or not any mind grasps them—so that the idea does genuine structural work: it should determine which metaphors she can allow (she may speak of a system missing or satisfying a rationale, but not of it “seeing” its error) and which hopes she can voice (that we might design for rationales rather than for simulated reasoners). She must ask whether a model can be wrong in a way that is not mere malfunction, without implying the model holds beliefs. Write the speech in three named parts—“The threshold” (opening, ~150 words, establishing stakes for both audiences), “The chess move” (middle, ~250 words, walking through one concrete, non-technical example of a rationale that exists independently of any reasoner), and “The shared work” (closing, ~150 words, offering an aspiration both sides can share without resolving the metaphysical question). Then provide “Architectural notes” (~120 words, explaining how the speech balances the two audiences) and “The protagonist’s stake” (~100 words, describing why she chooses this framing over a simpler debate about artificial consciousness).
