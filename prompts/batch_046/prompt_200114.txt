I am preparing to moderate a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends a Dennett-style compatibilist account of moral responsibility, specifically in response to Gregg Caruso's sceptical challenge that retributive punishment presupposes a metaphysically unwarranted notion of just desert. I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that go far beyond asking him to restate the position. Each question should press on the actual substance of the debate, anticipate the standard reply, and include a follow-up designed for that reply.

Please ensure the sequence covers the following four areas:

1. The distinction between just desert and instrumental deterrence: ask how the view distinguishes them in practice when both can justify identical sentencing outcomes, and provide a follow-up for the likely answer that the distinction lies in the institutional rationale rather than the outcome.

2. The normative weight of Dennett's elbow-room conception of freedom: ask whether this conception can genuinely ground moral blame or whether it collapses into a pragmatic attitude that Caruso's public-health quarantine model already accommodates, with a follow-up that addresses the probable claim that blame serves an indispensable social-coordinating function.

3. The could-have-done-otherwise condition under determinism: ask whether the compatibilist reinterpretation of this condition is genuinely descriptive or ad hoc if determinism makes the counterfactual permanently unsatisfied, and include a follow-up that blocks the deflection that this is merely a verbal dispute about the meaning of ability.

4. The expressive function of punishment: ask whether condemning a wrongdoer expresses an objective moral truth about them or merely performs a social role, and whether Caruso is correct that this expressive function can be retained without retributive intent; the follow-up should press on what remains of the condemnation if its retributive content is stripped away.

In addition, please include one further question that introduces a somewhat under-discussed tension: recent empirical patterns suggest that both offenders and victims tend to evaluate the justice of sentences primarily by their future-oriented outcomes—such as rehabilitation, reconciliation, or public safety—rather than by backward-looking proportionality to the wrong done. Ask how the defender reconciles this empirical tendency with the claim that retributive justice is conceptually prior and not merely instrumentally valuable. Anticipate the reply that empirical attitudes are irrelevant to conceptual analysis, and craft a follow-up that shows why that separation may be unstable for a view that claims desert is rooted in our actual reactive practices.

Write the questions in a formal register suitable for an academic colloquium. Avoid generic biographical or career questions. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The response should be written in British English.
