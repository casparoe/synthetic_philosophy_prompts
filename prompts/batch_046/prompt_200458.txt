I have recently begun administering a small retirement plan that enrolls employees automatically at a default contribution rate that escalates each year unless they actively opt out; the design is defended on the grounds that people suffer from present bias and weakness of will, so the architecture protects their long-term interests against impulsive short-term choices. At the same time, the plan permits hardship withdrawals with very little friction, even though those withdrawals are typically driven by the same hyperbolic discounting patterns and are often later regretted. What philosophical commitments about agency, autonomy, and rationality does this dual design presuppose? Must it assume that there is a “true” or diachronically stable self whose long-range preferences have normative authority over present impulses, or can the auto-escalation feature be justified on purely welfare-maximizing grounds without any theory of authentic preference? Reconstruct the implicit account of akrasia at work: is weakness of will treated as a rational failure, as a predictable cognitive feature to be engineered around, or as something that undermines the legitimacy of the choice being overridden? Furthermore, is the practice philosophically consistent in correcting the same bias through enrollment architecture while respecting it through withdrawal policy? What theory of personal responsibility or paternalism licenses treating hyperbolic discounting as a defect to be repaired in one domain but as an exercise of autonomy to be honored in the other? Finally, if we replaced the implicit view with a position that denies strong diachronic identity—holding that the future beneficiary is not strictly the same agent whose authority should bind present decisions—what concrete features of the plan would have to change?
