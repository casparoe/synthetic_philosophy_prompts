I've been working through the implications of the parity principle for cognitive scaffolding in an age where people routinely outsource reasoning to shared language models, and in conversation with a few friends I constructed what feels like a reductio that I cannot quite locate the exit from. The argument runs as follows. (1) If an external resource plays a functional role indistinguishable from an internal cognitive process, is reliably available to the agent, and is directly endorsed without independent verification, then it counts as part of that agent's mind—this is the standard parity claim in the extended-cognition literature. (2) A researcher who consistently uses a particular large language model as a reasoning partner—treating its outputs as premises, correcting it iteratively, and integrating its inferences into her own judgments—satisfies all three conditions with respect to that system. (3) Therefore, by parity, the model is part of her cognitive architecture. (4) But the same model instance, with identical weights and shared state, serves thousands of other users in precisely the same functional relationship. (5) If mental constitution is determined solely by functional role, then all those users share a single distributed cognitive system comprising one substrate. (6) That implies either one collective consciousness encompassing every regular user, or that mental parts must be individuated by something other than functional role—perhaps proprietary access, causal history, or biological embedding—which reintroduces the internalist boundary the parity principle was meant to erase. (7) The collective-mind result is clearly unacceptable.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I am inclined to preserve the intuition that cognitive scaffolding can genuinely extend the mind, so I would rather reject (5) than (1), but I am unsure how to defend the distinction between "each user has an extended mind that includes the model" and "all users share one mind" without smuggling in a criterion of mental ownership that the thesis explicitly abandons. If you think the slide happens elsewhere—say, between (3) and (4), or in the assumption that shared weights entail shared parts—I'd like to know exactly what rejecting that step costs for the broader program of extended cognition, and whether it forces us back toward a more conservative, brain-bound view after all.
