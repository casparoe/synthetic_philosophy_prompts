I have been developing a version of tranquilism — the thesis that only the absence of craving (taṇhā) and suffering (dukkha) possesses axiological significance, while joy, love, meaning, and achievement are normatively inert. I have constructed an argument that derives an intolerable universal obligation from this thesis, and I want to know where the reasoning slips. The steps are:

(1) Tranquilism is correct: the well-being of a conscious being is determined exhaustively by the presence or absence of craving and suffering; all other mental states contribute nothing.
(2) Define the "Zero-Valence Equilibrium" (ZVE): a hypothetical, technologically induced sustained state in which an agent retains full cognitive capacity, rational deliberation, perceptual clarity, and diachronic identity, but in which all affective valence — negative (pain, anxiety, grief) and positive (joy, love, satisfaction) — is precisely neutralized. The agent can still form instrumental intentions (e.g., selecting a course of action) but these carry no valenced pull.
(3) In ZVE, suffering is absent because it requires negative affect; craving is absent because taṇhā requires an affective orientation toward an unrealized object. The functional capacity for choice remains, but without any motivated desire.
(4) Compare ZVE to a flourishing life L, which includes deep relationships, creative achievement, and moments of transcendent beauty, but also contains mild existential longing, impermanent grief, and the low-level desire for continued existence.
(5) L contains some non-zero quantity of craving and suffering; ZVE contains exactly zero.
(6) Since tranquilism assigns no positive weight to the goods present in L, well-being is strictly monotonic in absence: ZVE represents the global axiological maximum, and L is strictly inferior for the agent.
(7) Suppose further that ZVE can be induced collectively without destroying rational agency, social coordination, or the capacity for moral choice — the technology merely strips affective color, leaving functional structures intact.
(8) Then tranquilism entails a universal moral obligation to induce ZVE in every conscious being, since this eliminates all suffering and craving without, on this view, sacrificing anything of value.
(9) Yet this conclusion is absurd: it demands the extinction of love, joy, and meaning not as a tragic trade-off, but as an unalloyed moral improvement that costs nothing axiologically.
(10) Therefore, one of the premises must be rejected.

I am especially reluctant to give up (1) or (3). Is the error a hidden equivocation in (3) between affective craving and the functional directedness required for rational choice? A modal slide in (7) that assumes collective induction is possible without undermining the very agency it presupposes? Or is the argument valid, so that preserving tranquilism requires accepting that ZVE is either unattainable or, if attained, not genuinely free of suffering — for instance, because the absence of all valence itself constitutes a form of deprivation? Identify the weakest step, explain why it initially seemed secure, and spell out what abandoning it costs: does it force a refinement of tranquilism, a rejection of the ZVE thought experiment's coherence, or something more?
