I have recently begun adapting my late grandmother's life into a short film about her years in a postwar refugee camp, and I find myself unable to articulate precisely where artistic license becomes a moral failure rather than a tribute. Here are my verdicts. Inventing a dialogue between her and a camp official that expresses fears she documented in letters, though no record of the exchange exists: permissible, perhaps necessary for dramatic coherence. Depicting her documented refusal to take extra rations—she insisted on sharing—as instead a secret hoarding, in order to create dramatic irony about survival: not permissible, because it inverts the moral character that defined her. Showing her death in winter snow rather than the summer heat in which it actually occurred, in a film marketed as her true story, because snow symbolizes the isolation she felt: I think this is wrong, but I am less certain than with the ration case, since the alteration is atmospheric rather than biographical. Creating a clearly fictional composite lover, marked in the credits as symbolic rather than biographical, to dramatize her emotional isolation: permissible. Attributing to her, in a scene her descendants will see, a prayer from a religious tradition she never practiced but which they now consider part of her legacy: I keep reversing myself—sometimes it feels like an honoring intergenerational bridge, sometimes like an unearned spiritual appropriation that misrepresents her interior life. Is there a single principle—perhaps something like fidelity to the dead person's own narrative self-constitution, understood not as literal accuracy but as preservation of the moral and experiential structure she would recognize—that delivers all five verdicts, or must I accept separate principles for factual, characterological, and symbolic violations? If a unified principle exists, which of my verdicts does it force me to overturn? And what does it predict about the prayer case I cannot settle?
