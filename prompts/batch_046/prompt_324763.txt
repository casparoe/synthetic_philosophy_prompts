Following a long argument with a close friend about indirect normativity and the structure of idealized deliberation, I would like you to adjudicate the disagreement we kept circling back to. She held that indirect normativity is ultimately reducible: if one fully specifies an idealized deliberative procedure—complete information, perfect impartiality, and rational coherence—the rules it generates are merely efficient instruments for direct evaluation of particular actions, and any remaining binding force is just a heuristic for cognitively limited agents. I argued that the indirectness is constitutive rather than instrumental, because the norms derive their authority not from tracking outcomes case by case but from the procedural conditions of mutual justification that make the deliberation legitimate in the first place. To press her view, she invoked what she called deliberative shadow costs—the normative weight borne by the procedural resources of attention, reciprocal recognition, and justification-giving that are expended in reaching a collective decision. She claimed these costs persist even under full idealization and therefore provide a non-instrumental reason to adhere to indirectly generated rules. I responded that she was conflating structural conditions with empirical limitations: true idealization removes cognitive scarcity, so if shadow costs are genuinely normative, they must be features of authority itself rather than practical constraints. I added that treating a rule as disposable in a particular instance does not merely save effort; it erodes the mutual recognition that constitutes the deliberative framework, which is precisely why indirect normativity survives even for unbounded agents. She replied that this merely relabels the practical value of consistency, and that a genuinely ideal deliberator could re-evaluate every case afresh without undermining authority, because authority in each instance is regenerated by the fresh act of justification itself. Please work through this exchange step by step. At each stage—reductionism versus constitutive indirectness, the status of deliberative shadow costs as empirical or structural, and the possibility of case-by-case regeneration of authority—say who is right and where the argument fails. Then identify what both positions are missing, especially regarding whether mutual recognition requires the stability of rules across particular cases. Be precise, and do not treat this as a request for a literature review or for citations.
