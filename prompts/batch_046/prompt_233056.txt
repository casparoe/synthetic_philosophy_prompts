Construct a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree with text labels—for classifying positions on logical uncertainty and logical induction. Design it for an audience of advanced philosophy-of-mathematics students who understand the basic distinction between empirical and logical uncertainty, but who have not memorized a standard map of the literature. Do not organize the tree by listing well-known author names at the leaves; instead, organize it by concrete commitments and cases, then place named positions (where they exist) onto the branches they actually occupy.

Begin with a single root node expressing the shared premise of the debate: logical probabilities are distinct from subjective credences about unknown empirical facts, and they apply to statements whose truth-values are fixed by logic alone.

From the root, the first branching question must split on the metaphysical status of logical uncertainty: is it an epistemic limitation of finite reasoners that could in principle be eliminated by unbounded computation (the contingency view), or is it a permanent feature of rational belief even for ideal agents (the permanence view)?

Within the contingency branch, split on whether convergence to a unique, coherent limit distribution is required as a normative constraint. If yes, the leaf is the standard logical-induction framework; specify in one sentence its most awkward commitment regarding self-referential statements that assert their own unprovability within the system. If no—if convergence is merely one virtue among others—label the leaf Unoccupied / Pluralist Induction and diagnose whether the emptiness stems from incoherence, from unattractiveness, or from being unexplored. Then split the yes sub-branch on whether updates are driven solely by logical information (new proofs discovered) or can also be driven by changes in the reasoner’s approximation architecture (for example, switching to a faster theorem-prover with identical logical knowledge).

Within the permanence branch, split on whether the normative constraint is local (applied statement-by-statement) or global (applied to the entire belief system). Then, for each of those, split on whether probabilities may depend on the specific computational architecture of the reasoner—not merely on logical content—and include a concrete diagnostic case for this split: a reasoner assigns 0.72 to an open conjecture using algorithm A, then assigns 0.68 using algorithm B, with no change in available proofs; is this rational?

Every leaf or empty node must carry: (i) a descriptive label (named position or unnamed), (ii) a one-sentence statement of its defining commitment, (iii) its most awkward implication, specifically how it treats the update pattern in the algorithm-switching case and in the self-reference paradox case described above.

The tree should contain between seven and nine leaves, including at least two unoccupied nodes that you explicitly diagnose. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading. Where the tree genuinely fails to separate two positions that share a commitment, flag that overlap explicitly rather than inventing a false distinction. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for a graduate seminar handout.
