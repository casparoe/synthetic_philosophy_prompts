I write novels in which historical events serve as both setting and engine, and this has made me attentive to a particular pattern I keep encountering in historiography—one that I suspect has no precise name, though it sits near several familiar ones. The pattern is this: when an outcome is genuinely overdetermined, with several causes each sufficient in combination with background conditions, historians do not simply list them and assign weights according to counterfactual strength. Instead, they distribute the causes across narrative roles that seem to mirror the structure of a well-plotted story. One factor becomes the inevitable pressure—the long economic decline, the accumulated institutional rot—another becomes the contingent spark—the specific speech, the assassination, the sudden scandal—and a third, often equally decisive in any rigorous causal analysis, is quietly demoted to "context" or "precondition," not because it lacks explanatory power, but because admitting it as a co-equal cause would fracture the dramatic arc. I have observed this in treatments of regime collapses, where structural corruption and a singular triggering event are not merely emphasized differently but assigned different kinds of causal being: one is destiny, the other is accident, despite both being necessary for the particular path taken. Is there an established concept for this specific allocation of causal taxonomy according to narrative architecture? I am not asking about overdetermination itself, nor about selection bias in sources, nor about the broad narrative fallacy, but about the practice of mapping causal status—necessary, sufficient, triggering, background—onto the functional roles a cause plays in the historian's plot. If such a concept exists, how does it differ from ordinary historiographical emphasis? If it does not exist, can you name and delimit it precisely, with boundary cases that show when a historian is genuinely doing this rather than merely simplifying for clarity?
