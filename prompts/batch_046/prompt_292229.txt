I'm preparing a feature on decision-theoretic norms for belief, and an interviewee sketched an argument that seems to devour itself. (1) If beliefs are stakes in an epistemic portfolio, rationality requires maximizing the geometric growth rate of accuracy—the Kelly criterion applied to credences. (2) For any belief held with confidence c, a marginal shift toward 0 or 1 improves the expected geometric return if the belief is correct, with only a bounded penalty if wrong, regardless of how finely graded the evidence is. (3) So geometric rationality demands continuous extremization: every belief should be pushed toward certainty or denial. (4) Once first-order credences are extremized, the same norm applies to meta-beliefs about calibration, generating an infinite regress with no stable graded judgment. (5) Hence geometric rationality implies that rational agents must be either dogmatic or trapped in self-undermining skepticism—graded confidence is impossible. (6) Yet the norm was attractive precisely because it explains how small errors compound over time. Where is the slip? Does (2) illegitimately apply a portfolio-level condition to individual beliefs, or does (4) misapply the norm to meta-cognition? And if we stop the slide there, do we forfeit the claim that belief has a genuinely geometric structure?
