I have recently been working through the history of simultaneous discovery—specifically the parallel emergence of calculus in Newton and Leibniz and of evolutionary theory in Darwin and Wallace—for a course on the philosophy of science, and I keep encountering the same analogy: scientific discoveries are like islands in an archipelago that sit independently of any explorer. The idea is that calculus and natural selection were "there" to be found, large and prominent enough that multiple ships arrived at roughly the same moment, which is then used to argue that priority disputes overstate individual genius and that, had any one of these figures never lived, the same theoretical structure would have emerged within a generation anyway. I want to audit how far this archipelago analogy actually holds.

Can you lay out the correspondence explicitly—what plays the role of the island, the ocean, the navigator's instruments, the chart that makes an island visible, and the conditions that allow two ships to arrive simultaneously—then identify the precise points where the mapping breaks? In particular, I am struck by a potential asymmetry within the analogy's own domain: calculus seems driven largely by internal mathematical pressures and notational choices, whereas natural selection appears tightly coupled to specific empirical observations—Malthusian dynamics, biogeographic distribution patterns, and comparative anatomy—that one might miss without the right conceptual "telescope." Does the archipelago image smuggle in an assumption that all discoveries are equally "large" and visible regardless of the explorer's equipment, and does that assumption collapse once we compare a formal system like calculus with an empirically anchored theory like evolution by natural selection? Furthermore, what does the analogy quietly import about the independence of the "landscape" from the explorer—does it treat theories as pre-formed entities waiting in nature rather than as constructions shaped by the particular conceptual resources available to a given thinker? Finally, tell me whether the relaxed conclusion about the arbitrariness of credit and the inevitability of outcomes survives once these disanalogies are on the table, and if the analogy fails, what a more accurate image—perhaps one that distinguishes between "landmarks visible from many coasts" and "landmarks visible only through a specific lens"—would be.
