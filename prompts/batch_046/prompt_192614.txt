I have been working through whether Nozick's tracking conditions for knowledge—roughly, that a belief tracks the truth when it is sensitive to the falsity of the proposition and responsive to its truth across nearby possible worlds—necessarily sacrifice epistemic closure. The familiar objection is that while "I have hands" might track in ordinary worlds, "I am not a brain in a vat" does not, since in the nearest worlds where I am a brain in a vat I still believe I have hands, and therefore I do not know the skeptical consequence even if I know the ordinary claim. Against this consensus, I want to defend a more nuanced position: closure can be preserved within a tracking framework if we distinguish direct tracking, which applies to beliefs formed through world-sensitive perceptual or causal mechanisms, from derivative tracking, which applies to beliefs sustained through logically valid inference from directly tracked premises. On this account, my belief that I am not a brain in a vat does not need to satisfy the direct sensitivity condition independently; rather, it derives its epistemic standing from the modal stability of my inference mechanism combined with the direct tracking of my belief that I have hands. The closure principle is thus reconstructed not as a transmission of direct world-sensitivity but as a transmission of modal robustness through a reliable inferential architecture.

Play this position straight and tell me what would have to be true for it to fail. Which of its two core distinctions—direct versus derivative tracking, or the claim that modal robustness transmits through inference—are most vulnerable? Would decisive counterevidence come from a formal result showing that any transmission of modal robustness through deduction collapses back into direct tracking conditions, or from an intuitive case in which someone clearly knows the premise, validly infers the conclusion, yet intuitively lacks knowledge of the conclusion despite having a stable inference mechanism? I want you to distinguish between objections that are merely awkward—say, that the distinction looks ad hoc—and objections that genuinely undermine the view, such as a demonstration that derivative tracking cannot block skeptical scenarios without reintroducing the very closure violations it was designed to avoid. Furthermore, please say what would change your mind: if you find yourself concluding that the position is well insulated, is that because it has genuinely absorbed the criticism, or because its qualifications have made it unfalsifiable? Where exactly is the reasoning most likely to go wrong—whether in the modal logic of transmission, in the psychology of inference, or in the underlying assumption that tracking conditions can be applied differentially across belief-types?
