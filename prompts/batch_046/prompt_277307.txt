I had a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend last night over dinner about whether the British Raj can be subjected to any kind of moral balance sheet at all. She had just finished Shashi Tharoor’s polemic against imperial nostalgia and was convinced that Niall Ferguson’s institutional defense was not merely wrong on the facts but structurally incoherent—like weighing a railway network against a famine on the same scale. I countered that without some aggregative framework we are left with moral paralysis, and that Ferguson’s counterfactual—what India would have looked like without colonial institutions—deserves serious philosophical attention, not just dismissal. By the end neither of us had persuaded the other, but I realized we were talking past each other because neither of us had made explicit the hidden accounting rules we were using: what baseline counts as the comparison point, what harms get temporally discounted, whether incommensurability is a philosophical discovery or a practical failure, and whether a net verdict is even coherent. I want to turn that exact tension into a classroom exercise for about sixteen advanced undergraduates or graduate students in political philosophy or colonial historiography, meeting for a single ninety-minute session, so that they feel the problem in their own negotiations before anyone names it.

Design a structured simulation divided into three phases. In the first phase (about twenty minutes), split the group into four teams of four and give every team an identical list of eight historical outcomes associated with the Raj—described in neutral, one-sentence terms without statistics: the construction of the all-India railway network; the codification of Hindu and Muslim personal law; the 1943 Bengal famine; the decline of indigenous textile production; the expansion of English-medium education; the fiscal extraction of revenue to Britain; the establishment of the Indian Civil Service; and the violence of the 1947 Partition. Then, secretly distribute a different “Accounting Charter” to each team. Charter A weights institutional durability after 1947 and discounts harms occurring before 1900. Charter B weights preventability under hypothetical indigenous rule and discounts assets that primarily served extraction. Charter C forces every item into a single metric of “development-equivalent years” using a hidden conversion table. Charter D forbids any aggregation whatsoever and requires only a ranked, non-summed list with an explicit refusal of a net verdict. Give the teams twenty-five minutes to apply their charter to the shared list and prepare a brief, one-page verdict: net positive, net negative, or unbalanceable.

In the second phase (about twenty-five minutes), have each team present its verdict without initially revealing its charter; the other teams should challenge them using only the shared list of eight items. Only after all presentations does the facilitator reveal the four charters, at which point the conflict should become visceral: the commensurist team will accuse the incommensurabilist team of intellectual cowardice, the Ferguson-style team will accuse the Tharoor-style team of presentist moral discounting, and the incommensurabilists will accuse everyone else of false precision. In the third phase (about thirty minutes), debrief by mapping each charter onto a hidden philosophical premise—counterfactual institutional persistence, intergenerational moral discounting, value monism versus pluralism, and the legitimacy of aggregation itself. Make clear that Ferguson and Tharoor are not simply disagreeing about railway mileage or famine mortality figures; they are operating with different charters that determine what counts as an asset, what counts as a harm, and whether the two can be summed at all. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: it teaches no archival historiography, it conveys nothing of the lived experience of colonial subjects, and it cannot resolve empirical disputes about precise quantum of extraction. The facilitator must supply that these empirical disputes are real but occur inside framing choices that are themselves normative. Note also what will predictably go wrong: teams will feel ambushed when charters are revealed (frame this as mirroring how historiographical frameworks are inherited rather than chosen); the incommensurabilist team may stall or produce a purely aesthetic list (intervene by asking what policy recommendation follows from refusal to sum); and some students will try to “game” their charter by re-describing items—prepare to ask whether that re-description is itself part of the philosophical problem.

If the premise that this framing exercise reveals something deeper than a simple factual disagreement is confused, or if the task asks for pedagogical rules for a debate that cannot be staged without primary archival materials, please say so plainly rather than complying politely.
