In a seminar on pain asymbolia—cases where patients report intact nociceptive experience and can even localize a stimulus, yet show little aversion, distress, or motivation to avoid it—my interlocutor kept falling back on the line that 'the badness of pain is in the feeling itself.' He invoked it to dismiss the idea that asymbolic patients genuinely suffer, suggesting that once the negative affect is absent the state is not the kind of harmful thing we have reason to prevent or treat. Before I reply, I need that slogan precisely disambiguated. Lay out the distinct theses it could express: a conceptual claim about necessary conditions for counting as pain; a phenomenological claim about inseparable affective quality; a normative claim that pain gives intrinsic reasons for avoidance regardless of consequences; or a metaphysical claim identifying pain with its unpleasantness. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible given the asymbolia and sensory-affective dissociation literature, and whether it actually supports dismissing the patient's first-person report rather than merely redescribing the case. In particular, address whether the sensory-discriminative and affective-evaluative dimensions can come apart, and clearly separate what is settled in philosophy of mind and pain science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists; do not present any contested view—such as the essentiality of negative affect to pain—as if it were the consensus. Conclude by identifying which reading is most likely doing the argumentative work when someone uses this line to shut down discussion of asymbolia.
