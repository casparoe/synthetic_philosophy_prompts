I'm serving on the Humanities Institutional Review Board at a research university, and we're deadlocked over Protocol 47-B, a proposed six-month study in which adults with mild cognitive impairment co-construct "stabilized self-narratives" with a conversational AI. The system organizes participants' fragmented autobiographical fragments—journal entries, family interviews, medical timelines—into coherent first-person accounts that are then read back as conserved identity records. The principal investigator, a philosopher of mind on the faculty, defends the protocol by appealing to the idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity: personal identity, on this view, is not a fixed substrate hidden beneath experience but an organizing pattern that can be maintained through appropriate gravitational editing. To operationalize this, the PI introduces a distinction my co-members find suspicious: "gravitational editing" reorganizes pre-existing narrative mass without altering its trajectory, whereas "ex nihilo generation" creates narrative coherence where no prior gravitational center existed. The PI argues that as long as the AI respects "narrative inertia"—the directional momentum of the participant's pre-intervention autobiographical fragments—the intervention preserves rather than manufactures the self, and therefore informed consent remains valid because the consenting entity is being conserved, not replaced.

The opposing members, led by our clinical bioethicist, reject this framing. They argue that the narrative-gravity model, taken seriously, makes informed consent impossible to locate: if the self is merely a center of narrative gravity, then altering that center's organizational structure—even to "conserve" it—changes the very entity that was supposed to authorize the study. They further contend that "narrative inertia" is not an empirically measurable property but a value-laden interpretation of coherence, and that applying it as an operational test risks disguising a philosophical commitment as a procedural safeguard. I need a formal recommendation memo for the board's vote—either conditional approval with enforceable criteria or rejection with appeal conditions—that applies the PI's gravitational-editing framework as a practical decision procedure, specifies exactly what empirical evidence would distinguish editing from generation in this protocol, and includes an honest assessment of the strongest argument on the losing side. The memo should be actionable: it must tell reviewers what documents to demand, where the procedure stops and hands the decision to value judgment, and how to handle the possibility that the study modifies the consent-capable self regardless of which philosophical model the board adopts.
