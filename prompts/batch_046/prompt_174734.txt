For a seminar in African American political thought, I am trying to articulate my interpretation of Frederick Douglass's constitutional philosophy and need an audit before I present it. Here is where I have landed: first, I believe Douglass's well-known shift away from the Garrisonian view that the Constitution is a pro-slavery pact—occurring around the early 1850s—was driven primarily by strategic political considerations, especially his desire to justify black citizenship, military enlistment, and alignment with the emerging Republican coalition, rather than by a genuinely new hermeneutic discovery in the text itself. Second, despite that strategic motivation, I think his textual argument is independently sound: the original Constitution never explicitly names slavery, and provisions such as the Fugitive Slave Clause and the Three-Fifths Clause should be read as narrow, non-expansive compromises that mention bondage only incidentally, not as national endorsements of human property. Third, I hold that Douglass was correct in arguing that the federal government possessed affirmative constitutional authority—rooted in the Preamble's commitment to justice, the Fifth Amendment's due-process guarantee, and the war powers—to suppress slavery in every state, not merely in federal territories. Fourth, I also maintain that Douglass consistently treated those same compromise clauses as real, binding constitutional obligations that protected slaveholder interests in specific, limited domains, which must be interpreted strictly so they do not expand. Fifth, I take seriously his claim that enslaved persons owed no allegiance to a government that excluded them from the body of people addressed in the Preamble, even as he insisted that the document's true normative architecture was anti-slavery.

Do these commitments hang together? I see at least three potential tensions. One is between the strategic explanation in my first commitment and the hermeneutic vindication in my second: if the shift was mainly political, am I entitled to treat the anti-slavery reading as textually correct on its own terms, or am I smuggling in a rationalization? Another is between my third and fourth commitments: if the federal government can constitutionally abolish slavery everywhere, why do the compromise clauses function as anything more than dead historical relics, and can Douglass consistently acknowledge them as binding obligations while denying that they establish a national property right in persons? A third tension, which may only be apparent, lies between my second commitment—the inclusive, anti-slavery normative core of the text—and my fifth commitment—that enslaved people stand outside the constitutional compact entirely. Is that contradiction resolvable by distinguishing the document's aspirational content from its actual social contract, or does it force me to abandon either the inclusive reading or the exclusion thesis? Please rank these tensions by severity, note any further inconsistencies I have missed, identify which pair is only superficially in conflict, and tell me what single revision would clean up the most damage without collapsing the overall interpretation.
