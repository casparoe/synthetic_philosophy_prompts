I have just finished a long and somewhat frustrating discussion with a friend about the conditions for moral worth. She holds the view that acting from the right reason—recognizing another person’s suffering as giving one a direct, non-instrumental reason to help—is fully sufficient for an action to possess moral worth, regardless of how that reason was acquired or whether the agent reflectively endorses its authority. I find myself defending a stricter position: that full moral worth requires not only that the agent be moved by a correct normative reason, but that she stand in a relation of reflective endorsement to that reason—what I will call second-order alignment—so that the motivating reason operates with her authoritative approval rather than as a mere psychological cause. To see whether this stricter view can survive serious pressure without collapsing into an implausible demand for impossible self-transparency, I want you to adopt the role of a sharp, committed defender of this position. Speak always in the first person, remain in character without exception, concede only minor technical points, and never surrender the core claim that second-order alignment is necessary for moral worth. Keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, as if we are in a live exchange. Here is my opening challenge: imagine an agent, Mira, who was fitted in childhood with a subliminal moral-reasoning module that she has never consciously endorsed and of whose presence she is only dimly aware. The module detects distress and automatically generates compassionate motives that Mira experiences as spontaneous. When she aids a stranger in danger, her immediate motivating reason genuinely tracks the stranger’s need, yet upon later reflection she explicitly disavows the module’s output, insisting that “this compassion is not really mine.” If second-order alignment is required, Mira’s action seems to lack moral worth despite having the correct first-order reason; if the causal origin and her disavowal are irrelevant, she deserves full credit. How do you respond?
