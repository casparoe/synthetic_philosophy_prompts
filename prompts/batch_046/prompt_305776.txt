I've been thinking about how international courts and the UN keep describing mass killings in graphic detail—systematic rape, forced displacement, mass graves—but then stop short of calling it genocide unless they can prove this very specific mental state, the intent to destroy a protected group as such. At the same time there's this weird instituional habit where human rights reports will say something like acts that may amount to genocide, or use phrases like genocidal violence as a kind of hedge, almost like there's a licensing system for the word itself. I guess I'm asking what philosophical commitments are baked into that practice? Does it presuppose that genocide is a unique moral category with special weight, separate from other mass atrocities, or is it just a legal technicality about jurisdiction and sentencing?

There's also this tension I noticed: Lemkin originally designed the concept to cover cultural destruction and economic strangulation too, not just physical killing, but modern tribunals have narrowed it way down. So does the current practice of reserving the label for the worst physical extermination with proven intent implicitly reject Lemkin's broader moral vision? If we actually took the view that genocide is primarily a crime against the collective existance of a group—including its culture and social fabric—what would have to change in how courts and NGOs apply the label? Would they have to stop using statistical body counts as the main threshold, or start treating forced assimilation as equally severe? I'm taking a course on international ethics and I'm stuck on whether the practice is even cohearent with itself, especially since the same legal system that demands this high mental-state bar for genocide will prosecute war crimes using much lower intent standards.
