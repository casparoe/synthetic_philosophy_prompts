Student answer: Wittgenstein holds that religious belief is not belief-that, which accepts propositions on empirical evidence, but belief-in, which expresses commitment to a form of life. In his writings on religious belief, he argues that statements such as there will be a Last Judgment are not predictions about future events but grammatical rules governing concepts such as sin and redemption. Because they do not state empirical facts, they are immune to scientific refutation and do not conflict with science.

The fideist objection claims that this insulates religion from rational scrutiny. Wittgenstein answers this through family resemblance: religious practices overlap with other language games, so belief is not isolated. He also deploys the private-language argument to show that religious terms like God are not names for private mental objects but gain meaning from public practice. Thus religious belief is embedded in shared life.

Regarding the Last Judgment, Wittgenstein treats it as a normative hinge—functioning like the hinge propositions in his work on certainty that ground empirical knowledge. Just as we do not doubt that the earth exists, the believer treats the Last Judgment as a fixed point structuring moral accountability. This confirms that religious pictures are public frameworks, not private feelings.

Therefore, Wittgenstein avoids fideism by locating religious belief in public language games anchored by hinge-like pictures, without reducing religion to empirical hypothesis.
