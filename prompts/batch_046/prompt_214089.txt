I'm writing a novel where the hero gets this weird ring — not full invisibility like Gyges, but an asymetrical kind: people forget they ever saw you, yet you still feel everything and can watch them. I want to use it in a chapter where characters argue about whether morality still binds you when there's zero reputational risk but full awareness of harm. So I need a taxonomy tree — built from the logical space, not just a list of philosophers — that sorts answers to "why be moral?" under this condition.

Start at the root: does erasing your social footprint destroy all moral reasons? From there split on whether any surviving reason is internal (soul harmony, self-respect), relational (you still hurt real bodies even if untraced), or systemic (hidden damage accumulates in ways you can't see). Each node should be a concrete claim a smart reader could take a side on without knowing the label it leads to. The leaves should include Plato/Socrates-style internalism, Hobbesian contract collapse, Kantian universalizability regardless of detection, utilitarian harm-counting despite anonymity, plus at least two unnamed combos that are logically open but apparently unoccupied — maybe something like "obligations vanish but kindness remains instrumentally rational because guilt is unpleasant" or whatever gap your tree exposes. For each leaf, name it (or write "unnamed") and give one sentence on its most awkward commitment — for example, the internalist has to explain why invisibility doesn't also dissolve self-knowledge. Also flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really seperate, like Kantian and contractualist both holding firm in hidden conditions? Keep it to about eight leaves, audience is sharp general readers, and please don't include bibliographies or reading lists since I'm running a workshop offline.
