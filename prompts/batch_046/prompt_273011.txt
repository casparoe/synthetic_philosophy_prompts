I am preparing a one-page diagnostic handout for a graduate seminar on classical Indian philosophy, focused specifically on how readers of Nāgārjuna navigate the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) when it is applied not merely to entities with svabhāva but to the relation between conventional truth (samvṛti-satya) and ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya) itself. Rather than asking students to match labels they have memorized, I want a taxonomy structured as a decision tree: each branch node must be a concrete philosophical commitment that a student can evaluate on its merits alone, and each leaf must be an interpretive position mapped onto the logical space of answers rather than the other way around. The root question should ask whether, once all entities are shown to be empty of intrinsic nature, conventional designation (prajñapti) retains any non-instrumental descriptive power or collapses into a purely pragmatic fiction. From there, the first split should concern whether the four corners—affirmation, negation, both, and neither—apply recursively to emptiness itself, that is, whether the claim “emptiness is empty” is itself subject to the same fourfold negation or whether it marks a stable stopping point. The second split should ask whether causal efficacy (arthakriyā) can be coherently attributed to dependently originated relations when both relata are already shown to lack svabhāva, without implicitly reifying at least one pole. Each leaf must carry a named stance—such as a radical Prāsaṅgika conventionalist position, a Svātantrika graded-ontology position, an unnamed hybrid that accepts recursive emptiness but denies causal efficacy to empty relations, or any other combination that actually occupies the space—together with exactly one sentence stating that stance’s most awkward or counter-intuitive commitment. Please also identify at least one logically possible combination of answers that appears unoccupied, and briefly diagnose whether its absence is due to incoherence, philosophical unattractiveness, or mere neglect. The audience understands basic Madhyamaka vocabulary—śūnyatā, dependent origination, the two truths—but should not need to recognize modern commentators by name to navigate the tree. Keep the instrument to no more than eight leaves so it fits on a single handout, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests, and phrase every node as a claim rather than as a pointer to a named school.
