In debates about legal self-reference, people often liken a constitution that contains its own amendment procedure to Peter Suber’s game Nomic, where players may vote to alter any rule—including the rules that govern voting. The analogy is usually pressed to show that a legal order can rationally govern its own change: just as Nomic employs rule numbering and priority schemes to absorb conflicts without collapse, so a constitutional system can embed self-amending clauses without paradox. Audit this comparison. Give an explicit mapping—what in the legal system corresponds to the initial ruleset, the players, the mutable rules, and the resolution mechanisms in Nomic—then identify where the mapping breaks down, especially regarding the absence of an “outside” referee, the role of tacit interpretive conventions, and the difference between voluntary game participation and binding legal authority. Consider in particular a hypothetical entrenched clause that forbids any amendment that “alters the conditions for altering this clause,” but leaves undefined whether an amendment may first redefine “alter” and then proceed; does this reproduce a Nomic-style paradox, or does the legal context provide resources—such as hierarchical interpretation or implicit meta-rules—that the game lacks? Finally, say whether the analogy’s reassuring conclusion—that self-amending law can be stable—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and specify exactly what consideration or counterexample would lead you to revise that verdict.
