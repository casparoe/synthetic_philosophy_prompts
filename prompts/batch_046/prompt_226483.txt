Here is an argument about Confucian role ethics that I think overreaches, but I cannot locate the exact slip.

(1) Confucian role ethics holds that moral obligations are not derived from universal maxims or explicit contracts, but are constituted by one's relational roles—father, son, ruler, subject, elder sibling, younger sibling.

(2) If an obligation is genuinely constituted by a role, then occupying that role is sufficient for the obligation, regardless of the causal or contractual mechanism that placed one there.

(3) So a child adopted in infancy acquires full filial obligations (xiao) toward her adoptive parents, even though no biological bond or prior emotional attachment existed; the role itself generates the duty.

(4) By the same token, a court-appointed legal guardian who never develops affection for her ward nonetheless occupies the role of guardian, and therefore bears thick, identity-constituting obligations to the ward that are not reducible to the terms of the court order.

(5) Extending the principle consistently: any socially recognized institutional role—manager, physician, citizen—similarly generates obligations that are thick, non-waivable, and prior to any contractual specification.

(6) But this entails that resigning from a managerial post, or renegotiating a professional contract, carries the same moral gravity as a child abandoning her parents, since in both cases one exits a role that partly constitutes one's moral identity.

(7) Moreover, if mere role-occupancy is sufficient, then temporary or accidental occupancy—a substitute teacher for a single day, an interim CEO for one week—generates obligations of identical weight and character to lifelong occupancy.

(8) The result is that Confucian role ethics collapses all distinctions among kinship, political, and professional obligations into a single, undifferentiated system of absolute, non-exit duties.

(9) Yet this is implausible: we clearly treat contractual roles as defined and limited by agreement, and we do not think a one-day substitute acquires the lifelong pedagogical vocation of a master.

(10) Therefore either the initial principle in (1) must be restricted to a specific subclass of roles, or steps (4) through (7) commit a hidden equivocation.

(11) I want to preserve the idea that adoption generates genuine filial obligations and that family roles carry weight beyond contract, but I cannot accept the collapse described in (8).
