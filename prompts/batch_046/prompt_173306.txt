Consider Switzerland’s institutional model of armed neutrality: rather than relying on a standing professional army, the state organizes defense through universal conscription into a citizen militia, in which ordinary residents serve as reservists, maintain periodic training obligations, and—under long-standing practice—retain service equipment outside formal military installations, so that the capacity to enforce neutrality and repel invasion is distributed throughout the civilian population rather than monopolized by a specialized caste. What philosophical conception of citizenship, sovereignty, and legitimate violence does this design presuppose? Must it reject the Weberian definition of the state as the entity holding a monopoly on legitimate physical force, or can that monopoly be interpreted as residing in the collective citizenry rather than in a professional apparatus? Does the practice require a civic-republican view of the citizen as an inseparable constituent of the state’s defensive power, or is it equally compatible with liberal contractarian accounts in which individuals temporarily loan their labor to a protective institution? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the state-citizen relation that renders this arrangement coherent, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what merely accommodates it, and indicate where the institutional design remains philosophically underdetermined—licensing rival interpretations. Finally, specify what structural or justificatory changes would be required if the underlying conception were replaced by a fully professionalized, standing-army model combined with a liberal-individualist theory of citizenship.
