Here is an argument I have been working through about Dewey’s experimentalist conception of democracy, and I think it proves too much—specifically, that democratic legitimacy is self-undermining. I want to keep both the claim that democracy functions as a mode of social inquiry and the claim that a community needs some stable institutional framework to operate, but I am unsure which step to sacrifice.

(1) For Dewey, democracy is not primarily a system of government but a form of associated living whose value derives from functioning as an experimental method: it treats social policies as hypotheses to be tested in experience and revised in light of their consequences.

(2) Dewey’s fallibilism applies reflexively. Not only must particular policies be revisable, but the very rules that govern how revision occurs—constitutional amendment procedures, the boundaries of democratic deliberation, and the separation of powers—must themselves be treated as working hypotheses subject to testing. To insulate any such feature from inquiry is to treat the framework of knowledge as a fixed representation rather than an active reconstruction, which is precisely the spectator theory that Dewey rejects.

(3) Therefore, on Dewey’s view, any democratic institution that entrenches constitutional rights, electoral mechanisms, or procedural safeguards against revision—through supermajority rules, judicial review, or similar devices—is epistemically illegitimate, because it blocks the very testing that justifies the institution’s existence.

(4) But if no structural feature can be entrenched, then every aspect of the democratic framework is perpetually open to dissolution. There is no continuity of association, no shared conditions within which social experiments can be coordinated, and thus no associated living left within which inquiry can occur.

(5) So Dewey’s view implies that democratic legitimacy requires conditions that make democratic association impossible.

(6) Hence Dewey’s experimentalism about democracy undermines itself.

Which step should I reject? I am inclined to hold onto (1) and (5)—that democracy is genuinely experimental and that perpetual institutional dissolution is not required—but I want to know exactly where the slide occurs, whether it involves an equivocation between being revisable in principle and being revisable in practice, a scope error about which institutional levels fall within inquiry’s reach, or a modal slide from fallibilism about beliefs to fallibilism about structures, and what each alternative costs for the broader theory of democracy as inquiry.
