I am facilitating a 75-minute advanced undergraduate or early graduate seminar—approximately twelve participants—on rational decision under multiplicative risk. I want a classroom game with tangible but small stakes (token chips convertible to a shared refreshment budget) that forces participants to feel the practical tension between geometric rationality—the Kelly criterion—and ordinary expected-value maximization, before either concept is named.

Design an activity structured as follows. Each participant begins with an identical capital endowment of 100 points. They face five sequential rounds of a repeated, identically structured favorable wager: on each round, a physical randomization device yields a win with probability 0.55, in which case the staked amount doubles and returns to capital; otherwise the staked amount is lost. Before each round, every player independently chooses a stake fraction between 0 and 1 of their current capital. The payoff is strictly multiplicative on remaining capital.

Inject the following second-order twist to keep the idea from being a standard textbook exercise: immediately after placing their primary stake, each player must also place a small calibration side-bet—equal to 10% of their primary stake—on whether their chosen primary fraction lies within 0.05 of the Kelly-optimal fraction for that round. The side-bet resolves after the primary outcome is revealed: if the player's fraction was indeed within the Kelly window, they recover the side-bet plus a 50% premium; if not, they forfeit it entirely. Because both bets draw from and return to the same capital pool, miscalibrated self-assessment erodes capital geometrically in parallel with poor primary betting.

The rules and payoff display should be arranged so that at least one participant is likely to choose an aggressive fraction and suffer a near-irreversible collapse—capital falling below a threshold where they are effectively eliminated—while conservative Kelly-style players survive but accumulate capital slowly enough to feel frustrating in real time. Do not reveal the Kelly formula or the term geometric rationality until after this collapse is observed.

Provide: (a) exact materials needed and how the randomization works without electronics if possible; (b) the payoff table and capital-tracking sheet; (c) step-by-step facilitation instructions with timing for each round and the precise disclosure point; (d) a debrief script that maps the collapse event onto the divergence between arithmetic expectation, which rewards large stakes, and geometric mean growth, which penalizes them, explains the role of the calibration side-bet as modeling second-order epistemic risk, predicts the two failure modes that occur on first run—participants treating side-bet losses as independent bad luck rather than structural miscalibration, and attempting to recover primary capital through larger post-loss stakes—and tells the facilitator how to correct these without breaking the experiential framing; and (e) an explicit acknowledgment of which feature of geometric rationality—its dependence on an infinite, stationary repetition horizon—this 75-minute session cannot replicate, and how the facilitator should supply that limitation conceptually after the debrief.

Keep the deliverable self-contained: do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Avoid references to obscure named theorems that depend on precise attribution; frame everything through the mechanics of the game and through the formal distinction between additive and multiplicative aggregation.
