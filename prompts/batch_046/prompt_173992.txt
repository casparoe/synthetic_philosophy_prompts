I'm taking a course on philosophy of mind and we got onto cognitive penetration — you know, whether what you beleive can literally change what you see. The usual examples everyone brings up are the expert who sees the bird as a specific species, or the scared hiker who sees a rope as a snake. But my group keeps stalling on the same point: half of us think the debate is about whether high-level concepts can enter the content of perceptual experience it'self, and the other half think it's about whether beliefs can redirect attentional resources before perception even starts. We never resolve it because everyone just repeats the same two experiments.

So here's my real question: is this stall actually a verbal disagreement about where 'perception' ends and 'cognition' begins? Or is it a substantive disagreement about the information flow in the brain, just hidden behind sloppy vocabluary? And specifically, I'm wondering if there's a hidden asymmetry doing work — people seem totally fine saying that years of birdwatching training 'penetrates' vision, but they get weird about saying that a single belief ('this is dangerous') does the same thing. Is that distinction — between skill-based attunement and belief-based penetration — actually a real division in the literature, or just my group's idiosycratic confusion?

I don't want a survey of every position. I want a diagnosis: what kind of disagreement is this, what would count as progress — a specific neural result, a conceptual distinction, a change in the framing — and whether we should expect anything to actually end it. If you think the question is just ill-posed, say so and explain why.
