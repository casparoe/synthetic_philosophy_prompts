I was arguing with mates on a rationalist forum about whether gradual brain replacement actually kills you, and it struck me that people just shout 'psychological continuity!' or 'substrate matters!' without spelling out what job a theory of personal persistence is even meant to do in these cases. So I'm trying to write down the actual desiderata.

If you were setting out what a theory of personal identity has to deliver to count as adequate for mind-uploading and gradual-replacement scenarios specifically, what belongs on the list? I mean the underlying requirements, not just a roll-call of Parfit or Sider: the phenomena it must explain (why I care about my future uploaded self but not a perfect clone running in paralell), the distinctions it must respect (survival versus qualitative sameness, first-person anticipation versus third-person tracking), and the consistency conditions (e.g. if every neuron is replaced one-by-one with silicon equivalents preserving causal role, there shouldn't be a mysterious moment where 'I' vanish, unless the theory bites that bullet explicitly). Which of these are hard constraints and which are just camp preferences — say, the rationalist-flavoured demand that the theory tell me whether I should anticipate waking up in the server, versus the metaphysical demand that identity be a one-one relation with no branching?

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than scoring philosophers. I'm particularly interested in whether there's a hidden contradiction between the 'no sudden death' constraint — gradual replacement shouldn't kill me at some arbitrary threshold — and the 'no branching' constraint, since upload tech seems to allow both copies running at once. If that subset is jointly unsatisfiable, that's the part I most want to see worked out, along with which single desideratum does the most to exclude rival candidates.
