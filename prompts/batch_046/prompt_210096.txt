As a retired experimental physicist who has recently taken up philosophy of economics and decision theory, I have noticed a recurring configuration in behavioural choice studies that does not sit comfortably in the standard taxonomy. Subjects repeatedly choose an option that is strictly dominated in terms of expected payoff and outcome profile, yet the choice is systematic rather than erratic: it tracks a structural feature of the option space, specifically that the selected option preserves a larger set of unresolved future decision nodes, whereas the rejected alternative collapses them. The subjects do not report valuing ambiguity in general, nor do they appear to derive procedural utility from the act of selection itself; rather, they seem to treat the maintenance of an open decision-tree topology as a constraint that overrides outcome value. Standard revealed preference theory reads this as a preference for the chosen item, but that feels like a misattribution—the selection targets the architecture of remaining choices, not the welfare yielded by the option. Is there an established concept, perhaps within procedural rationality, option-value theory, or the philosophical critique of revealed preference, that names this precise phenomenon? How does it differ from ambiguity aversion, from Sen's distinction between capabilities and achieved functionings, and from the claim that agents have direct preferences over choice sets? If the literature lacks a specific term, how ought it be defined, and what boundary conditions distinguish it from ordinary preference reversals?
