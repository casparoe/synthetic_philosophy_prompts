South Africa's Truth and Reconcilliation Commission let people recieve amnesty for politically motivated crimes, but only if they made full public disclosure of what they did. Argentina went with blanket amnesty with no confession needed, and Rwanda's gacaca courts did community-level confessions but still kept sentences on the table. So here's the weird part: the South African setup treats a perpetrator's own testimony as a kind of public good that the state is literally willing to buy with legal impunity. What philosophical commitments about justice, knowledge, and moral responsibility does that trade presuppose? Does it assume that building an accurate historical record is itself a reparative act that can outweigh punishment? Is there an implicit theory that confessing changes the moral status of the crime—like that transparency dissolves some wrongness, or that the harm was partly epistemic (society not knowing) rather than purely physical? I want you to reconstruct the hidden view, check if it's internally consistent, and tell me where it gets shaky—like, if the harm was mainly violence, why should telling the truth make it less punishable? And if we swapped in a straightforward utilitarian calculus weighing social value of information against retribution, which parts of the TRC practice would stop making sense?
