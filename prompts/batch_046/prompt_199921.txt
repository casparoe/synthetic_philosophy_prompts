I'm trying to map my actual judgments about borderline applications of gradable predicates and want to see whether they cohere under one principle or require giving something up. Here are my verdicts, case by case.

First, in the classic sorites sequence of plucking hairs from a clearly non-bald person, I deny that any specific hair-count n marks the boundary: there is no fact of the matter whether n hairs is bald and n+1 is not, and I reject both sharp-cutoff and hidden-boundary accounts for ordinary-language 'bald'.

Second, consider a traffic statute that defines 'reckless endangerment' precisely as driving more than 30 mph over the posted limit. Even though 'reckless' is vague in ordinary speech, I think the statute introduces a genuine sharp boundary: driving 30.0 mph over is not reckless in the statutory sense, driving 30.1 mph over is, and there is no further vagueness about the cutoff itself.

Third, for the medical category 'obese' defined by BMI ≥ 30, I initially accept the sharp conventional cutoff: a BMI of 29.9 does not qualify, 30.1 does, and the difference is administratively real. But I also hold that if we learn the measurement error for BMI in clinical settings is always ±1.5, then applying that sharp cutoff to individuals near 30 becomes epistemically unjustified, and we should treat the boundary region as genuinely indeterminate for those individuals.

Fourth, for language fluency assessed by error rate in spontaneous speech—say, 4.9 versus 5.1 grammatical errors per hundred sentences—I think there is no sharp boundary and no hidden fact that separates fluent from non-fluent speakers; the predicate remains genuinely vague across the range, and I resist conventional precisification more strongly here than in the medical case.

Fifth, for whether a melting ice sculpture remains 'the same artwork' as it loses 1% of its mass per minute, I think there is an extended middle period of indeterminacy, but I also think that once 60% of the original material is lost it is clearly no longer the same sculpture. Crucially, I do not think there is a hidden precise percentage—say, exactly 47.3%—that metaphysically fixes the boundary.

Sixth, for whether a gathering counts as a 'crowd', I think density alone is insufficient: at 2.1 persons per square meter with a shared protest purpose it is a crowd, while at the same density with people waiting independently at bus stops it is not. Yet I also think that at 0.3 persons per square meter no shared purpose can make it a crowd, so purpose modulates but does not eliminate density conditions entirely.

My questions are these. Is there a single principle—perhaps one that distinguishes conventionally anchored practical classifications from metaphysically or linguistically anchored gradations, and that incorporates an epistemic-accessibility condition—that delivers all six verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and predict its verdict on a case I have not listed: whether a standardized color sample at exactly 550 nm under controlled illumination is determinately 'green', determinately 'blue-green', or genuinely indeterminate. If no single principle can accommodate all six, which verdict should I overturn, and does the revised principle then collapse into a familiar position (supervaluationism, epistemicism, contextualism, or conventionalism) or remain distinctive? Please be explicit about any tension between the statutory sharp-boundary acceptance in the second case and the measurement-error sensitivity in the third, and about whether the purpose-dependence in the sixth case threatens to make all crowd-judgments conventionally constructed.
