I am drafting a novel in which a pivotal scene unfolds inside a synthetic-biology laboratory. The researchers have engineered a microorganism by splicing recovered ancient genetic material into a modern bacterial chassis; within the bioreactor, it produces a valuable compound with remarkable efficiency. When the team lowers the temperature to replicate conditions inferred from the fossil record, however, the organism stops synthesising the compound altogether and instead secretes a different metabolite. One scientist declares that the microbe has malfunctioned, arguing that its purpose—both evolutionary and engineered—is to yield the compound, and that its present behaviour betrays that design. Her colleague disagrees: he claims that without a continuous evolutionary lineage, the organism possesses no historical purpose to violate, and that its current metabolic pathways are functioning perfectly well according to their present causal roles. I should be grateful for your analysis, rendered in British English, of how to adjudicate this dispute within the narrative. Please address the philosophical commitments each scientist is implicitly making about biological function, and advise me on how a character might reason through them without resorting to generic platitudes. I do not require bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—only a conceptual unpacking that remains anchored in the concrete details of the laboratory scenario.
