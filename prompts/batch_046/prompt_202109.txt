My friend and I just finished a long, somewhat heated argument about Nietzsche's eternal recurrence. She reads it as a strictly cosmological hypothesis—identical repetition of all events—which she takes modern physics to have undermined; I want to defend it as a normative filter, a way of testing whether one can genuinely will an action if one imagines it recurring infinitely, but I struggled to show why this should matter for ordinary moral life rather than only for grand heroic self-affirmation. I want to close our discussion with a villanelle—nineteen lines in the standard form with two refrains and two rhymes—where the refrains are “What returns is not the world, but the will” and “The heaviest weight is the small unchosen hour.” The poem should render the recurrence with philosophical precision: it should acknowledge the cosmological reading only to set it aside, then apply the test to mundane, almost invisible habits—half-listening to a friend, the reflex to scroll past suffering—rather than to monumental choices. The two refrain lines should shift in meaning with each recurrence, tracking the move from cosmological claim to ethical burden, and the closing quatrain should leave unresolved whether this burden actually binds us or remains a dramatic metaphor. The tone should be formal, compressed, and open-ended, since that is exactly where our conversation stalled. Do not resolve the tension into either affirmation or dismissal; let the form itself carry the paradox of repetition.
