At a recent discussion group on comparative ethics, two participants became locked in a debate about whether Jain anekāntavāda can serve as a coherent practical framework rather than merely a descriptive metaphysics. The first speaker argued that syādvāda—the doctrine of conditional predication—together with the seven-fold scheme of predication and the standpoint theory of nayavāda, allows one to affirm that an act like killing is wrong relative to the ethical standpoint of ahiṃsā without denying that, relative to the biological standpoint of metabolic interdependence, life processes necessarily involve destruction. He insisted that this is not subjectivism, because the standpoints are ontologically structured by the genuinely multi-faceted nature of reality itself, not selected by personal preference. The second speaker replied that this collapses into a form of relativism with extra steps: if no single standpoint is exhaustive and anekāntavāda denies that any one perspective captures the whole truth, then there is no non-circular reason to privilege the ethical standpoint over the biological one when one must actually decide whether to administer antibiotics that will kill bacteria to save a human patient. She compared it to saying a food is healthy from one angle and unhealthy from another—true enough, but useless without an external decision criterion. The first speaker countered that his interlocutor confuses epistemic partiality with ontological equality: the standpoints illuminate different real properties of the same act, and conflict between them is a real feature of the world, not an arbitrary framing choice. He added that anekāntavāda does not demand equality of weight, only recognition of partiality; the spiritual standpoint oriented toward mokṣa functions as a regulative ideal that integrates other perspectives by revealing the suffering of sentient beings more profoundly than biological standpoints reveal metabolic necessity, thereby guiding action without asserting an absolute, context-independent prohibition. The second speaker closed by arguing that this is precisely the hidden hierarchy she suspected: if one standpoint integrates, weighs, and regulates the others, then anekāntavāda has abandoned its core claim that no single viewpoint is supreme, and has smuggled in a deontological absolutism about non-violence dressed up in conditional language. She concluded that the framework is either normatively inert—merely describing conflicts—or secretly monistic about value, in which case it owes an account of why that particular hierarchy is justified without appealing to the very absolutism it rejects.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six argumentative moves outlined above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say which speaker is more right overall, identify what both are missing—particularly regarding whether anekāntavāda can supply a decision procedure without a meta-standpoint that violates its own premises—and specify whether there is a consistent middle position that preserves both genuine many-sidedness and practical guidance.
