In my reporting on intergenerational ethics, I have begun tracking a recurring argumentative pattern I call the "intergenerational identity trap." Analysts observe that long-range climate pathways—say, a stringent carbon-tax regime versus a technology-subsidy regime—will generate entirely different cohorts of people by 2150, with different welfare distributions and different aggregate outcomes. From this path-dependency they invoke the nonidentity problem to conclude that, since the two cohorts share no members, nobody in either future can be said to have been harmed by the selection of the other trajectory. They then extend this point into a broader claim: that whenever future populations are path-dependent, aggregate welfare comparisons between worlds carry no moral weight, rendering impersonal evaluation—such as total-utilitarian optimization—morally incoherent for climate policy. Given that the nonidentity problem establishes the impossibility of cross-world moral comparison whenever identities differ, should ethicists indeed abandon impersonal frameworks for long-range planning? Or can comparative evaluation survive without relying on person-affecting restrictions? I ask for a response that is explicit about its own vulnerability: identify the precise step where this reasoning is most likely to fail, and state what conceptual clarification or empirical finding would cause you to reject the premise that path-dependent identity nullifies moral comparison.
