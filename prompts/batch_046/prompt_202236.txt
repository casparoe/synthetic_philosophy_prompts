I've noticed that in many university history courses students must open every essay with a brief statement of their own social position and present-day moral commitments before they analyze any primary source from the past. The instructors say this makes interpretation transparent and prevents hidden bias. But the practice puzzles me: if the aim is to understand people in the past on their own terms, why does the institutional form require the present self to be the mandatory lens through which all past evidence is filtered? What theory of historical knowledge does this presuppose? Does it treat temporal distance as an epistemic obstacle rather than a methodological resource, or does it assume that objectivity is impossible without first declaring one's standpoint? And is it consistent with the same discipline's expectation that historians should practice historical empathy and suspend present judgment? If the implicit view is that all interpretation is irreducibly situated in the present, what would have to change about grading, archives, or the concept of anachronism if that view were replaced by one that treated the historian's present concerns as a contamination to be minimized rather than a framework to be declared?
