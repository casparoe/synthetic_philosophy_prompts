Newton's bucket experiment is usually presented as an argument that rotational motion is absolute—thereby favoring substantivalism about space—because the concavity of the water occurs without any change in the bucket's relation to nearby bodies. I have read, however, that general relativity can be interpreted as incorporating Mach's principle, according to which inertial effects are produced by the distribution of distant matter rather than by motion relative to an independent spatial substance. If that interpretation is correct, then the bucket's behavior should be explicable in purely relational terms: the water responds to its rotation relative to the cosmic mass distribution, not to empty space itself. Given this apparent relational resolution, why do philosophers of physics still cite Newton's bucket as a decisive difficulty for relationism rather than for substantivalism? Or does the confusion lie precisely in treating Mach's principle as a built-in feature of general relativity?
