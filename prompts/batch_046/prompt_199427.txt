In the novel I am presently drafting, there is an aging metaphysician who maintains a private ledger of ontological verdicts regarding the existence of absolute space, rendered across stripped-down cosmological thought experiments. His recorded judgments are these: in the case of Newton’s rotating bucket, where water climbs the walls of a spinning vessel, he rules that the concavity demonstrates genuine rotation against an absolute spatial frame; in the case of two globes tied by a cord and rotating in an otherwise empty universe, he rules that the tautness of the cord similarly proves rotation is not merely relational; in the case of a solitary rocket accelerating linearly through empty space, whose pilot feels a constant pressing backward, he rules that this inertial effect requires an absolute frame of reference; and in the case of a universe containing only three bodies arranged in an equilateral triangle whose sides expand uniformly without interaction, he rules that pure relational change fully accounts for the phenomenon and that no absolute space need be invoked.

I have now introduced a new scene for a later chapter: four point-masses arranged at the corners of a square, maintaining fixed distances from one another and from a geometric center, all traversing circular paths about that center with no material connections between them and with no other bodies present, the motion sustained only by mutual gravitational attraction precisely calibrated to provide the necessary centripetal force. I need his verdict on this configuration to remain consistent with his ledger, yet I wish the ambiguity to be preserved for the narrative. Would he judge that the circular motion of these four bodies requires absolute space, or that their mutual gravitational relations exhaust the explanation? Which of his four earlier rulings is the closest analog—the rotating globes, the accelerating rocket, the expanding triangle, or perhaps the bucket? If his precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, what principle would he be adopting with each possible choice, and what further case—one that I might write in a subsequent chapter—would finally settle which rule he has been running all along?
