I am not an academic, but I have been thinking about whether democratic politics really involves a special kind of moral stain—the "dirty hands" problem—or whether that is just ordinary hard choice dressed up. To test it, I invented a small framework I call the moral absorption thesis: in a legitimate democracy, citizens delegate authority to representatives, and any necessary moral wrong done in that role gets absorbed into the office itself, leaving neither the voter nor the representative personally stained in the usual way. I tried to defend this, and ended up with an argument that seems to prove the absorption thesis is either redundant or impossible.

Here are the steps:

(1) If an act is genuinely required—you have decisive reason to do it and no stronger reason against—then it is not wrong all-things-considered.

(2) The dirty-hands claim is that political leaders must sometimes do acts that are genuinely required (for example, deceiving the public to prevent catastrophe) and yet remain morally wrong, leaving the leader's hands dirty.

(3) My absorption thesis adds that in democracy, this residue does not stay with the leader personally but is absorbed by the institutional role, so citizens share neither the blame nor the stain.

(4) But if the residue is fully absorbed by the office, then the leader personally is not dirty; the dirt belongs to the institution. So dirty hands, as usually described, disappears for the individual.

(5) To save the idea that the leader is personally stained, the residue must not be fully absorbed; some of it must stick to the person who performed the act.

(6) But if some sticks to the person, then delegation does not protect citizens from sharing in it, because they authorized the agent who retained personal moral liability.

(7) So either the stain is fully institutional (no personal dirty hands) or it is personal and therefore shared by those who delegated (no democratic isolation from the stain).

(8) The only escape is to deny (1): allow that an act can be genuinely required and still be wrong all-things-considered, so that the leader is required to do wrong and bears the personal stain, while citizens, having only authorized a required act, do not share it.

(9) But if (1) is false, then morality permits—indeed demands—that agents do what is all-things-considered wrong. This is not unique to politics; a parent who must break a promise to save a child, or a doctor who must deceive a patient, faces the same structure. So dirty hands is not a special political problem; it is the general form of moral conflict.

(10) Therefore, either the absorption thesis eliminates personal dirty hands, or denying (1) universalizes dirty hands beyond politics. In neither case do we get the distinctive idea that democratic representatives alone bear a unique, non-transferable moral burden.

Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost me? I am especially reluctant to give up (1) because it seems like a basic feature of rational choice, and I also want to keep the intuition that there is something specially burdensome about political wrongdoing that ordinary citizens do not shoulder. If you reject a different step, explain how that saves both intuitions.
