Here is an argument I have been turning over about the Hart-Fuller debate that now strikes me as suspicious, since if it holds there is no real disagreement left to have.

(1) Fuller's internal morality of law requires that a system of norms be general, promulgated, non-retroactive, clear, consistent, feasible, stable, and applied congruently with its own rules; lacking these, he says, we do not have law at all.

(2) Hart's minimum content of natural law requires only that a viable legal system include rules restricting violence, protecting property, and enforcing promises, since human vulnerability and limited altruism make these empirically necessary for social survival.

(3) Now imagine a jurisdiction governed entirely by an autonomous system—say, an AI-administered code—that issues and applies rules with perfect procedural regularity: every decision is public, prospective, clear, consistent, feasible, stable, and congruent.

(4) Within that framework, the system assigns all legal rights and protections according to a single heritable trait and enforces this assignment without exception, thereby protecting the dominant group's persons and property while systematically excluding everyone else.

(5) This regime satisfies all of Fuller's procedural conditions and also satisfies Hart's substantive survival conditions for the group it protects.

(6) So on Fuller's account it is law, and on Hart's account it is law.

(7) If both theories classify this regime as valid law, then Fuller's claim that law is necessarily connected to morality—through its internal procedural morality—is hollow: the connection is purely structural and fully compatible with extreme substantive evil.

(8) But then Fuller's natural-law position offers no constraint on legal content that Hart's positivism does not already accommodate, since Hart explicitly allows that valid law may have any moral content whatsoever, provided only that it meets the rule of recognition and minimal survival needs.

(9) Therefore the celebrated Hart-Fuller debate collapses into a terminological difference about which necessary conditions to emphasize, not a genuine disagreement about whether immoral rules can be valid law.

The conclusion feels wrong—the debate is clearly substantive—but I cannot locate the error. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? If forced to preserve the reality of the disagreement, I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact.
