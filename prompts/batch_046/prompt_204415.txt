I'm auditing a graduate seminar that pairs historical texts on language with contemporary philosophy of science, and last week we spent considerable time on Quine's indeterminacy of translation—specifically the "gavagai" scenario, in which a field linguist cannot determine whether a speaker's utterance refers to rabbits, to undetached rabbit parts, or to temporal stages of rabbits. Our discussion remained largely within the standard analytic framework, but I am now trying to prepare a brief for my study group that connects Quine's thesis to an older view about how words acquire meaning. To that end, I have been working through a passage in Book III of John Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding, in which Locke argues that words become general terms not because they represent universal essences existing in nature, but because they are made voluntary signs of abstract ideas formed in the speaker's own mind; consequently, the same sound may be tied to very different internal conceptions across different speakers, and there is no necessary link between the word and the object in the world except through this conventional mental mediation.

I'm struggling to see exactly how Locke's conventionalist picture relates to Quine's claim that translation is underdetermined by all possible behavioral evidence. In particular, my study group wants to apply both frameworks to a somewhat niche calibration problem: suppose we are interpreting the vocalizations of a synthetic distributed system—say, a network of environmental sensors that has developed an emergent signaling protocol without explicit programming—where different nodes consistently emit the same acoustic token in response both to discrete objects and to continuous property-fields (for instance, the token might be triggered either by the presence of a particular animal or by the sustained temperature-gradient that typically accompanies it). The engineers want to know whether there is a fact of the matter about what the token means for the network as a whole, or whether the interpretation is genuinely indeterminate in the Quinean sense, and whether Locke's emphasis on private mental mediation makes the problem worse or offers a resolution, given that these sensors have no minds in Locke's sense.

Could you help me understand Locke's passage—specifically how his account of words as signs of private abstract ideas bears on the possibility of radical translation failure—so that I can explain to the group whether Quine's indeterminacy is essentially a modern restatement of Locke's conventionalism, or whether the two theses diverge in some crucial respect that matters for interpreting non-human or artificial signaling systems? I would especially appreciate clarification on whether Locke's view allows any objective constraint on interpretation beyond the speaker's own mental state, and how that might affect our analysis of the sensor-network case.
