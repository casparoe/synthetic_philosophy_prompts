Please consider the following judgments I make when applying Nāgārjuna's four-cornered negation — the denial of inherent existence, inherent non-existence, both together, and neither — across several ontological cases, and tell me whether a single principle can unify them.

In the standard chariot analysis, I reject all four extremes with respect to inherent existence: the chariot is not identical to its parts, not other than them, not both, and not neither in some ultimate sense; at the same time I fully accept its conventional designation and practical function. I apply the identical fourfold pattern to a permanent self (ātman) distributed across psychophysical aggregates: no extreme holds ultimately, yet conventional personhood is intact. When I turn the analysis on emptiness (śūnyatā) itself, I again deny all four corners, because if emptiness possessed inherent existence it would become a fixed absolute, defeating its purpose.

The pattern breaks, however, in two further cases. First, consider a concept that is structurally self-undermining rather than merely contradictory — an object whose defining conditions are such that assigning any property to it immediately cancels the conditions required for that assignment, leaving no consistent framework for even conventional designation. Here I still reject all four extremes at the ultimate level, but I want to add that conventional existence is unavailable too, which distinguishes this case from the chariot. Second, take a closed temporal loop in which a future event is causally necessary for its own past origin; denying the event's reality undermines the present cause, yet affirming it seems to grant it a fixed, inherent existence. In this loop I feel pulled to accept the first extreme — the event exists, since the loop requires it — and to reject only the others, which violates the symmetrical denial I used for chariot, self, and emptiness.

Can you formulate a precise Madhyamaka principle that accommodates all these verdicts? For each clause, identify the case that motivates it. Then apply the principle to a case I have not judged: an artificial system that continuously revises its own decision rules in direct response to predictions about those very revisions, so that the rule-change and the prediction are mutually constitutive. What does your principle say about the system's ontological status? Finally, be honest about which of my judgments the principle cannot preserve without ad hoc complication, and whether consistency requires me to drop one.
