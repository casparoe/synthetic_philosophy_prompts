I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of technology preparing a position paper for an interdisciplinary seminar on automation and political economy, and I want a consistency audit of my current commitments before I present them. Here is where I stand.

(a) I take Marx's analysis of machinery to be an account of real subsumption: capital does not merely apply technology to labor but reshapes the labor process so that machinery becomes the dominant productive agent and the worker is reduced to an appendage of the machine.

(b) I also hold that the general intellect thesis in Marx's notes implies a structural contradiction: as automation makes direct living labor marginal to production, capitalism's value-form—which requires living labor as the sole source of surplus value—becomes unsustainable from within.

(c) Nevertheless, I think technological development is never autonomous; the same automated infrastructure could, under non-capitalist social relations, reduce necessary labor time without generating unemployment or crisis, because the mediation of value by exchange would be abolished.

(d) At the same time, I reject the orthodox Marxist claim that rising organic composition necessarily depresses the rate of profit. I think contemporary automation—especially platform monopolies and data extraction—can stabilize or even elevate profitability by generating surplus through mechanisms that are not reducible to direct labor-time extraction in the factory.

(e) From this I derive a normative commitment: the appropriate response to AI-driven automation is not Luddite resistance but social ownership of algorithmic infrastructure and training data, treating them as collectively produced elements of the general intellect that should be governed as commons rather than as private capital.

My worry is that these hang together less well than they feel to. Specifically, (b) and (d) seem to pull in opposite directions about whether automation undermines or reinforces capitalist valorization; (a) and (c) may look like I am smuggling in technological determinism on one hand and social constructionism on the other; and (e) might depend on a theory of value extraction that (d) undermines. At the same time, some of these tensions might be only apparent—perhaps the determinism and constructionism in (a) and (c) dissolve once we distinguish material conditions from social form, or perhaps (b) and (d) can be reconciled by distinguishing different temporal scales or sectors of capital.

Please go through my commitments pair by pair and tell me: which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; rank the genuine tensions by severity; for each, say what revision would be least costly, specifying whether I should give up a commitment, qualify a claim, or distinguish a scope; and finally tell me which single revision—if I had to make only one—would clean up the most tension across the set. I am genuinely unsure which commitment is the weakest, so do not assume I will defend (a) over (d) or vice versa. The deliverable should be an audit, not a lecture on Marx.
