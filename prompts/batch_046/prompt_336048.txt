I am preparing a graduate seminar on causal metaphysics, and I have grown dissatisfied with the standard pedagogical examples—failing to water a houseplant, neglecting to flip a switch—that dominate every introductory discussion of causation by omission. I want to push the conversation into a more conceptually treacherous space, specifically where omissions are not isolated individual failures but are structurally embedded in designed institutional architectures. I am therefore looking for a concrete, technically detailed example of a conceptual issue in this domain: a scenario in which a regulatory or organizational framework deliberately omits a monitoring or enforcement mechanism, and that engineered absence interacts with distributed agent expectations to produce an outcome that no single actor’s omission fully explains. The example should be sharp enough to force a three-way distinction between (i) omission as a bare absence of intervention, (ii) omission as an active structural feature that shapes the causal field, and (iii) omission as a normative baseline that only acquires causal relevance under a specific counterfactual comparison. Crucially, the case should not be reducible to the familiar action-omission asymmetry in individual moral philosophy; instead, it should illustrate how collective or systemic omission generates ambiguity about whether causation belongs to the design-level absence, to the aggregate of individual non-actions that inhabit it, or to the interaction between the two. I do not need named theories, bibliographies, or citations—only a philosophically rigorous example that a model without web access could construct and analyze from general conceptual resources.
