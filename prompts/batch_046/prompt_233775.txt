Consider how civilian bravery honors—awards granted by bodies analogous to the Carnegie Hero Fund Commission and similar national institutions—are distributed in practice. These recognitions are given almost exclusively to people who risk serious harm to rescue strangers. When an individual performs an objectively comparable or greater rescue, such as entering a burning structure, but does so solely to save a spouse or child, selection committees routinely withhold the award, often reasoning that the act was insufficiently altruistic or was driven by personal attachment rather than by concern for an unknown other. By contrast, criminal and civil law treats the defense of one's own family as an equally valid justification for using force; the legal system does not diminish protection based on whether the beneficiary was kin or stranger. Meanwhile, ordinary moral education teaches that helping others is good without sharply distinguishing strangers from relatives, yet the institutional recognition system enforces exactly that distinction.

What philosophical commitments about moral worth and about acting for the right reasons does this asymmetry between recognition and legal justification presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume that moral credit requires acting for agent-neutral reasons, that agent-relative attachments reduce the moral value of an act, or that exemplary public virtue must be detached from private bonds? Is the pattern philosophically consistent with the legal refusal to rank justifications by the identity of the beneficiary? And if the underlying view were replaced by one holding that the moral worth of a rescue depends only on objective risk, the successful saving of life, and the agent's control, with no discount for saving kin, what exactly would have to change in how these institutions draft criteria, interview witnesses, and justify exclusions?
