I'm in this philosphy of mind course on intentionality and we're stuck deep in teleosemantics — like, can evolutionary selection actually naturalize mental content without sneaking in some hidden interpreter? There's this analogy everyone in the seminar keeps reaching for: evolutionary selection works like a patent office for mental representions. The representation is the patent application, selection history is the examiner, and the approved claim about the world is just the content. People lean on it to say misrepresentation is just a patent that got approved by mistake — still has content, just false — and that no mysterious intentionality is needed because a patent office runs on rules, not minds.

But then my friend pointed out that real examiners literally read the apps, understand prior art, and make judgments — exactly the intentional machinery we're trying to ditch. So I want to audit how far it actually holds. Can you lay out the correspondance table — applicant, examiner, prior art database, rejection letter, patent term, infringement — then mark where the mapping breaks? Specifically, does it smuggle in an interpreter at the selection stage, and if so, does the naturalization conclusion survive? There's also this weird case we talked about where one neural state gets selected for two totally different conditions — flight from predators and flight from falling branches. In patent terms it's like one application covering two unrelated inventions. Does the analogy tell us whether the representation has disjunctive content, or does it collapse there? And if the patent picture fails at that point, is there a better analogy — maybe something from control-engineering feedback loops — that handles plural selection histories without falling back into interpretation?
