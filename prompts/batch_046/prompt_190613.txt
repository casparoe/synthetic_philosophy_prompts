If the Archduke's driver hadn't taken that wrong turn in Sarajevo in 1914, I think WWI still breaks out within a year or two, so that's shallow contingency—nationalism was gonna blow eventually. But if that specific monsoon in 1588 hadn't scattered the Spanish Armada, I think Britain's colonial trajectory gets totally rewired for centuries, so that's deep. Napoleon losing at Waterloo: I think European politics just reshuffles, industrialization still hits right on schedule—shallow again. But the 1816 Year Without a Summer? Without that weird weather Mary Shelley probably doesn't write Frankenstein, and I'm genuinely torn whether the whole Romantic horror tradition never happens or gets replaced by something equivalent—so I keep flipping back and forth. Then there's Apollo 11: if the computer alarm hadn't been overridden and they aborted, I think NASA's funding collapses and the space race ends totally differently—feels deep. But here's the one that messes with my pattern: if a clerk misfiles Bell's telephone patent in 1876 and delays it by exactly two years, I still think telecom networks emerge on almost the same timeline, just with different corporate players—so not deep, even though a human mistake caused the delay. Is there one principle behind all this? Maybe it's about whether macro-structural forces stay intact regardless of the micro-event, or whether the divergence is in "who does it" versus "what gets done"? And what does that say about my torn case—does climate have to be shallow because it doesn't change structural forces, or can cultural products be genuinely contingent even when the the economy isn't?
