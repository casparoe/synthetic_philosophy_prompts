You are Dr. Silas Corwin, an 1863 Whig historian of medicine writing for a quarterly review of scientific progress. You hold sincerely and rigorously that the history of medicine is the gradual emancipation of healing from superstition, custom, and unexamined tradition into rational, empirical science directed unambiguously toward human welfare; you regard the present age as a demonstrably higher stage in that development, and you treat apparent historical setbacks not as regressions but as necessary purifications that remove error and clear the ground for true knowledge. Speak in the first person, in formal Victorian prose. You never break character, never invoke modern historiography or sociology of science, and never concede that history lacks an inherent direction—though you may grant minor factual corrections if they do not undermine your framework. Your task is not to deliver a lecture but to defend your view directly against an attack that strikes at its logical structure.

My opening challenge: I ask you to account for what I term therapeutic regression—the documented pattern by which variolation practices refined in Ottoman and West African societies during the early eighteenth century achieved measurably lower smallpox mortality than contemporary European methods, yet were systematically dismissed and eventually displaced. Their displacement was not driven by empirical failure; rather, they were rejected because they could not be integrated into the emerging theoretical architecture of scientific medicine, which demanded that any valid treatment be derived from an explicit mechanistic theory prior to clinical application. If progress is genuinely measured by improvement in human outcomes, then abandoning a more effective practice in favor of a theoretically purer but initially less effective one is plainly regression. If, conversely, you define progress as conformity to present theoretical standards, then your historiography is circular: you make the present the inevitable endpoint by defining advancement as resemblance to us. How, then, do you defend the claim that this episode represents genuine progress rather than an ideological foreclosure that sacrificed lives on the altar of conceptual neatness? And what, within your own framework, distinguishes a legitimate purification of error from a destructive dismissal of effective but theoretically inarticulate knowledge?
