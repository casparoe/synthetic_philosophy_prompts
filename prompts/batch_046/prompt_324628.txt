Consider the institutional practice in higher education and media distribution where graphic fictional narratives—novels, films, dramatic performances—are routinely flagged with content warnings and accommodated through opt-out policies on the grounds that they can cause genuine psychological distress, while equally graphic nonfictional materials—archival photographs, forensic reports, statistical accounts of mass violence—are treated as requiring no such precaution and are instead governed by norms of professional or scholarly detachment. At the same time, literature and film pedagogy actively cultivates emotional identification with fictional characters as a legitimate learning outcome, treating such identification as cognitively valuable precisely because the objects are not real. What philosophical commitments about the nature of fictional representation, emotional response, and harm does this asymmetrical practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of fiction that makes both policies coherent, distinguishing what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates; identify whether a single view of fictional emotion can underwrite both the warning regime and the pedagogical norm; and say what would have to change in institutional policy if that underlying view were replaced by one that treats fictional and nonfictional representations as psychologically equivalent in their capacity to harm or to educate.
