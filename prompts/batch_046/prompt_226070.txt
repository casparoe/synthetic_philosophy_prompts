I'm working through a puzzle at the intersection of philosophical psychology and formal epistemology, and I've constructed an argument that seems to show full rational self-trust is structurally impossible for any sufficiently representational agent. I think the conclusion must be wrong—rational agents do seem capable of justified confidence in their own faculties—but each premise feels difficult to deny, and I can't locate the exact slip. Here is the chain:

(1) Any agent that counts as fully rational must be capable of endorsing its own reasoning: if it systematically doubted every output of its cognitive procedures, it wouldn't be reasoning at all, but merely generating guesses. So rational self-trust requires accepting some version of the principle: if my procedures yield belief in p, then p is true—call this the Reflection Principle.

(2) A rational agent with sufficient representational capacity (say, one that can represent arithmetic and its own belief states) can formalize this principle inside its own representational system, not merely hold it as an external observation. It can represent the universal claim: for any proposition φ, if I derive φ via my valid procedures, then φ.

(3) By Löb's theorem, in any consistent formal system strong enough to represent arithmetic, if the system proves for every φ that provability implies truth (□φ → φ), then the system proves every φ—collapsing into logical omniscience or outright inconsistency. So an agent whose internal logic includes the universal Reflection Principle must either believe contradictions or have unlimited derivational reach.

(4) But logical omniscience is incompatible with rationality: a rational agent neither believes contradictions nor derives every arithmetical truth. Therefore the agent in (2) cannot consistently maintain the universal Reflection Principle inside its own system.

(5) Hence no rational agent with sufficient representational power can fully endorse its own reasoning across the board; at best it can trust itself locally or heuristically, never globally.

(6) This seems like a reductio: we ordinarily think rational agents can have robust, justified self-confidence, and the idea that self-trust is structurally blocked for any smart enough reasoner sounds more like a misuse of formal logic than a discovery about minds.

Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'm inclined to preserve (1) and treat (6) as the intuition to save, which pushes the error toward (2) or (3)—perhaps there's a scope or formalization mistake in embedding psychological reliability inside a syntactic provability predicate. But I need to know whether the right move is to deny that agents can formalize their own reliability, or to deny that Löb's theorem applies to belief states rather than to object-level derivability, or whether (4) mischaracterizes what omniscience requires. Please diagnose the exact step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives: what do I pay if I reject (2) versus (3) versus (4)?

Also, please say explicitly what would change your mind—where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and whether a sharp distinction between object-level belief and meta-level endorsement (or between syntactic derivability and psychological acceptance) actually resolves the tension without collapsing into skepticism about self-knowledge.
