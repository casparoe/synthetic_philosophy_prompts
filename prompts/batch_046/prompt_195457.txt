hey so i'm trying to map my actual intuitions on collective guilt across generations, with jaspers in the back of my mind but not following him exactly. here are my gut verdicts.

a german citizen born in 1995 feeling crushing personal guilt over the holocaust: not guilty. maybe metaphysical weight, but not blameworthy.

the current german state bearing political guilt and paying reparations: yes, the institution survives.

a japanese citizen born in 1985 feeling guilty for unit 731: same, not personally guilty.

but if she's the direct granddaughter of a unit 731 researcher and inherited family money from that period: there's a repair obligation, but i think that's tainted inheritance, not collective guilt proper.

a u.s. corporation existing since 1820 that used slave labor now owes reparations: yes, institutional liability survives personnel change.

now the pair i keep flipping on. an american born in 1990, family arrived in 1920, never owned slaves, lives in a region built partly on plantation profits, and feels deep guilt. part of me says that's appropriate awareness of structural legacy; part of me says it's the same misplaced feeling as the german case, since she had no causal role. structurally they look identical, but i can't settle it.

also east germany dissolved into west germany. does the unified state inherit east germany's political obligations? i want to say yes, but only where there's institutional continuty, not where the state just vanished.

so is there a principle behind all this? i'm toying with something like guilt attaches to diachronic institutional agents with normative continuity, while individuals only carry it through direct causal or beneficiary links. but it's messy. can you state a cleaner version, say what it predicts for a nation that was completely destroyed and rebuilt with different people and laws, and tell me honestly which verdict it can't save? if i have to drop either the german or the american case to keep the principle simple, which one hurts less?
