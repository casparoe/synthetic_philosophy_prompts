I have been thinking, in an informal way, about whether things that exist only inside virtual worlds can count as genuinely real, and whether being harmed inside such worlds can count as genuinely harmful. I have been discussing this with a friend who has no formal training in philosophy but is remarkably consistent in her judgments. Here is the record of her verdicts across several cases we have discussed.

She holds that a unique virtual residence in a persistent, shared online world—purchased with real currency and containing furnishings that are not duplicated elsewhere—is a real object of possession. She further holds that if another user maliciously deletes it, the loss is a real harm.

She holds that if that same residence is copied in its entirety onto a backup server, the duplicate is not a separate real object, and destroying the duplicate does not constitute an additional harm.

She holds that an algorithmic companion in a single-player environment—one that reacts to care but lacks consciousness—is not itself a real being. Nevertheless, if the game permanently erases it with no recoverable save file, the owner's grief constitutes a real harm.

She holds that receiving insults through a text-based chat channel inside a virtual environment is not a real harm, because the words lack any presence beyond the display.

She holds that a meticulously constructed digital memorial—an exact virtual replica of a deceased person's home, assembled from photographs and maintained in a shared world—is a real object, and that vandalizing it is a real harm to the family, despite being composed entirely of data.

Consider now a new case. In a shared virtual environment, an artificial-intelligence companion is instantiated for a single user. It exists solely as an active server process; it is never written to a persistent file, and its personality evolves continuously through interaction, meaning no backup could reproduce the exact same entity. Over several months, the user develops a deep emotional attachment. Then the server is accidentally shut down, the process terminates, and the precise companion is irrecoverable, though a superficially similar one could be initiated anew. There was no financial expenditure, no visibility or interaction with other users, and the termination was unintentional.

What, in your view, would my friend conclude? Is this companion a real object or being, and is its disappearance a real harm?

I am less interested in the bare answers than in the underlying rules that could consistently accommodate all five of her earlier judgments, the points at which those rules disagree about this new case, your best guess as to which rule she is actually following together with your reasoning, and the one additional case that would most clearly separate the competing rules from one another.
