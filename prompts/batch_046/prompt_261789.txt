Question: "Compare the testimonial economies of South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) and Argentina's National Commission on the Disappearance of Persons (CONADEP). Apply the tripartite framework—retributive (testimony serves prosecution), restorative (testimony serves social acknowledgment), and archival (testimony is preserved as a non-justiciable historical record)—to explain how each institution handled the trade-off between legal accountability and narrative disclosure. Be precise about which bodies granted amnesty, on what terms, and how those terms shaped the epistemic status of perpetrator testimony versus victim testimony in each case."

Student answer:

"In both cases, truth commissions replaced trials with disclosure, but they structured the exchange differently. South Africa's TRC offered conditional amnesty to individual perpetrators who made full disclosure of politically motivated crimes. Because the amnesty was contingent on completeness rather than mere appearance, perpetrators had strong incentives to be truthful, which meant their testimony could be archived with high epistemic authority even though it was shielded from criminal use. This placed the TRC in both a restorative economy—victims received acknowledgment—and an archival economy: the record was preserved for history but not for prosecution.

Argentina's CONADEP, established in 1983, explicitly rejected any form of amnesty and therefore operated within a purely retributive testimonial economy. Its mandate was to gather evidence for future trials, so it collected extensive victim testimony but almost no perpetrator testimony, since perpetrators had no legal incentive to speak. The Alfonsín administration later enacted the Full Stop and Due Obedience laws, which granted blanket legislative amnesty without disclosure requirements; however, because these laws were not linked to CONADEP's work and involved no testimonial exchange, they did not alter the commission's retributive framework. Consequently, Argentina never developed an archival economy: its record remains prosecutorial and one-sided.

The essential difference, then, is that South Africa's commission engineered a deliberate shift from retribution to a combined restorative-archival economy by embedding amnesty within the disclosure process, whereas Argentina's CONADEP maintained a strictly retributive posture and the subsequent amnesty was externally imposed without testimonial conditions."
