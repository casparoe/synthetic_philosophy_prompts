For an upper-level philosophy of mind final exam, set a 60-minute essay question (target length about 800 words, 25 marks total) on Dennett’s brain-transplant scenario, but frame it through a distributed-variant twist that cannot be answered by reciting the standard “brain versus body” summary. Present the classic setup—brain removed, placed in a vat, wired to a remote body, prompting the first-person question where am I—then introduce the niche modification: rather than a single move, the brain’s functional architecture is gradually offloaded in increments (10%, then 50%, then 100%) to an external processing cluster, while the original biological substrate remains intact as a passive relay and the remote body continues to receive signals. At each stage the system reports an unbroken first-person memory and a continuous biography, so that by the end there are three spatial candidates for the self—the original skull, the cluster, and the remote body—without any clear duplication event.

Ask students to do three things: (1) reconstruct Dennett’s view that the self is not a Cartesian point but a center of narrative gravity—the point from which a life is authored and told—and explain why he thinks the “where am I?” intuition is ontologically misleading rather than decisive; (2) apply that framework to the distributed variant, analyzing whether Dennett is committed to locating the self in the cluster, allowing a non-localized self, facing a hidden branching problem, or absorbing the case without loss; (3) critically assess whether the variant exposes a suppressed premise in Dennett’s reasoning—specifically, an implicit dependence on spatial contiguity or singular substrate authorship that his anti-Cartesianism should reject, and whether he can drop that premise without losing the ability to answer “where am I?” at all.

Provide a model answer of about 700 words that makes the specific moves an A answer needs: it should distinguish Dennett’s narrative-center view from Lockean psychological continuity; it should show that gradual functional distribution preserves narrative continuity but destroys unique spatial localization; it should identify the hidden premise that narrative gravity requires a single spatiotemporal locus of authorship; and it should argue that Dennett faces a dilemma—either accept a genuinely distributed or non-localized self (which undermines the singular first-person reference of “I”) or reintroduce a localization criterion that looks suspiciously like the Cartesian ego he denies.

Give a marking scheme totaling 25 marks, split as: reconstruction of the narrative-self framework and the anti-Cartesian move (5 marks); application to the distributed case, including recognition of continuity-without-branching and the three-candidate problem (8 marks); identification and precise formulation of the hidden localization/authorship premise (6 marks); critical evaluation of the dilemma and whether Dennett can revise (4 marks); philosophical clarity, structure, and direct engagement with the framing question (2 marks). For each component, include a one-line note on the typical B-level failure: e.g., summarizing the brain-in-vat story without theoretical reconstruction; treating the variant as simple duplication rather than gradual distribution; naming a “hidden premise” vaguely without linking it to spatial contiguity; or resolving the dilemma by assertion rather than argument. Specify that answers relying on citation lists, appeals to unspecified “studies” on predictive processing, or bibliographic references receive no credit, since the exam tests conceptual analysis rather than literature recall.
