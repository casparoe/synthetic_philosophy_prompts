I'm drafting a proposal for a six-month interdisciplinary fellowship at the intersection of African philosophy and analytic metaphysics, and I want to avoid the two standard framings—treating Ubuntu either as an ethical ideal of communal harmony or as a generic social-constitution thesis about the self. My working idea centers on what I am calling the problem of relational asymmetry: in relational conceptions of personhood, often captured by the idea that a person is a person through other persons, recognition is rarely uniformly distributed. Some members are structurally positioned as the ones who confer personhood, while others are positioned primarily as recipients. If personhood is genuinely constituted by reciprocal recognition, then such asymmetry seems to threaten not just ethical failure but ontological instability—yet the literature rarely asks whether Ubuntu contains internal conceptual resources to diagnose and correct this asymmetry without covertly reintroducing an individualist baseline in which autonomy is prior to relation. Turn this into a formal proposal that does the following. First, state the precise research question: can a relational ontology distinguish between constitutive and contingent relations of recognition in a way that allows for the moral correction of asymmetry without collapsing into either pure collectivism or hidden liberal individualism? Second, explain why existing metaphysical and ethical readings of Ubuntu leave this open—specifically, how they either absorb all relations into a uniform collective identity or treat asymmetry as merely an empirical deviation rather than a conceptual problem internal to relational personhood itself. Third, outline the method: a philosophical analysis of recognition dynamics through conceptual mapping rather than empirical sociology or textual exegesis alone, working through specific cases of fractured or hierarchical communities to test whether the framework can sustain normative distinctions. Fourth, describe the deliverable: a conceptual framework that allows practitioners and theorists working on moral status in divided societies to apply relational personhood without losing critical purchase on internal power imbalances, together with a clear statement of what success and failure would look like—for instance, failure if the analysis either dissolves the relational core or reinstates an unacknowledged individualist subject as the bearer of rights prior to community. Keep the scope realistic for six months without requiring archival fieldwork or extensive citation tracking, and do not include a bibliography or reading list, since I will supply references myself.
