I was debating with my roommate last night about biotech and suffering, and I walked away convinced of this hybrid view I'm calling threshold lexical negative hedonism. The core claim is that pleasure and pain arent on the same commensurable scale unless suffering crosses a specific intensity line — below that threshold you can't justify a minor headache to get euphoria, but you also shouldn't suppress mild joy just to avoid mild boredom. Once pain hits that lexical boundary, though, it dominates every other value, and the moral aim shouldn't be net wellbeing but engineering consciousness to stay permanently subthreshold, even if that caps extreme happiness.

I'm pretty committed to it, but I want an honest map of where it's actually exposed. What psychological data, value-theoretic arguments, or formal results about preference agregation would genuinely count against it — not just annoying objections, but decisive ones? Has the view already been insulated into unfalsifiability, like saying any pleasure above the cap is inauthentic or that failures just mean we 'havent crossed the line yet'? I want to know what defenders themselves would concede as a real defeater, whether there's a consequence it commits to that turns out unacceptable, and whether being well-insulated is a problem for a view like this. Also, I'd find it especially persuasive if there's a case where staying subthreshold forces us to destroy something we clearly value, but I'm open to being wrong about that framing.
