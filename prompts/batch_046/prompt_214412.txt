I've been taking this course on ancient historiography and got into a long argument with a friend about why some old stories get upgraded from "just legend" to "probably has some history behind it" while others stay stuck in myth-land. We kept going back to three examples: the Trojan War, where Schliemann digging at Hisarlik seemed to flip a lot of people's minds; King Arthur, where even if you found some 5th-century warlord named Artorius it wouldn't really settle whether the Round Table stuff is real; and the Exodus, where the total absence of Egyptian records gets treated as either fatal or totally expected depending on who you ask.

So here's the framing I want to test — call it the "seperability-plus-motivation" view if you want, though I just made that up. The idea is there's no single consistent criterion for legend-becoming-history. Instead, a legend gets rehabilitated when its core claim can be chopped up into smaller parts that archaeology can confirm without touching the supernatural bits, AND when the scholarly community has some non-evidential reason to want it true. For Troy that works because "there was a big fortified city destroyed around 1200 BCE" is totally seperable from Achilles being invulnerable except for his heel. For Arthur it fails because "there was a British leader named Arthur" is basically the legend itself with the magic stripped out, so confirming the small part doesn't help. And for Exodus it fails in reverse: the core claim is one giant mass event that should leave traces everywhere, so there's no small seperable piece to rescue.

Anyway, what's your actual credence that this framing captures most of what's actually going on — that if you applied it consistently you'd predict which legends get rehabilitated and which don't? Give me a number between 0 and 1. Then break it down: (1) is there even a real pattern here or just ad hoc judgments, (2) is "seperability of the core claim" the main structural driver, (3) do non-evidential community motivations play an independant significant role, and (4) is there some other factor I missed that should be in the mix? Rough probabilities for each, one line of reasoning, then how you combine them.
