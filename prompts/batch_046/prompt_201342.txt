As a software engineer working mostly on inference systems, I have been trying to understand the philosophical debate about whether moral status comes in degrees—specifically the comparison between insects, which many people grant partial status, and thermostats, which almost no one includes. The way I naturally model this is as a latent-trait problem: sentience is an unobserved continuous variable, and we observe noisy proxies like behavioral flexibility, neural integration, or homeostatic feedback loops. On that view, assigning moral status becomes a decision-theoretic question about where to place a cutoff that balances false positives (treating a thermostat as a patient) against false negatives (treating an insect as an object). Yet when I read philosophical accounts, the resistance to this framing seems stronger than just complaining about noisy data. Some authors seem to deny that moral status is the kind of property that admits degrees at all, while others treat the insect-thermostat comparison as revealing a category mistake rather than a miscalibrated boundary. Given that graded classification with optimized thresholds is how we handle exactly these kinds of properties in engineering, what is the precise conceptual error in importing that framework here? Is the problem that sentience is not actually a single scalar latent variable, that moral patienthood is conceptually binary even when its underlying basis is continuous, or something else entirely?
