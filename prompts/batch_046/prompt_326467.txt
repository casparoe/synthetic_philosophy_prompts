I'm trying to understand a pattern I keep seeing in debates about whether nations can be guilty for historical wrongs. Philosophers discussing collective guilt—Karl Jaspers among them—often distinguish criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical dimensions, but I keep encountering a situation that does not map neatly onto any of those categories. Consider two concrete cases. First, a country that acquired territory and economic infrastructure through colonial conquest. Its present citizens overwhelmingly reject the ideology of colonialism; they have replaced monuments, rewritten school curricula, and issued formal apologies. Yet the state's borders, its resource distribution, and its international legal standing remain direct products of that conquest. Second, consider descendants who inherit wealth generated by slave labor. They personally hold anti-slavery convictions, have contributed parts of that fortune to related causes, and live in societies that officially condemn slavery. Still, they possess capital that would not exist in that form without the original exploitation.

In both cases, there is a specific tension: the present agents morally repudiate the source of a benefit, bear no direct culpability for the original harm, and often actively work to dismantle the ideology that justified it. At the same time, they continue to occupy structural positions—citizenship, property rights, institutional roles—that are continuous with the harmful system and confer advantages they did not earn. The demand for redress in these situations does not seem to rest on personal guilt, because the agents disavow the wrong; it does not rest purely on political responsibility in the usual sense, because that typically implies some capacity to have acted differently within the system; and it does not rest on metaphysical guilt, since that concerns a kind of universal human solidarity rather than a specific structural inheritance.

What I want to know is whether philosophy or political theory has an established name for this particular configuration—the coincidence of explicit moral repudiation with uninterrupted structural inheritance of benefits, producing an obligation or responsibility that is neither guilty in the personal sense nor merely charitable. If there is a precise term—perhaps something like beneficiary responsibility, structural inheritance, or a more specific variant—I want to understand exactly how it differs from collective guilt, inherited obligation, and political liability. If there is no precise term, I want to know what the nearest concepts are, where the boundaries lie, and whether the absence of a label reflects a genuine conceptual gap or merely a difference in emphasis. I am not asking for a bibliography; I want the carving of the phenomenon itself: what it is, what it is not, and where its edges become blurry—especially in cases where a society practices what might be called institutional amnesia, formally condemning a past it continues to inhabit structurally.
