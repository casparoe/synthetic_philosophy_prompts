After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend last night about whether consciousness is a user illusion in any serious philosophical sense, I tried to write down my exact commitments to see if they form a coherent package, and I am no longer sure they do. Here is where I stand:

(1) Consciousness is literally a user illusion: there is no phenomenal consciousness, only a virtual self-model that represents itself as undergoing qualitative experiences.
(2) The mechanism behind this illusion is best explained in predictive-processing terms: the brain builds a hierarchical model of the agent-environment boundary, and “what it is like” is just a high-level representational format inside that model.
(3) Phenomenal concepts are not metaphysically special; they are indexical tags in the self-model, functionally on a par with “here” or “now.”
(4) The zombie scenario is conceivable, but conceivability is a fallible guide to possibility—especially when the conceiving itself is executed by the very representational architecture being evaluated.
(5) There genuinely is “something it is like” to be in pain, yet this is fully reducible to the informational structure of the self-model’s pain-representation, leaving no residual qualitative residue.
(6) If a thermostat instantiated an identical hierarchical self-model with the same indexical tagging, there would be something it is like to be that thermostat.

For each tension, state its severity and what the cheapest revision would be; then identify which single revision would clean up the most conflicts at once. Rank the tensions rather than treating them all as equal, and be explicit about whether any apparent conflict dissolves on inspection—particularly whether (5) and (1) can genuinely coexist, and whether (6) forces me to abandon (3) or merely shows that (3) has broader implications than I assumed. I want an audit of my position, not a survey of the literature or a lecture on illusionism.
