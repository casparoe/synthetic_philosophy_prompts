everyone in conservative philosophy circles uses Chesterton's fence to justify Burkean slow-change: you don't tear down a fence if you can't see why it was built, so don't mess with old norms unless you understand their hidden function. i want to audit how far this actually holds as an argument, not just as a vibe. map it out for me: what's the fence, what's the hidden purpose, who's the reckless passerby tearing it down, and what's the equivalent of 'not knowing why it's there' in actual policy? then hit the breaks — where does Burke's view of institutions as evolved prescriptions clash with Chesterton's image of a single builder with a hidden plan? and does the analogy smuggle in a false idea that every old rule had a good reason, when a lot of them were just power grabs that stuck? finally, does the conservative conclusion survive once you seperate the epistemic humility part (we might miss side effects) from the builder-intent part? or was the fence doing all the rhetorical work?
