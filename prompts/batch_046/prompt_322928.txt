Focus on three canonical boundary cases: the Trojan War cycle, the Arthurian legend complex, and the Exodus narrative. Rather than asking whether each is "true" or "false," rank them—assigning each a tier from S (strongest recoverable historical substrate with multi-domain convergence) to D (no recoverable substrate independent of the narrative itself)—according to the robustness of their transition from legend to history. For each case, identify in one sentence the specific evidentiary bottleneck: the single weakest link in the chain connecting the textual tradition to an externally anchored past event, whether chronological misalignment, absence of material correlation, or source singularity. Next, evaluate three methodological principles that philosophers and historians invoke in these transitions: (A) minimal narrative disruption—prefer the interpretation requiring the fewest alterations to the received text; (B) independent attestation—require confirmation from politically or culturally disconnected sources; and (C) structural analogy—accept a historical kernel if the narrative's macro-structure mirrors patterns confirmed in analogous, well-documented contexts. Rate each principle on a scale of 1 to 5 for its reliability specifically across these three cases, and in one sentence per principle explain whether its strength derives from epistemic independence or from vulnerability to false positives. Do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific scholars; reason entirely from the conceptual architecture of historical demarcation and the internal logic of each case.
