I'm a retired biologist who used to study sensory pathways, and lately I've been trying to wrap my head around that Mary's room thought experiment and whether physicalism can really survive it. Here are my gut reactions to a few cases, and I can't quite square them.

First, the classic Mary: she knows every physical fact about color vision, then sees red for the first time. I think she definately learns something new — not just a new skill, but a fact that wasn't in her physics textbooks. So I'm inclined to say there's real knowledge gained.

But then take someone born with congenital anosmia — she knows every molecular pathway for smell, then one day her receptors start working and she smells coffee. I feel like she learns something, but it also feels like her brain is just finally receiving the signal her books already described. So is that genuinely new knowledge, or just new sensory data hitting old theory?

Now here's the weird one: there's these sensory-substitution gadgets like BrainPort where blind people navigate space via tongue electrodes. If someone masters all the physics and neuroscience of that system and has used it for years, then gets actual eyes, does she learn anything? My intuition says no — she already had the functional information, just through a different port. But that conflicts with my Mary verdict, doesn't it?

Then there's an AI that runs a perfect simulation of a human brain tasting chocolate, but it's running on silicon with no body. I think that system doesn't learn "what it's like" at all, even if it outputs all the right reports. So knowledge seems tied to having the right kind of biological hardware?

But wait — if hardware matters, then Mary's learning depends on her being made of neurons, which sounds like physicalism after all, just a more complicated version. I'm stuck.

Is there one principle that captures all four — Mary learns, the anosmic researcher learns, the BrainPort user doesn't learn with eyes, and the silicon brain never learns — without being totally ad hoc? Or do I have to drop one? And whatever principle you land on, what does it predict for a case I haven't mentioned: a deaf physicist who knows every physical fact about sound waves, then gets cochlear implants and hears music for the first time? Does she learn a new fact, or just gain access?

Please don't just list philosophers; I want the principle itself, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accomodate.
