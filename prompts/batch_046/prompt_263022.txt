I run an upper-level metaphysics seminar, about 10 grad students, one 90-minute session. Weve been talking about verbal disputes and metaontology — Carnap-style framework choice, the idea that some ontological arguments are really just arguments over which language game to play, and the realist pushback that even verbal disputes leave a real normative residue about how to structure our concepts. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of metaontological deflation, not just understand it from a distance.

Design a structured role-play or deliberation with small real stakes. Invent a concrete institutional case — maybe a "shadow committee" that has de facto authority but no charter — and assign roles with different internal rules for what "exists" means: functional efficacy, explicit charter authorization, and intersubjective recognition. The rules and payoff structure (maybe they have to decide whether they're obligated to attend its meetings) should be arranged so they argue passionately for 20-30 minutes convinced they're disagreeing about reality, then suddenly hit a wall where they realize they're using "exists" with different criteria. Then a debrief that maps that moment onto deflationism, and also onto the counter-argument that framework choice isn't arbitrary because it tracks practical needs.

Be honest about what feature the idea the game can't reproduce — probably the long-term normative commitment to living inside one framework rather than just noticing the ambiguity — and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, like one student breaking character to "solve" the dispute too early or one side dominating. Keep it casual, a typo or two is fine, and don't ask for bibliographies or citations.
