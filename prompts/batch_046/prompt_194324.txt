I am preparing a long-form feature on how nineteenth-century metaphysics of freedom anticipates the structure of contemporary debates about moral responsibility, and I have found myself stalled on a particular conceptual move in Schopenhauer’s prize essay on the freedom of the will, composed in the 1830s. In that work, Schopenhauer draws a sharp distinction between the empirical character—the observable, causally determined pattern of behavior that unfolds in time and is fully explicable by motives and external conditions—and the intelligible character, which he associates with the will as thing-in-itself, lying outside time and not governed by the principle of sufficient reason. He maintains that the familiar feeling of being able to do otherwise is an illusion produced by the empirical character’s expression of the intelligible one: we perceive the choice in the phenomenal realm but remain blind to the underlying necessity that fixes it, rather as one might see a shadow without grasping the object that casts it.

What I am struggling to understand is whether this intelligible character is intended to function as a genuine causa sui—in the precise sense that Galen Strawson later makes central to his Basic Argument—meaning that the agent is ultimately the cause of herself in a way that avoids infinite regress, or whether it is merely uncaused and therefore situated entirely outside the causal chain. More specifically, I want to know whether Schopenhauer’s atemporal ground of action allows him to escape Strawson’s regress, which holds that true moral responsibility requires being the cause of oneself in a manner impossible under both determinism and indeterminism. Does placing the root of action in a realm outside time and causation actually satisfy the demand for self-authorship, or does Strawson’s objection apply even there, since the intelligible character still expresses a nature that the agent never chose and cannot alter? I have been wondering whether interpreting the intelligible character as a root node in a structural causal model—having no causal parents but also not being self-authoring—clarifies Schopenhauer’s position or merely relocates the problem without resolving it.

Could you help me parse the conceptual architecture of this distinction, explain precisely where Schopenhauer’s view intersects with and diverges from Strawson’s argument about the impossibility of being causa sui, and tell me whether the atemporal-causation view genuinely answers the demand for ultimate self-creation or instead concedes Strawson’s point by admitting that the deepest ground of action is something the agent never authored? I am especially interested in whether the analogy between moral authorship and root-node causation holds up under scrutiny, or whether it imports assumptions about causation that break down once we leave the temporal, empirical domain.
