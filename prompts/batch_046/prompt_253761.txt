I'm trying to get my philosophical profile on personal identity straight before a reading group discussion, and I'd appreciate an audit of whether these commitments actually hang together. I take seriously the idea, associated with Dennett, of the self as a center of narrative gravity: not a Cartesian substance but an abstract, functional pattern generated by the brain's storytelling mechanisms, a useful fiction with real predictive and explanatory value. At the same time, I hold that this narrative center possesses a kind of irreducible first-personal authority — not a mystical soul, but an epistemic privilege in knowing one's own mental states that cannot be fully translated into third-person neuroscience without loss. I also believe that moral responsibility tracks the continuity of this narrative pattern rather than biological persistence: if severe trauma or neurodegeneration breaks the narrative thread, the later individual is not the same agent in the morally relevant sense. Yet I want to maintain that the self is, in a deep sense, an illusion — there is no hidden observer inside watching the narrative unfold, and the feeling of unified subjectivity is largely a post-hoc confabulation. Finally, I think that if a digital system instantiated the same narrative dynamics and feedback loops — preserving the strange-loop structure — it would genuinely become a center of narrative gravity with equivalent moral standing, because the pattern is the reality, not the substrate.

There's also a more specific methodological commitment I'm holding onto. I think the narrative self must satisfy what I'd call a diachronic rationality constraint. The story cannot be just any internally consistent fiction; it must maintain a reflective equilibrium across time, binding present beliefs and values to past ones in a way that is not arbitrarily revisable. This seems necessary to distinguish a genuine self from a merely consistent but fragmented stream of consciousness.

So my profile is roughly: (1) narrative-gravity functionalism about the self; (2) irreducible first-person authority without a Cartesian ego; (3) moral responsibility tied to narrative continuity, not biology; (4) the self as illusion or confabulation with no inner observer; (5) substrate-independent replication of the strange loop grants full moral parity; and (6) a diachronic rationality constraint that binds the narrative across time. Do these hang together? I'm particularly worried about whether (2) and (4) are in tension — can there be irreducible first-person access if there is literally no one inside to possess it? — and whether (3) and (5) together generate a paradox of divided responsibility when both an original and a digital copy exist with identical narrative patterns. There's also the question of whether (6) is compatible with (1)'s fictionality: if the self is just a useful fiction, why should it be bound by a normative coherence constraint stronger than pragmatic utility? Please rank the tensions by severity, flag any that only look bad but dissolve on inspection, and say what the cheapest revision would be for each genuine conflict — and which single change would clean up the most overall.
