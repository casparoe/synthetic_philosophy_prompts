Our neighborhood cooperative drafted an elaborate charter regulating the use of shared courtyards and communal gardens, specifying maintenance duties, planting limits, and dispute procedures. However, the charter carries no sanctions; members who ignore the planting limits or neglect maintenance suffer no penalties, and compliance has gradually collapsed. At the same time, an unappointed private patrol—composed of residents with no formal role—has begun enforcing its own interpretation of the rules, removing unauthorized installations, issuing fines, and excluding non-compliant households from shared spaces. They possess the practical means to maintain order, yet they derive no authority from the cooperative's charter or any election. Some neighbors argue that we should formally delegate enforcement powers to this patrol, contending that rules without the capacity to be upheld are empty, and that order must be secured before it can be made legitimate. Others object that granting authority to an unelected body transforms the charter into an instrument of domination, and that legitimacy must precede power if the rules are to be more than coercive commands. I am trying to determine whether a normative framework can genuinely exist without effective enforcement, or whether effective enforcement necessarily corrupts a framework that lacks prior authorization. How ought one to evaluate the relationship between the validity of our rules and the power required to sustain them?
