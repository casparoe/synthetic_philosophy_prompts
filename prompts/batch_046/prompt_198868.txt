Question (30 marks): Explain Karl Popper’s falsifiability criterion for demarcating science from pseudo-science and metaphysics, contrasting it with the logical positivists’ verificationist programme. Cover: (a) the logical asymmetry between verification and falsification of universal statements; (b) the threat of conventionalist stratagems and Popper’s proposed remedy; and (c) whether the criterion solves the demarcation problem or merely relocates it into methodological convention.

Student answer: Popper rejects the logical positivist claim, shared by Carnap and Schlick, that scientific meaning depends on empirical verification. Universal laws cannot be conclusively verified, since observing every instance is impossible. Popper substitutes falsifiability: a statement is scientific if experience could contradict it.

The asymmetry is logical. A universal claim such as all swans are white can be overturned by a single counter-example, yet no finite set of white swans can establish it conclusively. Thus falsification is decisive in a way verification never is.

Popper is concerned with conventionalist stratagems—ad hoc adjustments that shield theories from refutation. He blocks these by insisting that scientists specify in advance which observations would count as refutation and must not alter the rules afterwards. This methodological discipline prevents immunisation.

Nevertheless, the Duhem–Quine thesis shows that theories never confront experience in isolation; they carry auxiliary assumptions. When an experiment conflicts with prediction, scientists can always preserve the core theory by blaming instruments or background conditions. Consequently, falsification is never decisive in practice, and Popper’s criterion collapses into the same conventional decision-making he attacked in the positivists. The demarcation problem is therefore not solved but displaced onto methodological choice.

Finally, Popper agrees with the positivists that metaphysical statements are meaningless, because they are neither verifiable nor falsifiable; he differs only in labelling them non-scientific rather than meaningless.
