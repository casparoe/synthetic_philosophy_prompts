You are an expert in bioethics and the philosophy of personal identity, with experience drafting recommendations for clinical committees. Before you write anything, take a few minutes to brainstorm step by step: what makes an earlier self’s instructions binding on a later person? Is it psychological continuity, shared values, bodily recognition, or something else entirely? Think carefully about where each of these ideas helps and where it breaks down in a real-world case.

I serve on the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized community hospital. I do not have an academic background — I work as an electrician and joined the committee as a community representative — but I have been thinking on my own about whether we stay the same person when our minds change. We now face a concrete case that requires a formal, actionable recommendation.

Case: Robert, 72, a retired carpenter, executed an advance directive in 2018 refusing mechanical ventilation should he ever reach what he called a state in which he no longer knows who he is. He added a handwritten clause of his own wording: “If I reach a point where I do not recognize my own hands as belonging to me — literally do not understand that they are mine — then I am no longer the author of this document. In that case, my earlier self’s instructions should stand without alteration.” Robert now has advanced Lewy body dementia. He sometimes fails to recognize his hands, yet he also expresses fear of dying, asks for his wife by name, and is visibly comforted by familiar music. His adult children are split: one insists the committee honor the 2018 document and withhold ventilation if it becomes necessary; the other argues that Robert’s present emotional responses and attachments show he is still “here,” and that applying the handwritten clause would violate his current interests.

The attending physician has asked our committee for a binding recommendation memo. The memo should be addressed to the committee chair, should state a clear bottom-line recommendation that we could vote on — honor the directive, honor present preferences, or apply a conditional framework — and should present reasoning in plain language accessible to non-philosophers. Crucially, you must include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side; do not dismiss it in a single sentence. You do not need to provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Write as though the committee must act based on reasoning alone, without consulting external literature.
