I've been thinking about that Grand Inquisitor passage in The Brothers Karamazov — you know, where he claims people can't bear freedom and would rather have bread and miracles. There's this angle I haven't seen discussed much in my circles: the Inquisitor treats freedom and happiness as if they're on the same scale, like you could weigh one against the other and come up with a net score for humanity. But what if that's the hidden mistake?

Please argue that the Inquisitor's case collapses not because freedom is sacred or whatever, but because it smuggles in a false assumption of value commensurability. You should develop the idea that freedom (understood as the capacity for moral self-authorship) and happiness (understood as contentment or relief from suffering) operate in incommensurable registers — you can't translate a loss of one into units of the other. Show how this undermines the Inquisitor's offer: he presents the choice as freedom versus bread, but if the values aren't comparable, there is no rational calculus that justifies surrendering one for the other. You might also address the objection that people do in practice choose comfort over liberty, and explain why observed preference doesn't establish commensurability. Make sure you don't just rehearse the standard line about freedom being intrinsic — I want the argument to turn specifically on the conceptual impossibility of the trade-off itself, and maybe touch on whether Dostoevsky recognised this tension or whether it's something the text accidentaly reveals.
