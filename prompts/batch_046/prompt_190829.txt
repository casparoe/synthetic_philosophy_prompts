I have no formal training in economics or history, but I have been thinking on my own about whether famines ought to be understood as natural disasters or as political crimes. Amartya Sen’s argument—that starvation often arises not from an absolute absence of food but from the collapse of people’s entitlement to access it—strikes me as convincing when I consider the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the mid-nineteenth century, and the Great Leap Forward in China. In each case, food existed somewhere in the system, yet specific populations lost the ability to claim it.

My question concerns Thomas Malthus, who died long before any of these events occurred. He held that population growth tends to outstrip the growth of food production, and that nature imposes corrective checks—famine included—when societies fail to restrain reproduction. Suppose Malthus could study Sen’s entitlement analysis of those three historical famines in detail. Suppose, further, that he also had to confront a modern problem with no direct parallel in his own era: in several countries today, centralized algorithms manage national grain reserves, and these systems can redirect surplus food away from populations that lack complete digital identity records. The redirection is not caused by crop failure or global shortage; it occurs because logistical optimization assigns those groups lower priority. The result is localized starvation amid overall abundance—an entitlement failure produced by code and bureaucratic classification rather than by any natural scarcity.

How do you think Malthus would respond? Would he treat Sen’s framework as merely describing the institutional mechanism through which his own deeper law—population pressing against available means—ultimately manifests? Or, seeing that the algorithmic case involves no actual food scarcity and no natural check at all, would he be compelled to abandon the claim that famine is fundamentally a natural corrective? More specifically, if he were asked to revise his theory in light of both Sen’s historical cases and this algorithmic distribution failure, which elements of his original view—about the relationship between population, subsistence, and human agency—would he preserve, and which would he reject? Please do not simply summarize the two thinkers; I am asking you to project Malthus’s reasoning onto issues he never lived to see, including the algorithmic problem I have described.
