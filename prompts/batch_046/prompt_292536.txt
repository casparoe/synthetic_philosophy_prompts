I'm working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments for a seminar on moral psychology, and my study group has constructed an argument that seems to drive his account of sympathy and the impartial spectator into a contradiction. Here is the chain we built:

(1) For Smith, moral approval is nothing but the sentiment of sympathy we feel when we imaginatively enter into the motives of an agent. (2) Sympathy requires that we place ourselves in the agent's circumstances and share their feelings, which presupposes some prior experiential or analogical grasp of those circumstances. (3) The impartial spectator is formed not by direct sympathy with the agent, but by seeing ourselves as others see us—an internalized social perspective that corrects our partial sentiments. (4) But internalizing "how others see us" requires that there actually be others whose views we can absorb; without a society to provide that external mirror, there is no raw material from which to construct the spectator. (5) Therefore, a person raised in complete isolation—without language, social interaction, or any human contact—would lack an impartial spectator entirely and, by (1), would be incapable of moral judgment altogether. (6) Now suppose such an isolated individual later encounters another person and feels an immediate, non-instrumental aversion to harming them. (7) By (1) through (5), this aversion cannot count as a moral judgment, since there is no spectator present to approve or disapprove; it must be classified as a bare, non-moral instinct, like a reflex. (8) Yet Smith repeatedly insists that the fundamental moral sentiments—resentment at injury, approval of benevolence, and the sense of justice—are innate and universal features of human nature, not artifacts of socialization. (9) So Smith is committed both to the claim that moral judgment necessarily requires a socially constructed spectator and to the claim that moral sentiments arise universally, even in the absence of society. (10) These commitments are incompatible: if the spectator is necessary for morality, then morality is not universal; if morality is universal, then the spectator is redundant. (11) Hence Smith's view proves that either the unsocialized person has no morality (contradicting his universalism) or the impartial spectator does no essential work (contradicting his social psychology).

We all agree that one of these steps smuggles in a false assumption—perhaps about whether sympathy can operate without prior social experience, or about whether the spectator is required for every judgment rather than only for correction—but we cannot settle which one. I am most reluctant to surrender (8), because it preserves Smith's humanist commitment to cross-cultural moral commonality, but I want to understand precisely what I must pay to keep it. Which step should be rejected, why did it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost for the coherence of Smith's system?
