I am revising the syllabus for my graduate epistemology seminar and want to move past the standard rehearsal of arguments for and against testimonial justification. Before I assign further readings, I need the full specification of what an adequate theory must deliver—specifically, the conditions under which a hearer’s belief formed on the basis of a speaker’s assertion counts as epistemically justified, and whether that justification can ever be identical to knowledge. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of named authors; I want the desiderata themselves, with the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind each.

Please write the list of requirements that any theory of testimonial justification must meet to count as adequate for explaining both ordinary and somewhat marginal cases of belief-formation through others’ words. The list must cover: (i) the paradigmatic face-to-face case in which a competent, sincere speaker asserts a true proposition to an attentive hearer; (ii) the unreliable-speaker case, in which the speaker generally lacks credibility but happens to be correct on this occasion; (iii) the case of what I will call “displaced testimony”—an unedited, transparent audio recording of a now-deceased speaker, accessed by a listener who has no live interaction, no possibility of follow-up questioning, and no independent evidential access to the subject matter, yet who fully grasps the speaker’s language and context; (iv) collective or overlapping testimony, in which several speakers each provide partial support for the same proposition without any single one being individually sufficient; and (v) consistency with broader constraints on justification and knowledge, including the possibility of epistemic defeat and the non-identity of justification with knowledge.

For each requirement, explain why it is genuinely required rather than a prejudice of one camp, and flag whether it is negotiable or non-negotiable. In particular, respect the distinction between the speaker’s epistemic position and the hearer’s—without collapsing them—and the distinction between testimonial justification and testimonial knowledge. Then identify which single requirement, in your judgment, does the most damage to the most popular theories, whether reductionist, anti-reductionist, or hybrid. Also indicate whether any pair or subset of your desiderata appears jointly unsatisfiable, since that tension is itself a result I want to examine. Finally, briefly score the three main contender-types—reductionism, anti-reductionism, and a moderate hybrid or dualist view—against the list, but keep the conceptual weight on the criteria rather than on the scorecard.

Please take a committed position throughout: do not merely lay out options and leave me to choose. State definitively which desiderata are non-negotiable, which are camp-specific preferences disguised as demands, and which contender-type best survives your list, with explicit reasoning. I need this framing for next week’s discussion, so the sharper the boundary between genuine constraints and partisan demands, the better.
