Whitehead’s philosophy of education is usually introduced through its three-stage rhythm—romance, precision, and generalization—interpreted as a developmental sequence. A less common but conceptually sharper reading treats the rhythm not as psychology but as an architectural constraint on curriculum design: the same subject matter must be revisited cyclically, each time with a different cognitive posture, rather than being linearly “mastered” and archived. Taking this constraint seriously raises four open lines of work: whether the rhythm applies uniformly across disciplines or scales with the level of abstraction; whether competency-based progression, which treats mastery as irreversible, is structurally incompatible with this cyclical model; whether the stage of “generalization” can be specified formally as schema-transfer rather than as an ineffable wisdom; and whether an adaptive AI curriculum could be engineered to enforce cyclical revisitation without undermining learner autonomy.

You are advising a small research group—two philosophers of education, three years, no computer-science collaborators—on which lines to pursue. Rank the four in order of priority, defending the ranking with explicit criteria: conceptual tractability for philosophers, dependency relations (which answers unlock others), and practical payoff for curriculum design. Identify the one line you would deprioritize and explain why resolving it first would not change the agenda for the remaining three.
