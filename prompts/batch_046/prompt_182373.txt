You are a professional moral and political philosopher with particular expertise in institutional ethics, comparative conceptions of corruption, and the logic of universalist versus contextualist norms. Before you begin your analysis, please take a few moments to think carefully and to brainstorm the distinct philosophical commitments that might be embedded in institutional design choices—especially commitments about transparency, obligation, moral particularism, and the boundaries of the public versus private sphere. Approach this as an expert reconstructing an implicit theory rather than as a commentator endorsing or condemning the practice.

Consider the following institutional arrangement. A multinational development agency maintains two separate compliance frameworks that are never applied to the same domain. Framework I governs every interaction with state officials in any jurisdiction: it prohibits gifts exceeding a nominal value, mandates pre-registration of any social meal shared with a regulator, and treats an undisclosed kinship or long-standing social tie between a procurement officer and a vendor as an automatic conflict of interest that voids the contract. Framework II governs internal hiring and subcontracting within the agency’s field offices in specific regions: it permits senior staff to nominate relatives, clan members, or long-standing community partners for positions and contracts, provided the relationship is recorded in a local social-obligation ledger and the nominee satisfies baseline competency criteria. The agency justifies Framework II by arguing that in these contexts reciprocity and kinship loyalty are socially expected, and that formal transparency neutralizes ethical risk; Framework I, by contrast, is presented as reflecting an unconditional prohibition on private influence over public power.

What theory of corruption, or of the conditions under which preferential treatment becomes wrongful, does this bifurcated design presuppose? Does it presuppose that corruption is essentially a hidden quid pro quo—so that disclosure transforms an otherwise suspect preference into a permissible one—or does it presuppose something about the domain-specific nature of moral obligation? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory: does the agency treat corruption as a property of acts, of institutional roles, or of social structures? Can the two frameworks be reconciled within a single universalist account—say, by distinguishing influence over sovereign authority from internal organizational reciprocity—or does their coexistence commit the agency to a form of moral particularism about favoritism? Be precise about what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely accommodates, and identify points where the design is philosophically underdetermined, such that rival theories of corruption or of transparency would license different adjustments. Finally, if the agency were to replace its underlying view with a straightforward universalist prohibition on all preferential treatment based on social proximity, regardless of disclosure or cultural context, what concrete changes would Framework II have to undergo, and what normative costs, if any, would that replacement entail?
