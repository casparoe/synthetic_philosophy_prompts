The philosophy-of-medicine dispute between biological naturalism—roughly, the view that health is statistically normal functional ability contributing to survival and reproduction, with dysfunction defined relative to species-typical design—and harmful-dysfunction normativism—roughly, the view that disease requires both an evolutionary malfunction and a judgment of harmfulness—has remained largely static for decades despite numerous hybrid proposals. I want you to diagnose why, focusing specifically on conditions of "allostatic overload": cases in which stress-regulatory, immune, and metabolic systems are operating precisely within their evolved parameters and therefore count as functionally healthy by naturalist lights, yet produce severe, sustained harm because the modern ecological context differs radically from the environment of evolutionary adaptation. Given that naturalists can classify these as healthy adaptive responses and normativists can classify them as diseases only by importing a normative baseline about appropriate ecological fit, does the stall reflect a hidden empirical disagreement about whether evolutionary function must be indexed to ancestral versus present environments; a substantive conceptual disagreement about whether health is an intrinsic property of an organism or a relational property between organism and environment; a verbal disagreement about the extension of terms like "function" and "harm"; or evidence that the framing itself is ill-posed? Analyze the structure using the actual commitments of each camp regarding these overload states, and specify what—if anything—would count as progress: a new conceptual distinction, a formal result, or a reconceptualization of evolutionary baseline. Then tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what specific argument or finding would change your diagnosis.
