Here is the argument:

(1) The classic St. Petersburg paradox arises because a game with unbounded linear monetary payoffs yields an infinite expected value, yet no rational agent should pay an infinite entry fee.

(2) The standard resolution is that rational utility is bounded or concave: each additional unit of the good is worth less than the previous one, so expected utility remains finite.

(3) The value of scientific knowledge, however, does not exhibit this concavity. Knowing the chemical structure of an obscure mineral is not necessarily less valuable than knowing whether complex life exists elsewhere in the solar system; there is no principled ceiling on how valuable a correct fundamental theory could be.

(4) Therefore, the epistemic utility of a potential revolutionary scientific discovery is linear and unbounded, unlike monetary utility.

(5) Any research proposal that carries a non-zero probability of yielding such a discovery therefore has infinite expected epistemic utility.

(6) If rational choice requires maximizing expected utility, a funding agency should always select the speculative proposal over the safe incremental one, regardless of the base rate of success or the opportunity cost of resources.

(7) Yet funding agencies routinely and justifiably reject many speculative proposals in favor of incremental ones.

(8) So either the bounded-utility resolution of St. Petersburg is incoherent, or scientific funding is systematically irrational.
