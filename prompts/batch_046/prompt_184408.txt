I'm preparing a feature on why the philosophical debate over transformative experience — the kind L.A. Paul made central, where you choose to become something you cannot presently imagine being — hasn't moved in years despite widespread agreement on the examples. The same three moves recur: revealed-preference reconstructions, mental-simulation defenses, and phenomenological objections that neither camp addresses. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about rational choice but about whether first-person authority can extend diachronically across a break in psychological continuity, and that both sides are using knowing what it is like to mean different things: one as an epistemic deficit, one as a metaphysical gap. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden empirical question — perhaps from recent work on constructive episodic simulation and predictive processing — that is actually driving the intuition that one cannot decide rationally? What kind of disagreement is this, and given your answer, what conceptual distinction or empirical result would actually count as progress rather than another round of the same exchange?
