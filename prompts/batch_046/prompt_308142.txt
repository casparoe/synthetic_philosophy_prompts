In my epistemology course, two classmates debated conciliationism versus steadfastness over a case they devised: two equally trained paleographers examining the same ambiguous manuscript fragment, one reading the script as supporting Theory X and the other as supporting Theory Y, with both recognizing each other as peers.

Maya argued for steadfastness: each has first-order access to the analogies and visual patterns grounding her own reading. Since neither detects an internal error, the bare fact of disagreement gives her no specific reason to think her own reasoning failed rather than her peer's. Lowering confidence treats disagreement itself as higher-order evidence that should always override first-order judgment, which seems epistemically arbitrary.

Jonas argued for conciliationism: if both share the same total evidence—including mutual peer recognition—symmetry requires adjusting confidence downward. To remain steadfast is to privilege one's own cognitive process in a way one wouldn't privilege the peer's, which is unjustified. He added that peer disagreement is itself first-order evidence about the reliability of one's processes in this domain.

Maya countered that Jonas's symmetry assumes a shared calibration scale that doesn't exist in interpretive disciplines. The options are discrete and incompatible; "splitting the difference" is incoherent because one cannot be half-confident in a specific script reading. At most, both should suspend judgment, but that applies symmetrically—so it doesn't license Jonas's specific downward revision of only his own belief.

Jonas conceded that without quantitative measures, conciliation might mean mutual suspension rather than averaging credences. But he asked whether Maya's view makes peer recognition epistemically empty: if every dispute ends in steadfastness, then knowing someone is a peer never rationally affects one's beliefs.

Please adjudicate each step—whose point stands, what is missing, and whether either view survives once we account for discrete belief options, the absence of an independent calibration scale, and the asymmetry of access to one's own reasoning traces. Also indicate whether the peer/steadfast binary is adequate for interpretive disagreements of this kind, or whether a different framing is needed.
