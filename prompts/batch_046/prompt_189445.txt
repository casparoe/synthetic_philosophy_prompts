I am taking an undergraduate course in historical sociology and have become stuck on the causality debate surrounding Weber's argument about Protestantism and capitalism. At a recent seminar, two fellow students — neither of whom is me — had a sharp exchange that I have been unable to resolve. I am reporting it here exactly as I heard it, without taking sides.

The first student, Clara, opened with four moves. (1) She said Weber's thesis commits a category error: treating a collective value-orientation — what she described as an ascetic world-view — as though it possessed efficient causal powers like a physical mechanism. Values, she insisted, are hermeneutic interpretations people offer for actions, not forces that independently push economic systems in one direction or another; to explain capitalism's rise by citing such an orientation is therefore to confuse narrative decoration with genuine explanation. (2) Marcus replied that Clara was misreading Weber's notion of elective affinity, which was never intended as an efficient-causal law but rather as a meaningful-adequacy relation: the ethic made specific capitalist behaviours — systematic reinvestment, contractual bookkeeping, and the separation of household from enterprise — appear morally obligatory rather than merely profitable, thereby altering the long-run selection environment for those behaviours. (3) Clara countered that even if one grants this selection framing, Marcus had not shown that values do any independent causal work. The legal protection of private contracts, access to colonial markets, and shifts in commodity prices already explain institutional expansion; the ethic is therefore at best a rationalising epiphenomenon — people invoke it to justify choices they would have made anyway for material gain. For values to matter causally, Marcus would need to defend a counterfactual claim that capitalism would not have taken root without the ethic, and modern economic historians largely reject that counterfactual. (4) Marcus responded that Clara's demand for an isolable counterfactual treats cultural causes like chemical reagents, which is inappropriate for path-dependent processes. He proposed instead that the ethic operated as a stabilising feedback loop: once initial capitalist practices emerged, the doctrine of predestination interpreted economic success as a sign of divine election, which lowered risk-aversion and locked in patterns of reinvestment rather than consumption. Without that interpretive loop, Marcus argued, identical material conditions might have produced speculative consumption or patrimonial redistribution rather than sustained accumulation. Clara interrupted to say this made the ethic a post-hoc psychological comfort, not a cause of origin; Marcus insisted that in path-dependent systems origins and stabilisation cannot be cleanly separated, so the feedback loop is genuinely causal even if it is not an originating spark.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of Clara's and Marcus's four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which specific point, and identify what both parties are missing — particularly whether there is a defensible middle position between treating values as efficient causes and treating them as mere epiphenomena. In doing so, please separate what is relatively settled in the philosophy of social science from what remains genuinely contested among scholars who specialise in this area, and do not present any contested interpretive claim as though it were the consensus view. Finally, please write your response in British English.
