Here is an argument about panpsychism and the combination problem that seems to drive me to a conclusion I want to avoid — that the view is self-defeating. (1) If micro-experiential properties combine into macro-experience, they must do so via some relation R, such as causal integration, spatial contiguity, or functional organization. (2) Whatever R is, it is structurally definable, so any scattered set of micro-subjects arranged with the same R-structure should combine just as well as the ones inside my skull. (3) So if R is sufficient, there exist "scattered subjects" — conscious entities made of, say, parts of my retina and distant particles arranged isomorphically to a brain's causal graph. (4) That is absurd; consciousness does not arise for arbitrary spatially dispersed sets merely because they mirror an integrated system. (5) So the panpsychist must restrict R with an extra boundary condition: combination occurs only within a unified organism or spatiotemporally continuous whole. (6) But apply that restriction inward: within my brain there are thousands of continuous subsystems (individual neurons, cortical columns, hemispheres) that each satisfy R plus the boundary condition. (7) Hence I am not one conscious subject but a host of overlapping ones, which contradicts the experienced unity of consciousness. (8) Therefore panpsychism either tolerates scattered subjects or destroys internal unity; either way it fails.

I would prefer to keep both (4) and (7), which forces me to reject (1) or (5). Where is the weakest step? Is there a principled restriction on R that avoids internal multiplicity without being ad hoc, or does the argument slip between structural and substantive senses of combination? Please say which premise you think is actually false, explain why it looked plausible, and identify where your own reasoning is most likely to fail — especially whether a panpsychist could reject (2) by making R essentially non-structural, and what that costs.
