1. i check my tax returns twice, find the same number, and trust the result. that seems definately rational.
2. i'm in a debate and realize i'm motivated reasoning. i then conclude "i'm not biased"—and i feel like i shouldn't trust that conclusion, even though the reasoning looks identical to case 1.
3. there's an exact digital copy of me running on a server with it's same weights and history. she concludes i'm rational. i wouldn't defer to thier conclusion about my own rationality, which feels right, but i can't say why it's different from case 1 where i defer to my past self.
4. my neurologist tells me i have a specific bias. now i distrust my self-assessments in that domain. that's rational, but it's also me using my reasoning to recieve her authority—so there's a loop, but it doesn't feel vicious like case 2.
5. here's the tension: i think every rational agent should have a default policy of self-trust, otherwise you never act. but i also think an agent can't justify that policy without circularity. so i hold both that self-trust is mandatory and that it's unjustifiable. those seem to clash.

what principle, if any, captures these? does it force me to drop the idea that self-trust needs justification, or the idea that it's mandatory? and what about an AI that can read it's own source code—should it treat it's self-readout as case 1 or case 3?
