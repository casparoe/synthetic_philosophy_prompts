Question: Why do promises bind? Contrast the expectation-harm account with the normative-powers account, and apply both to a case where someone promises an AI scheduling assistant they'll finish a task by 5pm but then gets a genuinely better opportunity.

Student answer:
Promises create obligations because they alter what people can legitimately expect. The expectation-harm view says breaking a promise wrongs the promisee by violating an expectation that the promisor deliberately raised. In the AI case, this view might seem weak because the assistant lacks feelings, but actually the harm is functional: the system planned around the commitment, so breaking it disrupts coordination.

The normative-powers view says promising is an exercise of practical authority through which the speaker binds themselves. This doesn't depend on the promisee relying or feeling hurt. So with the AI assistant, the obligation still exists because the speaker invoked the power, even though the AI can't be disappointed.

But there's an important limit: on the normative-powers account, the speaker retains the authority to release themselves if the burden becomes too great, since they are the source of the power. Meanwhile, the expectation-harm account only applies when the promise was made with serious intent; if it was just a casual remark, no legitimate expectation was formed, so there's nothing to violate.

Overall, promises bind when either legitimate expectations are formed or normative powers are properly exercised, but both conditions require some baseline seriousness.
