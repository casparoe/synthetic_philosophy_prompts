Consider the judgments rendered by a philosopher of practical reason regarding whether a decision architecture counts as rationally defensible under conditions of bounded computation or information. Her track record is as follows.

She judges rational the case of a shopper who stops searching for pasta once she finds a brand meeting her price and ingredient thresholds, ignoring possibly superior options further down the aisle.

She judges rational the case of doctors in a mass-casualty event with two hundred patients and three staff, who apply a four-color triage heuristic rather than computing individual survival probabilities.

She judges rational the case of a climate-policy model that deliberately omits a second-order feedback loop because running it would miss the legislative deadline, accepting wider confidence intervals.

She judges rational the case of a navigation system that returns a near-optimal route in two hundred milliseconds rather than computing the globally optimal route over forty seconds, because the driver must depart immediately.

She judges rational the case of a university admissions office that fills its class using a weighted-score cutoff and stops evaluating remaining files, even though later applications contain stronger candidates.

She has not yet evaluated this architecture. A mortgage-approval AI operates under a hard-coded fifty-millisecond computation limit per applicant. The limit was not chosen through any analysis of error costs, stakeholder stakes, or available hardware; it was inherited unchanged from a legacy batch-processing system. Within that window, the system deploys a shallow linear classifier that meets minimal regulatory fairness thresholds but misses complex fraud patterns that a two-second computation would detect. Here, false approvals cost the lender millions, while false denials are easily appealed and corrected. The designer defends the architecture as an instance of bounded rationality.

Predict her verdict—whether she will deem the architecture rationally defensible. In your answer, identify at least two competing rules or principles that are jointly consistent with her five prior judgments but diverge on this case, and for each state what it predicts here. Identify the closest analog among her prior cases. Give your best-guess verdict with the reasoning that favors one interpretation, and specify one further hypothetical case—distinct in kind from the six listed—that would definitively distinguish the competing rules. Finally, state what general principle she would be implicitly adopting with each possible choice in the new case.
