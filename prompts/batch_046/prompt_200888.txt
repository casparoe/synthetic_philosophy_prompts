I'm working on a long-form feature about whether classic philosophy-of-mind puzzles retain their force once cognition is no longer treated as skull-bound. Daniel Dennett's brain-transplant thought experiment — in which a philosopher's brain is separated from his body, sustained in a vat, and connected by radio links to the original body left behind — is usually read as showing that our intuitive answer to the location question can come apart from bodily position. I want to push it into less-charted territory: suppose the radio links, the vat's life-support apparatus, and eventually external devices are not mere prosthetic replacements but continuous parts of a single cognitive system. Under that distributed-cognition reconstruction, does the scenario still expose a Cartesian intuition that personal identity carries a unique spatial locus, or does it dissolve into the different claim that location was never univocal to begin with?

Give me your credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that this reconstructed puzzle genuinely undermines the thesis that personal identity has a singular, determinate spatial location, as opposed to merely showing that we can be epistemically uncertain about it. Decompose the reasoning into three parts: first, the probability that the original scenario targets a substantive metaphysical intuition about embodiment rather than a linguistic ambiguity in how the first-person pronoun refers; second, the probability that extending the system to include the vat, the links, and external networks preserves the identity conditions that make the location question philosophically interesting, rather than dissolving them entirely; third, the probability that such an extended system still supports exactly one determinately located subject, versus forcing plural, indeterminate, or distributed location. Show how these combine into your overall number, identify which sub-credence is doing the most work, and indicate what kind of consideration — conceptual, empirical, or phenomenological — would shift your estimate the most.

Please separate what is broadly settled in philosophy of mind and cognitive science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any particular reading of Dennett's intentions or of extended-mind theory as if it were the consensus view.
