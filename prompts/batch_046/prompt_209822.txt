I'm preparing a unit for an advanced undergraduate course in jurisprudence, and I'd like to use Robert Bolt's play A Man for All Seasons — and, secondarily, its well-known film adaptation — as a case study rather than a mere illustration. The play dramatizes Thomas More's refusal to sign the Act of Succession and his eventual execution for silence rather than assent, and it is usually read as a straightforward defense of conscience over state command. I want a philosophical reading that takes the Hart–Fuller debate seriously — legal positivism's separation of law and morality, and Fuller's counter-claim that law possesses an "inner morality" constituted by procedural principles such as publicity, clarity, and the prohibition of demanding the impossible — but that also presses on a point the standard literature tends to gloss over: the problem of silent or inarticulate dissent.

Specifically, More refuses not only to sign but to state his reasons, treating his moral knowledge as something that need not, or perhaps cannot, be translated into the public language of legal justification. Read the play through this tension. Where does More's position map onto Fuller's eight principles — for instance, does his demand that law be publicly knowable and non-arbitrary align with Fuller's view, or does his own refusal to speak undermine the publicity Fuller's inner morality requires? Where does Hart's distinction between being legally obliged and being morally obligated help us understand More's acceptance of the court's authority even as he rejects its verdict? And where does the play resist both frameworks: does More's silence suggest that natural law can be genuinely private and incommunicable, thereby breaking Fuller's assumption that law's moral force is tied to rational accessibility, or does it expose a gap in Hart's account of the "internal point of view" when that point of view is held by someone who accepts the system's legitimacy precisely by refusing to validate its particular commands?

Please don't reduce the drama to a philosophical allegory: attend to specific scenes — the trial, the confrontation with Cromwell, the moment More chooses execution over explanation — and say where the work complicates the theory rather than confirming it. I want the reading to end with a claim about what Bolt's dramatization adds to the jurisprudential debate that a standard argument about separation or inner morality cannot capture on its own: perhaps something about the performative cost of legal silence, or about the limits of procedural morality when conscience claims authority without offering reasons. Keep the analysis faithful in both directions, and avoid any request for citations, bibliographies, or further reading lists — I'm writing this for students who won't have access to external sources.
