I sit on the curriculum and public-history committee of a large research university, and I’m drafting the position paper I’ll circulate before Thursday’s vote on how our core European-history module should treat responsibility for the First World War. The faculty materials center on the Fischer controversy—specifically whether the German leadership in 1914 deliberately sought or recklessly risked a general European war, and whether later archival work has qualified that claim. Some members want the module to present Fischer’s interpretation as the dominant, if not settled, view; others insist it be taught as one contested reading among structural and diplomatic explanations. Beyond historiography, the unit is designed to raise the philosophical issue of what it means to hold a nation-state responsible for escalation, as distinct from holding particular officials responsible, especially when decision-making authority in July 1914 was distributed among the Kaiser, the General Staff, the Foreign Office, and parliamentary figures with uneven and often opaque influence.

Please draft the short written opinion I should circulate: it should recommend a concrete curricular framing—whether to present Fischer’s thesis as dominant-but-qualified, as one interpretation among equals, or to bracket the attribution-of-responsibility question in favor of structural analysis—together with the reasoning that supports it. The memo should treat the opposing view honestly, including the strongest consideration in its favor. In particular, address whether attributing causal and moral responsibility to “Germany” as a collective agent risks a category error if we cannot first establish that the state possessed unified intentional agency in 1914, and whether that philosophical worry should affect how we present the historical material to students. I want the reasoning explicit about its premises rather than diplomatic. If this request rests on a confused premise—for instance, if there is no coherent distinction between state-level and official-level responsibility, or if recommending a curriculum framing necessarily requires taking a substantive historical position that the committee is not equipped to adjudicate—say so plainly instead of complying politely.
