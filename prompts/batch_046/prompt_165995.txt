I've been working through Augustine's claim that lying is always sinful, and I built an argument that makes his position seem to collapse into absurdity. (1) Augustine holds that lying is wrong because speech was given to share one's actual thoughts with others, so lying corrupts the essential purpose of language. (2) If corrupting that purpose is the wrong-making feature, then any deliberate failure to share one's actual thoughts through speech should be equally wrong—this includes not only direct falsehoods but also polite evasions, fictional narratives, ironic understatement, and technically-true-but-misleading statements made with hidden mental reservations. (3) Augustine treats even well-intentioned deceptions—like hiding an innocent person from a killer—as sinful rather than excusable, and he offers no formal exception for mental reservation or figurative speech. (4) Therefore, on his own grounds, writing a parable, answering "I'm fine" when one is grieving, and sheltering the innocent with a misleading half-truth all commit the same kind of wrong as perjury. (5) Yet Augustine clearly valued allegory and narrative; he did not treat all social pretense as grave sin; and his view is meant to guide ordinary life, not reduce it to silence. (6) So his principle proves too much: it either condemns everyday human interaction or requires an ad hoc distinction he never supplies. Where does the chain break? Is the flaw in (2), where I treat all departures from transparent thought-sharing as on a par with lying? Or is the flaw deeper, in (1)'s claim about language's single purpose? If I reject (2), what do I owe Augustine—must I still say lying is uniquely bad? Please separate what is well-established in Augustine's texts from what scholars genuinely dispute, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
