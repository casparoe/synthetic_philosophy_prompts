I've been arguing with my studdy group about the liar paradox — you know, that classic "this sentence is false" loop — and honestly we're going in circles. One friend keeps insisting it's just a proof that natural language needs a strict heirarchy of object-language and meta-language, like the Tarskian approach. Another friend thinks that's total overkill and we should just bite the bullet: accept that some sentences are both true and false, or say the liar doesn't actually express a proposition at all. Then there's this third view floating around that "truth" is only a disquotational tool, so the paradox is just a grammatical glitch with no metaphysical weight. My working theory is that none of them are really fighting about logic; they're fighting about what an explanation has to do. Some people need a formal mechanism that blocks self-reference cleanly, others need to preserve the intuition that every well-formed sentence carries exactly one stable value, and the deflationists think both camps are treating truth like a mysterious property. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden formal disagreement — maybe about fixed-points in self-referential arithmetic, or about which sentences even count as well-formed — that's actually driving the bus?

What I'm after isn't a survey of interpretations but a real diagnosis: are we dealing with verbal disagreement, with different standards of evidence, with hidden metaphysical commitments about propositions, or with the sides answering different questions under the same name? And given whatever you think the stall is, what would actually count as progress? Could a new theorem about semantic closure end it, or is the problem just that we're asking language to do something it was never designed for? Be honest about whether anything should be expected to resolve this, and say why.
