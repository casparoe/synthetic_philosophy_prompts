I am a retired experimental physicist who, in retirement, have begun reading philosophy of science with the seriousness I once reserved for calibrating interferometers. I want you to take the philosophical debate about the underdetermination of theory by evidence — specifically the Duhem-Quine thesis and its critics — and restate it entirely in the vocabulary of laboratory practice: calibration curves, auxiliary hypotheses rendered as control conditions, instrumental noise, model selection criteria (parsimony, predictive range, robustness to perturbation), replication protocols, and the distinction between global recalibration of an apparatus and local parameter adjustment.

For each component — Duhem's claim that an experiment tests a whole theoretical web rather than an isolated hypothesis; Quine's stronger claim that empirically equivalent global theories are always possible; the standard responses involving simplicity, novel prediction, and the distinction between local and global underdetermination — translate it as precisely as you can without smuggling in philosophical assumptions disguised as scientific ones.

Then provide the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto actual scientific decision-making (e.g., adjusting a control variable versus overhauling a theoretical framework)? Which parts require distortion — for instance, does the logical possibility of globally equivalent but conceptually distinct theories correspond to anything in how experimentalists actually adjudicate between models, or is it a formal artifact with no laboratory counterpart? And what, if anything, cannot be expressed at all in the vocabulary of measurement and calibration?

Finally, assess the residue: does the fact that some philosophical distinctions vanish in scientific vocabulary reveal a limitation of the scientist's framework (it cannot capture logical structure), or does it suggest that the philosophical debate was inflating a practical problem of model selection into an epistemological crisis? I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between local and global underdetermination survives translation into the difference between tweaking a single instrument parameter and rebuilding an entire measurement protocol — and whether that translation illuminates or obscures the original philosophical point.
