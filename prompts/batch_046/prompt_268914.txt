I'm putting together teaching materials for a graduate seminar on historical reasoning in international relations, and my students keep reaching for the same three reference crises—the 1938 Munich Agreement, the Vietnam escalation, and the Thucydides trap—as if they were calibrated diagnostic instruments rather than contested rhetorical frames. To help them see the machinery behind this habit, I'd like a deadpan parody of an official U.S. government Request for Proposals soliciting vendors to build and maintain a "Historical Analogy Validation Engine" (HAVE), a proposed decision-support system for the foreign-policy bureaucracy. The document should read exactly like flat, optimistic federal procurement prose—complete with sections on "Mandatory Analogical Coverage," "Disanalogy Suppression Protocols," "Vendor Qualifications," and an invented "Analogical Fidelity Index" that scores current crises against the three certified reference events. The satire should exaggerate real practices in analogical reasoning—stripping temporal and structural context, forcing isomorphism between unlike cases, treating historical comparison as predictive algorithm, and suppressing countervailing detail—without ever stepping out of character to state the objections explicitly. Invent a bureaucratic taxonomy (for example, a "Munich Coefficient" measuring appeasement risk, a "Vietnam Quagmire Probability Score," and a "Thucydides Power-Transition Compliance Rating") and embed the critique in the logic of the specifications themselves. Keep it legible to a reader with no IR background but rewarding to anyone who knows why Munich, Vietnam, and Thucydides comparisons are usually misleading. Aim for about 700 words, sustained deadpan irony, no named living persons, and no requests for citations, bibliographies, or further reading. The target is not a particular scholar but the institutional practice of using these analogies as policy shortcuts.
