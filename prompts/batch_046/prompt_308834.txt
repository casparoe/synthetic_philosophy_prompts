I'm preparing material for a philosophy-of-mind seminar and have become preoccupied with the apparent stagnation in debates over global workspace theory's explanatory reach. For years, defenders of broadcasting architectures have maintained that making information globally available to systems for report, memory, and decision-making captures the core of conscious experience, or at least exhausts what a cognitive science of consciousness needs to account for. Their critics, typically operating with a phenomenal-access distinction, counter that widespread availability explains only how information becomes accessible to cognition, not why there is any subjective feel or qualitative character accompanying it; they invoke dissociations—such as reported absence of visual experience alongside preserved behavioral discrimination—to argue that broadcasting and phenomenology can diverge. Both sides possess well-rehearsed replies: proponents stress the empirical difficulty of isolating pure phenomenology independent of access conditions, while critics insist that functional integration, however complex, never closes the explanatory gap to qualitative presence. The result is a cyclical exchange in which the same moves are replayed without obvious convergence.

Rather than asking you to take a side, I want a structural diagnosis of why this disagreement persists and what kind of disagreement it is. Is it primarily substantive—hinging on empirical claims about whether broadcasting mechanisms always co-occur with qualitative states—or is it partly verbal, driven by competing stipulations about whether the term consciousness should be tied to cognitive accessibility or to intrinsic phenomenal properties? Might the two camps be answering different questions—one about the functional architecture of cognition, the other about the ontological status of subjective experience—while treating them as rivals over a single phenomenon? I am also interested in whether a hidden disagreement about explanatory standards is doing the work: for instance, whether one side demands mechanistic decomposition of neural dynamics while the other requires phenomenological fidelity, with neither recognizing that they apply incompatible criteria of success. Finally, please indicate whether this stalemate suggests the question is ill-posed, and specify what development—an empirical finding, a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or a change of framing—would genuinely count as progress given your diagnosis. Ground your analysis in what each position actually asserts about broadcasting, availability, and qualitative character, and present your diagnosis as a hypothesis rather than a settled verdict.
