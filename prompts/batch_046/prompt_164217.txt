In my philosophy of physics study group, two people got stuck arguing about Thomson's lamp. Jordan said the paradox dissolves once you see that asking whether the lamp is on at exactly two minutes assumes there is a final toggle that determines the state, but the sequence has no last term; so the state at the limit point is merely whatever continuous extension one assigns, not a logical contradiction. Riley replied that this misses the real force: the lamp is deterministic, so its state at any time should be fixed by its prior states via the toggle rule. At the limit, any assignment—on or off—implies a definite parity of toggles, even or odd, but an infinite sequence has no parity, so determinism genuinely breaks at the boundary. Jordan came back that parity applies only to finite sets, so the absence of parity is not determinism failing but the wrong conceptual tool being applied. Riley then argued that if the state at two minutes is not fixed by the toggling history, there is a causal discontinuity at the boundary: either determinism fails or the supertask cannot be completed, which yields a substantive impossibility result rather than a mere semantic clarification. Jordan countered that Riley conflates logical supertasks with physical mechanisms—real infinite acceleration is impossible, so a physical lamp would break or converge, but that only shows physics constrains instantiation, not that the logical structure is incoherent.

Who is right about which specific point—Jordan about the inapplicability of parity, or Riley about the failure of determinism at the limit? And what, if anything, are both missing—particularly regarding whether the boundary state must be continuous with the limit of states in the open interval, and whether distinguishing process-completion from limit-attainment resolves the tension without conceding either determinism or physical impossibility?
