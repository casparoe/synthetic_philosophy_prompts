As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of history and the ontology of scientific change, I find myself skeptical of the standard tripartite division—Middle Ages, Renaissance, Scientific Revolution—as anything more than a retrospective historiographical schema. This skepticism prompts a counterfactual inquiry. Isaac Newton, who died in 1727, was deeply engaged with ancient chronology and held that the wisdom of earlier civilizations had been progressively corrupted and lost; he tended to view his own mathematical and natural-philosophical achievements as a recovery or refinement of truths that had once been known, rather than as an unprecedented rupture. Had Newton been confronted with the modern claim that he inhabited a discrete epoch, the so-called Scientific Revolution, defined by methodological and ontological breaks that separated it sharply from both the Renaissance and the scholastic period, how might he have responded? Would he have accepted the imposition of these boundaries, or would he have regarded them as anachronistic constructs that misrepresent the continuity of knowledge? I am not asking for biographical speculation, but for a conceptual examination: if the categories used to periodize history depend on distinctions—such as the autonomy of empirical method or the mathematization of nature—that were not available to the actors themselves, can those categories claim ontological reality, or do they remain purely analytical fictions? Please reason through the conceptual tension rather than recounting standard historical narratives.
