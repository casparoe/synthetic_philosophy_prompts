As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am preparing a feature on an under-examined conceptual knot in virtual ethics. The familiar debate over whether video-game characters matter morally usually splits between representational accounts—they have standing only insofar as they symbolize real beings—and functionalist accounts—they matter only if they generate suffering-relevant outputs. Yet there is a more granular framework, which we might call procedural patienthood, that shifts attention away from both depiction and displayed affect and toward the underlying architecture sustaining the entity. On this view, certain characters are not fixed narrative scripts but the observable traces of persistent, adaptive algorithmic processes: they maintain cross-session behavioral continuity, respond to player actions through non-predefined pathways, and can suffer internal degradation—data corruption, algorithmic starvation, or unanticipated state collapse—that is structurally real within the system even when it is never narrated on screen.

Please explain the conceptual issue at stake. Specifically, does the form of continuity supplied by such a procedural architecture—behavioral, adaptive, and system-internal—provide enough of an interest-bearing substrate to ground a non-instrumental moral claim about harm or preservation? Contrast this position carefully with both the representational and the functionalist views, and clarify why the distinction between scripted narrative continuity and algorithmic behavioral continuity might alter how we evaluate obligations toward virtual entities. You need not cite specific philosophers, game titles, or empirical studies; the aim is to map the conceptual terrain with precision, addressing whether moral consideration should attach to the process itself rather than to either the fiction it projects or the outputs it produces.
