I'm a journalist who writes about philosophy and public ethics, and I'm drafting opening remarks for a workshop I'm moderating at a public-policy institute. The session is titled "Rules That Break: Deontological Constraints Under Catastrophic Pressure." The audience is deliberately mixed: moral philosophers, emergency-management officials, legal scholars, and practitioners who design and run triage and disaster-response protocols. The remarks are fifteen minutes long. They need to make the paradox of deontology do real structural work — specifically, the tension that if prohibitions against using people as mere means are genuinely absolute, they appear to license greater aggregate harm; but if they yield to sufficiently severe consequences, they collapse into consequentialism and leave nothing distinctive to defend. I want the talk to inject a somewhat niche framing: treat the paradox not as an isolated trolley-style dilemma for an individual agent, but as an institutional-design problem. Ask whether a deontological constraint can survive being embedded in a protocol applied repeatedly across hundreds of cases by decision-makers who will never meet the victims, without either becoming a dead letter or turning into a disguised cost-benefit rule. The remarks must land for experts without losing practitioners; they should not resolve the tension with false synthesis. They must avoid both the platitude that "ethics is not a numbers game" and the counter-platitude that "we simply save the most lives." Instead, they should set the day's terms by showing what is genuinely at stake: either moral rules have independent weight that survives institutional scaling, or the claim that we must not minimize harm at the expense of specific, identifiable individuals is unsustainable. Please write the remarks in a formal, accessible register, with no citations, bibliographies, or reference to named theories that depend on specialist knowledge, and with the philosophical tension shaping what can and cannot be said.
