Hey -- I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about mind uploading and gradual replacement, going by gut reactions to concrete cases. Here are my verdicts, and I'm wondering if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Case 1: classic neuron-by-neuron replacement with silicon chips that replicate firing patterns exactly. Verdict: still me, no problem.

Case 2: same swap, but the chips have a 3ms processing lag that the brain learns to compensate for over months. Verdict: still me, surprisingly.

Case 3: high-res scan, original brain destroyed, perfect functional copy booted on a server. Verdict: not me -- it's a copy with my memories, but the thread is broken.

Case 4: the "interleaved stutter" protocol. They replace every other cortical column with synthetic analogs, leave the biological ones running in paralell, coupled across hemispheres, for about eighteen months. Then they remove the biological columns. Here I genuinely can't decide. Part of me says it's just case 1 spread out in time; part of me says running two implementations of my cognition side-by-side for that long means the biological one dies and what's left is a successor, not me.

Case 5: they only replace the limbic and emotional circuitry, leaving all cognition biological. Verdict: still me, but emotionally "off" -- like I'm watching myself from a distance.

Case 6: reverse of case 3. They build a synthetic copy from my scan data, then gradually implant biological tissue into it until it's fully biological. Even then I think it's not me, because the origin was the copy.

The case I keep flip-flopping on is case 4. Sometimes continuity feels preserved; other times the parallel existence feels like duplication with one survivor.

Is there a coherent account of personal identity across substrate change that captures all of these, or close? And what does your best principle predict for a case I haven't listed: gradual replacement where the synthetic parts don't replicate my neural architecture at all, but instead run a compressed predictive-coding model that produces behaviorally identical outputs?

Please also say which of my verdicts has to go if we want the simplest story, and be honest about whether case 4 is the one that breaks it.
