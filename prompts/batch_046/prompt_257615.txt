In my work as a novelist I have consulted for writing rooms where a dispute keeps recurring, now frozen for years without movement: do the rendered figures in interactive fiction carry any moral weight beyond the aesthetic? One camp—the relationists—holds that when a player spends dozens of hours with a represented consciousness, making irreversible narrative choices on its behalf, a genuine moral bond is formed rather than merely simulated; the other—the ontologists—insists that because every state of the figure is recoverable from a save file and lacks phenomenal continuity across branching timelines, there is no unified subject of harm and therefore no moral patient. Both sides grant the same premises: the figure is executable code, the player experiences attachment, and branching structures undermine diachronic identity. Yet they draw opposite conclusions, and neither has yielded an inch.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but structural: they disagree about what makes a relation morally load-bearing, with the relationists treating sustained ludic attention as sufficient and the ontologists requiring an irrecoverable, singular narrative arc. But I worry this is too tidy. Is the disagreement genuinely about the criteria for moral standing—perhaps a hidden conflict between relational and intrinsic theories—or is it verbal, with “character” functioning differently for each side? Might an empirical question about whether players actually experience these figures as continuous agents be doing hidden work, or does the impasse reveal that “mattering morally” has been stretched past coherence when applied to entities that can be instantiated in contradictory states? I ask for a diagnosis of the disagreement’s real structure—factual, normative, conceptual, or bedrock intuitive—supported by what each side actually asserts, together with an honest account of what could move it: a sharper distinction between diegetic presence and ludic contract, a formal analysis of identity across save states, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the concept of harm requires a kind of ontological singularity these figures necessarily lack.
