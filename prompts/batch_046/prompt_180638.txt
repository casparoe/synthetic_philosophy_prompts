I run a biweekly discussion group for software engineers at my company — roughly ten people, no formal philosophy training, meeting for seventy-five minutes in a conference room. I want you to design a structured, in-person activity that makes the group experience the force of Alfred North Whitehead’s view that education proceeds through a rhythmic cycle — romance, precision, and generalization — with particular emphasis on the idea that this cycle is recursive rather than linear. Specifically, I want participants to discover for themselves why rushing past romance produces what Whitehead called inert knowledge — facts received but never actively utilized — and why reaching the stage of generalization does not end learning but instead generates a new, deeper romance. To keep the idea tangible, the activity should have the group collectively invent a small formal notation system (something like a micro-programming language or a logical calculus) from scratch, progressing through roles or rules that mirror each stage.

Please include: a clear timeline broken into phases; specific roles or rules that create real incentives without using money; materials limited to pens and paper; and a mechanism by which the group physically or structurally moves from one stage to the next. The design should ensure that participants feel the pull of romance (broad, playful exploration), then the discipline of precision (rigid syntax or rules), then the abstraction of generalization (pattern recognition across cases), and finally the unexpected collapse of that abstraction back into a renewed romance — so that they experience the recursion rather than hearing it described.

After the activity description, provide a debrief plan that maps specific moments in the session onto Whitehead’s stages and onto his warning about inert, unused knowledge. Be explicit about what feature of Whitehead’s broader philosophical system — particularly his process metaphysics or his claim that education is fundamentally the acquisition of an art in the active utilization of knowledge — this exercise cannot reproduce, and that I as the facilitator must supply verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with engineers, and how to handle it.

Please do not ask participants to read anything beforehand, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests in your response; the idea should emerge entirely from what the group does and decides together.
