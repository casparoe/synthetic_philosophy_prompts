Two colleagues argued after our reading group discussed Simone Weil’s essay attacking political parties. The first claimed Weil’s core point is that any collective with a shared program necessarily demands members suppress independent judgment, so even modern “issue networks” with no formal membership fall under her critique if they enforce ideological alignment through social pressure. The second replied that Weil specifically targets hierarchical bodies with official platforms and disciplinary mechanisms, and that loose coalitions lack the “programmatic loyalty” she condemns; he added that if we extend her argument to every group with shared opinions, we erase the line between a party and a casual conversation. The first countered that Weil compares parties to religions in demanding total adherence, and that modern networks achieve the same effect through “distributed reputation sanctions” rather than top-down rules—making them more dangerous, not exempt. The second said this stretches Weil into a universal sociology she never intended, turning her historical analysis of French parties into a claim about all collective belief. They asked me to referee: does Weil’s argument genuinely apply to non-hierarchical advocacy networks, is the mechanism of enforcement central to her critique or only accidental, and where does her actual text draw the boundary? Please adjudicate point by point—who is right about Weil’s claim, about the scope of her argument, and about whether enforcement mechanism matters—and say what both parties miss. Separate what is settled about her view from what remains genuinely contested among readers who know the text, and do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus.
