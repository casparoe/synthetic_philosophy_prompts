I serve on the University Staff Conduct Appeals Panel, and we must issue a formal written opinion in the case of Dean Marcus Chen, who was dismissed after authorizing a restructuring that eliminated two mental-health clinics serving low-income students. Chen does not deny signing the order, but he appeals the finding of personal culpability by invoking what he calls an institutional Frankfurt condition: he alleges that an embedded, non-transparent budget-allocation algorithm—the Resource Lock Protocol, whose parameters he designed three years earlier but never disclosed to students or staff—had already made the closures causally inevitable. According to his defence, had he refused to sign, the protocol would have executed the same restructuring automatically, substituting another administrative signature; therefore, he lacked the ability to do otherwise in the precise sense that the principle of alternative possibilities demands, and moral blame is inappropriate. A minority of the panel accepts this analogy to Frankfurt-style counterfactual intervention and argues that an institutional artifact can indeed play the role of the hidden intervener, especially since Chen’s prior design of the protocol does not restore his present ability to prevent the harm. The majority regards this as a category error: causal preclusion by an algorithm one designed and concealed is not equivalent to the kind of external manipulation that undermines responsibility, and Chen’s failure to disclose the protocol to affected parties is itself a separate breach of fiduciary duty. We need a decision memo that recommends a concrete outcome—uphold the dismissal, overturn it, or overturn with conditions—and that takes the strongest version of Chen’s defence seriously, distinguishes between causal inability and morally exculpatory inability, addresses whether artifacts or automated institutional architectures can function as responsibility-defeating interveners, and proposes one specific, enforceable policy change for future administrative accountability regarding disclosure of automated decision protocols. Please present the response as the memo itself, beginning immediately with the document and with no preface or commentary outside it; within the memo, lead with the recommendation in the first sentence, then present the reasoning, with no restatement of the charge and no citations, bibliographies, or requests for further reading.
