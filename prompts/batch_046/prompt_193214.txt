I'm in a philosophy seminar on Marx's analysis of exploitation and alienation, and for our closing session I want to present a poem that does more than name-check the concepts. Write a sestina—six six-line stanzas followed by a three-line envoi—using these rotating end-words in order: labor, product, species-being, wage, commodity, estrangement. The poem should be spoken in the first person by a factory worker, but the rotation should gradually shift grammatical roles so that by the envoi the worker has become an object among objects and the commodity seems to possess human qualities, mirroring Marx's claim that under capitalism relations between people appear as relations between things. Place the structural turn in the fifth stanza, where "species-being" and "commodity" must share a single line to suggest that the worker's life-activity has been reduced to a mere means of physical survival. Keep the imagery concrete—machines, hands, finished goods, a pay envelope—rather than purely abstract, and embed exploitation in the visible gap between what the worker produces and what returns as wage rather than treating it as a separate sermon. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Marx's accounts of alienation and commodity fetishism wouldn't wince, yet it should still work as a poem for someone who doesn't know the texts.
