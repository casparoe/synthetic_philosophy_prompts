I’m writing from an upper-level philosophy-of-future-studies course I’m taking through an independent study, and last week we discussed Nick Bostrom’s concept of a singleton—a hypothetical world order in which a single decision-making agency operates at the highest level, permanently suppressing internal and external threats to its supremacy. During a study-group conversation, one of my peers mentioned Wheeler and Feynman’s one-electron universe, in which all electrons and positrons are manifestations of a single entity traversing time in both directions. We began wondering whether the singleton hypothesis might be read not merely as an institutional or governance structure, but as an ontological thesis: that eventually all apparent agents, preferences, and sub-systems are really just temporal phases or manifestations of one underlying decision-substance, analogous to that single looping electron. If that reading were correct, it seemed to us that many standard objections to centralized authority—particularly problems of preference aggregation, social-choice impossibilities, and internal disagreement—would dissolve, because there would not be genuinely distinct agents with conflicting values, only one entity experiencing different states. So my question is this: is there a defensible interpretation of the singleton hypothesis on which it functions as an ontological reduction rather than a political architecture, and if so, does that interpretation actually eliminate the coordination problems that Bostrom treats as challenges for the singleton to manage, or does it merely rename them? I would particularly like to know where the analogy to the one-electron model breaks down, since my intuition is that decision-making involves normative conflicts that do not map cleanly onto physical identity over time.
