Compare Jorge Luis Borges's denial of temporal succession—developed in his philosophical essays through a Berkeleyan argument that successive moments cannot cohere into a continuous flow without a perceiving consciousness that itself stands outside time—with the contemporary analytic indexical account of temporal location, on which 'now' operates like an indexical such as 'here' and marks no ontologically privileged slice of reality. Focus in particular on how each view explains the asymmetry between remembering a past event and anticipating a future one: does Borges's collapse of all temporal distinctions into an eternal present erase that directional structure, or does his appeal to consciousness recover it? Conversely, does the indexical view genuinely preserve the asymmetry, or does it merely relocate it into causal or phenomenological relations that its own anti-privilege thesis cannot justify? Evaluate which framework gives a more coherent account of lived temporal experience without reifying a 'moving present', and identify the precise point—Borges's immaterialist premises versus the indexical view's physicalist commitments—where the comparison ceases to be parallel.
