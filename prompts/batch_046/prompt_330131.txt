I'm writing this for a historiography seminar I'm taking on narrative and explanation in the writing of history. I constructed the following argument to defend a minimal form of Whig interpretation, but I'm suspicious of it because the conclusion seems too strong—if it's right, then the standard injunction against Whig history is self-defeating rather than merely a caution. Here are the steps:

(1) Whig history, properly understood, is the practice of interpreting past events as stages in an inevitable progression toward present institutions and values.
(2) To reject Whig history is therefore to deny that the present can serve as the implicit telos or endpoint that gives past events their narrative significance.
(3) But historical explanation requires selection: the historian must distinguish causally or structurally significant events from mere background.
(4) The only non-arbitrary principle for such selection is relevance to an outcome; without an outcome in view, every event is equally worthy of inclusion, producing not a narrative but an undifferentiated chronicle.
(5) If that outcome is barred from being the present (by (2)), the historian must choose some other endpoint—say, the fall of Rome, or the Reformation.
(6) But selecting any other endpoint either is arbitrary (there is no reason to stop there rather than here) or smuggles back a Whiggish structure by treating that endpoint as a hidden telos.
(7) Therefore the consistent anti-Whig historian faces a trilemma: she must be Whiggish about some endpoint, make an arbitrary narrative cut, or abandon narrative judgment and write pure chronicle.
(8) Yet pure chronicle fails to meet the disciplinary standards of history, which require explaining change and assessing significance—standards that critics of Whiggism themselves typically uphold.
(9) So the consistent rejection of Whig history undermines the possibility of historical narrative itself.
(10) That is absurd.
(11) Hence some minimal Whig structure—treating the present as the unavoidable vantage point for selecting significance—is inescapable.

If I have to choose, I'd rather preserve (1) and (8) than (11). Where does this reasoning fail? Is the flaw in (4), where I conflate causal relevance with teleological significance? Or is there a different way to justify narrative selection—perhaps through the historian's own situated questions, or through thematic and causal coherence without any endpoint—that doesn't collapse into Whiggism?

Please also say what would change your mind: which distinction, counterexample, or clarification about how anti-Whig historians actually construct narratives (for instance, whether they can use explanatory relevance to a particular problem rather than to an endpoint) would lead you to reject my diagnosis, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
