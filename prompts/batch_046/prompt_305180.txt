For an essay I am writing, I have been studying the long-running debate about sphexishness—the use of the sphex wasp's rigid behavioral loops as a boundary case for free will. One camp argues that human action differs only in degree from the wasp's mechanistic sequence: our neural plasticity and contextual learning produce a highly flexible, open-ended version of the same sphex-like routine, not genuine authorship. The opposing camp insists that free will requires a kind of self-directed openness that no amount of mechanistic adaptability, however context-sensitive, can ever provide. Despite broad agreement on the relevant psychology and biology, the debate seems frozen. Is the deadlock substantive—turning on whether moral responsibility requires non-mechanistic causation—or verbal, depending on whether "mechanical" is defined to exclude adaptive systems? Could it be that the two sides are answering different questions, one about causal explanation and one about normative status, under a single heading? I suspect a hidden assumption about whether "open-ended flexibility" in complex biological or artificial agents can ever count as non-mechanistic is doing silent work. Using what each side actually claims about rigidity, flexibility, and authorship, diagnose the structure of the disagreement and say what kind of progress—a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, or a formal model—would count as resolving it, or argue that the stalemate shows the question itself is ill-posed.
