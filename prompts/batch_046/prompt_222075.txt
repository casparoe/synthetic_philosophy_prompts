I just finished a long and frustrating argument with a friend about moral luck, and I am trying to determine whether my judgments form a coherent pattern or merely an ad hoc mixture of control-based and outcome-based reactions. Here are my verdicts on five cases.

Case A: Two surgeons perform an identical high-risk operation with identical skill and care. One patient dies from an unforeseeable, idiosyncratic allergic reaction; the other survives. I judge that the surgeons are equally blameworthy—namely, not at all blameworthy for the death—and that the outcome does not alter their moral standing.

Case B: Two drivers are equally negligent, speeding and texting through a residential zone. One strikes and kills a child who suddenly darts into the road; the other reaches home without incident. I maintain that they are equally blameworthy, since their mental states and actions were identical. At the same time, I feel a strong pull to say that the driver who killed the child has done something morally worse, and I am unsure whether that pull should be resisted or accommodated.

Case C: A factory manager selects a cheap safety protocol that saves two million dollars but carries a 0.1 percent risk of catastrophic failure. The failure occurs, killing twenty workers. Had the failure not occurred, I would judge his decision reckless but not murderous. Given that it did occur, I want to say he bears a heavier moral stain—perhaps even that he killed them—despite the fact that his mental state and choice were exactly the same in both worlds.

Case D: A person born with a rare neurological condition that eliminates empathic response commits a violent assault. A genetically and environmentally identical counterpart, lacking only that condition, does not. I judge the first agent less blameworthy. Yet I also think that if he had somehow overcome the condition and then committed the identical assault, he would be fully blameworthy—even though the causal history of the action differs only in the presence or absence of the condition.

Case E: A diplomat negotiates a treaty in good faith, with no reason to foresee that a coup in the partner nation will transform the treaty into an instrument of genocide. She bears no guilt for the genocide, I think, but her life should be marked by a special form of tragic ownership—an irreducible narrative burden—that the diplomat whose treaty succeeded peacefully does not carry.

My friend claims these verdicts are incoherent: they invoke control in A and B, then smuggle in outcome-dependence in C, constitutive luck in D, and a sui generis category of non-culpable ownership in E. Is there a unified principle—perhaps distinguishing culpability, moral taint, and narrative responsibility—that delivers all five? If not, which verdict must I abandon, and at what cost? Finally, what does the surviving account predict for a case I have not mentioned: two soldiers are ordered to fire on what they are told is an enemy camp; one obeys and the target is indeed enemy combatants, the other obeys and the target is a civilian shelter, the error being due to faulty intelligence that neither could have questioned. Are they differently situated morally?
