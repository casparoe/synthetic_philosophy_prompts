I am a doctoral student in philosophy of cognitive science, working at the intersection of mechanistic interpretability and philosophy of mind. I want you to compare two positions on whether artificial neural networks genuinely instantiate representations: first, the content-realist view that hidden-layer activations carry literal semantic or propositional content (for example, that a specific subcircuit in an early vision layer represents curvature or edge orientation); second, the structural-instrumentalist view that talk of "representations" is merely a heuristic label for causally relevant patterns of activation and weight-space geometry, with no actual semantic properties being instantiated inside the network.

Please conduct this comparison through the specific conceptual lens of explanatory dependence: does explaining a network's behavior genuinely require attributing content to internal states, or can the same explanations be given purely in terms of causal structure, dynamical trajectories, and geometric relations? Structure your answer around three issues: (a) the ontological status each view assigns to putative representations; (b) whether the predictive and interventional successes of mechanistic interpretability—such as ablation studies, activation patching, and the identification of feature-selective subcircuits—provide evidence specifically for content-realism, or whether those successes are fully available to structural-instrumentalism without any semantic ascription; and (c) what specific conditions in a network's internal dynamics would make content-realism false while structural-instrumentalism remains true, and vice versa.

Please separate clearly what is settled in this area—for instance, that interpretability techniques can isolate causally relevant subcircuits whose activity correlates with identifiable input features—from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of science and cognitive scientists who study artificial systems, such as whether causal relevance entails representational status, whether the absence of a unified interpretation procedure undermines realism, and whether explanatory indispensability arguments drawn from biological cognition carry over to artificial networks. Do not present any contested framework—such as the universal indispensability of representational talk across all of cognitive science—as if it were the consensus view specifically regarding neural networks.
