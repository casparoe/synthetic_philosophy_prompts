I’m applying for a three-month summer grant to sketch out one dissertation chapter. My half-baked idea: everyone reads Aristotle’s money passages in Politics I and NE V as either a proto-Marxist critique of capital or a proto-Austrian defense of exchange-value, but I think both miss the actual ontology he’s trying to build. He seems to draw a line between exchnage that is conservative — goods move between households but the total pool of use-values stays tied to natural need — and exchange that is generatively weird, creating new value-claims like interest that don’t map back onto any prior use-value. I want to ask whether that distinction can be made precise, and whether it actually separates usury from legitimate trade in the text or just serves as post-hoc moral coloring.

Precise question: can we formalize an Aristotelian ontology of exchange that distinguishes conservative redistribution from non-conservative value-generation, and does this distinction structurally separate tokos from ordinary trade, or does it collapse once applied consistently?

Why existing answers leave it open: economic historians treat the passage as primitive monetary theory; ethicists treat it as a warning about greed. Almost nobody treats it as an exchange-ontology with formal conditions, so there is a conceptual gap that close reading plus a little conceptual engineering could fill.

Method, month by month:
Month 1 — close reading of Politics I.8-10 and NE V.5, tracking every verb for exchange (allassein, kerdos, etc.) and noting which ones imply preservation versus transformation of the value-pool.
Month 2 — build the conservative/non-conservative framework. Conservative exchange: post-exchange use-values equal pre-exchange use-values minus natural consumption. Non-conservative: any exchange introducing a claim not reducible to prior goods. This is the injected part — not standard scholarship.
Month 3 — apply the framework to two modern cases, interest-bearing debt and carbon-credit markets. Not to claim Aristotle predicted them, but to test whether his conditions classify them consistently with his treatment of tokos. If they both come out non-conservative, that supports structural coherence; if they split arbitrarily, that’s a failure signal.
Month 4 — draft the chapter with explicit failure conditions: (a) the line collapses into “exchange I personally dislike,” or (b) Aristotle’s own examples of legitimate trade turn out non-conservative under the framework, rendering the distinction useless.

What counts as success: a clear typology showing Aristotle’s critique of usury is structural, not just moral, and that the conservative/non-conservative line can be applied without anachronism. Realistic scope: one chapter, not a book. I won’t do a citation-heavy literature review; I’ll map the standard positions myself and fill in references later. The grant buys teaching-release time to do the close reading and framework-building without rushing.
