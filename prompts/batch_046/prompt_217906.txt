Write an essay on the following controversy: when cognitive scientists interpret the color phi phenomenon—the illusion in which two differently colored dots appear in rapid succession at separate locations and observers report seeing a single dot move and change color midway—there is a dispute about whether the brain's retrospective construction of the experience should be understood as an Orwellian revision or a Stalinesque revision. In your essay, first define both revision-types precisely in conceptual terms: an Orwellian revision rewrites the content of a prior mental state so that it now conforms to the present narrative (the past is altered but acknowledged as having existed), whereas a Stalinesque revision excises the prior mental state from the record entirely, treating it as if it never possessed any determinate properties worth preserving. Next, apply this distinction directly to color phi. Does the first flash retain its original color in consciousness only to have that content overwritten by the interpolated motion and second color (the Orwellian reading), or is the first flash denied any specific color-property altogether, functioning in the final experiential record as though it never occurred as a determinate qualitative event (the Stalinesque reading)? Weigh the main arguments for each interpretation, drawing only on the structural features of the illusion itself and on general considerations about the timing of conscious experience. Address at least one serious objection that a defender of the opposing view would raise. Then reach and justify a definite conclusion: state which revision-model better captures what happens in color phi and explain why the other model mischaracterizes the temporal dynamics of the experience. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke specific named texts or authors; construct the argument entirely from the conceptual distinction and the phenomenology of the illusion.
