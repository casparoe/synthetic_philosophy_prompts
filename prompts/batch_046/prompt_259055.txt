You are delivering the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes—at an interdisciplinary symposium titled "Collective Blame and the Archive," held in a room divided evenly between diplomatic historians steeped in the Fischer controversy and philosophers of collective agency who have never read Fritz Fischer's 1961 study but are fluent in debates about distributed moral responsibility. Your task is to construct an address in which the Fischer controversy does not serve as historical color but structurally constrains what you can claim about holding a nation responsible for the First World War. You must navigate a precise tension: Fischer's argument that Germany deliberately pursued expansive war aims gave normative content to national blame, yet subsequent structuralist historiography demonstrated that escalation was overdetermined by alliance dynamics and bureaucratic inertia; lean too far toward structure and you dissolve the object of blame into impersonal forces, lean too far toward intentionalist blame and you ignore the archival evidence that other powers actively shaped the escalation dynamics. The philosophical idea should do real work—explain to the historians why the conceptual distinction between causal contribution and normative accountability matters for reading an archive, and explain to the philosophers why archives do not speak for themselves but require an interpretive frame that decides which causal chains count as actions attributable to a collective agent. Open with a concrete image: the moment in 1961 in Hamburg when Fischer presented his thesis and the audience realized that the question "who started it?" was being transformed into "who is answerable for it?" Close with a single, carefully qualified claim about what it means, in 2025, for a democratic polity to inherit responsibility for a war its present citizens did not choose, without collapsing that inheritance into either collective guilt or mere causal explanation. Do not ask the audience to consult a bibliography, do not cite specific page numbers, and do not treat either half of the room as novices. The speech should feel as though it could only be given here, to this particular divided audience, and should leave both groups with a problem they recognize as theirs.
