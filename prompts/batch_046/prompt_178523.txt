I am a journalist writing on the intersection of moral philosophy and technology, and I am preparing a ninety-minute public seminar for an audience of computer-science graduate students—bright, technically fluent, and skeptical of anything that smells like armchair ethics—who have encountered trolley-style dilemmas in machine-learning fairness forums but have no training in normative theory. I want you to act as an expert in the doctrine of double effect and as a pedagogical designer experienced with technically minded non-philosophers, and I ask you to think carefully, step by step, before constructing the plan. My goal is not for them to memorize Aquinas’s four conditions, but to understand why double effect is structurally different from both consequentialist optimization and from a naive “good intentions excuse bad outcomes” slogan, and specifically how it applies—without collapsing—into algorithmic triage and autonomous-vehicle decision architecture.

Please design the explanatory arc: the exact dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the distinction between intended ends and foreseen-but-unintended side-effects feels like a logical structure rather than a psychological rationalization; identify the one concrete carrying case—drawn from algorithmic resource allocation or autonomous navigation rather than the classic railway switch—that should anchor the entire session, and specify precisely what features that case must possess and what distractions it must lack in order to do the philosophical work. Then map the misconceptions these students will actually bring: the conviction that double effect is merely utilitarianism with rhetorical window dressing, the conviction that any foreseen harm automatically violates the doctrine, and the subtler misconception that “intentionality” can be read directly off source code or loss-function architecture. For each misconception, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and propose a brief check-for-understanding—something they must be able to articulate in their own technical vocabulary—that would show the structural logic has landed rather than been parroted. Finally, tell me explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level—perhaps the theological genealogy of proportionality or the full casuistry of cooperation with evil—and state that refusal openly, so the session ends with clarity rather than false completeness. Begin with a brief brainstorm of the conceptual dependencies, then deliver the teaching plan in full.
