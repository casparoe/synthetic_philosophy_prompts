As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I have been attempting to understand Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation—specifically the thought experiment in which a linguist observing a speaker utter gavagai in the presence of a rabbit lacks any empirical basis for deciding among translations that treat the term as referring to rabbits, to undetached rabbit parts, or to temporal rabbit stages, since all competing translation manuals are consistent with the available behavioral evidence. I wish to test whether a structurally similar problem appears in programming language semantics and formal verification, where a specification serves as a translation manual between syntactic expressions and mathematical behavior models, and where a compiler may generate machine-code implementations that are observationally equivalent across all inputs and outputs yet diverge radically in internal representation and optimization. The conceptual issue I need explained is this: if Quine's argument is taken at face value, does it imply that there is no objective fact of the matter about what a correct program means beyond the complete set of its input-output traces, thereby dissolving the distinction between a specification and its implementation? Or does the deliberately constructed, formally defined semantics of a programming language provide a principled barrier that prevents the indeterminacy from arising in formal systems? Please provide a precise mapping between the elements of radical translation and those of compiler semantics, identify the exact points at which the analogy holds and the points at which it breaks down, and determine whether the breakdown is robust enough to preserve a uniquely correct interpretation in software verification or whether Quinean indeterminacy genuinely extends into engineered formal languages.
