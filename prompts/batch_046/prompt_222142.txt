A study partner claims Descartes's cogito secretly proves other minds exist with Cartesian certainty, which feels like overreach. Here is the chain: (1) The cogito is not an inference from premise to conclusion but a performative identity — in doubting, I do not observe a thinker and then deduce existence; the doubting and the being are one witnessed act. (2) To understand another person's speech or gesture as meaningful rather than as mechanical motion, I must already treat it as expressing thoughts that are present-to-themselves for that other subject. (3) So recognizing another mind is not inferring a hidden cause but recognizing the same first-person structure — existence-as-thinking — that the cogito reveals in my own case. (4) Therefore, if my own existence-as-thinking is indubitable, then wherever that same structure is present in another being it carries the same certainty. (5) Yet Descartes explicitly said other minds are not given with the same immediacy and required a separate argument appealing to God rather than to the cogito itself, so the cogito must not extend this far. I want to keep (1) and (3) if possible. Which step should be rejected — is the mistake in treating interpretation as structural recognition rather than causal inference, or in assuming first-person presence can be attributed from the outside? Separate what is standard Cartesian interpretation from genuinely contested phenomenological readings, and avoid presenting either as the settled view. Give your answer in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
