I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been working through an idea on my own and would value your comments on the following essay outline. The topic concerns Bradley’s regress and the unity of the proposition, though I want to apply it to something I have been wondering about recently.

As I understand Bradley’s argument, whenever we try to explain a relation—say, between a subject and a predicate—by introducing another relation to connect them, we only push the difficulty back one step. That new relation must itself be related to the terms, which requires yet another relation, and so on without end. His conclusion, as I take it, is that genuine unity cannot be assembled from separate parts plus external relations; real unity must be immediate, prior to analysis, like the wholeness of a feeling or of a single experience that is not built up from pieces.

My essay idea is to ask whether a modern language model, which generates text one token at a time, can ever achieve true propositional unity in Bradley’s sense. Each token is linked to the others only through statistical weights—relations of correlation and probability, not logical necessity. If Bradley is right that relations can never genuinely bind terms without an infinite regress, then a system that produces sentences purely through successive token-relations never actually forms a unified proposition. It only produces the appearance of one, much as Bradley thought the world of separate things and relations is mere appearance.

I plan to develop three claims:
1. That statistical correlation between tokens counts as a genuine instance of the kind of “relation” Bradley attacked, and therefore falls under his regress.
2. That attention mechanisms do not solve the problem, because they remain just more complex relations among vector-representations of tokens.
3. That if Bradley’s argument holds, then either such models do not really think in propositions, or we must reject the idea that propositional unity is necessary for thought.

I would especially like your comments on accuracy—whether I am distorting Bradley’s distinction between relations and qualities—and on whether the outline is interesting or collapses into a category mistake. I am particularly unsure about point 2: is an attention weight a “relation” in Bradley’s specific sense, or am I importing a modern technical concept where it does not belong? Please also tell me what specific argument or consideration would change your assessment of the analogy’s soundness, and identify the point in your reasoning here that is most likely to fail or depend on a hidden assumption.
