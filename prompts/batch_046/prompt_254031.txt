Many universities enforce speech codes that restrict expression not when it directly threatens violence, but when it is judged to create a hostile environment for members of particular groups — for instance, by systematically undermining their perceived credibility or by altering the social conditions in which they can participate as equals. The justification is usually framed in terms of harm, yet the harm in question is diffuse, mediated through others' beliefs and attitudes, and impossible to trace to any single utterance. What theory of harm does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it require treating epistemic or social-structural damage as on a par with the direct physical injuries Mill seemed to have in mind when he limited interference to harm to others, or can his framework accommodate it through the distinction between direct injury and indirect moral coercion? Reconstruct the implicit view of the speech-action boundary that makes the policy coherent, say whether it is consistent with Mill's separation of self-regarding and other-regarding conduct, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits well with it, and indicate what practical adjustments to enforcement would be necessary if that implicit theory were replaced by a strictly direct-injury account.
