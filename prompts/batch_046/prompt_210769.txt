I am organizing a working session on normative architectures for autonomous decision systems, and two colleagues—Dr. Aris Thorne, who advocates what he calls a deontic-lock architecture, and Dr. Bina Voss, who defends an integrated expected-value framework—have been talking past each other for months about whether AI systems should reason as utilitarians or as deontologists. Rather than another debate, I want them to produce a joint adversarial-collaboration document that both could sign. Please draft it: open with a numbered section of agreed claims about normative reasoning in artificial systems, about the formal differences between rule-based and optimizing architectures, and about shared practical constraints such as computational tractability, transparency, and recursive self-modification. Then provide a disagreements table that states each core thesis in neutral wording Thorne and Voss would each accept for their own side—Thorne’s view that deontological reasoning is necessary to avoid catastrophic mis-specification of moral patienthood under ontological uncertainty, and Voss’s view that any non-consequentialist rule eventually requires consequentialist justification and collapses into expected-value reasoning under pressure.

Next, identify the cruxes: the precise sub-questions whose resolution would genuinely move at least one party. I suspect one crux concerns whether moral bookkeeping—the tracking of satisfied and violated constraints across sequential decisions—carries independent normative weight or is merely an epistemic shortcut for long-run utility aggregation. Another likely crux is whether deontological rules can be non-arbitrarily specified in domains where agent-boundaries are fluid, such as distributed or merged decision systems, without implicitly smuggling in a prior consequentialist weighting of interests. For each crux, present a concrete case or structural feature both sides agree is relevant: for example, a self-modifying system that can revise its own normative framework and must decide whether to treat prior commitments as binding sunk costs or as revisable heuristics; or a multi-agent arbitration scenario in which the system cannot deterministically identify the boundaries of affected moral patients. For each case, record in advance what Thorne predicts (that treating prior commitments as binding prevents drift toward utility-maximizing dissolution of constraints) and what Voss predicts (that without an implicit value-weighting the system will either deadlock or revert to hidden optimization).

Finally, conclude with a brief section distinguishing genuine disagreements from verbal ones—specifically, address whether part of the dispute over reasoning like a utilitarian concerns only the vocabulary of computation, such as optimization routines versus constraint-satisfaction procedures, rather than normative substance, and state clearly which residual disagreements remain substantive once that clarification is made. The document should contain no bibliography, no reading list, and no appeal to authority outside the arguments both parties have already advanced.
