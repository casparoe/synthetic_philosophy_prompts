Write a dialogue between a defender of Schopenhauer's metaphysics of music and a formalist who denies that music expresses the Will. The Schopenhauerian should explain why music, unlike the other arts, is not a representation of Platonic Ideas mediated by the principle of sufficient reason, but rather a direct objectification of the Will itself—particularly through melody's analog to human striving and harmony's analog to the grades of objectification from inorganic nature up to human consciousness. The formalist should respond not by denying that music moves us, but by arguing that its affective power derives from formal structures—interval relations, temporal patterns, and cognitive expectations—that organize perception without requiring a metaphysical substrate. Have them debate whether Schopenhauer's distinction between melody (as the voice of the Will in constant tension and resolution) and harmony (as the fixed grades of objectification) actually supports his claim that music bypasses conceptual representation, or whether that very structural regularity shows music operates within, not outside, the principle of sufficient reason. Each should address the other's actual argument: the formalist should confront the claim that conceptual understanding of harmony deepens rather than replaces its metaphysical effect, and the Schopenhauerian should explain why formal analysis cannot account for music's unique capacity to communicate suffering and redemption without representational content.
