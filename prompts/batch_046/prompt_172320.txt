For my advanced metaphysics seminar, I am asking students to translate abstract modal disputes into narrative form, and I would like you to write a short story—approximately 800 to 1,000 words—that stages the conflict between counterpart theory and transworld identity without simply replaying familiar textbook examples.

Set the piece in the Institute for Cross-World Preservation, a bureaucratic yet strangely poetic archive where curators maintain ontological exhibits linking artifacts across possible worlds. Your protagonist, a senior conservator named Dr. Elara Voss, must adjudicate a merger proposal involving two violin exhibits: in World Alpha the instrument was carved from a specific Alpine spruce and performed a lost concerto, while in World Beta an apparently identical violin was built by a craftsman with a different mental history from wood sourced in a different valley. The Institute’s official doctrine holds that these are mere counterparts—no object literally inhabits both worlds—yet Voss uncovers an unclassified archival notation, which you should invent and call indexical resonance logs, that records a non-qualitative, world-transcendent identifier tied specifically to the Alpha instrument. The plot should turn on whether accepting this identifier forces the Institute to recognize transworld identity, thereby undermining the counterpart-theoretic framework that governs funding, taxonomy, and curatorial ethics. Include a dialogue scene in which one colleague defends strict qualitative similarity reduction and another insists on an irreducible haecceity, but do not let either speaker win conclusively; instead, have Voss make a practical decision—perhaps authorizing the merger, or refusing it, or inventing a third institutional category—that leaves the metaphysical question genuinely open. The tone should be restrained literary realism: avoid overt philosophical lecturing in the narration, yet let every institutional rule and emotional beat be shaped by the underlying modal logic. I am particularly interested in whether the story can make the reader feel the cost of abandoning counterpart relations—say, the loss of world-bound uniqueness—rather than merely understand it intellectually.
