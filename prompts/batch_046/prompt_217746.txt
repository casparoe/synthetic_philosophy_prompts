Consider the winter of 1945, when Han van Meegeren—having sold a forged canvas, *Supper at Emmaus* (1937), through intermediaries to Hermann Göring and then facing trial for collaboration—stood before the court not merely as a suspected traitor but as an artist who had deceived the very connoisseurs who had once praised his hand. The painting had been authenticated by leading Dutch scholars, entered the mental museum of the nation’s cultural patrimony, and then, upon revelation of its origin, expelled as an aesthetic nullity—despite being chemically and visually unchanged. Analyse this episode through the lens of what I shall term the *provenance-essentialist* view of art-value: the claim that an artwork’s aesthetic and monetary worth is ontologically anchored in its actual causal origin, not merely in its perceptible properties—set against its rival, *perceptual functionalism*, which holds that value supervenes solely on experienced form and structural relations.
