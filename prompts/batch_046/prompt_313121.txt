Consider a democratic state's archival and record-keeping policy, which applies three distinct regimes to different categories of past conduct. For state-sponsored atrocities, the law mandates perpetual, redundant preservation and criminalizes destruction or revision—treating collective forgetting as a moral failure that dishonors victims. For ordinary criminal convictions that are legally "spent," the state requires automatic expungement and prohibits employers from inquiring, treating retention as an unjust prolongation of punishment beyond rehabilitation. Between these extremes lies a less familiar regime: a "liminal registry" for serious but non-criminal public moral failures—plagiarism in high office, violations of professional ethics that caused no direct harm, broken public commitments—where records are never destroyed, but the subject may append annotations, contextualizations, or revised interpretations over time, and where the archive itself is required to display the record alongside its subsequent framing.

A citizen observing this system might find it puzzling: why is forgetting a collective duty in one domain, a protected individual right in another, and a permanently suspended possibility in the third? What theory of the ethics of memory and forgetting does this tripartite arrangement presuppose? Must it rely on a distinction between harmful forgetting and liberating forgetting, and does the middle regime assume that personal identity is fluid enough to make erasure inappropriate but static enough to make uncontextualized preservation unjust? Is the system consistent with an ethics of shared remembrance in the vein of Margalit, or does the liminal registry implicitly adopt Rieff's suspicion that certain forms of remembering become tyrannical? Furthermore, if the justification for the middle regime appeals to the "structural significance" of these failures rather than to the subject's desert, does that smuggle in a consequentialist logic about collective learning that conflicts with the apparently deontological framing of both the atrocity and the expungement regimes?

Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined—such that rival theories of remembering and forgetting could license the same institutional design.
