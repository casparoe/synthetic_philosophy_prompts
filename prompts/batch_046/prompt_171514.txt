I am writing my position paper for a graduate seminar on comparative philosophy and ontology, and I want a rigorous internal consistency audit before I finalize my argument. Here is my current profile:

(a) I read Zhuangzi’s butterfly dream not as a skeptical argument against knowledge of the external world, but as demonstrating that the boundary between self and world is phenomenologically fluid and lacks any metaphysically fixed substrate.

(b) I think the proper response to dream skepticism is not to discover a discriminating criterion between waking and dreaming, but to treat wakefulness as a normative status internal to a form of life—analogous in spirit to a Wittgensteinian dissolution rather than to an epistemic justification.

(c) At the same time, I maintain that there are genuinely ontologically inaccessible situations: cases in which no observer embedded within the relevant system could in principle acquire the information that would determine whether a particular entity belongs to the ontology (for example, whether an indeterminate quantum state involves one branching world or many, or whether a figure encountered only in dream has independent being).

(d) From (c) I draw an anti-realist inference: when the information required for ontological identification is in principle inaccessible, the corresponding ontological question is not substantive but conventional—there is no further fact of the matter beyond what our conceptual framework stipulates.

(e) Nevertheless, I want to resist global ontological relativism. Within a fixed framework, I hold that ontological identification is genuinely objective: once the rules and the accessible data are settled, there is a correct answer about whether something exists, even if practical or theoretical limits prevent us from reaching it.

(f) Finally, I reject physicalism about consciousness, not by positing non-physical substances, but by arguing that phenomenal concepts reveal an explanatory gap that forces our ontology to include irreducibly perspectival entities—entities whose identity conditions depend on the viewpoint from which they are assessed, as suggested by Zhuangzi’s transformation.

Please take a definite position and make definite choices: rank the tensions among these commitments by severity, say for each whether it is genuine or only apparent, identify the least costly revision that would restore consistency, and tell me which single commitment I should drop if I want the cleanest overall repair. Give your reasons rather than laying out options neutrally.
