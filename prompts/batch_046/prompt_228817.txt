I'm in a phil of lang course and we got sidetracked into Kripkenstein's skeptical argument about rule-following. I tried to push it further than our prof did and now I'm stuck with an argument that seems to eat itself. Here it is:

(1) To mean addition by '+' is to be disposed to sum any pair of numbers. (2) Any finite history of use is compatible with infinitely many other rules (quaddition, etc.) that match past use but diverge later. (3) There's no mental fact—no occurrent mental image, no algorithm in the head—that picks addition over quus, because mental states are also finitely manifested and open to the same reinterpetation. (4) So there's genuinely no fact of the matter about whether I mean addition. (5) The same reasoning applies to logical vocabulary: my past use of 'therefore' or 'valid' is also finitely manifested, so there's no fact about whether I mean standard logical consequence or some deviant version (tvalidity) that agrees up to now but diverges on long proofs. (6) If there's no fact about logical consequence, then there's no fact about whether step (4) actually follows from (3). (7) But the skeptical argument is supposed to show that (4) follows from (3). (8) So the argument undermines its own entitlement to be accepted. (9) The only escape is to deny that mental or social facts are subject to (3)—but that looks like the hardest move to defend, and Kripke seemed to think so too.

I really want to keep (1)-(3) because they capture the core skeptical challenge, and I'm okay with giving up (9) if I have to, but I don't want to just throw away the whole framework. I have a hunch the slip is somewhere in the jump from arithmetic rules to logical ones—maybe logical constants are learned differently?—but I can't formalize the objection. Which step should I reject, and what's the price? If I keep (1)-(3) and reject something else, do I end up with a stable skeptical position or just a mess?
