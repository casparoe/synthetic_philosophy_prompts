I have recently been appointed to an interdisciplinary advisory board evaluating whether highly idealized integrated climate-economy models should guide long-term carbon-policy decisions. The board includes economists, climate scientists, and philosophers of science; my task is to articulate the conceptual standards for judging these models. This responsibility has made the old debate about unrealistic assumptions in science — especially the argument, associated with Milton Friedman, that predictive success should outweigh the literal truth of a model’s premises — suddenly personal and practical.

I am not asking for a general survey. I need a defended agenda: among the open conceptual questions surrounding idealization, unrealistic assumptions, and the so-called F-twist, which should a small working group of philosophers and methodologists prioritize over the next three years, and in what order? Defend the ordering with explicit criteria — tractability for conceptual rather than empirical work, upstream dependence (whether resolving one question unlocks others), and direct relevance to whether policymakers can legitimately trust counterfactual predictions generated by structurally false models.

In particular, treat the following tensions not as isolated puzzles but as competing priorities:

First, whether a coherent distinction can be drawn between representational idealization (the model misdescribes its target system) and procedural idealization (the reasoning steps from model output to policy recommendation involve unrealistic simplifications), and whether Friedman’s defense applies symmetrically to both. If it applies to only one, the evaluation of integrated assessment models shifts dramatically.

Second, whether predictive accuracy for historical or baseline scenarios can ever license counterfactual policy recommendations when the unrealistic assumptions concern precisely the mechanisms that the intervention is meant to alter — for instance, assuming perfect market adjustment in a model used to justify carbon-tax design.

Third, whether there is an overlooked mechanism-transparency requirement that ought to sit alongside predictive success, and whether it is genuinely independent or collapses back into a disguised demand for realistic assumptions.

Fourth, whether the F-twist should be classified as a methodological principle about model evaluation or as an empirical claim about which kinds of falsehood are benign — and what hangs on that classification for interdisciplinary collaboration.

Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citation guide; I need reasoning I can deploy without library access. For each priority you rank, explain what conceptual clarification or evidence over the next couple of years would cause you to revise its position upward or downward. Also flag any pair of priorities you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable given current conceptual resources.
