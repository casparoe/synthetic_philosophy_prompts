I am delivering the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary symposium on classical political economy and imperial governance, held in a university lecture hall before a deliberately split audience. About half the room are historians of economic thought who know Adam Smith’s Wealth of Nations closely, including his chapter on colonies and his scattered remarks on the East India Company; the other half are scholars from post-colonial studies and political philosophy who know Smith mainly as a canonical market thinker and are skeptical of recruiting him for anti-imperial argument. I need roughly eight minutes—about 1,100 words—that do not read like a literature review.

Here is the structural constraint. I want to build the remarks around a somewhat under-discussed tension: Smith treats colonies as economically ruinous—the cost of defense and administration exceeds revenue—but he also treats this economic failure as a symptom of a deeper political failure, namely the substitution of arbitrary authority for the rule of law and the suppression of local markets by mercantile privilege. I want to claim that, for Smith, economic inefficiency and moral corruption are not two separate objections to empire but the same objection expressed in different registers: the mercantile system that makes colonies expensive is precisely the system that makes them tyrannical. This should shape what the speech can and cannot say. I cannot praise Smith as a straightforward anti-imperialist hero, because he still accepted slavery in the colonies and did not extend his sympathy for American independence consistently to other colonized peoples. Nor can I dismiss him as merely an apologist for capital, because his critique of empire is genuinely rooted in a commitment to liberty that undermines the logic of domination from within.

Please take a committed position: either defend the view that Smith’s economic and moral critiques of empire are genuinely continuous and mutually reinforcing, despite his glaring omissions, or argue that the economic and moral strands ultimately pull in opposite directions—that his efficiency argument actually depends on conditions he was unwilling to apply universally—and that recognizing this fracture is more intellectually honest and more useful for the audience than resolving it. Choose one side clearly, give your reasons, and let that choice determine the arc of the speech. The remarks must work for both halves of the room: precise enough that Smith scholars will recognize accurate references to the Navigation Acts, the division of labor, and his comparison between American and Indian colonial administration, but accessible enough that non-specialists can follow the conceptual architecture without prior study of eighteenth-century economics. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to secondary literature.

At the end, include a brief, self-critical note on where the interpretation takes liberties with Smith’s text or relies on contested readings—specifically, whether the continuity between economic and moral critique is as tight as the speech suggests, or whether I am overstating the coherence of his position for rhetorical effect. I want the answer to take a definite stand rather than laying out both options neutrally.
