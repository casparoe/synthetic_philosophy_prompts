As a retired condensed-matter physicist now auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of science, I am preparing an assessment for an upper-level course on the relationship between philosophical analysis and scientific practice. I would like you to design a single in-class essay examination—answerable in approximately ninety minutes and running to about one thousand words—together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme.

The examination should address the question of whether philosophy is useful to science, specifically engaging with the well-known criticisms associated with Steven Weinberg (who likened philosophy of science to ornithology for birds), Stephen Hawking (who argued that philosophers have not kept pace with the development of modern physics), and Richard Feynman (who compared philosophers debating scientific foundations to people arguing over the rules of chess while the game is being played). The philosophers’ standard defenses should also be available for students to draw upon.

The question must not be answerable by simple summary of these critics or of standard rebuttals. Instead, it should require students to apply a specific tripartite taxonomy distinguishing three modes of philosophical engagement with science: heuristic framing, in which philosophy guides the selection and structuring of research problems; conceptual disambiguation, in which philosophy resolves ambiguities in theoretical terms that obstruct empirical testing; and normative boundary-setting, in which philosophy prescribes what counts as legitimate explanation or admissible evidence within a discipline. Students should be asked to apply this framework to two precise historical episodes—first, the influence of Machian philosophical considerations on the conceptual development of special relativity, and second, the role of interpretive debates in the design and interpretation of experiments testing Bell-type inequalities—in order to determine whether Weinberg’s charge of disciplinary irrelevance, Hawking’s claim of empirical obsolescence, and Feynman’s charge of pragmatic irrelevance apply uniformly across all three modes, or whether each critic targets only one mode while leaving the others untouched.

Please provide the following:

1. The exam question itself, clearly stating the time allowance, expected length, and the requirement to use both historical episodes together with the tripartite taxonomy.

2. A model answer of approximately nine hundred to one thousand words demonstrating how a strong response should separate the three critics’ objections by mode, deploy the two historical cases with precision, avoid collapsing philosophy’s utility into a single undifferentiated category, and arrive at a nuanced, conditional conclusion about philosophy’s value.

3. A marking scheme out of twenty-five points built around five must-hit analytical moves. For each move, state the point allocation (with values summing to twenty-five), the specific content or reasoning step required, and a brief one-line description of how candidates typically fail to achieve it.

Please do not ask students for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from Weinberg, Hawking, or Feynman in quotation marks unless you have fetched and verified the exact source text; paraphrase their positions without quotation marks. Avoid invoking highly specialized recent theories, obscure technical formalisms, or niche interpretive debates that would not be expected knowledge in a general philosophy-of-science curriculum. The difficulty should come from the conceptual architecture—specifically, the demand to map familiar criticisms onto an unfamiliar three-mode taxonomy and to sustain that mapping across two distinct scientific contexts—rather than from recall of obscure material.
