Trying to make sense of Braudel's levels of time and I think I've constructed a reductio that dosen't quite land where I want it.

(1) Braudel distinguishes geographical time (almost static), social time (slow cycles), and individual/event time (surface foam).
(2) If an explanation at level L cites only factors at L, then adding factors from lower levels (faster, more particular) should never change the explanatory outcome.
(3) So if a drought and tax revolt are both explained at the social level by long demographic pressure, then citing the specific king's tax policy shouldn't change whether the revolt was explained.
(4) But if citing the king's policy dosen't change the explanation, then the king's policy is causally irrelevant to the revolt.
(5) Generalizing: every specific event-factor is causally irrelevant to the events it participates in, because the real explanation is always at the structural level.
(6) So either every specific event is overdetermined by structures (making individual agency an illusion), or Braudel's levels are not explanatory levels at all but just descriptive categories.
(7) Both options seem wrong, but I can't see which premise fails.

The part that feels slippery is between (3) and (4) — maybe there's a difference between 'not needed for this explanation' and 'not causally relevant'? But then I'm not sure how Braudel's framework avoids collapsing into either determinism or useless taxonomy. Which step is the weak link, and what does fixing it cost for the idea of long-duration structures?
