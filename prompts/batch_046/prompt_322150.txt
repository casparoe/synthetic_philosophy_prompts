Six months ago my spouse died from cerebral malaria, and I have since become sole trustee of the charitable foundation we established together. By profession I am a control-systems engineer, and my reasoning about resource allocation now runs instinctively through that vocabulary—error signals, loop gain, bandwidth, observability, stability margins, noise floors, and feedback topology—rather than through economics or normative ethics. Before I commit our capital, I need to understand whether the cause-prioritization framework that structures global-health and philanthropic allocation—specifically the trio of importance, tractability, and neglectedness—can be translated systematically into control-theoretic terms without smuggling in foreign assumptions. Please restate each pillar as precisely as possible: for instance, whether importance corresponds to the magnitude of an unregulated error signal, tractability to the available loop gain or controllability of an intervention, and neglectedness to low observability or high noise in existing feedback channels. Then provide a residue report: identify which elements translate cleanly, which survive only through conceptual stretching, and which parts of the framework—perhaps the counterfactual logic of funding displacement, or the normative aggregation across incommensurable outcomes—have no structural counterpart in control theory. I want to know whether that residue exposes a genuine limitation in control vocabulary for moral allocation, or whether it reveals that parts of the cause-prioritization framework are less formally defined than they appear. This is not a request for analogies to justify a predetermined gift; I am asking for an honest translation whose value lies equally in what it carries and in what it fails to carry, because that failure will determine how I architect the foundation's grant-making logic going forward.
