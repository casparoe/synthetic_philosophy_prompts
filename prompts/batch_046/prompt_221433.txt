Write a dialogue between two equally competent moral philosophers—Dr. Rena and Dr. Vale—debating whether moral preferences can ever be cleanly separated from cognitive biases. Frame the exchange around a specific, non-standard diagnostic that Rena defends and Vale attacks: the “counterfactual retention protocol.” Under this protocol, a preference counts as unbiased only if (1) the agent still holds it after adversarial framing that targets every identifiable bias vector (framing effects, status-quo bias, in-group favoritism, etc.), and (2) the preference exhibits diachronic stability across multiple deliberative contexts with varied emotional and informational inputs. Vale contends that this protocol is circular, because selecting which “bias vectors” to neutralize already presupposes a normative baseline that favors certain preferences, and because diachronic stability is equally compatible with a deeply entrenched, structurally reinforced bias. Rena replies that circularity can be avoided by appealing to inter-subjective convergence under the protocol, but Vale counters that such convergence may merely track shared social conditioning rather than unbiased preference.

Require real back-and-forth: each speaker must respond directly to the other’s last point, not talk past it. Neither should be a strawman. Keep the discussion in the weeds—avoid generic claims like “bias is bad” or “all preferences are subjective.” Instead, have them dispute whether the retention protocol actually distinguishes bias from preference or merely re-labels normatively approved preferences as “unbiased,” and whether Vale’s circularity objection can be met without begging the question.

Structure the response as follows:
- Setup (~120 words): Introduce Rena and Vale, state their disciplinary backgrounds briefly, and precisely define the counterfactual retention protocol and the stakes of their disagreement.
- The Dialogue (~800 words): The full exchange, including Rena’s defense of adversarial framing plus diachronic stability, Vale’s circularity and shared-conditioning objections, Rena’s convergence reply, and Vale’s final counter—ending on an open point, not a resolution.
- Unresolved Tension (~200 words): Identify whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, pinpoint the exact argumentative stall, and specify what further condition or distinction would be required to break the deadlock—without actually breaking it.
