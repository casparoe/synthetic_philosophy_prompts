After retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have turned to philosophy and found myself preoccupied with how societies metabolize defeat into enduring collective memory. I have reached a set of verdicts about particular national myths that I cannot unify into a single framework, and I want to know whether a coherent principle exists behind them or whether I must surrender one of my judgments.

My verdicts are these.

First, the American Confederate "Lost Cause" narrative transforms military defeat into a claim of moral superiority and deferred historical vindication. I judge this a lost-cause myth in the strict sense: the enemy’s victory is treated as illegitimate, and the defeat is projected to be reversed by history.

Second, the modern Israeli narrative of Masada (the Roman siege of 73–74 CE and the mass suicide of the defenders) also converts defeat into endurance, but I think it operates differently. Here the defeat is neither denied nor deferred; it is embraced as a sacrificial boundary condition—what I call sacrificial inversion. The myth establishes a non-negotiable limit on surrender rather than promising eventual reversal.

Third, the Serbian myth of Kosovo Polje (1389) treats battlefield defeat as a cosmological promise of eventual restoration and moral election. I feel this is closer to the Confederate case in its temporal logic (defeat now, victory eventually) but closer to Masada in its willingness to glorify death over survival.

Fourth, the British narrative of Dunkirk (1940) reframes a strategic evacuation after military defeat as a moral triumph—the "Dunkirk spirit"—yet I hesitate to call it a lost-cause myth at all. There is no claim that the enemy’s victory was illegitimate, nor any expectation of historical reversal; the defeat is simply redescribed without being denied. I call this re-described defeat.

Fifth, the Alamo narrative (1836) converts total defeat into an immediate call for revenge and eventual victory at San Jacinto. I classify it with the Confederate case.

Now my difficulty. Is there one principle—perhaps something like "a lost-cause myth requires both the denial of the enemy’s moral legitimacy and the projection of eventual reversal"—that correctly sorts these? Or must I split my taxonomy? Specifically, can I maintain that Masada and Kosovo belong to different categories despite both glorifying death? And what should my principle predict about a case I have not listed: the Japanese public framing of the 1945 surrender, initially treated as an unacceptable defeat but subsequently restructured under occupation as a "peaceful transformation"?
