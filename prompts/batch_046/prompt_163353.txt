The long-running comparative debate about why mechanized, sustained industrial growth emerged in Britain rather than in Qing China — discussed variously as the Needham question and the Great Divergence — has accumulated explanations (resource geography, institutional incentives, colonial surplus, cultural dispositions, historical contingency) without reaching convergence. I am less interested in adding another causal factor than in diagnosing why the dispute stalls. Do the competing accounts actually answer different questions hidden under one label — some seeking sufficient conditions for British take-off, others necessary conditions absent in China, others questioning whether "industrial modernity" is a unified outcome that can be compared across societies? Or does the impasse reflect a deeper, unacknowledged disagreement about the philosophy of history — specifically, about whether explaining divergence requires demonstrating that Chinese social structures were counterfactually stable enough to have supported a British-style path, or whether such stability conditions are irrelevant? Give a diagnosis: is this verbal, substantive, or a clash of bedrock methodological intuitions? And specify what would count as progress — a formal framework for counterfactual comparison, a conceptual clarification of "modernity," or an empirical result — or argue that none should be expected because the sides are answering incommensurable questions.
