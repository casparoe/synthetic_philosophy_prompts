There's this thing people call Chesterton's fence — don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up — and in my philosphy of politics reading group someone used it last week to defend leaving an old zoning law alone even though nobody could remember its original purpose. I got annoyed because it felt like the slogan was doing three jobs at once. Can you lay out the different precise theses it could be expressing? Like, is there a trivial epistemic version that's just be careful, a Burkean version about accumulated social wisdom being smarter than individuals, and maybe a stronger evolutionary or functionalist version? For each, say whether it's actually defensable, whether it's false, and whether it really supports keeping the law. Also, which reading was probably the one my classmate meant? And does the argument change if the fence was deliberately designed versus if it grew up organically — like a zoning board rule versus an emergant neighborhood boundary? I want a menu I can bring back, not just it depends on context.
