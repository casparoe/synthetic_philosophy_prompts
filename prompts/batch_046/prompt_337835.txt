As a journalist covering science, policy, and conceptual debates for a general audience, I am trying to convert the philosophical problem of harm aggregation—specifically the tension between holding a single grave wrong incomparable with innumerable trivial injuries and permitting those injuries to sum into a collective wrong—into a reusable editorial checklist. I apply this repeatedly when reviewing regulatory proposals, public-health studies, and environmental impact assessments in which a concentrated, severe harm is weighed against a diffuse population-level risk that unfolds with generational latency. The checklist must be something I can run in about fifteen minutes without specialist consultation: an ordered sequence of questions or tests, with explicit criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, clear markers for where calculation ends and human judgment takes over, and explicit misfire flags for arguments that invoke aggregation illegitimately or reject it by appealing to non-comparability when the harms actually share a measurable common metric. Because standard aggregation intuitions collapse when damage is both sub-threshold for individual experience and separated in time from other instances, I need the procedure to include a specific module for what I am calling "distributed latency harms"—cases where temporal dispersion and imperceptibility jointly block both direct moral comparison and standard probabilistic summation—without allowing that module to become an automatic excuse for dismissing diffuse risk.

Please construct this procedure, then apply it to three cases: first, an industrial-chemical restriction that eliminates a rare acute poisoning in a small cohort while imposing imperceptible, decades-delayed cellular stress on millions; second, a decision to delay carbon-capture investment on the grounds that per-capita climate damage in any single year falls below individual noticeability; and third, a universal vaccination program that causes transient, individually negligible discomfort to prevent a severe epidemic in a small subgroup. Include at least one result that is counterintuitive or unwelcome to me as a writer generally sympathetic to precautionary regulation, and clearly distinguish the steps that turn on empirical facts from those that depend on value judgments about comparability, temporal discounting, and distributive fairness.
