I am a Year 13 student writing an extended essay for my philosophy coursework on long-range ethics. I have been examining the dispute between strong longtermists, who maintain that the welfare of sentient beings in the far future should dominate present moral reasoning, and presentist pluralists, who hold that obligations to merely possible future people are severely constrained and that present goods carry independent weight.

Please draft the joint statement that a committed strong longtermist and a committed presentist pluralist could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration. Structure your response exactly as follows, in British English and in formal academic prose suitable for an appendix:

1. Agreed claims (approximately 200 words): a numbered list of propositions both sides can endorse concerning the potential scale of future civilisation, the epistemic limits of long-range forecasting, and the distinction between actual and merely possible moral patients.

2. Disagreements table: present the residual disagreements in a table with three columns — Issue; Strong longtermist formulation (worded so that side accepts it); Presentist pluralist formulation (worded so that side accepts it). Include at least: (a) whether aggregate welfare is the dominant moral consideration irrespective of temporal location; and (b) whether there exists an independent 'archival duty' — an obligation to preserve the material and cultural conditions for future civilisation that does not derive from welfare maximisation.

3. Cruxes and predictive tests (approximately 250 words): identify the precise sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one party. I suspect the central cruxes are, first, whether continuity of cultural transmission possesses intrinsic value beyond its welfare effects, and second, how epistemic humility about far-future predictions should be weighed against the expected magnitude of outcomes. For each crux, describe a concrete hypothetical case or piece of reasoning both camps agree is relevant — for example, a binding two-century international treaty that achieves verifiable knowledge-preservation metrics but produces negligible present welfare gains — and record, in advance, what each side would predict about whether that case generates a decisive moral reason.

4. Verbal disputes (approximately 150 words): assess whether any portion of the disagreement is purely verbal, specifying which terms (such as 'value', 'obligation', or 'prediction') are operating with different extensions that could be mapped onto a shared framework without resolving the underlying dispute.

Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific texts. Write in British English throughout.
