Consider an argument that extends Jain anekāntavāda into the logic of reference, which seems to undermine every definite assertion. (1) Anekāntavāda holds that every entity possesses infinitely many aspects, each grasped by a distinct naya (standpoint). (2) Syādvāda permits seven-fold qualified predication—e.g., "in some respect P, in some respect not-P"—provided each attribution is restricted to its proper standpoint. (3) Take the predicate "is self-identical over time." From a dravyārthika naya (the substantial standpoint of permanence), the object remains the same; from a paryāyārthika naya (the modal standpoint of constant change), it is never the same entity twice. (4) So syādvāda allows us to say that, in one respect, the object is self-identical, and in another respect, it is not. (5) But self-identity is not an ordinary property like color or weight; it is a logical precondition for being the subject of any true statement. (6) If an entity genuinely lacks self-identity in any unqualified sense, then it cannot serve as a stable referent, and no definite claim about it can be made. (7) Therefore, applying syādvāda to logical properties produces epistemic collapse: every assertion lacks a definite subject. (8) Yet Jain philosophers clearly intend to reject specific rival doctrines—such as the Ājīvika thesis of absolute determinism (niyati)—which requires that their own denials have definite targets and that falsehoods attach to definite positions. (9) Hence anekāntavāda appears self-defeating if it reaches this far.

Which premise or step should be rejected? Explain why that step initially looks plausible, and discuss whether the standard Jain distinction between substance (dravya) and modes (paryāya) can block the slide without smuggling in an absolutist commitment that anekāntavāda was designed to avoid.
