You are acting as both a philosopher of epistemology with particular expertise in the ethics of belief and a poet attentive to how verse form can carry conceptual argument. Before composing anything, please take a moment to think carefully and note briefly: in William James’s taxonomy of belief-options, what makes an option live, forced, and momentous all at once, and why does Clifford’s universal evidentialist prohibition—that belief without sufficient evidence is always wrong—fail to apply there without itself becoming a hidden commitment with moral weight? Then write a poem of about 20 lines in blank verse, structured explicitly as a palinode, a poetic retraction, spoken by an unnamed voice that first affirms the evidentialist rule and then, at a clear volta near line 11 or 12, partially retracts not the rule itself but its universal scope. The speaker should realize, through the poem’s own movement, that in a forced option—where belief and disbelief are both live, unavoidable, and genuinely consequential—the refusal to commit is not neutrality but a stance with its own ethical weight. Use enjambment to break lines exactly where evidence runs out, and allow the iambic meter to loosen and become more irregular in the lines after the volta, so that the rhythm itself enacts the loss of evidential constraint. Do not name James or Clifford; render their positions through a sustained image—perhaps a bridge suspended over a gap, or a door that must be opened or left shut—so that someone familiar with the debate recognizes both views and someone unfamiliar still finds the poem coherent. The poem should close without resolving the tension: the speaker should remain caught between the duty not to believe without proof and the impossibility of suspending judgment when suspension is itself a choice. Present your brief internal brainstorm first, then the poem. The result should be philosophically precise enough not to misrepresent either position, and should work as poetry even for a reader who has never encountered the debate.
