I'm preparing a ninety-minute first-year philosophy tutorial for eight undergraduates who have just encountered G.E. Moore's proof of an external world and arrived convinced it is either circular or a rhetorical sleight-of-hand; they have no prior epistemology beyond a passing mention of Cartesian doubt. I want a teaching plan—not the lecture script itself—for leading them to grasp Moore's specific logical maneuver: that he supplies a demonstrative proof of particular external objects (his hands) precisely in order to show that the general skeptical premise ("no one knows there is an external world") can be false, even if he cannot dialectically compel the skeptic to abandon her framework. The plan should lay out the exact dependency order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced—what must be in place before the distinction between proving a particular and undermining a universal premise can even be articulated; it should identify the single concrete scenario or modified thought experiment that should carry the idea, specifying what that scenario must include (a direct perceptual entitlement that does not pass through an inference) and what it must exclude (any reliance on scientific testimony or on analogical reasoning from other minds); it should map the predictable misconceptions—circularity, the confusion between proof and persuasion, the belief that Moore is offering an empirical hypothesis rather than a logical demonstration, and the assumption that he owes the skeptic an answer within her own terms—indicating precisely where in the sequence each should be anticipated and defused; it should propose a concrete check for understanding that reveals whether the students have absorbed the logical structure rather than merely memorized the hand example; and it should state explicitly what should be left out at this level (for instance, Moore's broader common-sense philosophy, later analytic responses from Stroud or Wright, and any technical discussion of external-world skepticism as a "closure" problem) with a brief justification for each omission. In constructing this plan, please separate what is philosophically settled about Moore's argument from what remains genuinely contested among specialists—particularly whether his proof succeeds against the skeptic or merely changes the subject—and do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
