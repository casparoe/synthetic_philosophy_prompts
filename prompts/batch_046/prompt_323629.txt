I'm preparing discussion notes for an upper-level philosophy of language seminar, and I've noticed that my students keep falling back on the slogan "water is necessarily H2O" as if it were a single, self-evident thesis. They invoke it to settle debates about Twin Earth, about whether science discovers modal facts, and about how kind terms differ from proper names, but I suspect they are running together several distinct claims. Could you disentangle the precise theses compressed into that sentence?

In particular, please separate: (i) the semantic claim about whether "water" functions as a rigid designator for a natural kind, and whether that rigidity is de jure (fixed by linguistic convention) or merely de facto (tracking H2O across worlds without being semantically anchored to it); (ii) the metaphysical claim that being composed of H2O is essential to the substance we call water; (iii) the epistemic claim that this modal fact is knowable only a posteriori; and (iv) any hidden commitment about how kind-term rigidity compares to the rigidity of proper names like Hesperus. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or genuinely false. Then say which reading someone is likely invoking when they use the slogan to argue that chemistry yields necessary truths. Finally, address this more specific wrinkle: if we treat "water" as only de facto rigid—so that Twin Earth speakers could call XYZ "water" without semantic error—does the necessary a posteriori thesis still hold for our term, or does the distinction between de jure and de facto rigidity cause the slogan to collapse into something much weaker?
