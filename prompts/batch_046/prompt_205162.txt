I've been thinking about Zhuangzi's take on death—specifically the bit with the butterfly and the story about him drumming on a tub when his wife dies. Most people read it as 'death is just transformation of qi, so don't grieve.' But I think that's too simple. My view is that Zhuangzi isn't actually making a metaphyscial claim about qi at all; he's performing a kind of theraputic dissolution of the very framework that lets us ask 'what happens after death?' The sage doesn't know more about death—he knows less, in a deliberate way. The problem is this seems to undercut itself: if there's no stable self to have insights, then who is the sage and why should we listen? So I'm treating it as an 'as-if' stance rather than a doctrine. What would actually count against this? I don't want the usual 'he really does believe in transformation' reply—I'm asking what evidence in the text, or what philosphical pressure, would show that this self-underminning 'as-if' reading collapses into either standard metaphysical naturalism or just incoherance. Rank the defeaters, and say which ones a defender of my view could just absorb without damage.
