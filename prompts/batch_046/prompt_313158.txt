I'm organizing a discussion for my high school's philosophy of science club—mostly juniors and seniors who have heard Galileo fought the Church and assume he was secretly anti-religious or just using astronomy to dodge authority. I want to teach Galileo's actual hermeneutic framework for interpreting scripture in light of natural knowledge, specifically his claim that biblical language about the physical world is accommodated to ordinary understanding, and that conflict only arises when an interpreter mistakes a probable, appearance-based opinion—such as geocentrism derived from everyday sensory experience—for a demonstrative conclusion that should override science. Rather than delivering a lecture, I need a teaching plan: what prior idea must be firmly in place before they can understand why Galileo saw this as an error in interpretation rather than an attack on faith? Which single carrying example should anchor the session—I am considering the passage in Joshua about the sun standing still, but I need to know precisely what that example must illustrate and what pitfalls it should avoid? In what sequence will misconceptions surface—likely that Galileo was merely protecting himself from punishment, that he believed science always supersedes religion, or that he rejected Christianity entirely—and at which point in the explanation should each be absorbed? How can I check whether they have genuinely grasped the distinction between demonstrative knowledge and accommodated probable opinion, as opposed to simply repeating that the Bible is not a science textbook? Finally, what should I deliberately omit at this level—perhaps the technical mathematics of Copernican orbits or the biographical details of his trial—so that the conceptual structure does not collapse into either astronomy or biography?
