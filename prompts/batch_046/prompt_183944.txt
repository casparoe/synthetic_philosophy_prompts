I sit on teh university's digital-arts ethics board and we have to vote thursday on whether to approve funding for an immersive VR installation called The Last Letter. The piece works like this: participants interact with an AI-driven character over about forty minutes, making real choices that shape a shared narrative, and then the character dies in a way that is procedurally tied to those choices. In the pilot study people reported genuine grief, disrupted sleep, and a sense of missing someone — even though they knew the entity was fiction and signed a disclosure form saying so. One faction on teh board thinks this is just the paradox of fiction in a new interactive skin: we have real emotional responses to made-up things constantly, and that doesn't make the experience harmful or deceptive. The other faction argues that because the grief is co-authored through actual decisions rather than passively recieved, the emotional attachment isn't merely fictional in the usual philosophical sense; they want us to treat it like a psych study with debriefing, exclusion criteria, and a follow-up check-in.

Please draft the board's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete recommendation and a timeline for implementation. The reasoning should honestly engage the strongest argument on the losing side, not just dismiss it. Also, please separate what is fairly settled in philosophy of fiction and emotion from what is genuinely contested among people who actually study this; don't present a contested view as the consensus. There's no need for a bibliography or citation list — I just need the memo we can circulate before the meeting.
