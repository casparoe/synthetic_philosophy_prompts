I'm a high school senior, and next Thursday I have been asked to deliver the opening address—roughly six minutes—at my school's annual "Mind and Society" symposium. The room will be split between fellow students, many of whom talk casually about mental-health labels on social media, and visiting faculty that includes a philosopher of science who works on classification. I want the speech to be organized around Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects in psychiatric classification, but I do not want a lecture; I want the idea to do real structural work, shaping what I can and cannot say. Specifically, there is a bind I need the remarks to inhabit rather than resolve: if I praise our counselors for using diagnostic categories to direct support, I risk treating those categories as fixed natural kinds; if I stress that the categories change the people they describe, I risk sounding as though I am undermining the very support systems I am thanking. To keep this from being a generic "labels are social constructs" speech, please ground the philosophical work in a specific hypothetical scenario I have devised: imagine our school introduced a voluntary "cognitive-style profile" for every student that determined study-group assignments, and within two years the measured traits of students in each group converged on the profile descriptions—not because the profiles discovered pre-existing differences, but because the grouping itself reshaped study habits and self-conception. I want this institutional taxonomy to illustrate the looping mechanism in miniature, so the students hear something recognizable from their own lives while the philosopher hears that I understand interactivity is not limited to large psychiatric diagnoses. The tone should be formal but personal, like a student who has actually read the material and is thinking aloud, not a polished TED talk. Please do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to texts I should read; I am writing this without access to a library and need the speech to stand on its own reasoning.
