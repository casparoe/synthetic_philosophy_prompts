In discussions of epistemic luminosity and the KK principle—that knowing p entails knowing that you know p—philosophers often invoke the analogy of a pilot light on a gas appliance: if the burner is lit, the pilot flame is visibly glowing, so there is no state in which the flame is on but the indicator is invisible to anyone nearby, and anyone in a position to observe can tell. This is used to motivate the idea that knowledge is “luminous”: being in the state puts you automatically in a position to know you’re in it, with no gradual or hidden intermediate cases. A colleague has recently pressed a physical disanalogy: real pilot lights can flicker below visibility, be washed out by ambient glare, or sit in a dim “glow” state detectable only from certain angles, and she argues that if knowledge behaves like this, then KK fails rather than holds—there may be cases where you know p but the “indicator” is obscured. Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the epistemic argument versus those that are merely cosmetic: does the flicker correspond to a genuine epistemic possibility (e.g., knowing without being able to tell), or does it import physical contingencies that have no mental analogue? Next, examine what the source domain (combustion indicators) quietly smuggles in about sharp boundaries, third-person observability, and the absence of self-deception that the target domain (knowledge) may lack. Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that KK is supported by the luminosity of mental states—survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the weight all along. If the pilot-light picture collapses, is there a different physical system that could repair the mapping without importing the same problems, or should the luminosity intuition be abandoned altogether?
