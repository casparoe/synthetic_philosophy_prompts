I'm an ABD in philosophy of history and I'm stuck on teh distinction between covering-law and narrative explantion. I cooked up an argument that seems to kill narrative explanation dead, and my advsior says it proves too much but won't tell me where.

(1) Any genuine explanation of an event E must either subsume E under a covering law together with initial conditions, or it doesn't actually explain E — it just describes it.
(2) Historical narratives plainly do answer why-questions (e.g., why did the French Revolution happen?).
(3) So if narratives aren't law-based, they're not explanations at all; they're just chronologies with rhetorical polish.
(4) But if they *are* law-based, then the narrative machinery — selection of events, emplotment, ordering — is redundant, because the law plus conditions does all the explanatory work.
(5) Therefore narrative form either contributes nothing (if laws are present) or fails to explain (if absent).
(6) So there's no distinctive job for narrative explanation; it's either disguised Hempel-style covering-law or not explanation.

This feels wrong because it wipes out Danto-style and Mink-style views without even engaging them. I especially want to hang on to (2). There's also this weird middle case I keep imagining: what if the narrative does the work of picking out which conditions C are relevant, and only *after* that selection does a law apply? That sounds like a third option, but maybe it's just (4) in disguise.

Where does the chain break? Which premise should I ditch, and what does ditching it cost? Also, is my middle case a real escape or a collapse?
