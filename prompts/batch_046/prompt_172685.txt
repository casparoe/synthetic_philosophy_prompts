I've ended up with a kind of split view on the experience machine thing. I think Nozick's intuition is right for what I'd call agentive goods — stuff where the value comes from actually causally shaping the world, like raising a kid or building a bridge. But I think it's wrong for receptive goods, where the value is just in the experience or understanding itself, like grasping a hard proof or hearing a symphony. So I'd plug in for a life of deep understanding and aesthetic richness even if it's all fake, but I wouldn't plug in for a fake life of saving people. What's more, I think there's a weird middle ground: if you're in a 'partial machine' where your reasoning is real but your sensory input is simulated, then solving a real math problem inside the machine should still count for well-being because the cognitive acheivement is genuinely yours, even though the 'paper' you write on is virtual. So my view is that well-being tracks real causal efficacy in the domain that defines the good — physical for agentive goods, cognitive for intellectual ones, untill you hit some boundary I haven't fully mapped.

What would actually count against this? Not just the generic 'contact with reality' objection, but specifically things that show the split between agentive and receptive is unstable. Would it be fatal if we found that even purely mental achievements depend on some minimal causal interaction with the world? Or that people in partial machines gradually lose the ability to do real reasoning? What about the problem that my view makes well-being depend on a taxonomy of goods that might be culturally variable? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones I'd probably concede versus dismiss. Also, is there a risk that I've just qualified the experience machine away into unfalsifiability by saying 'it depends on the good'? I want an honest map of how exposed this position is.
