Construct a decision tree that sorts actual positions on the inefficacy objection—applied to climate emissions, meat consumption, and democratic voting—by the answers respondents give to concrete diagnostic cases, not by the labels they prefer.

Start with the commitment: "In each case, my single act makes no detectable difference to whether the harm happens." Then branch through four dimensions, each framed as a specific scenario or principle a respondent can accept or reject without knowing the literature:

A. The sufficient-set test: If your act is one of thousands that together cause harm, but removing only yours leaves the harm unchanged, is that enough to make your act wrong?
B. The domain-parity test: Must climate emissions, meat purchases, and votes in large elections be assessed by the same moral logic, or does the replacement structure of markets and the tie-breaking structure of elections justify treating them differently?
C. The non-causal-participation test: Can joining a harmful collective practice be wrong even when you cause no additional harm—say, because it expresses solidarity with the practice, takes an unfair share of a collective good, or violates a fair-share rule?
D. The unit-of-assessment test: Is the proper target of moral evaluation the single act, a recurring pattern (e.g., one's diet or voting record), or one's structural role?

Build the tree from the logical space first: list the combinations these four choices allow, then place named positions in the occupied cells—pure inefficacy acceptance; uniform collective-participation duty; probabilistic or expected-harm reasoning; fair-share or distributive obligation; domain-differentiated views; and institutional or structuralist rejection of individual duty. Identify any empty cells—combinations permitted by the logic but apparently held by no one—and briefly diagnose whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored (for instance, accepting non-causal wrongness in climate but denying it in voting despite parallel probabilistic structures).

Include 6–8 leaves total, calibrated for an audience of philosophy undergraduates familiar with basic moral theory but not with the specialist literature. Flag any pair of leaves that the four branch dimensions fail to separate. Do not ask for bibliographies, named scholars, or citation lists; the tree should be usable by someone reasoning from first principles.
