I'm a compliance analyst at a regional power exchange. Every tuesday I get a new bidding protocol for the day-ahead market and I have about 45 minutes to flag whether any bidder strategy is strictly dominated in mixed strategies, and whether iterating that elimination collapses the rationalizable set in a way that violates our fairness rules. My director signs off, she's not technical, and she only cares if the final rationalizable set includes outcomes where one party captures more than 60% of surplus. I need a reusable procedeure I can run in under half an hour — a checklist with ordered tests, a flowchart in words, and clear flags for where I have to use judgment because the theory doesn't settle it.

The protocols are often extensive-form with imperfect recall — bidders forget their own earlier bids — so I need the steps to work on behavorial strategies, not just normal-form mixtures. I also need the procedure to catch the off-path conditioning problem: a strategy looks dominated only if you condition on reaching an information set that the strategy itself assigns zero probablity to. Tell me when that's a legitimate elimination step and when it's a cheat, especially since these designs sometimes involve correlation devices that might sit outside the convex hull of independent mixtures.

Please build me:
- The ordered steps (pure-strategy dominance first, then mixed over same support, then cross-support checks, etc.)
- A small set of test conditions I can apply line-by-line to a protocol spec.
- Three worked cases: (1) a simple 2x2 auction where collapse is clean; (2) an extensive-form protocol with imperfect recall where the procedure throws a false positive and I have to ignore it; (3) a case where the rationalizable set still includes an unfair outcome (>60% surplus) and the procedure forces me to report it even though the designers will hate me.
- Explicit escalation points — where I should hand off to the math team instead of guessing.
- An honest list of what the procedure can't do, particularly around correlation structures that don't decompose into product distributions.

Keep it practical. I'm printing this and sticking it to my cubicle wall.
