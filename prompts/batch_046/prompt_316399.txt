I'm drafting an essay for an upper-level course in early modern philosophy, and I'd appreciate your critical comments before I revise the argument. My central claim is that Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education is usually misread as a behaviorist manual for producing obedient subjects; instead, I argue it contains an implicit theory of pedagogical non-arbitrariness that parallels his political account of legitimate authority. Specifically, I distinguish between "structural conditioning," in which the tutor arranges environment, diet, and routine to shape habit without issuing explicit commands, and "arbitrary command," in which the child is subjected to the tutor's unaccountable will. I propose that Locke's prescriptions—cold baths, plain clothing, sparse diet—belong to the first category because they are transparent and rule-governed, whereas the beating and will-breaking he warns against belong to the second. From this I derive a stronger, perhaps anachronistic thesis: that Locke's ideal education aims not at obedience but at a form of retrospective self-consent, in which the mature rational agent can endorse the habits that formed him precisely because they were never imposed as capricious decrees. I worry that Locke never applies the language of arbitrary power or tacit consent to child-rearing, and that my reading may downplay genuinely coercive passages, such as his insistence on breaking the child's will early.

Please assess the accuracy of this Lockean interpretation, whether the parallel with his political theory is illuminating or forced, and whether the essay's structure—moving from pedagogical method to political analogy—holds together. Respond in no more than 300 words. Lead with a one-sentence bottom line on the thesis's overall viability, then use brief bullet points addressing accuracy, the analogy, and one structural revision.
