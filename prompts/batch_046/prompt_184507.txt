Two colleagues in my moral philosophy reading circle have been arguing about whether the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism collapses into a general prohibition on redistribution. The first, Marcus, contends that if we take the objection seriously—namely, that utilitarianism wrongly treats society as a single subject of experience and permits sacrificing one for the aggregate good—then even mild progressive taxation becomes unjustified, since it deliberately makes the wealthy somewhat worse off for the benefit of others. He claims this reductio shows the objection is either vacuous or applies only to extreme cases like framing an innocent person. The second, Elena, replies that Marcus conflates two kinds of aggregation: she distinguishes “intramodal trade-offs,” where goods and burdens are distributed within one continuous life, from “intermodal allocations,” where they are assigned across genuinely distinct experiential centres. On her view, progressive taxation is an intramodal adjustment within a shared cooperative scheme, not an intermodal sacrifice, whereas utilitarianism treats both as fungible entries in a single welfare ledger. Marcus then presses a structural counter-example: in large-scale economic policy, no individual agent explicitly selects who bears the cost; harm is diffuse and statistical. He argues that utilitarianism actually respects separateness here, because it demands that every affected individual’s welfare be entered into the calculus separately, whereas rule-based alternatives might ignore dispersed, predictable harms behind a veil of procedural neutrality. Elena responds that this precisely inverts the danger—statistical aggregation is where separateness is most obscured, since specific persons become invisible coefficients, and the objection targets not the absence of individual entries but the illegitimacy of summing them across boundaries.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess Marcus’s reductio about taxation, Elena’s intramodal/intermodal distinction, Marcus’s structural-policy counter-example, and Elena’s reply about statistical invisibility. In each case, say which move is stronger and why, identify any hidden premises each is relying on, and state who is more right overall about what the separateness objection actually prohibits. Please write your answer in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
