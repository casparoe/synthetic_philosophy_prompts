Consider the institutional design found in several modern democracies: a written constitution establishes an explicit amendment procedure—say, supermajorities across legislative chambers and possibly subnational units—while also containing an “eternity clause” that declares certain provisions, such as human dignity, the federal structure, or the democratic form of government itself, to be permanently beyond revision. Alongside this, the same legal order recognizes a separate mechanism, often described as “constituent power” or “constitutional replacement,” through which the people may adopt an entirely new constitution via direct referendum or a specially convened assembly, thereby bypassing the ordinary amendment rules altogether.

This pairing produces a marked asymmetry. Courts in such systems routinely treat proposed amendments that touch the protected provisions as legally void, yet they almost never subject replacement processes to the same procedural scrutiny, treating them instead as expressions of popular sovereignty that stand outside the amendment framework. The replacement mechanism, however, is usually authorized—if at all—by provisions within the very constitution it might supersede.
