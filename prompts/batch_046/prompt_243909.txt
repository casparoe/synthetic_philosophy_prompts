Consider the institutional practice—common in university syllabi, museum exhibits, and diplomatic archives—of framing Cold War origins through the attributive question which superpower started it, organizing primary sources, timelines, and policy briefs into competing blame narratives (Soviet expansion versus American encirclement) rather than into interactionist or structural-systemic accounts. The same institutions routinely explain economic crises or climate dynamics through emergent, multi-causal frameworks, yet revert to a moralized, agent-centered causation when the subject is geopolitical conflict. What philosophical commitments about collective moral agency, historical explanation, and the function of blame does this framing practice presuppose? Does it require viewing states as unitary moral persons capable of bearing culpability in the same sense as individual agents? Can the practice be sustained by a purely structural theory of international relations, or does it necessarily import a conception of causation that privileges decisive actions over systemic interaction? Reconstruct the implicit theory of historiographical blame at work, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and say whether abandoning the who-started-it frame in favor of an emergent-system historiography would force a change in institutional practices—such as ranking documents by culpability or organizing exhibits around decisive moments—or whether those practices could survive under a radically different philosophy of history.
