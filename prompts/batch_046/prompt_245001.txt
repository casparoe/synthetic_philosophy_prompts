Consider the following line of reasoning about Van Fraassen's Reflection principle (RP), which I have been trying to formalize and now fear proves too much.

(1) RP states that if an agent at time t expects her future self at t+1 to hold credence r in proposition P, and she expects no evidential change between t and t+1 other than the reasoning that yields r, then her own credence in P at t ought to be r.

(2) Now suppose an agent, call her S, knows she is about to enter a calibration-lock procedure: she has committed in advance that at t+1 she will accept a controversial inference rule R if and only if her current (t-time) reliability rating for R exceeds a fixed threshold θ; otherwise she will reject R and withhold judgment on P.

(3) S's direct evidence about P is weak—say, she initially leans slightly toward P with credence 0.55. Her reliability rating for R is 0.82, which exceeds θ = 0.75, so she expects to apply R at t+1 and thereby reach credence 0.9 in P.

(4) By (1), RP requires that S's credence in P at t be 0.9, not 0.55.

(5) But S also knows that her reliability rating for R is itself subject to small, unbiased introspective fluctuation: by t+1 it might have drifted to 0.70 (below θ) with probability 0.3, in which case she expects to reach credence 0.3 in P.

(6) Therefore S's unconditional expectation of her t+1 credence is (0.7)(0.9) + (0.3)(0.3) = 0.72. Applying RP to this expectation yields that S's current credence should be 0.72, contradicting (4).

(7) One might reply that RP applies only to the credence S expects conditional on the reasoning path she actually takes, not to the unconditional expectation. But then consider the recursive step: at t, S expects that at t+1 she will apply RP to her own expectation about t+2. To do that, her t+1 self needs a single expected credence for P at t+2, which requires her t+1 self to resolve the same ambiguity—either conditioning on applying R or averaging over possibilities.

(8) If she conditions, she gets one value; if she averages, she gets another. Whichever rule she adopts at t+1, she expects her t+2 self to apply the same rule. This generates a fixed-point equation c = F(c), where c is S's current credence and F encodes the nested expectation structure.

(9) For the parameter values above, F has no fixed point. Hence RP demands that S assign a credence that solves c = F(c), which is impossible.

(10) Even if one adjusts the parameters so that a fixed point exists—say at c* ≈ 0.68—this value is entirely determined by the architecture of the calibration lock and the fluctuation distribution, with no dependence on S's direct evidence about P. Thus RP either demands an impossible credence or wipes out evidential autonomy.

My question: which step should be rejected, and at what cost? I am especially reluctant to abandon RP in its standard diachronic form, and I also want to preserve the idea that rational agents can maintain different direct evidential assessments of P without being forced into convergence. Is the flaw an illicit application of RP across branching reasoning paths? A hidden equivocation between conditioning and averaging? Or is the real problem that the calibration-lock mechanism itself violates a coherence constraint that RP presupposes? Explain why the targeted step looked plausible, then outline what the main standard responses—perhaps a restricted-reflection view that limits RP to evidential updates, a diachronic-coherence view that allows temporary divergence, and a self-trust view that denies the relevance of anticipated fluctuation—each have to pay to reject it.
