As a journalist covering the intersection of political history and moral philosophy, I am drafting a long-form piece on whether the dirty-hands framework—traditionally framed as the tragic collision between private morality and the demands of public office—can survive an extension into collective authorization. I want to apply it to the imposition of martial law in Poland on December 13, 1981, when General Wojciech Jaruzelski suspended Solidarity, interned activists, severed national communications, and justified the measure as a necessary barrier against Soviet invasion. The basic chronology is well known: the declaration, the suppression of the union movement, and Jaruzelski’s eventual role in the Round Table negotiations of 1989 that produced democratic transition. Rather than asking for historical documentation, I want a disciplined conceptual mapping. Specifically, please analyze the episode through dirty-hands theory, but with a twist that I think pushes the framework into unfamiliar territory: treat the moral wrong not merely as a private burden the leader must bear, but as a collective moral debt that can only be rendered permissible—if at all—through a structure of democratic acknowledgment, whether prospective or retrospective. Did the absence of any democratic mandate at the moment of decision structurally disqualify Jaruzelski from invoking dirty hands, regardless of the later Round Table? Who exactly bears the moral residue—the leader, the security apparatus, or the polity that eventually ratified the transition? And running in the opposite direction, does the Polish case show that dirty-hands theory, developed largely around individual tragic choice, misfires when applied to collective political violence that acquires legitimacy only after the fact? Please be precise about where the theory explains the divergence in Polish public memory—why some citizens read the act as preservation and others as bare authoritarianism—and where it is silent. I am especially interested in whether introducing collective acknowledgment collapses the framework into consequentialism or preserves a distinct, non-reducible moral stain, and whether Jaruzelski’s later justifications functioned as the required acknowledgment or merely as political rehabilitation.
