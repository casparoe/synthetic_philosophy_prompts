The historiographical standoff between Ranke's injunction that the historian should show events as they actually occurred and the sceptical line running through E.H. Carr's claim that facts only become historical through selection and interpretation to Hayden White's thesis that historical writing is governed by deep narrative structures—emplotment, tropology, and ideological implication—has persisted for generations without either camp conceding ground. Rankeans continue to insist that archival discipline and source criticism can approximate an unmediated view of the past; Carr and White respond that every account, however well-sourced, imposes form, selects causally relevant events, and employs rhetorical figures that shape what the past means. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not ultimately about whether historians can access the past, but about two incompatible conceptions of explanation—causal-analytic versus narrative-integrative—being compressed into the single term 'historical objectivity.' Is this correct, or is there a hidden empirical dispute about whether narrative form systematically distorts causal inference, or perhaps a verbal dispute in which both sides use 'objectivity' for different achievements—evidential fidelity versus explanatory coherence?

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Analyze whether the stalemate arises because the sides answer different questions under one heading—Ranke asking about evidential warrant, Carr and White asking about the conditions of intelligibility—or because they share a substantive disagreement about the possibility of non-narrative representation of the past. Ground your answer in what each side actually says, not in generic labels. If the disagreement is verbal or cross-purposed, specify the conceptual distinction that would dissolve it. If it is substantive, identify what would count as genuine progress: a formal model of narrative explanation, an actual historiographical study comparing narrative and causal-analytic accounts of the same events, or a reconceptualization of objectivity that accommodates both demands. If no progress should be expected, argue why—whether because the dispute turns on irreconcilable starting intuitions, or because the concept of 'the past as it was' is itself incoherent once narrative form is acknowledged as ineliminable.
