I’ve been trying to settle my own view on determinism, chaos, and moral responsibility, and I’d like an audit of whether these commitments hang together or whether I’m smuggling in incompatible motivations. Here is roughly where I’ve landed. First, I am a determinist about physical processes: every event, including every human deliberation, unfolds from prior states according to fixed natural laws, with no fundamental ontological randomness or uncaused branching. Second, I take chaos theory to establish something stronger than mere practical difficulty: for nonlinear dynamical systems—weather patterns, turbulent flows, and likely neural dynamics—exponential divergence of trajectories means that long-term forecasting is impossible in principle for any observer restricted to macro-level or finitely precise initial conditions, not just a failure of current computing power. Third, on free will I am an incompatibilist: I reject standard compatibilist reconstructions of “could have done otherwise” and hold that genuine moral responsibility requires that the agent had access to genuinely open alternatives, in a sense that strict determinism rules out. Fourth, despite that, I want to claim that the epistemic openness produced by chaotic dynamics is morally significant; because no agent or observer can in principle trace the deterministic chain forward to a unique, accessible future, holding people accountable remains justified—the future is not “written” in any way available to us or to the agent. Fifth, I insist that whether a system is deterministic is a purely metaphysical question about law-governed evolution, entirely independent of our predictive capacities, measurement precision, or computational limitations.

My worry is that I’m helping myself to different intuitions in different contexts. The pairing of (1) and (2) might look contradictory—if determinism holds, shouldn’t a Laplace-style demon with perfect knowledge still be able to predict?—but I suspect that dissolves once we distinguish ontological determination from epistemic accessibility, since chaos only blocks prediction from finitely specified macro-states. More seriously, (3) and (4) seem to pull in opposite directions: if responsibility requires metaphysical openness that determinism denies, then the mere fact that chaos hides the future from us should not rescue it. At the same time, (5) denies that metaphysical status should depend on epistemic access, yet (4) seems to derive moral significance precisely from such access. So I am unsure whether the package is consistent with minor adjustments, or whether one of the central planks has to fall. Please audit these commitments in order of severity: identify which tensions are only apparent and dissolve on inspection, which are genuinely incompatible, what exactly would have to be given up in each case, and whether there is a least-cost revision that preserves the spirit of the view. Be specific about whether (3) or (4) is the weak link, and about whether (5) undermines (4) from within.
