I'm drafting a scene in my novel where Elena, a retired constitutional drafter, gives the keynote at the retirement gala for the old Precommitment Council — the group that wrote binding Ulysses clauses into law so future legislatures couldn't undo basic rights, including a second-order clause that binds the amendment mechanism itself. The room is half constitutional scholars, half staff and family who came for the free meal and don't care about theory. The speech has to actually turn on the philosphical tension that a Ulysses contract is both wisdom and self-betrayal: by tying your future self to the mast you're declaring your own future preferences illegitimate. That should shape what she can say — she can't just praise binding the future without facing the fact that she's silencing a future self who might disagree, especially since the clause also locks away the key to unlocking it. I want it to land for both crowds without being patronizing, and I want her to get stuck in the middle rather than resolving it neatly.
