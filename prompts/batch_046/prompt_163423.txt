As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy with the same patience I once applied to long-exposure experiments, I want to test a conceptual distinction that sits between empirical measurement and normative judgment. Consider the West German Historikerstreit that erupted in the mid-1980s, when the historian Ernst Nolte published an argument that the Holocaust was not historically unique but bore structural similarities to earlier Soviet atrocities—particularly the Gulag system—and when philosophers and historians led by Jürgen Habermas responded that such comparison risked functionally relativizing Nazi crimes. The salient facts are these: Nolte proposed a causal and structural parallel; Habermas and others insisted that comparison across these regimes obscured the specific, ideologically driven character of the Holocaust; and the dispute quickly became a national controversy about whether remembering one atrocity requires treating it as incomparable to others.

Now, analyze this episode through the lens of what I will call metric pluralism about moral harm—the view that comparing evils may require either a shared cardinal scale (allowing claims like A is twice as grave as B), a shared ordinal scale (allowing only A is worse than B), or may be conceptually blocked when the harm-types are ontologically distinct, analogous to the way physical quantities with different dimensions cannot be added without bridge laws. Your task is disciplined mapping: identify which participants in the dispute implicitly assumed a shared cardinal metric, which defended an ordinal comparison, and which treated the categories as dimensionally incommensurable. Next, identify what the intensity and framing of the public reaction reveals about the theory: does the episode show that comparison is always morally obscene, that it is epistemically impossible, or merely that the available metrics were politically contested?
