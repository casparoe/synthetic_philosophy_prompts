Write a short story set in a polity governed by an applied synthesis of George Berkeley's immaterialism and his economic writings on currency, labor, and national welfare. In this society, physical money possesses no independent material substance; coins and notes are treated strictly as social tokens—signs that represent labor and goods but carry no intrinsic value apart from being perceived and acknowledged. The narrative should center on a treasury official who discovers that a wealthy merchant has been sequestering large denominations in private, unobserved vaults, thereby withdrawing them from collective perception and, according to the local metaphysics, weakening their ontological status and draining public value. Include a sustained dialogue in which the merchant defends his hoarding by appealing to the very distinction between sign and thing signified: if money is only a mental counter, he argues, then private perception should be as legitimate as collective agreement, and removing tokens from common view is no different from withholding any other mental idea. The story should treat economic value, social visibility, and ontological existence as governed by a single set of rules, without invoking modern banking concepts such as central banks, interest rates, or digital ledgers. Instead, explore how a metaphysics that denies matter independent of mind might reshape the moral obligations of wealth, transparency, and shared reality.
