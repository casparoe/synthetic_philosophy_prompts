I have been working through philosophy of mind on my own, mostly through popular books and long-form essays, and I keep returning to the same standoff: Dennett’s example of the two bits, where one register detects temperature and a second derives “too hot” from it, versus Searle’s insistence that syntax is never semantics and that only biological brains possess original intentionality. Every discussion I have found treats this as a clean binary—either intentionality can be derived through interpretation, or it must be intrinsic and original—but neither side seems to address what happens when there is no single designer, interpreter, or biological origin point that anchors the derivation. I want to propose a six-month independent conceptual project, pitched as a structured research plan rather than an empirical study, that asks exactly this: Does Dennett’s distinction between original and derived intentionality presuppose that original intentionality is localized in one source, or can it survive in a distributed system where no individual component carries original aboutness yet global functional relationships constrain interpretation? More specifically, if we generalize the two-bitser architecture into a network of nodes that adjust each other through feedback loops—without any external engineer bestowing meaning and without any central “mind” inside—does Searle’s Chinese Room objection apply to the whole system, or does it misidentify the problem by assuming that derived meaning must always trace back to one original substrate?

The standard treatments leave this open because Dennett’s examples almost always rely on an outside interpreter or designer to confer derived status, while Searle’s critique treats syntax as uniformly meaningless unless anchored in a biological brain. Neither framework, at least in the versions available to a general reader, examines whether a middle case is coherent: a system in which original intentionality is absent from every part, yet the global dynamics are not arbitrarily syntactic because they are constrained by non-accidental functional relationships. My approach would be purely philosophical and conceptual. I would first reconstruct the two-bitser case precisely, isolating what makes the second bit’s state derived rather than original. Then I would model the distributed variant—multiple interdependent representations with no semantic origin— and determine whether its content can be genuinely derived without being traceable to any original source, or whether the absence of such a source collapses the distinction into either pure original intentionality (if the system as a whole counts) or pure syntax (if no part counts). I would map three possible conclusions: that Dennett needs a third category of distributed or emergent intentionality; that Searle is correct but for the wrong reason, since the issue is centralized versus distributed anchoring rather than syntax versus semantics; or that the middle case is incoherent and the binary distinction holds once we clarify where the original must reside. Success would be a clear taxonomy with a decision procedure for classifying systems; failure would be if the distributed case reduces without remainder to one standard position, or if assessing it requires technical computational details beyond conceptual analysis, making the project unfinishable in the time available.
