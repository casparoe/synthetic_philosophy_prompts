I sit on the governance board of a small mutual-aid cooperative that operates harm-reduction clinics and tenant-advocacy services in a post-industrial city. Last month we were approached by a private infrastructure firm with the following proposal: they will fully endow our clinics for five years and construct a permanent facility, provided we sign a public memorandum endorsing their pilot programme that applies predictive-risk scoring to public-housing allocations. The scoring model is proprietary, but leaked internal reviews indicate it systematically elevates eviction-risk ratings for households from particular ethnic and linguistic minorities. Our acceptance would almost certainly be weaponised in the firm’s regional lobbying, accelerating adoption in neighbouring districts. Rejection means our clinics close within two quarters, with predictable mortality and displacement effects. Several board members—trained in quantitative decision analysis—have constructed a net-harm ledger showing that the lives preserved by continued clinic operation exceed the marginal damage attributable to our single endorsement, especially if we negotiate audit rights as a condition. A minority counter that this treats institutional credibility as a fungible commodity, and that by lending our name we become structurally complicit in a system whose harms we neither design nor directly cause, irrespective of the arithmetic. I have attempted to formalise the disagreement using standard consequentialist and deontological frameworks, yet both seem to miss the specific phenomenology of the choice: it feels less like selecting the lesser evil and more like being forced to soil one’s own purpose in order to preserve it. Without appealing to generic management advice, could you analyse the philosophical architecture of this dilemma—particularly the distinction between direct causal responsibility, reputational complicity, and the corruption of an institution’s telos—and advise whether there exists a principled resolution, or whether this is precisely the kind of situation in which moral purity and practical efficacy are genuinely incommensurable? Please write in British English.
