I am working through material on epistemic modesty for a forecasting seminar and have noticed a recurring failure mode at the boundary between inside-view reasoning and outside-view base rates that I do not have a precise name for. A decision-maker examines a specific venture in rich detail, identifies genuinely distinctive features—such as an unusually experienced leadership team or a one-time regulatory exemption—and uses those features to justify excluding the case from the reference class that supplies the relevant statistical base rate. Once the outcome is known, the same features are invoked not merely as narrative explanations but as if they had already been independently validated as the decisive causal drivers of the deviation, even though no evidence outside the outcome itself supports that decisive weight; the feature thus licenses departure from the base rate beforehand and then retrospectively confirms the departure afterward, without any intermediate validation that it actually carried the causal load assigned to it.

I have seen this pattern in venture-capital pitches, academic grant evaluations, and personal long-term planning. It feels different from ordinary neglect of statistics, because the reasoner accepts that reference-class base rates exist, and different from simple post-hoc rationalization, because the feature is genuinely present rather than invented after the fact. Does this configuration have an established name in decision science or forecasting literature, and if not, can you coin and delimit a precise term for it, distinguishing it carefully from the narrative fallacy and from hindsight bias, and specifying the boundary conditions under which a distinctive feature legitimately supports both exclusion from a reference class and causal explanation of the result versus being used in a circular, self-confirming loop?
