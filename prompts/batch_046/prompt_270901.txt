Having spent three decades in condensed-matter physics, I have lately taken up philosophy of action and now find myself holding a cluster of commitments that I cannot tell whether cohere. Before presenting them in a reading group, I should like a rigorous consistency audit—tensions ranked by severity, with the least costly revisions specified, and with apparent conflicts distinguished from genuine ones.

My commitments are these:

(a) I accept the core of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument: genuine moral responsibility requires being ultimately self-caused, and since every feature of an agent—her neural architecture, her values, her deliberative habits—traces back to factors outside her control, ultimate responsibility is impossible.

(b) At the same time, I defend a compatibilist account of practical agency in the reasons-responsiveness tradition: an action is properly attributable to me when it flows from my own deliberative capacities, regardless of how those capacities were originally caused.

(c) I regard the causal closure of the physical domain as well supported, and I see contemporary neuroscience as confirming that brain states are fully determined by prior physical conditions.

(d) I have also become persuaded by predictive-processing models in neuroscience that conscious deliberation is largely a post-hoc narrative constructed to explain actions already initiated by subpersonal prediction-error minimization.

(e) I maintain that moral praise and blame are justified purely instrumentally, as social mechanisms that reshape the predictive models of others, and that they require no desert in an ultimate sense.

(f) Finally, I draw a distinction—one I have not encountered in the standard literature—between proximate causal authorship (being the physical system through which an action is realized) and normative authorship (being the proper object of reactive attitudes). I take compatibilism to require only the former.

Please audit this set. Does (a) undermine (b) more severely than (d) does? Does the distinction in (f) actually rescue (b) from (a), or does it merely rename the difficulty? Is there an apparent tension between (c) and (d) that dissolves once one notices that predictive processing is itself a deterministic mechanism? If I must make a single revision to clean up the greatest number of conflicts, which commitment is the least costly to abandon, and what exactly would I lose? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally damaging, and indicate for each pair whether it is genuinely incompatible or only looks so on the surface.
