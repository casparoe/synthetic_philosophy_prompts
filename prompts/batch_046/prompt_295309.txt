I've been asked to advise on whether our company should expand a voluntary peer-skills program. The pilot ran in our local office of about two hundred people, and only twelve—including me—opted in. Management is now deciding whether to roll it out company-wide, across all regional offices and remote staff that I never see.

I'm caught between two ways of reasoning from my own position in that small group of twelve. On one hand, I treat myself as randomly drawn from the local pool: with only a six percent uptake, the program probably isn't locally valuable, so we shouldn't scale it. On the other hand, I find myself thinking that if there are actually thousands of employees in the hidden parts of the organization, the fact that I exist and am one of the interested ones is less surprising in a large population—there are more slots for people like me—so my presence shouldn't make me pessimistic about local demand; instead, it might even suggest that a much larger hidden pool of interested people exists, which would support expansion. A colleague insists that using my own existence to adjust my estimate of the total population size is a mistake in this context, and that only the random-sample reasoning is legitimate. I'm not convinced the distinction is so clean. How should I think about whether being one of the twelve should affect my belief about how many people there really are, and consequently whether to recommend scaling the program?
