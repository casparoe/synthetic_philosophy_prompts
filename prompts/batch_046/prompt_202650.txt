For a graduate seminar on moral and political philosophy, please construct a diagnostic taxonomy — presented as a branching decision tree — that sorts philosophical positions on the nature and value of privacy according to what they imply about three concrete cases, rather than according to the familiar labels found in textbooks.

Start from this root commitment: privacy is not reducible to secrecy, nor to a legal property right over information.

The first branch turns on predictive inference without disclosure. Consider a case in which an algorithm infers, with high accuracy, a person's unarticulated religious doubts solely from biometric patterns — pupil dilation, typing cadence — and then silently reshapes her information environment (search rankings, recommendations) so that those doubts become less likely to be resolved, without her ever knowing the inference occurred. Does this constitute a privacy violation even though no false belief about her is formed by a human observer and no data is literally disclosed? Answer yes (Branch A) or no (Branch B).

The second branch asks whether privacy carries non-instrumental value. Consider an individual who lives in perfect informational isolation, suffers no material harm, yet reports a distinct loss of dignity upon learning that her biometric patterns have been modelled by an institution with which she has never interacted. Does this show that privacy can be violated for reasons that do not reduce to consequential harm? Answer yes or no.

The third branch introduces a distinction not standard in the literature. Ask whether privacy protects a 'counterfactual mental space' — the domain of hypothetical preferences, imagined futures, and unrealised self-conceptions that have never been expressed or acted upon, but which predictive systems can now render probabilistically tractable. If privacy extends to shielding this space from inference, take Sub-branch C1; if it extends only to expressed or enacted information, take Sub-branch C2.

Each leaf must be named only after the combination of answers is fixed. The tree must include at least the following positions as leaves: a pure control-based view; a contextual-integrity view (treating privacy as appropriate flow of information within specific social contexts); an instrumentalist-reductionist view; a relational-autonomy view; and at least one 'unnamed' combination permitted by the logical space of answers that is either incoherent or currently unexplored — briefly diagnose why the space is vacant.

For any pair of named positions that your tree cannot genuinely separate — that is, positions that yield identical answers on all three cases — flag them explicitly rather than inventing a spurious distinction.

Every node should be phrased as a concrete question that a student could answer without already knowing which philosophical label it corresponds to. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write the response in polished British English, and design the taxonomy so that it remains fully usable for someone without access to external sources.
