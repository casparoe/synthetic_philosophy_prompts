I am a retired physical chemist who has only recently begun reading philosophy with any seriousness, and I am preparing to lead a single ninety-minute session for a small reading group of fellow scientists and engineers—none with formal training in Buddhist studies—who are comfortable with emergence, functional explanation, and reduction but who tend to treat the self as either a Cartesian substance or a convenient fiction reducible to neural dynamics. Our group has been working through Western problems of personal identity, and I want to teach the Buddhist doctrine of anattā: specifically, the claim that the self is not a persistent, unitary substance but a conventional designation (prajñapti) arising dependently from the five skandhas, and that this bears on diachronic identity without collapsing into either dualism or the eliminative materialism my colleagues already accept in other domains. I need a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

Please lay out the dependency arc: which conceptual pieces must be in place before the problem of identity can even be posed in this framework, and in exactly what order they must be introduced so that the audience does not immediately map anattā onto “the brain is just atoms” or onto “there is no subject, therefore experience is an illusion.” Next, specify the one carrying example or analogy that should bear the weight of the session; I am inclined toward the classical chariot comparison but worry it is both over-familiar and too static to capture the processual, moment-to-moment nature of the skandhas, so I want you to either rehabilitate it with precise conditions on what properties it must possess and what properties it must lack, or propose a different case that makes the conventional-designation claim vivid for people used to thinking in terms of dynamic systems. Then map the misconceptions: the ones they will bring in from scientific reductionism, the ones they will form as defensive reactions during the session, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted and redirected. Provide one precise check—an exercise or a single question—that would reveal whether they have grasped that the self is conventionally designated rather than merely eliminated. Finally, state plainly what technical material should be omitted at this level—whether the Abhidharma taxonomies of mental factors, the Madhyamaka debates about inherent existence (svabhāva), or the full soteriological context of dukkha and liberation—and say so without apology.

If this framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if treating anattā primarily as a theory of personal identity for Western philosophical consumption fundamentally distorts its purpose—say so plainly rather than constructing an elegant plan around a mistake. Structure your response in four named sections, in this order: The dependency arc (approximately 250 words), describing the logical sequence and what depends on what; The carrying case (approximately 200 words), identifying the example, its required and excluded properties, and its relation to scientific analogies; The misconception map (approximately 300 words), listing predictable misunderstandings in the order they will arise with the precise intervention point for each; and The check for understanding and omissions (approximately 200 words), giving the verification task and the explicit list of what to leave out. Use a formal, direct register appropriate for a retired scientist addressing peers.
