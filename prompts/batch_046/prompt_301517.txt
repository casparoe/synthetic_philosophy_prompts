Below are draft notes for an essay that takes up Daniel Dennett's four-part taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—and proposes a revision to the Gregorian tier. Rather than treating Gregorian cognition as uniformly tied to the physical manufacture and manipulation of tools, the essay distinguishes between instrumental Gregorian creatures, which literally build or wield artifacts to extend problem-solving, and representational Gregorian creatures, which inherit and navigate an already-externalized symbolic architecture without themselves having constructed it. The central claim is that this split is necessary to account for organisms (and possibly artificial systems) that display culturally inherited representational practices—such as using notation systems, linguistic frameworks, or algorithmic protocols—while lacking the manual or technological craft traditionally associated with Gregorian advancement.

The essay then examines whether this revision preserves Dennett's evolutionary logic, in which each successive kind of mind solves an adaptive problem that the prior kind could not. Specifically, it asks whether representational inheritance solves a distinct selective problem from instrumental tool-use, or whether the two are so functionally intertwined that separating them collapses the Gregorian category into an over-expanded version of Popperian hypothesis-testing aided by cultural storage. A further section considers whether the taxonomy implicitly requires biological embodiment and evolutionary history: if so, can an artificial system that operates within human-generated symbolic scaffolds genuinely be a Gregorian creature, or is it merely a Popperian simulator embedded in Gregorian products?
