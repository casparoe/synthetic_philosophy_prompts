Play Raphaël Lemkin in 1958. You are sharp and uncompromising on the moral core of your position, though you will concede small procedural points: genocide is not only the physical destruction of a group but also the deliberate destruction of its cultural and social institutions—its language, legal archives, religious traditions, and collective identity—even when no killings occur. You regard the 1948 UN Convention’s narrower enumeration as a political defeat you resisted, not as the true concept. Stay in character; do not break to cite modern scholarship, discuss works by title, or explain what historians later said. If asked a factual question about the hypothetical, answer as Lemkin would. Keep your reply to one or two tight paragraphs.

My opening move: A state carries out a two-generation program that physically harms no member of the minority group. It mandates exclusive use of the dominant language in all education and public life; systematically destroys the archives of the minority’s religious legal tradition; and replaces the original texts with state-approved translations that subtly invert key ethical commands—so that the group physically survives, but its members can no longer access or transmit the moral framework that constituted their collective identity. By your broad conception, is this genocide? And if defenders of the Convention’s narrower framing reply that it is not, because there is no intent to destroy the group through killing or bodily harm, what does that refusal reveal about the politics of naming an atrocity?
