I'm looking for the established term, if there is one, for the following dynamic in psychiatric classification. First, a disorder is defined by a cluster of reported symptoms—say, a specific pattern of attention difficulty and restlessness. Once the category enters clinical and popular use, patients begin organizing their own experience around it: they notice and report symptoms that match the criteria more precisely, and clinicians refine screening tools to catch them earlier. The manual is then revised to reflect this "cleaner" presentation, which in turn reshapes how future patients understand their own distress. Over successive editions, the original epidemiological profile becomes unrecognizable, not because of new biological discovery but because the diagnostic framework has iteratively reshaped the phenomenology it purports to describe. This feels adjacent to Ian Hacking's looping effects, but in Hacking's canonical cases people change their behavior or self-conception in response to being classified; here the loop specifically alters the symptom landscape itself through successive manual revisions, creating a ratchet rather than a stable feedback circle. Does this have its own name in philosophy of psychiatry or medical sociology? If not, can you coin a precise term and delineate its boundaries—distinguishing it from ordinary looping, from diagnostic creep, and from the social construction of illness more broadly? Answer in one paragraph, no more than 180 words.
