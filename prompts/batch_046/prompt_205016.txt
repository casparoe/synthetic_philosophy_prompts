I'm delivering the opening address at the dedication of the Watershed Civic Archive, an institution established to preserve the records of what local residents call the Lower Valley Commons tradition. The room will be divided: on one side, historians of nationalism and sociologists who will immediately think of Hobsbawm's work on invented tradition and Anderson's on imagined communities; on the other, local union descendants, municipal officials, and families for whom these documents are not an academic case study but the tangible record of who they understand themselves to be. The archive commemorates a unified watershed identity that, as the historians present will know, was largely assembled in the 1980s by heritage activists who stitched together fragments of nineteenth-century guild rituals, interwar cooperative statutes, and postwar environmental slogans into a narrative of continuous communal life. I need the address to navigate this without either falsifying the chronology or reducing the present community's attachment to mere false consciousness. To do that, I want you to introduce and work with a distinction—call it mnemonic federalism—between traditions that claim diachronic purity, a single unbroken line stretching backward, and traditions that function as an open federation of borrowed practices held together by present solidarity rather than by historical continuity. The speech should make clear to the experts that I am not naively ignoring the construction, and should make clear to the residents that recognizing construction is not the same as denying legitimacy. It should be about ten minutes long, suitable for a formal civic occasion, and should treat the idea as doing real structural work—shaping what I can and cannot claim about the community's past—rather than as decorative framing. Please write it. Then, separately, say how confident you are in the historical framing I have just sketched—particularly in the claim that the 1980s assembly was selectively synthetic rather than wholly fabricated—and single out the specific point in your argument where you are least sure that the distinction between diachronic purity and mnemonic federalism actually holds up under pressure.
