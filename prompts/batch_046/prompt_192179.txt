Two participants in a philosophy-of-mind seminar disagree about whether emotions are judgments, perceptions, or feelings.

(1) The first holds that emotions are evaluative judgments: fear is the judgment that something threatens something one cares about, which is why emotions are rationally assessable—if the threat is illusory, the fear is inappropriate.

(2) The second holds that emotions are interoceptive perceptions of bodily states that become directed at objects through contextual framing. She notes that people often feel dread before forming any explicit judgment, and that arousal can persist even after one judges the situation safe.

(3) The first replies that the perception model misses the normative structure of emotion: if fear is only perceiving one's own arousal, there is no basis for criticizing it as unwarranted—only for noting misperception—whereas we clearly treat emotions as answerable to reasons about the world.

(4) The second concedes that pure bodily feeling misses directedness, but counters that judgment is too slow and too linguistic: sub-cortical responses occur before propositional evaluation. She then introduces a further consideration both have ignored: emotions display what she calls social-indexical calibration—the same event, such as being interrupted, produces different emotional textures depending on whether the interrupter is a peer, a superior, or a stranger, and this relational modulation is not reducible to either propositional content or bodily intensity. She suggests emotions might instead be attunements to relational affordances—direct perceptions of what a situation demands—rather than judgments about value or mere feelings.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Identify what, if anything, both parties are missing, and specifically address whether the social-indexical calibration point undermines one view more than the other or points instead to a third option. Finally, say what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding would lead to a different verdict, and where your reasoning in this adjudication is most likely to fail.
