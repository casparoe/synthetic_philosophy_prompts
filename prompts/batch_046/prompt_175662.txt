As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science, I am struck by how little progress seems to have been made on the Quine-Duhem problem of underdetermination. One camp treats global underdetermination—where any body of evidence can be accommodated by infinitely many mutually incompatible theories through adjustments in auxiliary hypotheses—as a live threat to scientific rationality; another treats it as a logical triviality that vanishes once we distinguish legitimate theory revision from ad hoc rescue. The debate has run for decades, yet neither side appears to have changed its core commitments. Is the stall substantive—do they disagree about the actual history of scientific revision, about what counts as an admissible auxiliary adjustment, or about whether underdetermination is an empirical feature of laboratory practice rather than a formal feature of logic? Or is it verbal, with "underdetermination" meaning something different to philosophers concerned with epistemic justification than to those modeling scientific inference formally? Or are they simply answering different questions—one about the limits of rational belief, one about the dynamics of actual instrument calibration—under a single heading? Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually claims about auxiliary hypotheses and theory choice, and specify what would count as progress: a formal result about uniqueness of choice given a constraint, an empirical study of how scientists reject auxiliaries, or a conceptual distinction that dissolves the problem. If you think no progress should be expected, say why.
