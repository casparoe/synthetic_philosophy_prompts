A colleague who writes about mathematics and philosophy for a general-interest magazine recently sent me her summary of Thomson's lamp paradox, drawn from a long-form essay she was reviewing. She describes the scenario precisely: a lamp is toggled on and off at intervals that halve with each step—one minute, then thirty seconds, then fifteen—so that after exactly two minutes an infinite sequence of switches has been completed. At that endpoint, she says, the lamp must be either on or off, yet the sequence offers no rule for deciding which, because every prior state is immediately reversed by the next and there is no final toggle to break the symmetry. According to her account, early commentators treated this as a logical contradiction—an outright violation of the law of excluded middle—but the contemporary consensus resolves it by treating the endpoint as an undefined thermal state: just as a mathematical limit need not equal any term in its sequence, the filament's temperature can approach a boundary without ever reaching a determinate binary condition. She adds that this makes the supertask physically coherent, since physics operates with continuous variables rather than discrete logical states, and that determinism is therefore untouched. She concludes by attributing the lingering confusion to a category error—critics invoke Zeno's paradoxes to argue that infinite acceleration is empirically impossible, but that objection misses the point, because the lamp thought-experiment concerns logical, not mechanical, possibility; once that distinction is respected, the paradox collapses into a harmless curiosity about applying discrete logic to continuous time.
