I co-direct a synthetic-biology lab, and my colleague and I are deadlocked over an institutional protocol review. We have engineered a lipid-vesicle system that catalyzes its own membrane growth from environmental precursors, divides upon reaching a critical size, and shows heritable variation in division rate across successive generations—yet it contains no nucleic acids, no ribosomes, and no shared evolutionary ancestry with terrestrial organisms. My colleague argues that because the system metabolizes energy, reproduces with variation, and adapts to selective conditions, it must be classified as a living organism and therefore submitted to biosafety oversight for live cultures. I maintain that without genetic inheritance or common descent, it is structurally closer to an autocatalytic chemical cycle—no more biologically alive than a flame or a growing crystal—and that submitting it as a living culture commits a category error. Our institution’s guidelines are silent on synthetic minimal cells that lack DNA. Is there a principled way to adjudicate whether this system counts as alive, or is our disagreement purely semantic? And if the verdict depends entirely on which definitional criteria one privileges, does that undermine the practical urgency of deciding how to regulate it?
