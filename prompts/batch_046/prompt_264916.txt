I have been working through the institutional logic of American collective bargaining—specifically the framework of exclusive representation under Section 9(a) of the National Labor Relations Act—after a seminar discussion in which a classmate insisted that labor unions are essentially voluntary associations no different from professional societies. The practice works as follows: once a union is certified by a majority vote within a defined bargaining unit, it becomes the sole agent for negotiating wages, hours, and conditions of employment for every worker in that unit, including those who voted against certification, those who never voted, and those who subsequently resign formal membership under right-to-work provisions. Those resigning members may be relieved of full membership dues under Beck-type objections, yet they remain covered by the collective agreement and are entitled to grievance and arbitration representation funded in part by remaining members; simultaneously, employers are legally prohibited from dealing directly with individual employees over terms and conditions during the contract term. The result is a system in which dissent is permitted—workers may exit the association—but the contractual and representational consequences of majority decision remain binding on all.

This strikes me as philosophically puzzling in ways that standard legal commentary does not resolve. The arrangement treats the bargaining unit as an entity with interests that can diverge from the current preferences of any given worker, and it obligates the union to act as a fiduciary for individuals who have explicitly withdrawn consent to be represented by it. At the same time, the broader legal culture treats employment as a voluntary contractual relation and regards compelled association with deep suspicion. What view of democratic authority, collective identity, and consent must be in place for this to be coherent rather than a mere pragmatic compromise? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory: does the practice presuppose a form of corporate realism about the bargaining unit, or a trusteeship model of representation in which present dissent is overridden in the name of an intertemporal collective welfare? Does it require a particular conception of fairness—perhaps one that permits compelled subsidy of non-excludable representation goods—or a specific ontology of groups that makes “the unit” the proper bearer of rights and obligations? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits well with it, identify whether its parts presuppose consistent things about voluntarism and authority, and explain what specific institutional features would have to change if we replaced its underlying commitments with a strictly individualist, continuously-consensual, or purely proportional-representation model of workplace governance.
