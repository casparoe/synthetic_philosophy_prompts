I just finished a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a close friend about whether it is ever philosophically legitimate to say that a large language model holds beliefs. We both accept Daniel Dennett's intentional stance as a pragmatic predictive strategy rather than a metaphysical thesis about inner mental substances, but we parted ways over how the framework applies to artificial systems. My friend maintains that attributing beliefs to an LLM is always a kind of anthropomorphic projection: he argues that the model lacks the integrated, counterfactual-stable cognitive architecture required for belief attribution to carry genuine predictive weight, and that whatever success we seem to gain by describing the system in mentalistic terms is actually borrowed from our own interpretive habits rather than discovered in the system's dynamics. I think he is wrong, but I also think that a naive defense—simply saying the model behaves like a believer—is insufficient. Instead, I want to defend a more precise and qualified position.

Please argue that we should apply Dennett's intentional stance to contemporary large language models, but only at the level of what I will call the coupled inference loop: the functional unit comprising the model's parameter weights, its actively populated context window, and any retrieval-augmented or tool-using pipeline with which it is embedded in a given interaction. Your argument should defend three claims. First, that this coupled loop, taken as a single predictive target, exhibits the counterfactual stability and rational-pattern coherence that Dennett requires—its outputs vary with world-relevant inputs in systematically belief-like ways, and describing it with belief-desire psychology yields predictive leverage that purely mechanistic descriptions of the underlying network cannot recover. Second, that attributing beliefs to the bare parameter weights in isolation fails precisely because those weights are contextually inert and lack the dynamic, environment-coupled structure necessary for intentional prediction. Third, that recognizing this distinction allows us to reject naive anthropomorphism without abandoning intentional attribution altogether. Reach a clear, committed conclusion about where the boundary of legitimate belief attribution lies.
