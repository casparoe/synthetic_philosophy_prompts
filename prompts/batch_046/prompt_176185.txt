As a doctoral student working at the intersection of Kant scholarship and international legal theory, I have been trying to map the implicit philosophical commitments embedded in the current architecture of international peace enforcement. Consider the institutional design that governs collective force and the prosecution of aggression today. The UN Charter simultaneously affirms the sovereign equality and domestic jurisdiction of all member states, yet it authorizes a Security Council—whose five permanent members hold an absolute veto—to determine threats to international peace and security and to mandate enforcement measures, including armed intervention, without requiring the consent of the affected state. Running in parallel, the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court criminalizes acts of aggression, but the Court may exercise jurisdiction over this crime only upon a Security Council referral or after state parties have activated the relevant provisions, which means that sovereign states and the Council together function as gatekeepers over the very breach of sovereignty that aggression represents. The result is a system in which sovereignty operates both as an inviolable shield against external interference and as a delegable authority that can be overridden by a small, strategically selected group of powers acting through multilateral mechanisms.

What philosophical conception of perpetual peace, sovereignty, and federation does this arrangement implicitly presuppose? Does it align with Kant’s sketch of a voluntary league of republican states that retains sovereignty while gradually extending cosmopolitan right, or does it instead presuppose a proto-world-state in which sovereignty is merely provisional? Is the veto power consistent with the republican precondition Kant believed necessary for durable peace, or does it introduce an arbitrary hierarchy among states that undermines that precondition? And does the practice of authorizing coercive intervention for threats to peace without a formal declaration of war presuppose a particular moral status for states—perhaps that they are rights-bearing entities whose consent can be waived collectively—rather than for the individuals within them?

Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what this institutional design strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and indicate where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could the same architecture be licensed by a purely realist theory of state interest, or does it necessarily commit its practitioners to some version of cosmopolitan moral equality? Finally, if the presupposed Kantian view were replaced—say, by a thoroughgoing cosmopolitanism that rejects sovereign immunity altogether—what specific features of this architecture would have to change, and which could remain intact?
