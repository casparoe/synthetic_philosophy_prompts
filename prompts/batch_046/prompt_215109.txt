I have been facilitating a philosophy-of-law reading group that includes one interlocutor trained in the analytical jurisprudence tradition associated with H.L. Hart and another who works in moral philosophy and defends a position in the spirit of Lon Fuller. After months of circular debate about whether legal validity can be separated from moral evaluation, I want them to stop exchanging polemics and instead draft a joint adversarial-collaboration document. The collaboration should treat the Hart-Fuller disagreement not as a historical commentary but as a live, precisely located dispute about whether the separation thesis survives careful analysis of procedural conditions.

Please draft the document these two camps could both sign. First, include a numbered section of agreed claims: what both sides accept about legal rules as social practices governed by a master rule or rule of recognition; about the eight procedural desiderata—generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, consistency, feasibility, stability, and congruence between official action and declared rule—that Fuller emphasized; and about the conceptual distinction between asking whether something is law and asking whether it is good law. State these in neutral wording that neither camp reads as already conceding the central dispute. Second, present the residual disagreements in phrasing each side accepts for its own view: specifically, whether those eight desiderata are purely instrumental conditions for the efficacy of a legal system or partly constitutive conditions for law's moral authority, and whether the separation thesis is best understood as a claim about conceptual possibility—that law can exist without moral merit—or as a normative claim about how legal validity ought to be assessed. Third, identify the cruxes—those sub-questions whose resolution would genuinely move at least one party—such as whether a legal system that is perfectly procedural but gravely unjust produces law in the full sense, and whether retroactive rules that are clearly promulgated fail as law or only fail as good law. For each crux, provide a concrete hypothetical institutional case or regulatory scheme both sides agree is diagnostically relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts the case demonstrates.

To keep the collaboration from collapsing into a verbal dispute about the definition of law, inject a conceptual framing that neither side typically addresses: apply the debate to rules formulated in formal, machine-readable notation—such as deterministic administrative algorithms—where human interpretive discretion is minimized or eliminated. Ask both camps to state whether, on their view, this formalization removes the domain in which an internal morality of law can operate, or whether it makes the moral conditions of legality more transparent and therefore more stringent. The document should not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, should avoid attributing specific verbatim passages to either thinker, and should conclude with a brief assessment of whether any remaining disagreement is substantive or partly verbal.
