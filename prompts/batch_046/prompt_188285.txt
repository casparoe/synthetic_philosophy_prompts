I have no university training in logic or philosophy, but over the past year I have returned repeatedly to Lewis Carroll's dialogue in which Achilles tries to compel the Tortoise to accept a logical conclusion and the Tortoise keeps demanding further rules for each rule applied. Most explanations I have read treat this as a simple warning about infinite regress. I believe the dialogue actually exposes a live disagreement about what justifies deduction itself, and I want a document that two opposing interpreters could both sign.

Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the following two positions.

Position A — the Terminationists: They hold that Carroll's dialogue demonstrates that any chain of deductive justification must end in primitive inference rules that are not themselves derived from prior rules. On their view, rationality has a hierarchical structure; the bottom layer consists of norms that are applied but not further justified within the system, and this termination is not a failure but a necessary feature of any coherent reasoning practice.

Position B — the Continuists: They hold that Carroll's dialogue shows that treating deduction as a chain of explicit rules is already a mistake. On their view, justification is not hierarchical but distributed across an ongoing practice of reasoning; there is no bottom layer of primitive rules, only a continuous process of self-correction in which any particular rule can, in principle, be questioned and refined without breaking the practice. The infinite regress is therefore not a structural limit but an artifact of a false picture of reasoning as a linear sequence.

The document should be structured as follows.

First, a numbered list of claims both sides accept about the dialogue itself — its logical structure, the moves Achilles makes, the nature of the Tortoise's resistance, and what any adequate account of deduction must explain. These should be specific enough to constrain the disagreement, not vague platitudes.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one precise point of divergence, phrased in neutral language so that each side recognizes its own view in the corresponding column. The table should separate disagreements that are genuinely substantive from those that are merely verbal.

Third, identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one side. For each crux, describe a concrete case, thought experiment, or feature of reasoning practice that both sides agree is relevant, and then record what each side predicts about that case. I am particularly interested in the crux that concerns blind spots in self-correcting systems: whether a reasoning system that can revise any of its own rules must nevertheless contain at least one inferential step that it applies but cannot represent as an object of its own scrutiny. Another crux I want included concerns whether formal verification systems — programs that apply logical rules at enormous speed with a hard-coded kernel — resolve the Tortoise's challenge or merely relocate it.

Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Write in a tone appropriate to a serious philosophical collaboration, but keep the language accessible to someone without specialized training. The goal is a text that both camps could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made as exact as possible.
