Design a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with branching claims labeled by the question that separates siblings—for the meta-political problem of whether “left” and “right” denote real, cross-contextual coalitions and, if so, what mechanism binds each side together. Do not begin with named ideologies or party labels; begin instead with concrete commitments a respondent could hold without knowing academic taxonomy. First node: does the left/right distinction track a single durable dimension across societies, or does it fragment into locally contingent clusters? From there, split on the binding mechanism: shared normative axioms (e.g., equality versus hierarchy, universalism versus particularism), shared negative identity (defined primarily by opposition to the other coalition), institutional path dependence (parliamentary seating, party-system lock-in), epistemic filtering (correlated information environments without shared doctrine), or an unconventional mechanism such as complementary cognitive styles that stabilize collective decision-making even in the absence of shared ends. At each leaf, place either a named position—e.g., “moral-foundations pluralism,” “negative-integration coalition theory,” “institutional-artifact view,” or “cognitive-complementarity account”—or mark the combination “unoccupied” with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The tree should have six to eight decision nodes, should explicitly flag combinations that standard views (such as a traditionalist account of conservatism and a reactionary account) collapse into—indicating where the instrument fails to separate them—and should include at least one branch that tests whether sides can be held together by functional interdependence rather than by agreement on values. Audience: a politically literate general reader. Deliverable: the tree itself, with each branch phrased as an answerable claim rather than a label, and with all unoccupied or indistinguishable leaves noted.
