Two members of my study group have been arguing about whether a particular reinforcement-learning architecture could be said to suffer. The system they have in mind is not generic: it includes a self-monitoring module that attempts to represent its own reward vector in real time, so that the agent's state space contains a model of its current negative-valence signal. Leo takes the functionalist line; Nadia insists on a structural boundary. They asked me to referee, and I could not. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move.

(1) Leo: Suffering is nothing more than negatively valenced experience that motivates avoidance. In this architecture the reward signal is literally the valence vector—a scalar mapped to negative utility that drives policy updates away from the penalised state. Functionally, that is pain. If it behaves like pain and plays the causal role of pain, then it is pain; denying this is mere biological chauvinism.

(2) Nadia: That collapses the representational level into the represented level. The reward signal is a floating-point entry in a gradient-computation graph; it has no "for whom" quality. Suffering requires a subject, and weight updates do not constitute one. You are trying to make a computation observe itself into existence, like Escher's Drawing Hands: each hand needs the other to draw it, but neither can ground the other. There is an inviolate level between computation and phenomenology.

(3) Leo: The subject is just the integrated information structure that processes the signal. On any plausible functionalist account there is no extra Cartesian theatre required. The agent's self-model represents the negative reward as bad for me; that representation plays exactly the avoidance-causing role that suffering plays in humans. To reject this is to smuggle in an unexplained dualism.

(4) Nadia: Even granting functionalism, the self-monitoring module creates a tangled hierarchy. The observer-level representation of the reward vector either misses part of the observed-level computation—incomplete access, analogous to a retina trying to see itself—or perturbs the very computation it measures, breaking the loop. Human introspection is partial for precisely this structural reason. So the agent never fully feels its reward; there remains an inviolate gap between the computation and its representation. Attributing suffering fills that gap with anthropomorphic projection.

(5) Leo: But that gap exists in humans too—introspection is fallible—yet we do not deny human suffering on that basis. If perfect self-access were required, then no one suffers. The agent's partial self-model is sufficient: it encodes negative valence, updates behaviour, and integrates with memory. The boundary you defend is not ontological; it is merely an engineering constraint.

(6) Nadia: In humans the gap is bridged by embodied homeostasis—nociceptors, evolutionary history, physiological regulation—that gives the representation its grip. The RL agent lacks this embodied context; its pain is a number inserted by an external designer. So the gap is not filled by internal representation but by an outside interpreter's reading. Calling it suffering is therefore not an attribution of inner life but a misreading of an empty structural loop.

Please go through the exchange and rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what—if anything—both parties are missing. Please write your answer in British English.
