Since Plutarch's Parallel Lives, a persistent pedagogical practice has paired biographies—exemplary lives arranged so that comparison across contexts reveals virtue—rather than presenting a single hero for imitation. A modern institutional version survives in professional-ethics training, where trainees study paired "moral exemplar" narratives (for instance, a clinician who refused to discharge a vulnerable patient against policy, paired with one who enforced rules despite personal cost) and are asked to map their own potential judgments onto the space between them. At the same time, these programs assess ethical competence through abstract principle-application, standardized dilemma analysis, and universalizable reasoning—methods that make no reference to the trainee's character or to the particular lives studied. What theory of moral learning and moral knowledge does that institutional pairing presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it treat biography as hagiographic testimony that proves virtue is embodied and therefore achievable; as a vehicle for moral formation through imaginative identification with a life-narrative; or as a heuristic illustration of principles that could be replaced by equivalent abstract cases? Is the practice philosophically consistent with the institution's explicit commitment to discursive, context-independent ethical reasoning, or does it quietly presuppose that moral truths are partly indexed to particular lives and that knowing the good requires knowing how it has been lived? And tell me exactly what in the curriculum—exercises, assessments, or framing—would have to disappear or be redesigned if the underlying theory were replaced by a purely procedural model in which lives carry no special justificatory weight.
