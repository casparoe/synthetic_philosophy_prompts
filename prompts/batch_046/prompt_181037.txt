Consider Karl Jaspers’s distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, formulated in the aftermath of 1945 to address whether and how a collective can bear responsibility. Now imagine that Jaspers is presented with a 21st-century policy framework—call it the Intergenerational Continuity Protocol—which holds present citizens of a state collectively liable for large-scale ecological and colonial harms committed by prior generations. The protocol does not allege personal wrongdoing; instead, it asserts that institutional identity persists across time, so that present benefits and structural positions are inseparable from past destruction. A distinctive feature is its opt-out clause: any citizen may formally renounce citizenship and forfeit all associated privileges, thereby escaping liability. Those who remain are therefore characterized as having accepted liability voluntarily rather than having it ascribed to them by birth or membership alone.

How would Jaspers likely assess this arrangement? In particular: (1) Would he treat the protocol’s appeal to “institutional continuity” as a legitimate extension of political guilt—tied to membership in a state and its present actions—or as a category mistake that collapses political guilt into moral or even criminal guilt distributed across time? (2) How would he evaluate the opt-out mechanism? Does converting liability into a voluntary, contract-like choice dissolve the specific nature of political guilt, which he understood as arising from unavoidable membership in a political community and its present decisions, irrespective of individual consent? (3) Might metaphysical guilt—guilt before an absolute or in solidarity with all human beings—have any meaningful role for citizens who knowingly retain inherited benefits, even though they played no part in causing the original harm? And (4) does the protocol fundamentally confuse the temporal logic of guilt, given that moral and metaphysical guilt concern the individual soul in its present existence, while political guilt concerns the state’s current conduct, neither of which can be “inherited” in the manner the protocol proposes?

Please reconstruct Jaspers’s likely position by working through his core distinctions, applying them to the invented mechanics of the protocol—especially the tension between ascribed membership and voluntary opt-out—without appealing to specific textual citations or secondary literature.
