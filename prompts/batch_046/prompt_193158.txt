I'm delivering the opening remarks at the retirement symposium for Dr. Lena Vasquez, who directed the Metro Transplant Allocation Consortium for thirty-two years. The audience is split: clinical ethicists and transplant surgeons on one side, who will catch every allusion to value pluralism and the parity thesis; her family, former patients' families, and hospital administrators on the other, for whom the speech must stand entirely on its own. Vasquez's defining institutional legacy is not an algorithm but a refusal. She insisted that urgency, long-term prognosis, equity of access, and what she termed "life-shape fit" — the degree to which a transplant restores a recipient's capacity to pursue projects that constitute their practical identity — are genuinely incommensurable. They cannot be translated into quality-adjusted life years or any other hidden common currency without distorting what makes each matter. She therefore designed an internal committee protocol that requires every allocation decision to document not only the recipient selected but the specific, non-fungible values that made each rejected candidate worthy, treating those exclusions as real losses rather than optimized-away costs. Write a twelve-minute address that uses this framework to explain why Vasquez's career exemplified a rationality of commitment rather than maximization: the task was not to find the correct answer hidden behind the values, but to choose in full awareness that the reasons supporting the rejected paths do not disappear. Include one concrete, invented-but-plausible allocation scenario — two candidates with profiles that are genuinely incomparable across these dimensions — that both halves of the room can follow without specialized knowledge. The speech should make clear why saying "we did the best we could with the data" would misrepresent her work, and it should avoid bibliographic references, further reading, or appeals to authority outside the reasoning itself. The philosophical structure should determine what the speech can and cannot claim about her character and the institution she built.
