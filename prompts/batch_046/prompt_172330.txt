Construct a decision-tree taxonomy of responses to the epistemic regress problem—the question of whether justification must terminate, loop, or extend infinitely—designed for an advanced undergraduate epistemology seminar. Build the tree from the logical space of commitments rather than from a list of named views. Each internal node should pose one concrete diagnostic question on which the positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing the label it points to; use specific scenarios (for example, a belief about one's present pain, a belief about a distant historical event, and a belief about a logical axiom) rather than abstract jargon wherever possible.

The tree must include at least these leaves:
- strong foundationalism (justification stops at beliefs that are infallible or self-evident);
- modest foundationalism (justification stops at beliefs that are fallible yet non-inferential);
- linear infinitism (an endless, non-circular chain of reasons is benign rather than vicious);
- holistic coherentism (beliefs mutually support one another with no basic stratum);
- an unnamed or rarely defended combination, such as a view that accepts infinite regress for empirical beliefs but demands circular or holistic structure specifically for first-person self-knowledge, or a view that permits circular reasoning within a single domain but forbids it across domains.

For each split, label the branch with the precise claim that separates it from its siblings. After locating the named positions, identify any unoccupied combinations in the logical space—for instance, a position that denies basic beliefs, rejects circularity, and yet declares infinite chains vicious—and briefly diagnose why that cell is empty: incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of named leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate with the questions you have chosen, and state what further distinction would be required to pull them apart.

The deliverable should be formal and polished. Each leaf must carry its name (or the label "unnamed") together with one sentence summarizing its most awkward commitment. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific texts; the instrument should be fully usable by someone without access to a library or the internet. Keep the tree to between six and nine leaves total, and ensure that no question asks the student to identify a view by its conventional name.
