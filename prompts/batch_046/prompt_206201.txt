I am currently taking an upper-division course on early analytic metaphysics and British idealism. For our study group, one of my peers produced a summary of Bradley's regress argument and its bearing on the unity of the proposition, apparently distilled from a popular philosophy podcast. The summary states that Bradley shows any relation R connecting terms a and b requires a further relation R' to link R with a, and then R'' to link R' with R and a, generating a vicious infinite regress. From this Bradley is said to conclude that relations are unreal and that all distinctions—including the internal structure of propositions—collapse into an undifferentiated Absolute. Russell, on this account, resolves the difficulty by treating relations as mental constructions that we impose on otherwise discrete atomic facts; consequently, the unity of a proposition such as Socrates being wise is only a psychological association among separate ideas, not a real feature of the world. The summary therefore concludes that Russell avoids the regress solely by abandoning genuine propositional unity and leaving us with a Humean bundle of unconnected particulars. I have the sense that this gets Russell badly wrong—especially the claim that he made relations psychological—but I cannot pinpoint exactly where the popularizer went astray. Could you provide an accuracy audit? Specifically, please distinguish (1) what captures Bradley's or Russell's actual positions, (2) what is a common but misleading simplification, and (3) what either philosopher would explicitly deny. I am particularly interested in whether the charge that Russell gives up propositional unity survives correction, and whether Bradley's worry genuinely anticipates contemporary debates about grounding and structural universals without being reducible to them.
