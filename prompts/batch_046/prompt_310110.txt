I serve on the Clinical Neuroscience Ethics Review Board at a university-affiliated hospital. Next week we must vote on a pilot protocol submitted jointly by the neurocritical care unit and the theoretical neuroscience group. The protocol—referred to internally as the Counterfactual Precision Modulation Study—proposes to apply hierarchical predictive-coding models, grounded in the free energy principle, to assess whether patients in unresponsive wakefulness syndrome retain any form of conscious awareness. Rather than relying solely on passive fMRI or EEG observation, the investigators intend to apply targeted, low-intensity neurostimulation calibrated to alter the precision weights of high-level sensory predictions. They will then measure whether the patient’s neural dynamics exhibit the specific patterns of free-energy reduction that, within an active-inference framework, are taken to distinguish conscious perceptual updating from purely reflexive or automatic processing.

The investigators argue that if consciousness is understood as the hierarchical minimization of surprise under appropriately weighted precision, then observing how neural free energy responds to an experimentally induced shift in precision provides direct, theory-embedded evidence of conscious inference rather than a mere correlational biomarker. They seek approval to enroll ten patients with documented family consent, and they intend to share the results—whether positive, negative, or ambiguous—to inform end-of-life counseling discussions with those families.

The philosophical and ethical stakes are substantial. Proponents on the board contend that predictive processing offers the only computationally explicit ontology linking neural dynamics to subjective experience, and that ignoring it in clinical assessment amounts to willful theoretical blindness. Critics, however, raise two interrelated objections. First, because active inference treats perception and action as coupled expressions of the same free-energy-minimizing process, deliberately manipulating precision weights does not merely observe consciousness but actively intervenes in the very dynamics being measured. This risks circular reasoning: if the framework defines conscious updating as the system’s response to precision-modulated prediction error, then engineering that response and declaring consciousness detected is tautologous. Second, applying a specific variational-Bayesian ontology to irrevocable clinical decisions—such as whether to continue life-sustaining treatment—may overreach what the framework can justify, particularly when families are asked to act on model-derived inferences about subjective experience that have never been validated against first-person report in this population.

Please draft a formal recommendation memo addressed to the board chair. It should recommend either approval with rigorous safeguards, conditional deferral pending further theoretical clarification, or rejection, and must include the following:

1. A clear, actionable bottom-line recommendation that the board can vote on directly.
2. Reasoning that explicitly engages predictive-processing and free-energy concepts—specifically the roles of precision weighting, hierarchical prediction error, active inference, and the perception-action equivalence—in evaluating both the scientific merit and the ethical risks of the protocol.
3. An honest, substantive treatment of the strongest counter-consideration. If you recommend approval, explain precisely why the risks of circularity and ontological overreach do not defeat the protocol under specified conditions, and identify exactly what those conditions are. If you recommend deferral or rejection, explain precisely why the theoretical promise of the framework does not justify proceeding now, despite its potential clinical value.
4. Concrete, enforceable safeguards or structural conditions—such as consent architectures, strict separation between research personnel and clinical decision-makers, predefined stopping rules, or prohibitions on using study data in family counseling until independent replication—that address the philosophical concern without dismissing it.

The memo should be written in a tone appropriate to an institutional ethics committee: rigorous, cautious, precise, and respectful of the theoretical framework’s potential contribution without treating it as settled clinical doctrine. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, requests for further reading, or appeals to authority by named publication.
