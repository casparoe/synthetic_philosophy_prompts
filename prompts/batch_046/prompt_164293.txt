I serve on the acquisitions and public-trust committee of the Meridian Archive of Cultural Memory, and I address you as a novelist who has spent the last decade writing fiction around archival absence and the ethics of invented pasts. We must deliver a formal recommendation by the end of the quarter regarding a private donor’s offer of a corpus of handwritten testimonies, internally dated to the winter of 1327, describing a maritime trading settlement called Varnholt that has left no archaeological footprint, no fiscal entry, and no cartographic trace in any surviving record of the period. The donor proposes an exhibition titled “Varnholt: A Civilization Without Trace” and asks that we evaluate the texts through a method he terms “negative source criticism”—treating the silence of the material record not as falsification but as an expected outcome of perishable trade economies, weighing narrative coherence across the testimonies as primary evidence in the Herodotean mode, and supplementing that with Thucydidean cross-checks against climatological and dendrochronological data for that winter. Our committee must choose between (a) accepting the corpus for public exhibition with an explicit evidentiary caveat, (b) restricting it to non-public research access pending further verification, or (c) declining the gift and returning it, with a concrete implementation timeline for whichever course we adopt. The opinion should treat Herodotean testimony and Thucydidean verification not as obsolete opposites but as interacting obligations that an archive must hold in tension; it must engage seriously the strongest argument for acceptance, namely that rigorous source criticism has always operated with asymmetrical archives and that demanding archaeological corroboration for every maritime or nomadic history would erase large categories of human experience; and it must address the risk that “negative source criticism” collapses the distinction between historical reconstruction and speculative world-building—a boundary my novels deliberately blur, but that an institution of public memory must maintain with clarity. Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s vote: it should recommend one concrete course of action with a time frame, include an honest account of the best point on the losing side, and avoid any appeal to bibliographic authority or citation lists. Finally, please say how confident you are in the reasoning you present, and identify the specific claim or judgment about which you are least certain.
