Consider the view that there are genuinely free-floating normative reasons—reasons that exist and carry weight even when there is no possible reasoner to be moved by them, and that do not depend for their existence on any mental state, social practice, or natural property that could serve as their ground. On this view, the fact that unnecessary suffering occurs can be a reason against it even in a permanently uninhabited universe, not because anyone is there to care, but because the suffering-state stands in a direct normative relation to an absence-state that needs no mediator.

What would count against this position? I want more than the familiar objection that reasons conceptually require a reasoner. Identify the specific intuitions, metaphysical costs, and analogies—to other apparently relational properties that turned out to be projective—that genuinely threaten it. Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which of them would a defender of free-floating reasons concede as damaging, and which could they absorb by modifying the view without collapsing it into a familiar naturalist or constructivist account? Be explicit about whether the view risks unfalsifiability by defining "reason" in a way that makes the absence of reasoners irrelevant by stipulation.

Finally, say what would change your assessment: which argument or observation would lead you to reject or accept free-floating reasons, and where is your reasoning here most vulnerable?
