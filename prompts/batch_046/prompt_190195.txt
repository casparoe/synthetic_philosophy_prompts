I have been moderating a graduate reading group on modal metaphysics that has now spent three semesters circling the same deadlock: David Lewis’s counterpart-theoretic analysis of de re modal claims versus the transworld-identity framework associated with Saul Kripke and Alvin Plantinga. Every canonical argument is fully visible on both sides. The counterpart theorists invoke relations of similarity across worlds, deny that anything literally exists in more than one world, and treat a claim like “I could have been taller” as true in virtue of a counterpart’s height; their opponents insist that the very same object can instantiate different properties in different possible worlds, charge that counterpart relations smuggle in unexplained similarity standards, and argue that abandoning literal transworld identity undermines the intuitive content of ordinary modal beliefs. Despite this, neither camp treats the objections as decisive—they are catalogued as theoretical costs to be weighed rather than errors to be corrected. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical or formal but methodological: the two sides are operating with incompatible standards for what counts as a successful metaphysical explanation, with one side treating ontological economy as primary and the other treating the preservation of pre-theoretic de re judgments as non-negotiable. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real structure, or whether there is a hidden disagreement about quantification, individual essences, and what counts as data, or whether they are in fact answering different questions—one semantic, one metaphysical—under a single heading.
