I am drafting a historical novel centered on Johannes Kepler during the years he composed his astrological treatises, and I have been attempting to work through a particularly dense passage in which he addresses the ontological status of planetary influence. In the passage—paraphrased here from my reading of his Latin—I encounter the claim that celestial bodies are not efficient causes of sublunary events in the manner of one billiard ball striking another; rather, they function as signs or indications, comparable to the hands of a clock that show the hour without producing it. At the same time, Kepler insists that the earth itself possesses a kind of animating faculty or sensitive soul that perceives the geometric figures formed by the planets—the angles, conjunctions, and aspects—and responds to them with corresponding changes in weather, temperament, or fortune. Thus the aspect is said to be both a mere sign and an occasion for real alteration, mediated not by mechanical force but by the earth's own perceptive nature.

What I find difficult to parse is exactly how Kepler means to hold these two commitments together without collapsing into either pure symbolism or covert mechanism. Does he intend the geometric configuration to act as a formal cause upon the earth's soul, analogous to the way a melody moves a listener? Or is the response of the earth's soul better understood as an autonomous reaction that merely correlates with the celestial sign, leaving astrology as a predictive art rather than a causal science? Furthermore, I am struck by a structural resemblance to narrative foreshadowing: in fiction, an author places an image or event that signals what is to come without physically causing the outcome, yet the reader's awareness of the signal shapes their experience of the plot. I wonder whether importing this literary analogy helps illuminate Kepler's distinction between signification and causation, or whether it risks anachronistically imposing a modern semiotic framework onto an early modern cosmology in which form and matter are not yet separated in our contemporary way.

Could you help me understand the passage by clarifying the precise relationship Kepler posits between celestial geometry, the earth's responsive faculty, and the predictive claims of astrology? I would particularly appreciate if you could separate the conceptual machinery that belongs to Kepler's own Aristotelian and Platonic commitments from those that might be artifacts of my novelist's tendency to read symbolic structure into physical description.
