I'm preparing a seventy-five-minute seminar for second-year philosophy students on Zhuangzi's view of death, and I want the session to turn on a distinction that survey treatments usually flatten. Most students will arrive with a therapeutic framing—grief stages, Stoic acceptance, death anxiety—so I need to establish early that Zhuangzi is not offering a coping strategy but an ontological claim about transformation (hua) in which personal extinction and cosmic redistribution are not opposed. I intend to use the tub-drumming anecdote after his wife's death as the carrying example rather than the butterfly dream, on the grounds that the emotional shock of the image can do more philosophical work than the epistemic ambiguity of dreaming; but I'm unsure whether to introduce the seasonal-cycle metaphor for transformation before or after that anecdote lands. Could you plan the explanatory arc—what conceptual dependency must be in place first, the precise order in which to move from seasonal change to personal death without making the leap seem like metaphor alone, how to deploy the tub-drumming scene specifically to separate transformation from both emotional suppression and from a nihilistic denial of loss, and at which points in the sequence to intercept the predictable misconceptions (that he is callous, that he denies grief, that he believes in literal immortality, or that he is advancing an Epicurean "death is nothing to us" argument)? I also need a single diagnostic question for the end that would show whether they have grasped the ontological claim rather than merely describing Zhuangzi as "chill about death," plus an explicit note on what comparative material—Confucian ritual mourning, modern grief psychology, or Daoist alchemical texts—I should leave out entirely to keep the dependency chain tight in one session.
