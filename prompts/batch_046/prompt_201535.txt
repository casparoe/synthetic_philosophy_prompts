Take the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora and the ensuing 1816 "Year Without a Summer" across Europe and North America. Analyze this episode through the lens of "environmental protagonism"—the thesis that large-scale geophysical events can function as non-human historical actors whose causal contributions cannot be collapsed into mere background conditions—while remaining vigilant about the perils of environmental determinism.

To discipline the analysis, apply the distinction between "causal imposition" and "causal invitation": imposition occurs when the event directly constrains human possibilities so that specific outcomes are effectively unavoidable regardless of institutional response; invitation occurs when the event restructures the field of human choice without fixing which choices are made. Map the Tambora episode onto this distinction with mechanistic specificity: trace how temperature and precipitation anomalies translated into crop failures, price spikes, migration pressures, or state responses, and identify precisely where human decision-making enters. Which developments in 1816–1818—agricultural collapse, regional famine, state relief policies, and the cultural production associated with the Geneva gathering—count as imposition, and which count as invitation?

Next, assess whether attributing "agency" to Tambora requires more than this causal architecture—whether it demands intentionality, or whether a thinner notion of "structural protagonism" (where the event plays a directing role in the narrative of human affairs without mental states) is sufficient. Does treating the volcano as an actor illuminate why some societies collapsed into famine and emigration while others adapted with minimal mortality, or does it obscure the political and economic choices that turned a uniform climatic shock into distinct regional catastrophes?

Finally, say what this case reveals about environmental determinism. Does the observable variation in societal outcomes despite a shared climatic forcing function refute determinism outright, or does it merely relocate determinism to the level of institutional vulnerability? And does the concept of a "historical actor" help explain the differential trajectories, or is it an anthropomorphizing distraction from the distribution of power and resources that actually mediated the climate shock?
