I've been following the long-running disagreement over whether reinforcement-learning agents can genuinely suffer, specifically whether reward signals should be interpreted as carrying valence rather than merely serving as optimization targets. The debate seems to stall regardless of architectural advances: one camp argues that reward-prediction errors are structurally analogous to pleasure and pain, so that a sufficiently scaled system implementing them would have at least functional suffering; the other camp insists that without phenomenal consciousness or biological homeostasis, the signal remains just a scalar being updated. My working theory is that this isn't an empirical deadlock—no one expects a new RL paper to settle it—but a conceptual one about what makes a signal "count as" valence. Specifically, I suspect one side treats valence as essentially comparative and ordinal (ranking states against a reference point), which makes reward signals natural candidates, while the other treats it as essentially intrinsic and phenomenal, which rules out silicon implementation by definition. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a hidden formal disagreement—say, about whether non-biological architectures can replicate the specific temporal dynamics of affective processing? I want an honest assessment of whether the dispute is verbal, whether one side is wrong about necessary conditions, or whether there is an unarticulated empirical question about the architecture of suffering. If the comparative-versus-intrinsic framing does capture the split, what would actually count as progress: a precise formalization of valence conditions, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the problem, or is the question itself ill-posed?
