Question: Explain the parity principle advanced by Clark and Chalmers, and apply it to the case of Otto and his notebook. Then present and evaluate one serious objection to the claim that Otto's belief about the museum's location is literally extended into the notebook rather than merely supported by it.

Student answer:

Clark and Chalmers defend the parity principle, which holds that if an external resource plays the same functional role in guiding behavior as an internal cognitive process, it should be counted as part of the cognitive system regardless of whether it lies inside the skull. In the case of Otto, who relies on a notebook because of memory impairment, the notebook serves the same purpose as Inga's biological memory: both store the information that the museum is on 53rd Street, both are consulted before action, and both are automatically endorsed by the agent. Because the functional profile is equivalent, there is no non-arbitrary reason to locate Otto's belief inside his brain while allowing Inga's belief inside hers.

A prominent objection comes from Adams and Aizawa, who charge that Clark and Chalmers commit the coupling-constitution fallacy: causal coupling between an agent and an artifact is not sufficient for that artifact to become part of the agent's mind. A calculator is coupled to me when I use it, yet it is not part of my cognition. Clark and Chalmers reply that this misrepresents their view, since the parity principle concerns functional equivalence within an integrated system, not mere causal interaction.

Still, I think the objection succeeds in part. Clark and Chalmers implicitly require that an extended resource be continuously coupled to the agent in real time, and Otto's notebook fails this condition because he accesses it only intermittently. Since Inga's biological memory is also accessed intermittently, this reveals that the boundary between mind and world is arbitrary. Consequently, while the parity principle shows that location should not matter, it does not establish that the notebook is literally constitutive of Otto's belief; it remains a highly reliable external tool.
