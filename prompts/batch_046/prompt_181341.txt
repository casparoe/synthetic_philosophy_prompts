I was grading papers for a development-economics semenar and got stuck on a tension I can't quite shake. The practice I want to think about is the standard institutional-diagnostic framework used by development agencies: consultants arrive in a poor country, run governance indicators, compare outcomes to success stories like South Korea or Botswana, and prescribe legal and bureaucratic reforms—property-rights tweaks, anti-corruption courts, streamlined business registration. The implicit causal story is that institutions are the deep driver of national wealth, and that history mainly supplies exogenous variation—colonial origins, settler mortality patterns, geographic accident—that lets economists identify institutional effects without running actual experiments. But here is the puzzle: the same literature insists that counterfactual histories are impossible, that geographic and cultural confounders never fully disappear, and that we cannot randomize nation-building. So the practice behaves as if we have solved the identification problem well enough to engineer societies, while methodologically admitting we have not.

What philosophical commitments about causal inferance, historical explanation, and collective agency does that practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that institutions are uniquely manipulable in a way climate or culture are not? Does it quietly depend on a normative theory of development—say, that GDP growth is the right metric, or that external experts can legitimately diagnose institutional failure? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what merely fits well with it, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined—whether a geographic determinist or a radical culturalist could license the same consulting routine with different recommendations. I am especially curious about the apparent inconsistency in treating historical path-dependence as both the proof that institutions matter (because colonial origins shaped them) and as something a five-year reform program can break. Say what would have to change in the practice if we replaced its hidden epistemology with a genuinely agnostic view—one that admits we cannot rank the causes of national wealth with any real confidence.

Please also say what would change your mind here—what specific argument or empirical finding would convince you that this practice actually rests on a coherent hidden philosophy rather than on a cluster of incompatible assumptions, and point to where my reconstruction is most likely to fall apart.
