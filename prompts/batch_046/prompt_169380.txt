As a student in my seminar on post-war moral philosophy, I am trying to stabilize an argument about whether Karl Jaspers's notion of political guilt can coherently extend across generations to citizens who played no part in the original crimes. We have read Jaspers's distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, and I want to push beyond the standard debate about whether nations are moral agents. Specifically, I want to examine a framing that treats the democratic successor state not as an agent inheriting blame, but as an unsettled institutional debtor: a legal and economic continuity whose present structures—territorial settlements, property regimes, constitutional foundations—remain unrectified because they were consolidated through atrocities that have never been fully acknowledged or repaired. The question is whether citizens of such a state bear a distinct form of collective guilt, not for having committed the wrong, but for democratically maintaining the benefits of an unredressed wrong within frameworks they have the power to amend. Write an essay that weighs this framing against its main rivals. On one side, defend the idea that diachronic institutional debt is a coherent category that captures something real about collective responsibility without collapsing into individual culpability for past acts. On the other, consider the objections that states are not persons and therefore cannot bear debts in any meaningful sense, that inherited structures are not chosen and therefore cannot ground guilt, and that demanding present rectification risks using living citizens merely as instruments for historical justice. Reach and defend a clear verdict: either that this institutional-debtor framing succeeds in identifying a genuine, cross-generational form of collective guilt, or that it ultimately fails and we should abandon inherited guilt in favor of purely present-oriented obligations of repair that apply to individuals regardless of national membership. Write entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a formal letter. In addition, say what specific argument or piece of evidence would cause you to change your verdict, and point out the part of your reasoning where you are least confident.
