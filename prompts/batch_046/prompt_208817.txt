I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been working through metaphysics and the philosophy of science with a small reading group. Last month, two members—Marcus, a philosopher of science, and Dr. Elena Vasquez, a metaphysician—became locked in a disagreement about modal identity and scientific counterfactuals. I am reporting their exchange exactly as it occurred; I have no preference between them.

The case they constructed was this. Consider Dr. Amara Okonkwo, a synthetic biologist in our world who pursues a gene-drive project that ultimately fails and derails her career. In a nearby possible world, W, she abandons the project after an early warning, shifts to immunotherapy, and receives a major award. The dispute concerns whether the successful immunotherapist in W is Okonkwo herself, or merely a counterpart.

Here is the exchange, round by round.

Round 1. Marcus: Counterpart theory is the correct framework for asking "what if Okonkwo had abandoned the project?" If origin essentialism is true, then in any world where she originates from a different fertilization event she does not exist; consequently, "Okonkwo could have succeeded" is false for nearly every modal scenario we care about. Counterpart theory saves our modal intuitions by allowing a suitably similar individual in W to stand in for her, without requiring literal identity across worlds.

Elena: That saves intuition only by emptying it. If the successful researcher in W is not Okonkwo but only a counterpart, then the claim "Okonkwo could have succeeded" is false; you have merely asserted that someone like her did. The purpose of modal self-reflection—whether for personal decision or scientific counterfactual—is to learn about one's own capacities, not about strangers.

Round 2. Marcus: But transworld identity requires selecting essential properties, and for persons that selection is arbitrary. Is it her DNA? Her psychological profile? Her institutional role? Without a principled criterion, you are stipulating identity conditions to preserve pre-theoretic intuition. Counterpart theory makes the similarity weights explicit.

Elena: The criterion is not arbitrary: origin essentialism holds that Okonkwo is essentially the product of that specific fertilization event. That yields a clear, non-stipulated boundary. Your counterpart relation, by contrast, conceals a hidden parameter—how much psychological or physical similarity is required? You smuggle arbitrariness into formal machinery.

Round 3. Marcus: Origin essentialism renders modal knowledge practically impossible. We cannot inspect other worlds to verify fertilization events. Counterpart theory permits reasoning about nearby worlds by adjusting specific parameters—career choice, location—while holding the rest fixed, which is precisely how scientists construct counterfactual models.

Elena: That is a pragmatic argument about reasoning, not a metaphysical argument about identity. Scientists routinely employ model organisms and analog simulations, but no one confuses the model with the target system. Your "counterpart" is a modeling instrument, not a metaphysical discovery about who exists where.

Round 4. Marcus: If counterpart theory is merely modeling, then your transworld-identity claim is also merely a model—one that arbitrarily privileges origin over function. Yet in scientific practice we identify entities by functional and relational roles: genes by what they do, particles by their interactions. If we apply the same functionalist standard to agents, then the immunotherapist in W—occupying Okonkwo's exact causal-niche in the research tradition—should count as Okonkwo herself.

Elena: That is a category error. Genes and particles are theoretical posits defined by their roles; persons are not. To reduce "me" to a functional role is to deny that I possess a first-person perspective that persists regardless of role change. If I become a hermit, I remain myself; if a stranger takes over my laboratory, that is not me continuing.
