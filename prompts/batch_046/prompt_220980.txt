Write an essay on whether the identity of a letterform—specifically, what makes every instance an "a"—is grounded in invariant geometric relations or in fluid, analogical patterns of style. Contrast an essentialist view, which holds that all "a"s share a necessary structural skeleton (for example, a closed bowl combined with an ascending stem), with an analogical view, which treats letterhood as a family-resemblance cluster maintained by proportional analogies among curves, weights, and spatial relations. To adjudicate between these accounts, introduce the following hypothetical: an adversarial letterform that inverts every conventional stylistic parameter of the canonical "a"—reversing stroke curvature, exchanging thick and thin axes, and mirroring aspect ratio—while preserving exactly the same internal analogical relations between its parts. Would such a form still be recognized as an "a" by a human observer, and does the analogical framework predict that recognition better than the essentialist one? Use this thought experiment to reach a definite conclusion—either that letter identity ultimately depends on invariant structure, that it is irreducibly analogical and style-relative, or that the adversarial case forces a synthesis—and defend it with conceptual reasons. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; argue from analysis alone.
