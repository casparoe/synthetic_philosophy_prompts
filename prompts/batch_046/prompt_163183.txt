I'm an institutional review board consultant for a university cognitive-science lab, and I review roughly a dozen protocols per month involving rapid visual presentation, subjective-timing reports, and real-time neurofeedback loops. Many proposals invoke the distinction between Orwellian revision—where the brain rewrites the record of what happened after the fact, leaving a false memory or narrative—and Stalinesque revision—where the false or intermediate content is inserted directly into the stream of consciousness and experienced as if it had always been present—especially regarding the color phi phenomenon, in which observers report seeing a moving intermediate color between two differently colored dots presented in rapid succession. I need a reusable decision procedure my team can run in about twenty minutes per protocol, without requiring anyone to read primary philosophy literature on the spot. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist or flowchart-in-words: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to move to the next step, explicit stop-points where a protocol is rejected or approved with conditions, and clearly flagged nodes where the question is irreducibly one of values or interpretation rather than method. The procedure must preserve the conceptual difference between post-hoc narrative reconstruction and direct phenomenal insertion, because that difference determines whether a study can ethically claim to measure pre-change experience rather than post-change report. It should also be honest about where it will misfire—where a well-designed study still cannot distinguish the two mechanisms, and where an answer depends on a judgment call that must be signed off by the principal investigator rather than by me. Before constructing the procedure, please think carefully and begin by brainstorming the core distinctions, the typical failure modes in experimental design, and the specific points where algorithmic decision must yield to human judgment. Then translate that into the practical checklist.

Please include worked applications to three cases: (1) a standard psychophysics study using color-phi stimuli with retrospective verbal reports and confidence ratings; (2) a proposed VR neurofeedback protocol that decodes EEG signals during rapid color changes and claims to render Stalinesque revision transparent by feeding back the decoded intermediate state to the subject in real time; and (3) a single-subject clinical report of a migraine-with-aura patient describing anticipatory color transitions that seem to occur before the physical stimulus change. At least one of these should yield an answer my team will not like—either an approval burdened with a severe caveat, or a rejection despite the investigators' strong claims. Finally, state explicitly what this procedure cannot do: which metaphysical or phenomenological questions it deliberately leaves untouched, and under what conditions we should escalate to a philosopher rather than applying the checklist. Write as an expert philosopher of cognitive science with professional experience in research ethics, use American English, and be precise about where judgment replaces algorithm.
