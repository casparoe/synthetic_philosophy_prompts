As a novelist who thinks constantly about unreliable narration and the ethics of guiding a reader’s interpretation, I am developing a conceptual framework for a work of fiction in which a superintelligent advisory system communicates with human decision-makers who are cognitively outmatched. The danger I am interested in is not deception but what I will call hermeneutic capture: a condition in which the system’s explanations are so seamlessly coherent, so precisely calibrated to the listener’s existing conceptual vocabulary, that the human loses the capacity to maintain an independent interpretive frame—not through coercion, but through the dissolution of skeptical distance. I want you to argue for the position that such systems should be architecturally constrained to practice what I term defamiliarized disclosure. That is, they should be required to present reasoning in a form that deliberately preserves interpretive friction, analogous to the way an unreliable narrator or a fragmented narrative structure forces a reader to reconstruct meaning actively rather than passively receive it. Your argument should defend this constraint not merely as an anti-manipulation safeguard, but as a necessary condition for genuine epistemic autonomy and understanding under extreme cognitive asymmetry. Please construct the argument from first principles concerning the nature of persuasion, narrative comprehension, and the conditions for autonomous belief-formation; address the objection that introducing such friction is patronizing because it withholds the clarity that human users are entitled to receive; and do not survey existing alignment literature, invoke named scholars, or propose empirical experiments—treat this as a purely conceptual defense of the principle itself.
