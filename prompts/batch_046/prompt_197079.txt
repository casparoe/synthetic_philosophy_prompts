I was talking with my friends after our philosphy of mind seminer and got stuck on a specific version of eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires. Most people discuss Churchland's argument that neuroscience will eventually replace folk psychology, but I'm holding a narrower view: beliefs and desires should be eliminated not merely because they're absent from neuroscience, but because they fail a specific integration criteron - namely, that a real mental kind must participate in cross-domain causal loops with measurable neural dynamics that can be independantly tracked across perceptual, affective, and motor systems. Beliefs, on my view, don't do that; they're just post-hoc interpretive labels we slap onto behavior.

What would genuinely count against this position? I want evidence and arguments sorted by how decisive they'd be, including whether there are empirical findings showing belief-like states actually do enter those causal loops, or conceptual arguments showing that the integration criteron either proves too much (eliminating other mental states we want to keep) or collapses eliminativism into a kind of pragmatic instrumentalism. I also want an honest assessment of whether people who defend this kind of criterion-based eliminativism have qualified their view into unfalsifiability - for instance, by saying that absence of integration is just absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence - and whether that insulation is intellectually costly. Please don't just rehearse standard objections to Churchland; focus on what hits this integration-framing specifically, and say what defenders of the view themselves might concede as damaging.
