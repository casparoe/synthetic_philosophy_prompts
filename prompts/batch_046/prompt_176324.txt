I am a novelist who convenes a fortnightly seminar for fellow writers and curious readers lacking any formal philosophical background. For next week's fifty-minute session I must construct an explanation of disjunctivism and the argument from illusion, not as an abstract academic exercise, but because our present collective writing project turns on how fiction renders perceptual error and mistaken appearance. I do not wish to be taught the doctrine itself; rather, I require a teaching plan that maps the precise order in which the ideas must be introduced, with the dependency relations made explicit—specifically, what must be secured first so that the audience can understand why disjunctivism denies that veridical and illusory experiences share a common mental kind, and how the argument from illusion appears to compel that shared kind upon us.

Please identify the single carrying example or constructed scenario that should bear the explanatory weight—preferably one drawn from narrative practice rather than the conventional philosophical illustrations—and specify exactly what properties it must possess and what properties it must lack, so that it illuminates the disjunctive claim rather than collapsing into a mere case of false belief or unreliable narration.

Next, lay out the misconceptions that this particular audience will predictably form: I expect they will confuse perceptual illusion with literary unreliability, and that they will interpret disjunctivism as the claim that illusory experiences are not genuinely occurring mental events. For each misconception, indicate the precise point in the explanatory sequence at which it should be anticipated and defused.

Then propose one diagnostic question I can ask at the close of the session to determine whether the participants have grasped the philosophical distinction itself, rather than having merely adopted a useful literary metaphor.

Finally, state explicitly what should be omitted at this level—perhaps the finer distinctions within the canonical responses to the argument, or the historical taxonomy of sense-data theories—and justify why leaving it out serves the clarity of the session.

Present the plan in British English, organised under clear headings for the sequence, the carrying example, the misconception map, the check for understanding, and the omissions.
