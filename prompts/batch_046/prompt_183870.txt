I'm part of an informal reading circle—mostly friends without formal philosophy or literature training—where over the past year we've read paired biographies in something like the classical mode: one life presented as a study in virtue, another in the same sphere fallen into failure, plus modern lives that discuss private failures openly. We've found that people draw wildly different moral lessons from the same account. Some treat the biography as a monument: the reader should measure the distance between herself and the subject's best moments. Others treat it as a warning: only broken lives show what virtue actually costs. A few of us have begun wondering about a stranger possibility—that sometimes the biography teaches precisely through what it suppresses, when an attentive reader notices contradictions between the events narrated and the moral portrait drawn.

I'm not asking for a bibliography or a survey of scholars. Instead, please design a decision tree that sorts these disagreements for our group. Begin with whether biography can convey moral knowledge at all. From there, branch on whether the lesson comes from the subject's actions, from the biographer's framing, or from the reader's detection of gaps and omissions—specifically including the position I'm tentatively calling detectable smoothing: the idea that a hagiographic life, by suppressing contradictions, can still instruct, but only for readers who perceive the suppression and treat the tension itself as the moral content. Next split on whether revealed private failures strengthen, weaken, or transform public exemplarity. Each leaf should be either a named stance (pure exemplarism, cautionary biography, narrative irony, or the smoothing-detection view) or labeled unnamed—likely unexplored, with one sentence on its most awkward commitment, such as requiring readers to be more perceptive than the author intended. Use concrete case-questions rather than abstract labels—for example, asking whether discovering that the subject lied about the episode that most moved you would destroy, deepen, or redirect the lesson—so that someone can place herself without knowing philosophical terminology. Include about eight to ten leaves, flag any pair of positions your branches cannot separate, and note any empty combinations of answers with a brief diagnosis of why they might be unoccupied, whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely untouched. Keep the tone polished but accessible, suitable for a handout at a community book discussion.
