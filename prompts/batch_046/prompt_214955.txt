Question: Epiphenomenalism maintains that conscious experiences are causally inert by-products of neural activity. The paradox of phenomenal judgment observes that if qualia exert no causal influence, then they cannot be the cause of our beliefs or reports about them; yet we routinely claim direct knowledge of our own experience. Evaluate whether this paradox genuinely undermines epiphenomenalism. Your evaluation should (a) explain why appealing to neural correlates of consciousness does not automatically dissolve the problem; (b) consider whether the difficulty is restricted to linguistic self-reports or also extends to non-linguistic behaviors—such as aversive conditioning or preferential looking in pre-linguistic subjects—that appear guided by experience; and (c) clearly distinguish this epistemic challenge from the evolutionary argument that consciousness lacks adaptive value if it is causally powerless. Reach and defend a reasoned conclusion.

Student answer: The core claim of epiphenomenalism is that mental states are produced by brain states but never cause physical events in return. The paradox of phenomenal judgment arises because if my judgment that I feel pain is caused only by C-fiber firing and not by the pain itself, then the judgment is not genuinely about the pain—much as a thermometer registers temperature without feeling heat. This is essentially Chalmers's zombie argument in another form, since both cases show that physical mechanisms can replicate mental functions without consciousness playing any causal role. Frank Jackson addressed a closely related issue in his knowledge argument by introducing phenomenal concepts: these are self-presenting modes of presentation that allow direct acquaintance with experience even when the experience does not cause the concept. Applying Jackson's idea here, the brain can cause my judgment while the phenomenal concept ensures the judgment is still about the experience. However, this solution works only for language-capable adults; for infants or animals displaying aversive conditioning or preferential looking, there are no phenomenal concepts, so the paradox is actually more severe in non-linguistic cases. There, the behavior tracks the neural state rather than the qualia, which means the epiphenomenalist must treat the appearance of experience-guided behavior as an illusion sustained by constant correlation. Consequently, I think the paradox does not fully defeat epiphenomenalism—systematic correlation is sufficient for tracking—but it does force the view to deny that phenomenal judgments enjoy any special directness. The evolutionary argument is a separate matter about natural selection, not about the epistemology of self-knowledge.
