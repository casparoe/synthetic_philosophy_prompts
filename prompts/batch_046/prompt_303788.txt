hey so i'm a phd student in moral philosophy and i do part-time mediation for a city arbitration board—like 2-3 urban development disputes a month, mostly demolition vs preservation, climate deadlines always looming, and the stakes are literally people's homes and shared public spaces. the board keeps forcing these principled checklists onto cases (utilitarian cost-benefit, deontological veto points) and they always feel wrong, like shoehorning round situations into square boxes. 

i want a reusable decision procedure grounded in moral particularism—think dancy-style particularism with some of the newer work on moral attention and salience—translated into steps our mediators can actualy run in a 90-minute session with zero philosophy background. it shouldn't list invariant moral principles; instead it should be an attentional flow: which situational features to notice first, how to check whether a reason's salience or weight shifts across contexts, how to navigate conflict without invoking universal rules. i also want it to handle this concept i'm playing with called intergenerational aesthetic harm—the wrong of destroying a particular built environment for future people not through rights-violation but through eliminating a specific form of shared perceptual coherence they could have inherited. apply the procedure to three cases: demolishing a brutalist library for solar panels, relocating a community garden for flood barriers, and one case where the procedure reccomends demolition despite strong local attachment—maybe where the intergenerational harm argument actually cuts against preservation. flag every step where judgment can't be eliminated, say where particularism itself might misfire, and be honest about what the procedure can't do, including who has final sign-off if deadlock remains.
