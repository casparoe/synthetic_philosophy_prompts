I was in a graduate seminar on comparative economic history, and two students—Mira and Julian—got into a prolonged argument about whether the classic Needham framing, “why Britain and not China?”, is a well-posed historical question at all. They asked me to referee because neither would concede any ground. Here is the exchange almost exactly as it unfolded.

Mira opened by saying the contrast is entirely legitimate: around 1700 Britain and Qing China had roughly comparable per-capita living standards, comparable agricultural productivity, and comparable technological repertoires, so asking why Britain alone broke through to sustained fossil-fuel-driven mechanization is just asking for the causes of an observed divergence, not smuggling in teleology. Julian replied that this already assumes “industrialization” is a single, well-defined endpoint that both societies were implicitly competing toward, when in fact Qing China was optimized for a very different equilibrium—labor-intensive, state-coordinated grain production with high living standards—so framing the comparison as Britain succeeding where China failed is like asking why a chess grandmaster lost at checkers. Mira countered that divergence is an empirical fact visible in GDP-per-capita trajectories after 1800, and explaining that split requires no commitment to industrial modernity being history’s natural endpoint; it is simply comparative causation about a documented outcome. Julian then argued that GDP itself is a metric that privileges capital-intensive growth, and that if we instead measured state capacity, agricultural output per labor-hour, or life expectancy, China was not clearly “behind” Britain in 1750; therefore the question retroactively imposes a British metric of success and then asks why China failed to meet it. Mira conceded that metric pluralism might matter but insisted that fossil-fuel mechanization reshaped global power regardless of which welfare metrics we prefer, so asking why that specific cluster emerged in northwestern Europe rather than in the Yangtze Delta remains a genuine causal puzzle. Julian’s final move was that even if it is a causal puzzle, the “Britain and not China” framing is misleading because Britain’s breakthrough depended on New World silver flows, colonial cotton supplies, and geographically concentrated coal deposits that were globally distributed but captured through specific imperial networks; China lacked not ingenuity but access to that particular global extractive architecture, so the right comparative unit is not “Britain versus China” but “Britain-plus-empire versus China-within-Asia.”

Please take on the role of a philosopher of history with expertise in comparative methodology and contrastive explanation. Before adjudicating, do a brief round of brainstorming: what does a “why X and not Y” question in history actually require—shared counterfactual baselines, comparable institutional starting points, or only a documented divergence in outcomes? Then adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate Mira’s opening, Julian’s chess analogy, Mira’s GDP reply, Julian’s metric-pluralism objection, Mira’s fossil-fuel reply, and Julian’s imperial-architecture reply, each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification. Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—whether there is a hidden empirical premise about technological possibility, whether the disagreement is partly about what counts as a legitimate comparative unit, or whether the impasse signals that the original question should be dissolved rather than answered. Think carefully and avoid generic summaries; engage each argument as actually made.
