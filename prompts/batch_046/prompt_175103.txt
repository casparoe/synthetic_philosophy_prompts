Write a short story—approximately 800 words—in which the Buddhist doctrine of anattā, or no-self, is not discussed but enacted through the formal architecture of the narrative itself. Adopt the first-person voice of a fresco conservator working alone in a deconsecrated chapel, repairing a wall painting from the fourteenth century that has been damaged by rising damp and salt efflorescence. Structure the story in five sequential sections, each corresponding without being named to one of the five aggregates (skandhas): form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), mental formations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa). Within each section, the narrator should seem to possess a continuous identity, yet the accumulating sensory details should gradually expose that continuity as a retrospective fiction—much as the fresco’s image is not an underlying picture waiting to be revealed, but only a provisional coordination of plaster, pigment, mineral salts, and moisture. The turning point must be physical and entirely mundane: a scaffold joint giving way, a sudden slip of the hand, a crumbling patch of intonaco that forces the narrator to recognize that the “restorer” is not an agent directing these events from behind them, but the events themselves distributed across materials and moments. Avoid all explicit Buddhist terminology, reincarnation imagery, mystical visions, and philosophical exposition; the dissolution of narrative identity should be felt before it is recognized. I want precise, sensory prose rather than abstract reflection, and I want the text constructed so that a reader familiar with the Abhidhamma analysis of consciousness as discrete, momentary flashes can perceive the structural echo without being instructed. Deliver only the story, with no author’s note, no framing commentary, and no explanation of the concept: I am testing whether fiction can make the provisional nature of personal identity—what the tradition calls a practical designation rather than a metaphysical substance—feel like an experience rather than an argument.
