I have been trying to work out an idea about moral responsibility without relying on heavy metaphysics, and I keep returning to P.F. Strawson's distinction between reactive attitudes—feelings like resentment and gratitude that we direct at people as moral agents—and the objective attitude we take toward things we merely manage or treat. I think I understand the basic picture: responsibility lives in these emotional responses rather than in some hidden capacity for free choice. But I want to push this in a direction I am not sure has been properly explored.

Here are my notes for a short essay. I propose that there is a second layer of reactive attitudes that Strawson does not clearly address: what I might call "structural reactive attitudes." These are not directed at a single conscious person but at an organized system—say, resentment toward a corporation for polluting a river, or gratitude toward a research collective for a discovery. The feelings seem genuinely reactive rather than merely objective, because they involve holding the entity accountable rather than just fixing it like a broken machine. Yet there is no single mind behind the action in the ordinary sense.

My tentative claim is that Strawson's framework can accommodate this if we allow reactive attitudes to target functional roles rather than individual minds. The danger, I think, is that this might water down the concept: if we can genuinely resent a corporation, do we risk treating actual people with an objective attitude whenever they act inside a large system?

Could you comment on these notes? In particular: have I misrepresented Strawson's distinction between the reactive and objective stances? Is the idea of "structural reactive attitudes" coherent, or does it collapse into either collective blame against individuals or a purely instrumental attitude? And is there anything genuinely interesting in framing the problem this way, or am I just mixing familiar ideas without adding clarity?
