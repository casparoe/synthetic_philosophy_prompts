I've been trying to move past the standard intro-philosophy framing of "objective versus subjective meaning" and actually specify what an adequate theory of meaning in life has to deliver. I want the full desiderata list—not just "it should feel right"—with each requirement tied to the specific case or distinction that motivates it, plus a judgment about which ones are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just camp-specific prejudices. Specifically, any adequate theory needs to: (1) preserve the distinction between global meaning (life as a whole) and local meaning (particular projects or relationships) without reducing one to the other; (2) accommodate cases where life seems meaningful despite extreme suffering or absence of conventional achievement, and symmetric cases where conventional achievement is present but meaning is conspicuously absent; (3) remain consistent with what we know about motivation, reward prediction, and the psychology of purpose—without treating meaning as a scalar good to be maximized like utility or preference satisfaction; (4) handle counterfactual stability: whether a life that derives its meaning from beliefs that turn out false retains that meaning upon discovery, and whether that retention is a feature or a fatal bug; (5) either preserve some non-eliminable form of transcendence or fully naturalize meaning in a way that doesn't collapse it into well-being, social approval, or evolutionary fitness; (6) distinguish meaning-in-life from the distinct question of whether a life is worth living or whether suicide is rational, since conflating them produces implausible dependencies between metaphysical status and practical decision. For each requirement, give the motivating case, explain whether failure is fatal or merely awkward, and say which popular theories it most directly threatens. Then tell me which single desideratum does the most damage to the dominant contemporary accounts—pure subjectivism, objectivist naturalism, hybrid theories, and absurdist responses. A brief scoring table is fine, but keep the conceptual weight on the criteria themselves rather than on ranking the theories. One epistemic constraint: separate what is broadly settled in the specialist literature (e.g., that meaning is not identical to happiness) from what remains genuinely contested (e.g., whether transcendence is indispensable), and don't present one camp's framing—say, that subjectivism is obviously correct or that naturalism is the only serious option—as if it were consensus. I come at this from a rationalist background that cares about not collapsing distinctions that do real work, so precision about what is being assumed versus argued is especially important.
