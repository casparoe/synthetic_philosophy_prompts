hey, i retired from molecular bio a few years ago and lately i've been hanging around philosophy of science and bioethics on weekends — mostly reading, not writing. there's this review board i sit on with grad students and one philosopher, and we've made a handful of calls on research consent that i'm trying to square with eachother.

our track record so far:
- okayed a study that hid the real hypothesis (said memory testing, actually blame attribution) but spelled out every procedure and risk, with a debrif right after.
- rejected one where a confederate pretended to be another subject and humiliated them a bit, even with immediate debrif.
- turned down deception on a cognitively impaired group despite minmal risk.
- approved a $2000 payment for a boring task, said money isn't coercion unless you're desperate.
- said yes to a study that kept the purpose hidden but let people pull out anytime via a phone app — they call it dynamic consent.

now the new proposal: they recruite students right after they get failing exam results, tell them everything — it's about stress and decision-making, involves mild frustration, immediate debrif, can quit — but the kids are clearly upset. no deception, no big money, adults, competant on paper. do we approve?

i think we might be running either a strict triad — information plus no coercion plus competance — or something about whether people are in the right headspace to really autorize it. what's the closest analog in our past cases, and if we say yes versus no, what principle are we actually adopting? also, if our precedents underdetermine it, what further case would split the two rules?
