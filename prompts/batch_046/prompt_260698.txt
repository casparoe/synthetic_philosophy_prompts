Three months ago my sister underwent a complete corpus callosotomy for intractable epilepsy, and observing her recovery has led me to draft an essay on post-surgical personal identity. I argue against both the familiar two-minds reading and the deflationary one-mind-with-divided-input account, proposing instead a framework I call adversarial monism: the idea that a single first-person perspective survives the procedure but becomes internally contested, with each hemisphere constructing a proprietary narrative of agency that treats the other hemisphere's outputs as external intrusions rather than as parts of a shared self. On this view, the split is not merely perceptual but epistemic—the hemispheres fail to share a common framework for attributing mental states to the body they jointly control—so that when her left hand undoes an action initiated by her right, the experience is not that an alien agent is acting but that her own body is disobeying her in a structured, rivalrous way. I want this framing to avoid multiplying persons metaphysically while still honoring the phenomenological reports of patients who describe their own limb as rebellious rather than alien. Before I discuss this with her care team, I would appreciate your comments on the conceptual coherence of adversarial monism, on whether it genuinely differs from existing unity-of-consciousness debates in split-brain philosophy, and on any missteps regarding hemispheric specialization or self-modeling.
