I've been noticing a recurring asymmetry in how democratic institutional innovations are justified and defended, and I'm wondering whether there's an established concept that captures it precisely—or, if not, how to delineate its boundaries without collapsing it into familiar fallacies.

The pattern looks like this. A proponent introduces a mechanism—say, a citizens' assembly with binding authority over climate policy, or a participatory budgeting process with no predetermined spending rules—and frames it in an explicitly Deweyan register: not a fixed system with a predetermined telos, but an open-ended form of social inquiry in which means and ends co-evolve through practice. Because it is an "experiment," they argue, we need not resolve every theoretical objection in advance; we adopt it provisionally and treat outcomes as data.

Then one of two trajectories unfolds. If the results align with broadly shared liberal-democratic norms—perhaps the assembly recommends aggressive but rights-respecting carbon pricing—the outcome is cited as evidence that the experiment "worked," and the provisional framing is quietly dropped in favor of treating the mechanism as having discovered something authoritative about collective will-formation. But if the results conflict with those same norms—say, the assembly endorses suppressing minority interests, or the budget process produces allocations that violate basic fairness—the defenders do not treat this as empirical feedback that should revise the experiment's aims or design. Instead, they invoke a distinction between the "pure" procedure and its "contamination" by inadequate briefing materials, pre-existing power asymmetries, or insufficient deliberative conditions. The failure is localized to implementation, and the core commitment to the mechanism remains insulated.

What strikes me is the selective mobility of the boundary between what is being tested and what is protected. The experimental rhetoric lowers the epistemic burden for adoption—we need not have theoretical guarantees—but it does not symmetrically lower the burden for abandonment when outcomes are unwelcome. Instead, normative guardrails are retroactively installed and presented as implicit preconditions of the original design, while those very guardrails are never themselves subjected to the same provisional, revisable treatment.

I've seen this with municipal participatory-budgeting pilots and with sortition-based legislative assemblies, though I take the pattern to generalize. Is there an established term in democratic theory or philosophy of inquiry for exactly this two-way use of experimental framing—where openness is invoked to justify initiation and closure is invoked to shield evaluation criteria from feedback? I know Popper's concerns about immunization strategies, and I know Dewey himself distinguished ends-in-view from fixed ends, but neither seems to pinpoint the specific asymmetry in how "inquiry" language is used to protect democratic preconditions from empirical revision. If there is no precise name, how should we define the phenomenon—particularly, how does it differ from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis adjustment in scientific practice, and from straightforward bad-faith rhetorical maneuvering?
