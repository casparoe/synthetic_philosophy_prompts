In debates over whether pain is intrinsically bad or only instrumentally valuable, philosophers often lean on a smoke-detector analogy: pain is like an alarm system that tracks tissue damage, and its unpleasantness is the cost of a useful warning signal rather than an evil in itself. Some extend the analogy to clinical presentations of pain asymbolia, in which patients appear to register nociceptive input without the characteristic aversive affect or avoidance behavior — as if the alarm sounds but the occupants do not evacuate. Others invoke the same analogy to argue the opposite, claiming that such dissociation is precisely a malfunction of the alarm's purpose, which is not merely to register danger but to motivate protective action.

Audit this analogy as it operates in the debate. Provide an explicit mapping of correspondences: what corresponds to the smoke, the sensor, the audible alarm, the occupant, the evacuation response, and — if relevant — the system designer? Identify where the mapping breaks: for example, whether pain has an external designer, whether its components are modular and replaceable like hardware, whether its signal is binary or graded, and whether the "occupant" can be separated from the alarm mechanism in the way a building's residents can be separated from a wall-mounted detector. Focus especially on what pain asymbolia reveals or obscures: does it demonstrate that sensory registration and motivational response are separable modules, or does it depend on interpreting a neurological deficit as a "disconnection" between them? Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the instrumentalist conclusion — that pain's badness is purely derivative from its signaling function — from those that are merely ornamental. Then evaluate whether the analogy can also support the opposing view that aversion is constitutive of pain, or whether it collapses once these breaks are made explicit. If the analogy fails, sketch what a better model might look like: perhaps one drawn from control theory, from homeostatic regulation without a separate "observer," or from another framework that avoids importing mechanical design features into a biological process.
