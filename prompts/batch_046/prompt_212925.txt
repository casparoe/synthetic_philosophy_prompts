I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy. I serve on the governing council of a scientific society that two years ago replaced its small expert committee with direct votes by the full membership on all major policies. Since then, well-funded minority factions have routinely blocked decisions by invoking procedural technicalities and principles absent from our charter—operating, in effect, outside the formal rules to reshape them—while defenders praise this as democratic accountability and critics invoke the fall of the Roman Republic, when popular mechanisms bypassed senatorial authority and destabilized the state. I am trying to determine whether our situation is best understood as democratic overreach, as a design flaw that lets actors escape the system meant to constrain them, or as evidence that any formal governance structure eventually faces pressure from outside its own rules. In addressing this, please separate what historians and political philosophers broadly agree about regarding republican decline from interpretations that remain genuinely contested, and do not present any single causal narrative as settled consensus. I seek the philosophical structure of the problem—particularly whether analogies to republican collapse illuminate our case—rather than practical organizational recommendations.
