I'm putting together a sorting worksheet for my polsci grad seminar on liberty — I want students to locate their own view before they learn the names. Write a decision-tree taxonomy with 4-5 branching questions, each split being a concrete yes/no claim or case they can answer without knowing the jargon.

The leaves have to include: pure negative liberty (only direct interference matters), republican non-domination (arbitrary power is a problem even if unused), thick positive liberty (real capacity and self-mastery is required), and any unnamed hybrid that occupies the logical space.

Use these three cases as your split nodes:
1. Silent archive — the state never bans books, but uses an opaque algorithm to bury dissident texts so they're practically invisible. Does this reduce freedom?
2. Benevolent lock-in — mandatory rehab removes addiction and widens future choices, but exit is blocked for six months. Is net freedom gained?
3. Revocable subsidy — everyone gets a basic grant, but a bureaucrat can cancel it without appeal if she judges your lifestyle non-productive. Is this liberty-enhancing?

Start from the logical space: lay out the yes/no combos the cases allow, then map the named positions onto the leaves. Flag empty combinations with a one-line diagnosis (incoherent? unattractive? unexplored?). If republican and negative liberty still end up on the same branch for one of the cases, note that as a limit of the tree rather than hiding it. Keep it casual — it's a handout, not a journal article. And please make sure you can seperate the branches clearly.
