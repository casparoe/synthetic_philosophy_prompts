As a journalist covering the science of affect, I have been tracking a philosophical debate about whether emotions are best understood as judgments, perceptions, or bodily feelings, and I keep encountering analogies that seem to do more work than their users admit. One longstanding comparison treats emotional awareness like vision: just as eyes register color and shape, fear registers danger and grief registers loss, with emotional misapprehension playing the role of optical illusion. A rival image, often used to resist cognitivism, casts emotions as internal weather reports—barometric readings of one's own physiology rather than maps of an external world. More recently, some affective scientists have floated a hybrid drawn from predictive processing: emotions are interoceptive predictions, analogous to the brain's Bayesian modeling of heart-rate variability, which might preserve object-directedness without reducing emotion to a propositional judgment. I want a rigorous audit of these analogies. For the perceptual model, specify the correspondence—what plays the role of sensory organ, distal stimulus, representational content, and error condition—then identify precisely where emotional intentionality breaks the mapping, especially regarding conceptual framing and cultural calibration. For the weather-report model, map the barometer, the forecast, and the climate system, and ask whether it can accommodate the fact that fear is typically directed at a specific threat rather than a diffuse bodily state. Finally, examine the interoceptive-prediction hybrid: does it genuinely split the difference, or does it collapse emotional cognition back into pure feeling? Which analogy, if any, can support the claim that emotions are genuinely cognitive without being reducible to judgments, and what does each quietly import about whether we bear responsibility for our emotional states?
