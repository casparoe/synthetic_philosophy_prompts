I have been developing a view I call the diachronic parity thesis: not only can cognitive processes extend synchronously into external artefacts, but they can also extend diachronically into the automated maintenance, predictive updating, and error-correction routines of those artefacts, so that my belief about an upcoming obligation is partly constituted by my calendar application's background algorithm running overnight. I hold this as a deliberate strengthening of the extended mind thesis rather than a departure from it, yet I want an honest map of its exposure rather than a defensive brief.

What observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or conceptual distinctions would genuinely count against this position? I am particularly interested in whether there is a decisive empirical or philosophical result that would sever the constitutive link between agent and automated routine, what predictions the diachronic version makes that rival internalist or purely causal-scaffold accounts do not, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is intellectually costly. Please distinguish evidence that defenders of the thesis would themselves concede as damaging from objections they would dismiss, rank the threats from fatal to merely awkward, and say plainly whether the position risks anything substantial or has been protected beyond repair.

Please present your answer in British English, as a numbered list of exactly five items, each comprising one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
