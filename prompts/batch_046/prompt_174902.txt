i'm trying to read The Remains of the Day through this weird framing i came up with: guilt is about broken rules you could still fix, shame is about a stained self that only hiding or exile can address. but i want to push it further and ask whether a culture can actually choose which emotion it cultivates, using the novel's world of english domestic service as a kind of emotional laboratory. Stevens clearly feels both, but Darlington Hall itself seems engineered to turn any guilt into shame — you don't apologize for serving a fascist-leaning lord, you just feel unworthy for having noticed.

read the novel through this lens. where does Stevens's emotional life map onto guilt versus shame, and where does the institution (the profession, the class system, the idea of dignity) actively convert one into the other? does Ishiguro show that the culture chose shame deliberatly, or is it just a structural byproduct? and where does the reading break down — are there moments where Stevens feels something that doesn't fit either box, or where the novel resists being turned into a case study for moral psychology?

i'm writing this up for a small philosophy discussion group, not an academic paper, so please don't hand me a bibliography or a list of papers — just take the idea and the book seriously and tell me where i'm stretching things. also please answer in american english.
