In a seminar on consciousness and physicalism I heard a classmate dismiss the knowledge argument with the compressed line: if Mary already knows every physical fact about color vision, then her coming to know what red looks like cannot be learning one more fact — otherwise physicalism would be false merely because Mary hasn't read the right books. The remark seemed to do work, but I couldn't tell whether it was pointing out a trivial ambiguity or making a serious metaphysical move. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that this one sentence could be expressing, arranged as a menu rather than a single interpretation? For each reading, say whether it is trivial, whether it is a substantive claim about ontology, epistemology, or conceptual structure, and whether it actually succeeds in blocking the argument against physicalism. Specifically, distinguish: (a) the epistemic-access reading, on which Mary already possesses the relevant fact de dicto but lacks a particular mode of acquaintance with it; (b) the phenomenal-concept reading, on which Mary acquires a new concept but no new property, leaving physicalism untouched at the ontological level; (c) the ability-knowledge reading, on which she gains a practical or recognitional capacity rather than any propositional knowledge at all; (d) the a-priori-derivability reading, on which physicalism is taken to entail that phenomenal truths must be analytically or deductively recoverable from physical descriptions, and Mary's case is said to refute only that strong entailment rather than physicalism itself; and (e) a more radical acquaintance-based reading, on which "knowing what it is like" denotes a direct, non-propositional, non-ability relation to phenomenal quality that is structurally incompatible with third-person descriptive completeness, regardless of what concepts or abilities Mary possesses. For each, indicate which version my classmate was probably invoking, whether that version is defensible, and whether using it genuinely saves physicalism or merely changes the subject of the debate.
