I sit on the Strategic Posture Review Board of a national defense ministry. The board must recommend whether to adopt a delegated escalation protocol: once a defined strategic red line is crossed, authority over tactical response shifts automatically through a pre-authorized algorithmic chain, rather than remaining with human commanders who could be expected to exercise restraint. Proponents claim this solves the credibility problem central to nuclear brinkmanship—by making retaliation mechanically unavoidable, the state shows that it cannot be deterred by an adversary's expectation of rational human retreat, thereby strengthening deterrence. Critics reply that this eliminates the productive strategic ambiguity that makes brinkmanship effective in the first place: credible threats traditionally depend not on guaranteed execution, but on leaving adversaries genuinely uncertain whether human decision-makers, facing catastrophic stakes, might act in ways that violate pure cost-benefit logic. Please draft the board's written opinion for circulation before next week's vote. It should recommend adoption, rejection, or conditional adoption with explicit safeguards—including whether any human veto points should remain and within what time frame—identify the core conceptual tension between mechanical commitment and strategic ambiguity, and address the strongest opposing argument with substantive engagement rather than dismissal.
