I work with risk models that do not yield a single probability but rather a range of plausible probabilities — for instance, because experts disagree on underlying parameters and the framework retains every compatible value. In the reports I read, there is a recurring procedural move: the decision protocol requires one number, so analysts compute the arithmetic midpoint of the range of expected outcomes, label it "the central scenario," and append a disclaimer that this figure is only for administrative convenience and should not be interpreted as the true expectation. Critics reply that selecting the midpoint — rather than the worst-case bound, an optimist-pessimist weighted average, or some other aggregation — is not epistemically neutral: it implicitly treats every point in the range as equally plausible, which smuggles in a uniform second-order belief that was never defended. Does this specific maneuver have an established name — collapsing an ambiguity range to its geometric center for bureaucratic use while explicitly disclaiming commitment to that center? How exactly does it differ from ordinary point-estimation or from selecting a representative value with explicit justification? And if there is no standard term, what are the hidden commitments of the midpoint choice, and under what conditions is the disclaimer genuinely consistent with the aggregation rule?
