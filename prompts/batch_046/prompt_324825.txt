Write the remarks for the retirement dinner of Dr. Miriam Soloway, who taught moral philosophy for thirty-five years and whose seminars consistently returned to Bernard Williams's integrity objection—specifically, the risk that moral deliberation introduces one thought too many by requiring a person to justify ground-level commitments through an alienating external calculus. The room is split evenly: half are philosophers and former students familiar with Williams's work on ground projects, moral luck, and personal integrity; half are family, university administrators, and colleagues from other disciplines who are not. The speech must run about five minutes and must use the concept structurally rather than decoratively: frame the act of honoring Soloway as itself a philosophical problem, asking whether a life shaped by non-instrumental commitments can be acknowledged without being translated into retrospective metrics—publication counts, citation impact, or net welfare—that would constitute precisely the kind of justification Williams warned against. To keep the idea from being generic, inject the following niche framing: treat retirement not as a career summation but as a limit case for whether a philosophical life can be witnessed without cost-benefit reasoning, and construct the toast so that it explicitly refuses to perform that reasoning even while explaining why such refusal is the only adequate tribute. The speech should avoid bibliographic references, reading lists, and citations; it should include and comfort the non-philosophers without diluting the idea for the experts; and it should land as affection and recognition rather than as lecture.
