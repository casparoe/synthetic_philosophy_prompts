I recently became trustee of a family land preserve whose soil chemistry, pollen records, and agricultural logs span nearly four centuries, and in trying to determine when local ecological conditions became irreversibly altered, I have been drawn into the Anthropocene boundary debate. Among stratigraphers, environmental historians, and philosophers of science, the disagreement over when the Anthropocene began—whether with the Orbis spike near 1610, the late-eighteenth-century industrial transition, or the post-1950 Great Acceleration marked by plutonium-239 and synthetic isotopes—has hardened into a stalemate. The International Commission on Stratigraphy has declined to formalize any epochal boundary, leaving the term suspended between geological description and historical argument.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a dispute about which global marker is most synchronous, but about what kind of claim is being made. The stratigraphers appear to be arguing about correlation in rock layers and the need for a globally detectable, unambiguous signal; the Orbis proponents and many historians treat the date as inseparable from questions of colonial extraction, unequal causation, and differential responsibility; and a growing group of philosophers treats the boundary question as a proxy for whether human agency and natural process can be conceptually separated at all. I suspect the sides are answering different questions—one about stratigraphic evidence, one about moral and causal attribution, one about conceptual taxonomy—under the single heading of "when did it start."

But I want a diagnosis rather than a survey. Is this a verbal disagreement about the word "Anthropocene," a substantive clash over evidentiary standards, or a case of disciplines using one term for incompatible projects? Do the geologists and historians actually disagree about any empirical fact, or do they disagree about which facts count as data for epochal classification? Is there a hidden formal question—perhaps about whether global stratigraphic signals can ever be cleanly isolated from regional variation—that is doing the hidden work? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, because "Anthropocene" is doing normative work that no precise date could settle?

Please engage concretely with what each side actually says: the stratigraphers who treat plutonium-239 and synthetic isotopes as definitive global markers; the critics who argue that fixing a date at 1950 obscures the earlier, distributed violence of colonial land use and the Columbian Exchange; and those who insist that without a formal geological boundary the term should be abandoned to historians. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? Would a formal result about global correlation of markers resolve the dispute, or only deepen the disciplinary divide? Is there a conceptual distinction—say, between "Anthropocene" as a geological event, a historical epoch, and a political framing—that could dissolve the disagreement, or would adopting it merely relocate the conflict? And if the stall rests on bedrock intuitions about whether nature and culture can be separated, should we expect any argument to move the parties, or is the impasse permanent?
