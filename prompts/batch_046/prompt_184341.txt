Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently turned to philosophy with any seriousness, I have been working through Marx’s treatment of exploitation and alienation and find myself drawn to a modified formulation that I have not encountered in standard expositions. I hold that the fundamental injury is not, in the first instance, the extraction of surplus value—though that is plainly real—but rather what I term procedural epistemic dispossession: the restructuring of labor so that a worker’s embodied practical knowledge is translated into standardized, externally governed protocols over which she exercises no revisory authority. On this view, the four dimensions of alienation that Marx describes—estrangement from the product of labor, from the productive act itself, from human species-being, and from fellow workers—are not parallel, independent harms but a cascade originating in this prior loss of cognitive sovereignty within the work process. The position carries a further predictive claim: that alienation should persist even in cooperative enterprises where surplus extraction has been eliminated, so long as those enterprises rely on rationalized production procedures designed outside the immediate labor process. I am not asking for a literature survey; I want an internal assessment of the view’s exposure.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong, or at least seriously damaged? I am looking for the evidence and arguments that should actually move a defender—empirical cases in which highly rationalized labor coexists with robust worker autonomy, conceptual difficulties in distinguishing this “dispossession” from ordinary technical specialization, or interpretive results showing that Marx intended exploitation to be irreducibly economic rather than epistemic in character. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that a committed defender should concede as damaging from those they could reasonably absorb by qualification, and include an honest assessment of whether the view risks being insulated into unfalsifiability—particularly through a broad definition of what counts as “externally governed”—and whether such insulation is a defect for a normative claim of this kind.
