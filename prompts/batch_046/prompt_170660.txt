I've been thinking about Daniel Dennett's idea that the self is not a hidden soul but a center of narrative gravity—the point where all the stories a person tells about themselves converge and gain causal pull. I don't have formal training in philosophy, but I've spent time observing long-running immersive theater groups and collaborative fiction communities where people maintain consistent fictional personas for months or years, sometimes reporting real shifts in memory access and decision-making patterns tied to those personas. This made me wonder whether Dennett's model can accommodate what I want to call "polycentric" configurations: stable, long-term situations in which multiple narrative centers coexist within one biological life, each with its own autobiographical coherence and predictive self-model, without being reducible to mere acting on one hand or clinical dissociation on the other.

I'm applying for a six-month part-time independent research fellowship to investigate this precisely. The question is: can the concept of narrative gravity be extended, or must it be restricted, to allow for voluntary, socially integrated, sustained multiplicity of story-centers? Existing discussions tend to treat fragmentation as either temporary performance or pathology; there is little conceptual work on the middle ground occupied by professional immersive performers, long-form live-roleplay practitioners, or sustained collaborative writers who maintain distinct in-character journals for years. I want to know whether Dennett's criteria for a center of gravity—causal integration, autobiographical coherence, and predictive modeling—are satisfied by each sub-center individually, by the biological system as a whole, or by neither, and whether an alternative framework like narrative identity theory handles these cases more gracefully.

The project would be philosophical rather than empirical. Over six months, I would construct three detailed conceptual cases—an immersive performer with a multi-year persona, a collaborative writer with parallel narrative tracks, and a designed hypothetical of distributed decision-making—and map them against the conditions for narrative gravity. Month one and two: refine the definition of a center and the conditions for its stability. Month three and four: apply the framework to the cases, identifying exactly where the model accommodates multiplicity and where it forces a collapse back into a single center. Month five: compare with one alternative theoretical framework to see if the problem is in Dennett's specific formulation or in the very idea of narrative-based selfhood. Month six: draft the deliverable, a 35-to-45-page essay arguing either for a precise amendment to allow stable polycentricity or for the conclusion that these cases ultimately reinforce a unitary center with auxiliary narratives. Success would be a clear, defensible answer to whether narrative gravity can be distributed within one life without losing its explanatory power; failure would be if the distinction between "real self" and "performed self" turns out to be purely normative, leaving the project with no descriptive work to do. The main risk is scope creep—if the comparison with alternative frameworks expands into a full survey, the project becomes unmanageable in six months part-time, so I will limit that comparison to one well-defined rival view and keep the clinical literature at arm's length, using only conceptual descriptions rather than diagnostic criteria. I am not asking for a bibliography or literature review by citation; I will integrate sources myself, but I want the proposal to show that the conceptual framing is original enough to be worth funding and disciplined enough to finish on schedule.
