I am a retired physicist who has recently begun working through the philosophy of probability and cosmological reasoning, largely because my old training in statistical mechanics left me uneasy about how anthropic arguments are formulated. In a recent essay—written, I believe, by a philosopher of science with a background in Bayesian statistics—I encountered a summary of Nick Bostrom's distinction between the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA). The author explains SSA as the principle that one should reason as if randomly selected from the reference class of all observers who actually exist within a given cosmological model, so that finding oneself in a small civilization supports theories that predict few civilizations. SIA, by contrast, is presented as the principle that one's very existence counts as direct evidence in favor of theories that contain more observers, since being alive is more probable in a larger population, regardless of which particular observer one is. The essay then introduces Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher" thought experiment: a philosopher assigns uniform prior probabilities over cosmological theories predicting anywhere from one to a trillion civilizations, observes that he exists, applies SIA, and immediately concludes—without any astronomical observation—that the trillion-civilization universe is almost certainly correct. The summary claims that this demonstrates SIA's fatal overconfidence, that SSA avoids the problem by conditioning strictly on membership in an actual reference class rather than on bare existence, and—most strikingly—that once the reference class is specified with sufficient precision, SSA and SIA yield identical predictions, so that the long debate between their defenders is essentially a verbal dispute about framing. The author further suggests that the presumptuous philosopher is not really an objection to SIA in particular, but rather a general reductio of applying Bayesian updating to cosmological priors at all.

Given my own background in sampling theory, I want to assess whether this summary is trustworthy. Is it fair to Bostrom's actual position? In particular, I would like to understand: does the presumptuous philosopher genuinely target SIA, or is it misrepresented here as a broader critique of Bayesian cosmology? Are SSA and SIA truly equivalent under a rigorous specification of the reference class, or does the summary elide a substantive divergence—perhaps in how they treat hypothetical observers who do not actually exist? And would a careful defender of either view accept the conclusion that their disagreement is "largely verbal," or does that conclusion depend on the very misrepresentations I have noted? Please go through the summary with precision: separate what is accurate, what is a common but misleading simplification, and what is simply wrong, and tell me whether the essay's final claim—that the debate dissolves into semantics—survives correction.
