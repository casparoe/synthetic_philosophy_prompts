In Bayesian confirmation theory, there is a long-standing tension about old evidence—data already known before a hypothesis is proposed. Because the standard update rule assigns near-unit probability to evidence an agent already possesses, that evidence cannot technically raise the posterior credibility of a new hypothesis that explains it. Yet scientists routinely treat a theory's successful accommodation of previously known anomalies, such as the precession of Mercury's perihelion under general relativity, as genuine and sometimes decisive confirmation. Over decades, responses have multiplied—distinguishing logical from incremental confirmation, invoking subjective ignorance of logical entailments, or treating theoretical discovery as altering the space of live hypotheses—without resolving the core conflict. I suspect the stalemate is not about formal probability but about hidden differences: one side may be offering a normative, diachronic account of individual belief revision, while the other offers a descriptive or synchronic account of scientific evaluation, with "confirmation" and "learning" functioning differently in each framework. Is that diagnosis right? More precisely, is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two questions—how rational agents ought to update and how scientific communities actually credit theories—being pursued under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually advance it: a formal result about belief dynamics, a conceptual distinction between types of confirmation, or an empirical study of how working scientists weigh old data?
