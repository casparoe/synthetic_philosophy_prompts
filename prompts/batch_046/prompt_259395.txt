I just came out of a long argument with a friend about whether switching from beef to chicken actually reduces suffering once crop cultivation and wild-animal harm are included. I kept noticing a reasoning pattern that I cannot name. We evaluate the substitution by following the harm we can trace directly—the feedlot animal, for instance—and treat the harm that is displaced elsewhere, such as rodents killed in grain harvest or ecosystems altered by pasture, as if it belongs to a separate moral category altogether. Because chicken involves smaller, less visible direct deaths, the switch feels like a clean improvement, even though the total displaced harm may be comparable or larger; the relocated suffering is not denied, but it is cognitively partitioned into a ledger for which we do not hold the choice responsible. I know this touches on indirect effects and possibly moral licensing, but I am after the specific conceptual move: treating a harm-relocation as a harm-reduction because the relocated damage is segregated rather than integrated into the same evaluation. Does this configuration have an established name in moral psychology or ethics, and if not, how exactly should it be distinguished from ordinary ignorance of indirect effects, from the non-identity problem in comparative welfare, and from a simple category mistake?
