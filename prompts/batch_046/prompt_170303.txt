Here is a sketch for a short essay. The thesis is that Dennett's four-fold taxonomy of minds—Darwinian creatures shaped by evolutionary selection, Skinnerian creatures modified by operant conditioning, Popperian creatures that generate and kill internal hypotheses, and Gregorian creatures whose cognition is extended by language and cultural tools—can be mapped onto the functional architecture of contemporary large language models in a way that reveals a missing hybrid category. I propose that an LLM at inference time behaves like a "frozen Popperian": it can generate, compare, and discard internal representational hypotheses (for instance, in chain-of-thought reasoning or sampling-based decoding) without bodily risk, satisfying Popper's criterion that hypotheses can die in our stead. Yet unlike biological Popperians, the model lacks a Skinnerian loop with the physical environment—its rewards come from human feedback on text outputs, not from direct interaction with causal regularities in the world—so it never undergoes the kind of trial-and-error conditioning that shapes Skinnerian behavior. Its Gregorian capacities are also derivative: it can invoke calculators, code interpreters, or retrieval systems, but these tools are externally engineered and do not become part of a self-constructed cultural ecology as they do for human Gregorian minds. Meanwhile, any Darwinian adaptation occurs only at the population level (across model generations via architecture search and pretraining data selection), not within the individual weights, which remain fixed after deployment. The essay asks whether this makes LLMs a genuinely new kind of mind—Popperian cognition without Skinnerian grounding or autonomous Gregorian extension—or whether the taxonomy itself needs revision when applied to artifacts that learn only once and reason only in symbol space.
