I was observing a discussion between two researchers—one in moral philosophy, one in welfare economics—about whether interpersonal utility comparisons are possible in any rigorous sense. The philosopher began by arguing that comparing suffering across individuals is conceptually incoherent because each conscious subject occupies a unique metric space of experience; without a shared thermometer, cross-personal judgments are not false but meaningless, like asking whether Tuesday is heavier than blue. The economist replied that policy analysis constantly performs such comparisons—using disability-adjusted life years, quality-adjusted life years, and contingent valuation—so denying them is not philosophical rigor but practical denialism. The philosopher countered that these instruments smuggle comparison in through monetary proxies or expert weighting that already assumes what needs proving: that a unit of distress in one life is commensurable with a unit in another. The economist then narrowed her claim: perhaps absolute comparison is impossible, but marginal comparison is defensible. If we observe that person A consistently sacrifices more income to avoid a risk than person B does, revealed preference gives us an implicit exchange rate for welfare impacts. The philosopher responded that revealed preference tracks behavior, not welfare, and that adaptive preferences—where people adjust their desires to oppressive conditions—mean observed choices can systematically invert actual suffering rankings. The economist conceded the adaptation problem but proposed correcting for it through objective-list benchmarks—capabilities like bodily integrity, practical reason, and affiliation—arguing that once we fix an independent standard of flourishing, we can compare how far each person falls short without relying on subjective reports. The philosopher said this merely relocates the problem: selecting and weighting capabilities requires precisely the interpersonal value judgments the economist hoped to avoid. At this point the economist shifted ground entirely. She argued that welfare is not a scalar to be summed but a vector with incommensurable dimensions—health, autonomy, belonging, knowledge—so comparison should be component-wise rather than aggregate. Any intervention improves some vector components for some people and diminishes others, and there is no legitimate aggregation function. The philosopher agreed that vector-valued welfare avoids false precision, but said it produces policy paralysis: if every trade-off is irreducible, distributive justice becomes impossible and technocratic allocation collapses into pure politics. The economist replied that this paralysis is salutary, forcing democratic deliberation instead of masking value conflicts behind a spurious numerical scale. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: is the philosopher right that subjective metric spaces block comparison? Does revealed preference genuinely help, or does adaptive preference destroy it? Do objective lists solve the problem or relocate it? Is vector-valued welfare a genuine escape, and does it necessarily cause paralysis? Finally, state your bottom line on whether interpersonal comparison is coherent in principle.
