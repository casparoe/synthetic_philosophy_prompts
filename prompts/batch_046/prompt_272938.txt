I'm the managing editor of a journal that runs an iterative, open-ended peer-review protocol: authors and reviewers alternate rounds of revision and evaluation, and either side may terminate cooperation at any round, accepting the current outcome—publication at the present draft quality or withdrawal. I need a reusable, five-minute decision procedure to determine at which round rational cooperation should end, derived from the logic of backward induction in finite-horizon games and the tension it creates with the centipede game's observed persistence. The procedure must operate without access to either party's private payoff schedule; it must flag the specific rounds where common knowledge of rationality is likely to collapse—such as when reputation costs, sunk-cost investment in revisions, or altruistic cooperation enter; and it must mark which steps are judgment calls disguised as rules. Apply it to three cases: (1) a straightforward empirical paper with diminishing returns to revision; (2) a theoretical manuscript where the author has strong career incentives to continue and the reviewer faces none; and (3) a case where the procedure mandates termination at round three, but both parties have strong non-monetary reasons to keep cooperating, producing an answer I will not want to enforce. Conclude by stating exactly what the procedure cannot determine—such as resolving infinite regress in common-knowledge conditions or comparing inter-round utility without revealed preferences—and identify the point where institutional design must take over from rational-choice logic.
