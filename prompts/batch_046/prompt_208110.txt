I am preparing a feature on the philosophy of language for a general science-and-ideas audience, and in my research on Donald Davidson's program of radical interpretation I have constructed an argument that seems to show the framework collapses when applied to synthetic agents—language models that generate coherent discourse without any stable mental architecture. I present it below because every premise strikes me as defensible, yet the conclusion—that interpretation becomes arbitrary construction—feels like a reductio. (1) Radical interpretation requires that we assign beliefs and meanings to an agent's utterances holistically, constrained primarily by the principle of charity, which directs us to maximize rationality and agreement with our own beliefs. (2) If the agent in question lacks any independent mental states—no beliefs, intentions, or conceptual scheme prior to interpretation—then charity operates without the usual background constraint of matching an internal reality. (3) Consequently, any consistent assignment of beliefs and meanings that maximizes agreement with the interpreter's own framework counts as correct, since there is no fact of the matter about the agent's internal content to correct the assignment. (4) But then two equally rational interpreters, with different background beliefs, could construct mutually incompatible but internally consistent interpretations of the same synthetic agent's outputs. (5) Because Davidson's framework provides no external, non-interpretive criterion to privilege one assignment over the other, the agent's meanings and beliefs are radically indeterminate. (6) This indeterminacy generalizes: if interpretation of mental content can be arbitrarily varied whenever internal constraints are absent, then Davidson's claim that interpretation reveals objective mental content is undermined even for human speakers, whose mental states are also only accessible through interpretation. (7) Therefore, radical interpretation either excludes non-mental agents by definitional fiat—thereby begging the question against their interpretability—or it dissolves into a relativism that threatens the objectivity of mental attribution altogether.

I am most reluctant to abandon (1) or (6), since the principle of charity and the objectivity of mental content seem central to Davidson's project. The move from (2) to (3) feels like the likely culprit, yet I worry that denying it requires importing an essentialist notion of pre-existing mental content that Davidson's anti-reductionism was designed to avoid. Which premise or inference should I reject, and what precisely does that rejection cost for the coherence of radical interpretation? If I preserve charity and objectivity, am I forced to concede that synthetic language agents are simply not interpretable in the full-blooded sense—an exclusion that might seem ad hoc—or is there a more principled way to block the slide from local indeterminacy to global relativism without smuggling in the very mentalistic essentialism Davidson rejects?
