I've been trying to work out for myself what it really means to say that something has a disposition—like being soluble, brittle, or compassionate—rather than merely behaving a certain way right now. Here are my judgments on several cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle behind them, or if I have to abandon some.

First, an ordinary sugar cube dissolves in water. I say it is genuinely soluble.

Second, the same cube is sealed inside an impermeable glass jar and dropped in water. It does not dissolve, but I still say it is soluble; the jar is only blocking the manifestation, not removing the property.

Third, imagine a synthetic crystal engineered so that the very chemical interaction with water that should dissolve it instantly triggers an internal polymerization, turning it permanently into an insoluble plastic. After contact with water it never dissolves. Here my intuition shifts: I want to say it was never truly soluble, because the condition that should reveal the disposition simultaneously destroys the property that was supposed to be revealed.

Fourth, a ceramic vase shatters when struck with a hammer. I call it brittle.

Fifth, that same vase is manufactured with an embedded shock-absorbing lattice that, upon impact, permanently transforms the ceramic into a flexible rubber. Once struck, it can never shatter again. I think this vase, too, was not genuinely brittle—similar in kind to the crystal, not to the jarred sugar.

But now a case that makes me hesitate. Consider a person who is deeply compassionate in private, feels genuine remorse after failing to help, and consistently acts kindly when no one is watching. Yet whenever a situation actually demands compassion in the presence of others, an involuntary traumatic reflex makes him freeze and behave coldly. The reflex is internal, not an external obstacle, and it is triggered precisely by the conditions under which compassion should manifest. Still, my intuition is that he genuinely is compassionate; the freeze functions like the glass jar, a blocker or mask, not like the polymerization that eliminates the underlying nature.

So my verdicts appear to be: in the crystal and the lattice-vase, the disposition is absent; in the jar-cube and the compassionate person, it is present but masked or interfered with. The difficulty is that in both the "absent" and the "present" pairs, the interfering factor is internal and activated exactly by the condition that should manifest the disposition. I cannot see what cleanly distinguishes them.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps about whether the interfering factor preserves the underlying nature of the object, or about the pattern of counterfactual dependence—that captures all five verdicts? If so, state it clearly, indicate which clause handles which case, and tell me what it predicts for two further cases I have not listed: (a) a medicine that cures a particular infection only in patients with weak immune systems, and in those very patients it also permanently weakens their immune system further; and (b) a bridge rated to hold thirty tons that contains hidden sensors which, if the load reaches twenty-nine tons, permanently reinforce the structure so that it can then hold fifty tons, though before that reinforcement it could hold only thirty.

If no single principle can save all five verdicts without contrivance, tell me honestly which verdict I should overturn, and whether overturning it collapses the distinction between the crystal and the jarred cube, or between the compassionate man and the lattice-vase. Please reason from scratch and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
