Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy-of-perception seminar (about 14 students, one session) that makes participants feel the live force of Molyneux's problem, not as a historical anecdote but as an unsettled tension between empirical associationism and rationalist spatial cognition. Rather than staging the familiar cube-and-sphere case, inject a constructed symmetrical variant: participants must work with three irregular solid objects—each matched for volume and weight but with distinct curvature gradients—through two sequential phases. In Phase 1, vision is occluded and participants learn the objects only by haptic exploration; in Phase 2, tactile feedback is eliminated and they must discriminate the same objects by sight alone, predicting whether prior haptic knowledge transfers immediately. Then reverse the sequence (visual-first, haptic-second) to stage the inverse Molyneux problem. Structure the session with explicit roles: one subgroup serves as associationist observers who must defend the claim that cross-modal recognition requires learned correlation; another as rationalist geometer-observers defending immediate recognition of spatial form independent of sensory history; and a third as enactivist couplers arguing that recognition depends on active sensorimotor engagement rather than internal representation alone. The rules should include structured forced-choice prediction rounds with small but real stakes—points that determine seating or speaking order in the next meeting—so that participants have an incentive to commit to predictions before testing. Specify the materials using common items with modified tactile coverings (e.g., fabric-wrapped objects or 3D-printed shapes if available, but describe them so the activity is runnable without special fabrication), a precise timeline dividing exploration, private prediction, public commitment, testing, reversal, and collective comparison, and a payoff scheme that rewards accurate cross-modal prediction without requiring money. The debrief must map each phase onto the corresponding philosophical position, explain what went wrong predictably—likely premature convergence on one modality and social pressure to align predictions with the dominant role—and state honestly which feature of Molyneux's original scenario this activity necessarily fails to reproduce: specifically, the total absence of any prior conceptual framework for geometric shape, which the facilitator must supply verbally rather than through the simulation.
