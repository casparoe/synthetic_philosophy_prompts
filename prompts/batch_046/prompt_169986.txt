Next week I present at a law-and-philosophy symposium a thesis that modifies Gregg Caruso's public-health model of criminal justice by importing Daniel Dennett's concept of elbow room—not to rehabilitate retributive desert, but to frame predictive containment as an evolved institutional design problem. The audience is mostly legal philosophers committed to just-deserts retributivism, plus two practicing criminal-defense attorneys who distrust philosophical abstraction and care about procedural fairness. The core claim is that desert-based punishment collapses not only because determinism undermines moral responsibility, but because any institutional attempt to measure just desert depends on counterfactual judgments about what the offender could have done that become epistemically unstable once those judgments are operationalized in algorithmic risk-assessment tools. Prepare a speaker-prep sheet: list the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as the retributivists and the practitioners would phrase them, noting which objections have real force, what I should concede up front about Dennett's claim that freedom is a real, evolved achievement, and identifying the single reply about counterfactual instability in predictive models that must land for the proposal to survive. Also include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely whether this view collapses into a purely consequentialist deterrence framework—and flag the weakest point in my own reply.
