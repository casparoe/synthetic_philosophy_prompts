I'm working on a long-form piece about how anthropic reasoning is migrating from cosmology into technology forecasting, and I've hit a conceptual snag I hope you can help me think through. In Nick Bostrom's framing, the Self-Sampling Assumption directs us to reason as if we were randomly selected from all actual observers within a specified reference class, which sounds like a conventional statistical correction for selection bias, whereas the Self-Indication Assumption treats our own existence as evidential weight in favor of worlds containing larger populations of observers. The well-known "presumptuous philosopher" scenario—where a philosopher concludes that the universe must contain vastly more observers than his rival theory allows, solely on the grounds that he himself exists—strikes me as an empirical reductio that has effectively discredited SIA among working cosmologists, especially now that exoplanet surveys and refined Drake-equation estimates have placed tighter observational bounds on the total number of technological civilizations in the observable universe. If those empirical bounds have indeed settled the debate in favor of SSA, then shouldn't forecasters who are predicting the timeline for artificial general intelligence apply the same sampling logic? Specifically, shouldn't they treat humanity's current technological stage as a randomly drawn sample from the full distribution of all civilizations that ever develop machine intelligence, and use that to correct downward any model that assumes we are unusually early? I am trying to understand whether this is a legitimate extension of the framework, or whether I am conflating two different kinds of reasoning—one about cosmic population sizes and one about temporal forecasting—under a single statistical label.
