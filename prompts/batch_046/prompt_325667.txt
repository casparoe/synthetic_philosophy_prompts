I am a PhD candidate in political philosophy and comparative legal theory presenting my thesis on "calibrated civic opacity" at an interdisciplinary faculty seminar next week. My central claim is that democratic citizens ought to maintain a strategic, domain-specific informational reserve against the state—modeled structurally on armed neutrality rather than on rights-based privacy—rather than adopting either the German GDPR framework of data minimization (which treats personal data as a waivable individual entitlement) or the American default-public model (which relies on procedural transparency and open-government norms to discipline state overreach). I argue further that Chinese social-credit integration reveals not the dangers of citizen concealment but the collapse of informational asymmetry: democratic accountability requires that citizens, not only states, control the terms of disclosure. I therefore defend a norm of asymmetric transparency—radical state disclosure of algorithmic decision-rules coupled with citizen-controlled, time-bound release of personal data only for explicitly authorized functions—while rejecting both contractual consent models and total openness.
