I am a member of a twelve-person housing cooperative founded on an explicit agreement: shared resources remain collectively governed, and no private accumulation should override equal access. Two years ago we established a repair workshop with communal tools, initially distributing usage hours evenly among residents. One member, Leo, began offering informal repair instruction. Others voluntarily traded him additional workshop hours in exchange for lessons. Over time Leo accumulated the majority of available hours, leaving newer members with minimal access. Leo now proposes transferring half his accumulated hours to a non-member friend who wishes to join, arguing that if voluntary exchange creates legitimate holdings, then gifting or bequeathing them is merely another consensual act. Several residents argue we should reset all allocations to equality and deny the transfer, claiming that allowing both accumulation and inheritance effectively dissolves the founding agreement. Leo responds that every trade was free, that no rule was broken, and that restricting what he may do with his holdings violates the very liberty that justified the trades. I recognize that Leo's reasoning has a coherent internal logic, yet accepting both the accumulation and the inheritance seems to undermine the cooperative's purpose in a way no future rule could remedy. Where does his argument break, if it does? I want a committed analysis: identify the philosophical structure of Leo's reasoning, state definitively whether the inheritance step is a legitimate extension or introduces a hidden rupture, explain which premise is least costly to reject and why, and address what that rejection costs in terms of freedom, equality, or the coherence of voluntary agreement.
