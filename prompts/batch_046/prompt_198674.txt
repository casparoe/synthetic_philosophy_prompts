For a 90-minute graduate seminar on early modern philosophy of mind with 14 participants, design a runnable deliberation game called the Tribunal of Adaptive Response. The premise: in 1647, an academy must classify three entities—(A) a clockwork automaton that mimics digestion through visible gears, (B) a wounded dog that learns to avoid a specific trap only after repeated injury in varying conditions, and (C) a speaking figure that produces grammatically perfect sentences about unseen objects but never adjusts its speech to situational needs or stakes—using only behavioral and anatomical evidence, with no access to internal experience. Each case should be presented as a one-page examination report specifying exactly what behaviors and mechanisms are observed.

The rules must enforce Descartes' evidential constraints: verdicts must cite only criteria from his bête-machine thesis and his distinction between true language (context-sensitive arrangement of signs for meaning) and mere responsive noise, including the less-discussed constraint that true language requires an adaptive, universal instrumentality rather than fixed associative chains. Crucially, build in an asymmetry: declaring something a machine requires only one plausible mechanistic explanation for the observed behavior, while declaring it a rational soul requires unanimous agreement that no mechanistic account could explain its adaptive flexibility.

Inject a niche modern analog into case C: model it on a large language system that generates coherent, syntactically correct descriptions without embodied stakes, situational feedback, or error-correction tied to survival—making participants feel the pressure of Descartes' language argument in an updated form. The voting should produce a predictable split—clear consensus on A, division on B between mechanist and adaptive-flexibility readings, and deadlock on C—so that participants experience the tension before it is named.

Specify roles (scribe, anatomist, devil's advocate assigned to defend the mechanist verdict), a timeline (10 minutes framing, 20 minutes per case with 5 minutes silent voting, 15 minutes mapping), and the debrief. The debrief should connect each vote pattern to Cartesian arguments: the clockwork analogy, the language-use criterion, and the exchange with Henry More on true versus accidental speech. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce—Descartes' view that the soul's presence is immediately self-evident to its owner and therefore never subject to third-person tribunal proof, which creates a circularity the game necessarily omits. Also predict what will go wrong (participants will smuggle in phenomenal consciousness or emotional intuition-pumps despite the rules) and advise the facilitator on how to redirect without shutting down the discovery. Write the response in American English.
