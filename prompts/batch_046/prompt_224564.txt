Please argue that Kierkegaard’s critique of the press and “the public” is best reconstructed not as a sociological lament about anonymity or the levelling of individual distinction, but as an epistemic-structural diagnosis of what might be called reflexive spectatorship: a condition in which every practical situation is transformed into material for detached, non-committal commentary. Show how this reading accounts for his otherwise puzzling insistence that the press corrupts not primarily by disseminating falsehoods, but by altering the very mode in which truth-claims are held—turning passionate, responsible commitment into idle, interchangeable opinion. You should address the obvious objection that Kierkegaard is merely nostalgic for pre-modern communal life, and explain why, on your reading, the problem of the public cannot be reduced to the later sociological concept of mass society. Keep your discussion anchored in the conceptual architecture of his authorship—individuality, the crowd, and the nature of commitment—without appealing to specific textual citations or bibliographic apparatus, and paraphrase rather than quote any particular passage.
