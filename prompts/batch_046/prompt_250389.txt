okay so i've been hanging out in rationalist forums since the old LW days and there's this pattern in class-culture threads that i can't name. people debate whether professional-class norms (therapy-talk, credential-checking, moving for jobs) are actually more rational, or whether working-class/rural norms (fixing your own stuff, not airing dirty laundry, staying near family) are unfairly pathologized. but there's a specific defensive move i keep seeing from the rural/working-class side, and it's not just "my culture is different."

the pattern goes like this: first they defend a norm on practical grounds — "not talking about mental health with strangers keeps your social network intact" or "tinkering builds real intuition." then when someone brings up counterevidence — therapy outcomes, specialist efficiency — they don't concede; they pivot to saying the norm is about identity or belonging, not utility. then if *that* gets hit with "that's just tribal irrationality," they pivot again to "this is a different epistemology; outsiders can't evaluate it by thier own metrics." each layer sounds okay alone, but together they form a kind of protective shell where the norm never actually takes a hit. it's not motte-and-bailey because there's no single defensible core being hidden; it's more like cycling through justification-types.

i've seen this in maybe a dozen threads and it feels structurally similar to how some religious arguments work, but the class dimension makes it distinct — the practical layer appeals to rationalist consequentialism, the identity layer appeals to cultural humility, the epistemic layer appeals to standpoint-type reasoning. does this stacking have a name? if not, how would you distinguish it from ordinary pluralism or from simple motivated reasoning? and please say what would change your mind here — like, what evidence that these layers are genuinely integrated rather than protective would make you drop the framing?
