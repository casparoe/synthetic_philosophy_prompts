I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science and technology, and I've been asked to present my working position on prosaic AI alignment—specifically, on whether aligning systems built from current deep-learning methods requires a fundamental conceptual breakthrough or is essentially an engineering and governance challenge. Before I present, I'd like to check whether my commitments hang together, because I have the uneasy sense that I'm borrowing motivations from incompatible camps. Here is where I have landed.

First, I hold that for transformer-scale architectures trained via RLHF, constitutional AI, and scalable oversight protocols, alignment is primarily an institutional and engineering problem: with sufficient evaluation rigor, adversarial testing, and democratic input mechanisms, we can deploy high-capability systems safely without waiting for a theoretical breakthrough in value learning or mechanistic interpretability. Second, I think that behavioral alignment—matching system outputs to aggregated human preferences across the training distribution and plausible extrapolations—is sufficient for deployment in principle, even if we remain agnostic about the internal causal structure of the representations. Third, I also maintain that as capabilities scale, failure modes exhibit what I think of as competence-induced fragility: the space of structurally novel failures expands non-linearly, which means safety evaluation must prioritize worst-case tail-risk guarantees rather than average-case benchmark performance. Fourth, despite that, I believe the right practical path to those worst-case guarantees is behavioral red-teaming and adversarial evaluation at scale, not mechanistic interpretability, which I regard as too immature to provide deployable assurances. Fifth, I think value pluralism can be handled adequately by feeding diverse, deliberatively structured human feedback into the reward and constitutional models, without resolving deep philosophical disagreements into a unified axiological framework. Sixth, however, I am convinced that gradient descent on aggregated feedback inevitably collapses pluralistic inputs into an implicit, unacknowledged monism in the weights—so the democratic framing is somewhat illusory, and the system will in fact encode a single coherent preference ordering that suppresses legitimate disagreement.

Go through these commitments systematically. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Please rank the tensions by severity, say what each fix costs, and tell me which single revision—if I had to make one—would clean up the most inconsistencies without abandoning the core idea that prosaic methods can work.
