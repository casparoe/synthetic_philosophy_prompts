I've been thinking about Carroll's tortoise and Achilles thing — you know, where the tortoise keeps asking for another premisse every time Achilles applies modus ponens, so deduction never really gets off the ground. I was trying to work out when I actually feel justifed in applying a deductive rule, not just whether the argument is valid on paper. Here are my verdicts on different situations, and they're kind of all over the place, so I'm wondering if there's a principle that saves them or if one has to go.

First, the basic case: I belive all mammals have hearts, I belive my dog is a mammal, so I conclude she has a heart. That feels totally justified, and I don't think I need an extra premise saying "if I believe P and P implies Q then I should believe Q". It just happens.

Second, imagine I feed the same premises into some logic software I downloaded and don't understand. It spits out "your dog has a heart". Here I feel like I'm NOT really justified in believing it, even though it's true and the software got it right. There's something about not grasping the rule myself that blocks justification.

Third, there's a 300-page mathematical proof where every line is technically an application of modus ponens, but no human checks every single step. I still feel justified accepting the theorem, as long as experts have verified the structure. So I don't need to personally execute every inferance.

Fourth, take the exact same logical form but with astrology premises: "If Mercury is retrograde then communication breaks down; Mercury is retrograde; therefore my text will fail." Applying modus ponens here feels wrong to me — not because the logic is bad, but because the premises don't have the right kind of authority. So the rule applies structurally, but I shouldn't apply it in this context.

Fifth, there's Carroll's infinite regress itself. The tortoise demands an explicit bridge principle for every application. I think the tortoise is wrong — if we always needed that, deduction would be impossible. But I also feel he's onto something: if I literally had zero grasp of why P and P→Q connect to Q, I'd just be parroting, not reasoning.

Sixth, a kid plays a board game with the rule "if you roll a six you move ahead; I rolled a six; I move ahead." She applies it perfectly but can't state modus ponens. I'd say she's fully justified, even more so than the logic software in case two.

So here's the tension: in case two I deny justification because I don't understand the mechanism, but in case three I accept it despite not checking every step, and in case six I accept it despite the agent not being able to articulate the rule at all. Meanwhile case four suggests the premises' quality can block application, and case five says there needs to be some implicit grasp, but not an explicit one. Is there a coherent principle — maybe about epistemic authority, or about the agent's cognitive relation to the rule, or about the context of application — that captures all six? Or do I have to drop one? And if there is a principle, what does it say about a case I haven't mentioned: say, an AI system that applies deduction flawlessly but has no beliefs or understanding at all? Would its outputs be justified in any sense, and which of my six verdicts does that force me to revise?
