You chair the clinical-ethics review board at a long-term neuropsychiatric care facility. Every month you must decide whether an advance treatment directive—written years earlier by a patient who now has advanced neurodegenerative dementia—should still govern care, or whether the present patient’s interests have diverged so radically that honoring the document causes harm rather than respects autonomy. Your board includes a neurologist, a staff ethicist, a family advocate, and a legal compliance officer; you have ninety minutes per case, the neurologist and ethicist must both sign any recommendation, and the legal officer may veto only on explicit regulatory non-compliance but cannot approve alone. You need a reusable decision procedure that translates the Buddhist doctrine of anatta—personal identity as a conventional designation of impermanent aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness) rather than an enduring substance—together with Western personal-identity frameworks (psychological-continuity, bodily-continuity, and narrative-coherence accounts) into operational steps your board can run without requiring anyone to hold a philosophy degree. The procedure must explicitly distinguish conventional truth (sammuti-sacca), where continuity is treated as a practical construct for obligations and care planning, from ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca), where there is no self to be preserved or violated, so that the board does not confuse legal continuity with metaphysical preservation.

Write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions with explicit pass, fail, or unclear criteria for each step, specifying where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation (honor the directive, override it, or suspend it pending further evaluation), where it mandates a hand-off to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than assessable fact, and where it is likely to misfire—particularly when family members project narrative coherence onto a patient who no longer possesses it, or when clinicians mistake intact bodily continuity for personal identity. Include a concise clinical-observation checklist based on the five aggregates (not metaphysical assertion), a set of three test conditions the board applies to distinguish conventional-identity preservation from attachment to a non-existent self, and hard procedural constraints: no step may take more than twenty minutes, any disagreement between the neurologist and ethicist forces an immediate hand-off, and the legal veto applies only to regulatory violations, not to philosophical conclusions.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. First, a patient with advanced Alzheimer’s whose directive refuses feeding tubes, but whose current behavioral responses suggest distress at hunger rather than at the tube itself; this should yield an answer your board will likely reject, and you must explain exactly what the procedure cannot resolve about that conflict. Second, a patient with frontotemporal dementia whose prior directive demanded aggressive life extension, but whose current emotional profile shows no narrative connection to the values expressed in the document. Third, a case where bodily continuity is intact, psychological continuity is partially preserved only in isolated emotional-memory fragments, and family members insist on honoring the directive primarily because they cannot accept that the person they knew is already gone; here show precisely where the two-truths distinction blocks the family’s reasoning without denying their grief.

Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure is incapable of deciding—where the absence of a self eliminates one kind of harm but introduces another, and why no procedural refinement can remove that gap. Write in American English.
