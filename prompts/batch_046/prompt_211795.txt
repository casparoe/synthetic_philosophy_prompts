I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy with any systematic rigor, and I have constructed an argument for a reading group on rational choice that now strikes me as suspicious: its premises seem individually plausible, yet its conclusion undermines the very possibility of deliberate action. I present it here with numbered steps.

(1) In Buridan's classic case, if an agent's preferences between two options are exactly balanced—equal expected utility, equal proximity, equal desirability—then rational choice theory provides no internal rule for breaking the tie; any selection is arbitrary rather than reason-guided.

(2) From my scientific training, I take the physical parameters that ground real-world options—distance, dosage, energy, probability—to be continuous real-valued quantities.

(3) If utility is a continuous function over such parameters, then for any two options A and B with U(A) not equal to U(B), the intermediate-value property guarantees the existence of some intermediate option C for which U(C) lies exactly midway, hence with preference exactly balanced against either neighbor.

(4) Therefore perfect preference indifference is not a remote pathological edge case but a dense, unavoidable feature of any continuous decision space.

(5) If (1) holds—indifference genuinely paralyzes rational choice—then rational agents should be unable to act whenever they lie near such equality points.

(6) Yet in both laboratory practice and ordinary life we constantly make near-boundary decisions—calibrating instruments near a threshold, selecting doses near a median, choosing paths near a bifurcation—without any experienced paralysis.

(7) One might reply that hidden asymmetries always break the tie, but this saves rationality only by denying that true equality ever obtains, which in turn requires either discontinuous preference functions or an epistemic barrier that prevents us from ever recognizing conditions of equality.

(8) Hence we must either accept discontinuous "jumps" in rational preference (contrary to the smooth behavioral and neural dynamics we observe), or concede that what we call "reasons" for near-indifferent choices are in fact post-hoc rationalizations of arbitrary selection mechanisms.

The conclusion in (8) seems absurd: it makes rational agency depend either on a mathematical discontinuity for which we have no empirical evidence, or on a systematic self-deception about our own motives. I should most like to preserve (1), (3), and (6), since the continuity argument feels as secure as any result in calculus, and the phenomenology of near-boundary action is unmistakable. Must I therefore reject (5)—the inference from indifference to paralysis—or is there a more subtle flaw elsewhere in the chain, perhaps a scope shift between logical models and continuous spaces, or an unnoticed ambiguity in what "no rational basis" means? Where exactly does the argument fail, and what does repairing it cost me if I wish to keep the premises I value?
