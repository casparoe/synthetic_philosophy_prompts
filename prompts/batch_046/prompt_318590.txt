In discussions of Tolstoy's aesthetics, commentators often update his infection analogy with contemporary language: art works like a contagious agent — the artist experiences a feeling, encodes it in the work, and the audience acquires it. Some extend this comparison toward viral marketing and emotional malware, arguing that if art is infection then counterfeit art — work that reproduces the surface signs of feeling without the artist's sincere inner experience — functions like malicious software: it exploits emotional transmission channels without carrying authentic content, and is therefore harmful rather than merely bad. Others object that infection is passive and non-intentional, lacking the directed communication, interpretive labor, and normative framing that artistic experience requires.

Audit this analogy in its updated form. Lay out the explicit correspondence: artist as host or pathogen designer, artwork as vector or payload, audience as infected party, counterfeit art as malware, and any immune response or resistance mechanism. Then identify the disanalogies that specifically threaten Tolstoy's normative conclusion — that genuine art must be sincerely felt and universally communicable — separating those that genuinely undermine the argument from those that are only decorative. Say whether the claim that counterfeit art is harmful in the way malware is harmful survives these breaks, and whether the infection model can distinguish good art from bad without smuggling in hidden assumptions about audience vulnerability, artistic authority, or the uniformity of emotional response. Take a committed position: defend the repaired analogy as adequate to Tolstoy's purposes, reject it in favor of a different model and name it, or argue that the normative evaluation should abandon biological analogies altogether. Do not present a balanced menu; deliver a reasoned verdict with its implications for how we should evaluate art that fails to transmit feeling sincerely.
