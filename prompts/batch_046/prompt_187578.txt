I have been trying to determine, without relying on textbooks, whether named generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and so on — correspond to anything real in the world, or whether they are only useful fictions. I want to see whether my specific judgments can be captured by a single principle, and if not, which ones should be dropped. Here are the cases. The post-1946 birth surge produced a group I think is genuinely real: the economic and cultural conditions these people shared in youth and early adulthood left a lasting collective imprint. By contrast, the label "Millennials" for those born roughly 1981 to 1996 strikes me as administratively neat but ontologically empty; there is no single formative rupture that binds the entire range, and many traits assigned to them seem like ordinary life-stage effects rather than cohort properties. "Gen Z," defined as anyone born after about 1997, feels even more like marketing — behaviors such as widespread climate anxiety or short-form video use appear to be period effects that touched people of all ages simultaneously, not marks of a specific cohort. Yet I do believe there is a real kind comprising people who were adolescents during the Great Depression, even though this group spans roughly a decade of births and does not map onto any standard generational label. Likewise, people for whom the events of 11 September 2001 served as a formative political awakening seem to form a genuine cohort, despite spanning ages from about five to thirty at the time, which breaks any birth-year scheme. I also reject "Gen Alpha" — people born after 2010 — as a real kind, since no distinctive shared historical shock has yet shaped them collectively. Where I waver is the 2008 financial crisis: those who entered the labor market between 2008 and 2010 appear genuinely marked by it, regardless of whether they were born in 1982 or 1988; but I also feel that being born in 1985 versus 1990 should not place someone in different generations, because both experienced the crisis at a comparable career stage. Meanwhile, statistical personality differences between Gen Z and older groups do not persuade me; these could reflect measurement drift rather than an underlying kind. So is there a coherent principle — perhaps that a cohort is real only when a specific historical rupture intersects with a specific life stage, with demographic coherence being neither necessary nor sufficient? Which of my judgments does such a principle preserve, and which must it overturn?
