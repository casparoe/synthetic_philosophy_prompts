Consider Glaucon's challenge in Plato's Republic — the ring of Gyges, which grants perfect invisibility and asks whether anyone thus concealed would still have reason to act justly. The standard literature divides between contractarian answers (justice survives as a useful convention vulnerable to exposure) and realist answers (justice is intrinsically rational regardless of detection). I think the debate has stalled partly because it treats invisibility as binary, ignoring intermediate structures of concealment that are now practically urgent: cryptographic anonymity, opaque algorithmic decision systems, and artificial agents whose internal weights are hidden from auditors even when their actions are observable.

Specifically, I want you to evaluate at least these four lines, substituting others if you think I am missing something more upstream:

First, does partial or conditional invisibility — where actions are visible but reasons, identity, or internal objectives remain hidden — alter the logical structure of Glaucon's challenge, or does it collapse back into the all-or-nothing case?

Second, can the challenge be reformulated for non-conscious agents that lack any phenomenology of getting away with something, yet can still operate with concealed goals or reward structures? Does this extension reveal that the original argument depends on a tacit assumption about conscious self-interest?

Third, is the core disagreement between contractarians and realists actually about the rationality of justice, or is it upstream — about whether moral reasons are agent-neutral — such that resolving that meta-ethical question dissolves the ring problem rather than answering it?

Fourth, which of these lines, if settled, would most change how we design transparency mechanisms in governance or in AI systems?
