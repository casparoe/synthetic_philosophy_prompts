I'm preparing a session for a philosophy of literature seminar on how metaphysical debates about time can be dramatized rather than merely illustrated, and I've chosen Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five as my central case. The novel's Tralfamadorian outlook — the claim that a dead person is merely in poor condition at one temporal coordinate while remaining perfectly well at countless others — reads like a popularization of eternalism, and Billy Pilgrim's involuntary jumps through his own biography seem to literalize the B-theoretic idea that all moments exist with equal ontological weight. But I want to avoid reducing Vonnegut to a diagram of the presentism versus eternalism debate, so I am asking you to read the novel through that debate with particular attention to whether Vonnegut implicitly tests a middle position — something like the moving spotlight theory, in which the present alone is metaphysically privileged though past and future moments still exist — through Billy's peculiar temporal dislocation.

Specifically, examine whether Billy's trauma functions as a presentist counterargument to the Tralfamadorian view: his suffering is not distributed evenly across his life-line but is insistently present, bleeding into moments that should be neutral. I would like you to discuss the Dresden firebombing, Edgar Derby's execution, and the barbershop quartet scene, asking in each case whether the narrative treats temporal location as metaphysically significant or as a psychological disorder that eternalism cannot accommodate. Where does the image of being unstuck in time hold up as philosophy, and where does it trade on a confusion between epistemic access to other times and the ontological status of those times? Does Vonnegut suggest that taking eternalism seriously as a lived orientation — rather than as an abstract thesis — might actually undermine moral urgency in ways that the standard philosophical literature on the B-theory misses?

Finally, I want a reading that takes both Vonnegut's craft and the metaphysical debate seriously: identify where the novel resists being read as philosophy, and say whether it contributes something to our thinking about time and suffering that argument alone does not. I am not looking for a bibliography or a summary of the academic literature on presentism; I want criticism that could guide students through the text without misleading them about either the novel or the metaphysics.
