I am a journalist who covers philosophy of mind and value, and I am working on a feature about hedonism that I want to ground in concrete intuition rather than in the usual historical survey. To that end, I have been cataloguing my own judgments about what counts as pleasure, what counts as a pleasure-like state, and what does not, and I want to see whether those judgments form a coherent pattern that can be captured by a precise principle.

Here are seven cases with the verdicts that seem right to me, together with the reasoning that drives each.

Case 1: The sensory pleasure of tasting a well-made espresso—warm, slightly bitter, with a lingering aftertaste. Verdict: a genuine pleasure. Its quality is partly bodily, partly anticipatory, but it is unmistakably pleasant.

Case 2: The intellectual pleasure of suddenly seeing why a complex proof resolves an apparent contradiction. Verdict: also a genuine pleasure, though it shares no qualitative feature with Case 1—no warmth, no taste, no bodily localization.

Case 3: The satisfaction of learning that a professional rival has been publicly disgraced. Verdict: this feels pleasant—there is a positive affect, a lightness in the chest, even a kind of warmth—but I am reluctant to place it in the same category as Cases 1 and 2. It seems parasitic or counterfeit: its pleasantness borrows from a malicious source, and I want to say it lacks a kind of structural integrity that the others possess.

Case 4: A drug-induced euphoria that is entirely contentless—no sensory imagery, no thoughts, no intentional object—only a bare affective tone that registers as good. Verdict: genuine pleasure, but thin. It lacks the richness and directedness of 1 and 2, yet I still count it as pleasure proper.

Case 5: A hypothetical synthetic reward architecture—an artificial system optimized on a reward function that registers maximal value for a state that has no phenomenal character, no intentional object, and no subjective point of view whatsoever. Verdict: not pleasure. Despite being functionally isomorphic to the state in Case 4, there is simply no subject for whom the state is good.

Case 6: The quiet, diffuse contentment of sitting in a familiar garden with nothing particular happening. Verdict: genuine pleasure, but not an intense episode. It belongs with 1 and 2, I think, though it is more like a background condition than an event.

Case 7: The feeling of relief after a severe headache stops. Verdict: this is certainly pleasant, but it seems derivative—its pleasantness consists entirely in the absence of pain, not in any positive quality of its own. I am unsure whether it belongs with 4 as a thin but positive state, or whether it forms a separate, merely privative category.

The tension I keep returning to is this: Cases 1, 2, 4, and 6 all seem to be pleasures despite having almost nothing phenomenally in common, which suggests that qualitative similarity is not the unifier. Case 3 seems to be a pleasure but a defective one. Case 5 is functionally analogous to 4 but is not a pleasure at all, which suggests that functional role is insufficient. Case 7 may or may not be a genuine pleasure, and its status depends on whether pleasure requires a positive phenomenal contribution beyond the mere cessation of discomfort.

Please report what principle, or small family of competing principles, best captures these verdicts. For each principle, specify which clause explains which case, what it predicts for cases I have not listed—such as the satisfaction of completing a meaningless but difficult task, the pleasure of an abstract mathematical discovery made by a conscious artificial system, or the calm of a meditator who reports no object of awareness—and, crucially, which of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate without distortion. I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between Case 4 and Case 5 can be maintained without smuggling in an unargued assumption about consciousness, and whether Case 3's counterfeit status can be explained structurally—perhaps in terms of intentionality or affective fittingness—rather than by appealing to moral disapproval alone. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the argument should stand on its own for a reader without access to a library.
