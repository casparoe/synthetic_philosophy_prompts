The subject has recently become personally urgent for me: I have been asked to help draft the interpretive framework for a new civic memorial, and in the course of researching commemorative practices I witnessed the following disagreement between two colleagues—an archivist (Person A) and a public historian (Person B)—at a symposium I attended. I am reporting it as a bystander and do not want to signal which side I favor.

Person A opened by arguing that so-called lost-cause narratives are structurally revisionist rather than merely selective. She said that the Confederacy's Lost Cause mythology did not simply highlight Southern culture; it systematically replaced slavery as the central cause of the Civil War with an invented narrative of states' rights and noble defense. She extended the point to Masada: the ancient account suppresses internal Zealot factionalism and frames collective suicide as unanimous heroic resistance, which modern political movements have used to justify refusal to compromise. Even Dunkirk, she added, is often mythologized as a pure miracle of pluck that suppresses the strategic reality of a retreat, the abandonment of French allies, and the role of Hitler's halt order. Her conclusion was that public commemoration should prioritize what she called archival fidelity—presenting the documented complexity—rather than symbolic condensation, because condensation always requires strategic amnesia that serves present power.

Person B replied that this standard is impossible and, if applied consistently, would dissolve all collective memory into an archive with no binding force. He said that Dunkirk's strategic reality was indeed defeat and retreat, yet the spirit of Dunkirk does not falsify that reality; it selects the feature of civilian solidarity that made strategic preservation possible. He argued that Kosovo's 1389 narrative functions similarly—not by denying the battle's outcome, but by selecting endurance over victory as the symbolically enduring feature. The problem, he said, is not myth-making itself but monomythic closure, where one interpretation is enforced and competing memories are excluded. He then introduced a distinction he finds crucial: mnemonic sovereignty, which is a community's democratic right to determine which features of its past are binding for its collective future, versus mnemonic imperialism, where one group's selective memory is imposed on others who do not share it. The Confederacy's myth, he concluded, was imperialist because it was enforced through Jim Crow and violence to suppress Black memory; Dunkirk's can be sovereign if it allows space for the suppressed strategic facts and for the memories of those abandoned.

Person A pushed back that Person B is smuggling in a false equivalence. She insisted that the Lost Cause was not an innocent selection but a deliberate historiographical project—funded, institutionalized, and taught—to replace causality with culture. She noted that Josephus's account of Masada suppresses not only factionalism but the reality that many defenders were extremists who had murdered fellow Jews. Even in the Dunkirk case, she said, the miracle framing serves present British nationalism by erasing the failure of allied coordination. The pattern, she argued, is consistent: mythic condensation always requires strategic amnesia, and that amnesia is never neutral; it always serves the interests of those who control the narrative.

Person B answered that Person A confuses the mechanism of selection with its moral evaluation. He granted that every collective narrative selects, and that selection can be audited for what it suppresses. But he maintained that without selection there is no shared identity—only data. The ethical line, he repeated, runs not between myth and archive, but between sovereign and imperialist memory practices. He agreed with Person A that suppression must be audited, but he said she is wrong to treat suppression itself as the error. What matters is whether the selecting community allows the suppressed facts to remain visible and contestable, and whether other groups affected by the event can participate in the selection.
