I am preparing the reading list for an advanced graduate seminar on moral status, aggregation, and epistemic risk, and I find myself returning to a puzzle that the standard treatments of insect sentience do not quite resolve. The familiar scale argument notes that if there is even a very low probability that insects are sentient, the sheer magnitude of their numbers—trillions of individuals—seems to generate moral obligations that dwarf those we owe to larger, more certain cases such as mammals or birds. Yet nearly every version of this argument I have encountered treats sentience as an all-or-nothing property and relies on a straightforward expected-value calculation that weights false positives and false negatives symmetrically. I want to push the discussion into more contested terrain. Suppose we reject the binary view and instead regard sentience as graded, varying in intensity and structural integration across taxa, and suppose we further combine this with an asymmetric moral-risk framework in which erroneously denying suffering carries greater weight than erroneously attributing it, but in which moral consideration still demands that we cross a substantive epistemic threshold rather than treating every non-zero probability as sufficient for full moral inclusion.

You should weigh the position that vast numbers multiplied by low but graded probabilities still yield overwhelming reasons to prioritize insect welfare against the position that the combination of graded sentience and threshold-sensitive risk assessment collapses the argument, leaving our obligations to insects comparable to or weaker than those we have toward vertebrates with high-certainty, high-intensity sentience. Reach and justify a concrete conclusion—whether the argument holds in a qualified form, fails altogether, or applies selectively to particular groups such as social insects or cephalopods.
