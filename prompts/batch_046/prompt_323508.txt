Write an essay on the following controversy: Does Timothy Williamson’s anti-luminosity argument — that mental states such as feeling cold fail to be luminous because a gradual transition can place one near a boundary without knowing which side one is on — also undermine the KK principle (that if one knows p, then one knows that one knows p) for propositional knowledge? Or can KK survive intact even if phenomenal states are not luminous?

Your essay should weigh the main arguments on both sides and reach a definite, justified conclusion — for instance, that the anti-luminosity argument does not generalize to propositional knowledge and KK holds in a restricted form; or that gradualist epistemology forces us to abandon KK for all but the clearest cases.

Structure your response in exactly these sections, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. Summary (about 150 words): State the controversy precisely. Sketch Williamson’s anti-luminosity argument (the temperature-gradient case and the claim that there is no sharp, knowable boundary in gradual change) and state the KK principle in its standard formulation.

2. The case for separation (about 300 words): Argue that state-luminosity and second-order propositional knowledge are structurally disanalogous. Address whether knowing-that-p requires a determinate threshold in a way that feeling-cold does not, and whether the failure of phenomenal transparency leaves propositional knowledge untouched.

3. The case for extension (about 300 words): Argue that if epistemic justification can accumulate gradually — through incremental evidential support rather than an on-off switch — then there will be borderline cases in which one knows p but lacks the higher-order access required to know that one knows p. Show how this mirrors the temperature-gradient structure.

4. A proposed structural diagnosis (about 250 words): Introduce and defend a framing of your own. Distinguish what you will call “state-luminosity” (awareness of being in a phenomenal condition) from “access-luminosity” (awareness of having satisfied an epistemic standard). Evaluate whether KK requires only one of these, whether gradualist epistemology threatens only one, and whether a threshold-sensitive account of knowledge can preserve a restricted KK principle.

5. Verdict and justification (about 250 words): Take a clear position. If you defend KK, specify the restriction (for example, that it holds only when the epistemic standard is determinately exceeded). If you reject it, explain what replaces it in accounting for ordinary second-order knowledge, and address one obvious objection to your replacement.

Constraints: Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote any text verbatim. Keep references to named thinkers minimal — Williamson and the standard KK formulation are sufficient. Make the argument conceptual and self-contained, suitable for evaluation without external lookup.
