While discussing the later Wittgenstein in a study group for my philosophy of mind course, a classmate offered what she presented as a faithful reconstruction of the private language argument, drawing on a distinction she introduced between "phenomenal privacy"—the uncontroversial fact that only I can feel my own pain—and "semantic privacy," the stronger claim that I could coin a term whose meaning is constituted solely by my inner acquaintance with that feeling and requires no shared practice. She summarized Wittgenstein's position like this: "Wittgenstein proves that semantic privacy is impossible because if a word referred to something only the speaker could access, there would be no public criterion to distinguish correct from incorrect applications. If I invent a name for a particular sensation and keep no record except my own memory, then whenever I apply the name I will feel justified, but that feeling is not a check; it is part of what needs checking. So the distinction between using the word correctly and merely thinking I am collapses. From this he concludes that language is necessarily embedded in shared practices, and that talk of private mental states is really just a misleading way of describing public behavioral dispositions—essentially logical behaviorism—while phenomenal privacy, though real, is philosophically irrelevant."

Something in this reconstruction strikes me as too neat, particularly the slide from "no private language" to "logical behaviorism" and the suggestion that Wittgenstein treats phenomenal privacy as irrelevant rather than simply as insufficient for grounding meaning. At the same time, I recognize that her emphasis on criteria of correctness, the collapse of the correct-incorrect distinction in the absence of shared norms, and her separation of phenomenal from semantic privacy do sound like serious exegesis of the rule-following passages. I am trying to determine which pieces of her account accurately represent Wittgenstein's reasoning about ostensive definition and normative practice, which are subtle misreadings—perhaps conflating the private-language argument with broader rule-following considerations, or importing a verificationist framework he resists—and whether her behaviorist conclusion survives once those misreadings are corrected. Could you audit this summary claim by claim: flag what a careful interpreter of Wittgenstein would accept, what they would correct or qualify, and whether the behaviorist reading is an overreach?
