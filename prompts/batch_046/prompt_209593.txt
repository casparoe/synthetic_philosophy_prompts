Consider an extension of the Epicurean argument about death. (1) For a state to be bad for a subject S, S must exist in that state to suffer or be deprived. (2) Death is the state in which S no longer exists at all. (3) So death is not bad for the one who dies. (4) The same reasoning applies whenever S is absent: deep dreamless sleep, permanent unconsciousness, and even never having existed are all cases lacking a present subject, hence none can be harmful to anyone. (5) From this it follows that there is no harm in failing to conceive a particular child, no special wrong in being instantly killed and replaced by a psychological duplicate who lives well, and no particular badness in an irreversible coma—since in each case the original subject is absent and no suffering occurs. (6) But these conclusions erase distinctions we want to preserve: we treat permanent unconsciousness differently from death, we think personal survival is not secured by replacement, and we allow that a specific never-born person can be deprived of life. (7) The argument therefore proves too much. Which step should be rejected? Is the flaw in (1)’s existence condition—perhaps allowing counterfactual or posthumous harm—or is there a hidden slide between “not existing now” and “not being the kind of entity that can bear interests”? If you keep (1), what do you pay in explaining why coma, replacement, and non-existence are not all on a par?
