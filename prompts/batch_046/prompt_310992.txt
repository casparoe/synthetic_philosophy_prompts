My closest friend and I argued well into the night about Bernard Williams's integrity objection, especially the scenario in which a husband saves his wife from drowning but pauses to ask whether morality permits him to prefer her—what philosophers often call the one-thought-too-many problem. I tried to formalize our disagreement, and the result now seems to prove far more than either of us intended.

(1) Integrity requires that an agent possess ground projects that are shielded from being overridden by impartial moral demands; otherwise her life lacks narrative unity and is not genuinely her own.

(2) This shielding must apply uniformly across ground projects, because there is no non-arbitrary, agent-neutral criterion that distinguishes a large project—say, dedicating one's life to music—from a small one—say, preserving a particular friendship or a weekly ritual—without already invoking the kind of impartial ranking that Williams's view rejects.

(3) Moral obligations routinely conflict with small ground projects: the duty of honesty conflicts with the project of protecting a friend's feelings; the duty of beneficence conflicts with reserving evenings for family.

(4) Therefore, if integrity protects all ground projects uniformly, then nearly every moral obligation becomes defeasible by appeal to some element of the agent's narrative identity.

(5) If moral obligations are systematically defeasible in this way, morality lacks categorical authority and functions merely as advisory guidance that applies only when it does not disturb one's personal narrative.

(6) That is unacceptable: we want moral demands to have real binding force, not optional status contingent on biographical convenience.

(7) Hence either (1) is false—integrity offers no protection against moral demands—or (2) is false, and there exists a principled cutoff between protected and unprotected projects, but Williams supplies no account of where that cutoff lies and any attempt to draw one seems to reintroduce the impartial perspective his objection was designed to exclude.

I want to preserve (1) rather than (5), but I cannot see how to restrict the protection in (2) without smuggling in exactly the kind of agent-neutral standard that makes the integrity objection attractive in the first place. Which step is weakest? If I reject (2), what principled distinction can I offer that does not collapse back into impartial moral reasoning? And if I reject (4) instead, am I committing myself to the view that integrity protects only central projects, and if so, how do I identify centrality without circularity?
