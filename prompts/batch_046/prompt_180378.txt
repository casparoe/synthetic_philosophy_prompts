So, quick one — at tuesday's workshop my colleague Raj and a visiting fellow, Elena, got into a real back-and-forth about just war theory and the moral equality of combatants. Raj argued that once you take liability seriously, the equality thesis collapses: soldiers fighting an unjust war of aggression are liable to defensive killing, while defenders aren't, so there's no moral symmetry in their permissions. Elena said that's too quick — liability means you're not wronged if killed, but it doesn't strip you of defensive rights. Even a culpable aggressor can resist lethal force without becoming a murderer; there's a gap between being justified and being excused, or between permission and justification. Raj pushed back that if both sides can permissibly kill each other, then for all practical purposes they're equal, and the distinction between 'justified defense' and 'excused aggression' collapses in the fog of war. Elena replied that practical collapse isn't conceptual collapse, and we still need to distinguish moral status even if soldiers can't tell them apart. Then they started arguing about collective liability — can you be liable for an unjust war if you genuinely don't know it's unjust? I was moderating and honestly couldn't split it. Can you adjudicate point by point? Who's right about whether liability destroys equality, whether self-defense permissions survive without justification, and whether epistemic limits salvage anything? And what are they both missing — maybe the blurring of combatant/non-combatant lines under collective action, or something about proportionalty? Just lay it out, no need for a bibliography, my head's already full.
