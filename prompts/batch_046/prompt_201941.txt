You sit on the University Interdisciplinary Research Appeals Panel. A grant proposal titled “Sustainable Agro-ecological Memory” was rejected by an external reviewer who argues its epistemology violates classical Nyāya norms. The applicant’s study of long-term land-use practices in a remote highland region relies on four sources: direct sensory surveys of current fields (pratyakṣa, perception); chemical inference from soil-core samples (anumāna, inference); analogical comparison with documented practices on another continent (upamāna, comparison/analogy); and structured oral testimony from elder local practitioners (śabda, verbal testimony). The applicant further claims that its predictive algorithmic modeling of soil degradation should be recognized as an autonomous, fifth pramāṇa rather than a derivative tool. The rejecting reviewer contends that, in Nyāya, upamāna cannot serve as an independent warrant when it conflicts with anumāna; that śabda requires an authoritative speaker (āpta) meeting disciplinary expertise conditions that local practitioners do not satisfy; and that the framework’s closure under exactly four pramāṇas makes the “fifth source” claim conceptually incoherent. The applicant appeals, arguing that modern interdisciplinary science integrates all four classical sources non-hierarchically and that insisting on classical closure conditions improperly privileges one philosophical tradition over empirical practice. Draft the panel’s decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressing whether the proposal’s integration of the four classical sources is sound, whether testimony can be weighted without full āpta status, whether the algorithmic-modeling claim must be rejected for the grant to stand, and giving an honest account of the strongest consideration favoring the side you do not adopt. Keep the recommendation actionable and avoid requiring the applicant to supply bibliographies or historical citations.
