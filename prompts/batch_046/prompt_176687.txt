I'm writing a philosophy essay for my high school English class and I'd like to read William Golding's Lord of the Flies through the lens of moral realism versus anti-realism. The novel depicts the boys' rapid descent from ordered rules into brutality, which at first seems to show moral norms as fragile social constructions rather than objective constraints. But I want to push past that obvious reading by bringing in the partners-in-crime argument—the claim that moral judgments share enough structural features with mathematical judgments that rejecting moral realism would also force us to reject mathematical realism. Does Golding's portrayal of the boys violating norms they still appear to recognize as binding actually presuppose a realist framework, or does the collapse of civilization show morality to be purely conventional? I'm especially interested in the scene where Ralph and Piggy discuss the conch as a symbol of authority, and in the ironic rescue by the naval officer, which might imply that civilized morality is just another form of power rather than an apprehension of mind-independent truths. Could you walk through how these scenes work for both a realist and an anti-realist interpretation, note where the novel resists being reduced to either view, and suggest what Lord of the Flies contributes to the metaethical debate that pure philosophical argument alone tends to miss?
