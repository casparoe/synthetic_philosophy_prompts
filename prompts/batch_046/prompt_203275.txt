I am a high school student preparing an adversarial-collaboration exercise for my independent study in political philosophy. The topic is church-state relations across four democratic models: the German system of church taxes collected by the state for registered religious communities with an opt-out procedure; the English model of an established church with formal constitutional and parliamentary roles; the French model of laïcité and strict separation, with its well-known regional exceptions and state ownership of pre-1905 religious buildings; and the American model of constitutional disestablishment at the federal level combined with substantial state-level variation and widespread religious practice.

Please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Camp One — the Cooperative Pluralists — defend cooperative arrangements. They hold that state collection of church taxes, establishment with democratic accountability, or formal separation with public maintenance of religious heritage can all be consistent with neutrality, provided no citizen is compelled to affirm religious doctrine. They argue that fiscal integration supports religious freedom by preventing religious institutions from becoming either impoverished or captured by wealthy private donors, and that administrative cooperation is not theological endorsement.

Camp Two — the Neutrality Separatists — reject all of these models. They hold that when the state collects taxes earmarked for religious bodies, grants constitutional privileges to one denomination, or manages religious property through law, it necessarily takes sides among worldviews. They argue that true neutrality requires the state to be not merely non-coercive but also non-entangled: it should neither collect religious revenues nor recognize religious authorities in legal or fiscal structures.

Your deliverable should be structured as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These must be substantive rather than platitudinous. Include agreement about the actual mechanics of each system — for instance, that the German Kirchensteuer involves state tax authorities collecting an additional income-tax surcharge for registered members of recognized religious communities, with exit possible through formal declaration; that the English establishment includes the monarch as supreme governor, bishops sitting in the House of Lords, and the Church of England’s legal status as the established church; that the French law of separation applies nationally but with notable territorial and property exceptions; and that the American Establishment Clause prohibits federal establishment while leaving states with diverse practices. Include agreement about shared normative commitments — specifically, that freedom of conscience and equality before the law are non-negotiable — and about at least one empirical regularity both sides accept but interpret differently, such as the long-term decline in formal religious affiliation across all four countries regardless of the institutional model.

Second, a disagreements table. For each camp, present the precise formulation of its position on the central disputes, worded so that members of that camp would accept the wording as accurate and not a strawman. The disputes should cover: whether state tax collection for religious bodies is a neutral administrative service or a form of establishment; whether the opt-out mechanism eliminates coercion or merely reduces its severity; whether the English establishment today functions as a meaningful legal privilege or as a largely symbolic historical residue with minimal practical effect; whether French laïcité achieves genuine neutrality or instead imposes a secularist worldview on public life; and whether American disestablishment has produced healthier pluralism or merely privatized religion without reducing social conflict. Crucially, address the concept I am introducing for this exercise: bureaucratic establishment — the idea that modern church-state cooperation often operates not through explicit doctrinal endorsement but through fiscal, administrative, and property-law integration, such as tax collection, state maintenance of religious buildings, and legal recognition of ecclesiastical authorities. Each side should state, in its own accepted wording, whether bureaucratic establishment is genuinely distinct from traditional theological establishment, and whether its presence in Germany or England makes those systems more defensible because less doctrinally coercive, or less defensible because more insidious, hiding privilege behind administrative routine.

Third, identify the cruxes — no more than four specific sub-questions such that resolving them with evidence or argument would move at least one side. For each crux, state the question precisely. Then record the argument, case, or comparative evidence that both sides agree would genuinely bear on it. Finally, write down each side’s explicit prediction in advance: what finding or outcome would weaken that side’s position.

Fourth, conclude with a brief assessment of whether any portion of the disagreement is verbal rather than substantive — for example, whether the two sides are using neutrality, establishment, or coercion in different senses — and specify exactly which part, if any, dissolves once terms are fixed, and which parts remain genuinely irreconcilable.

Please write this in formal, polished prose suitable for an academic exercise, but frame the entire response as the completed joint statement document itself. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific scholars or texts. Do not ask for external verification or web access. I want a self-contained document that stands on its own reasoning, requiring no outside sources to evaluate.
