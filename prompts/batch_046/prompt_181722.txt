Consider the following outline and central claims for an essay I am drafting, tentatively titled "Refractory Asymmetry and the Dynamic Boundary in Lexical Threshold Negative Utilitarianism."

The essay begins from the standard formulation of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism: suffering that exceeds a specified intensity or duration acquires lexical priority over any aggregate of pleasures, while below that boundary pleasure and pain are treated as fully commensurable on a single cardinal scale for the purpose of aggregation. I want to challenge the coherence of that boundary by introducing what I call "refractory asymmetry"—the claim that the subjective capacity for pleasure is not merely reduced by suffering in a linear, recoverable fashion, but is structurally deformed in proportion to the intensity and recency of pain experienced, and that this deformation does not symmetrically reverse when pleasure is experienced after suffering. On this view, a unit of pleasure experienced by someone who has recently endured severe-but-sub-threshold suffering does not carry the same normative weight as an identical unit experienced by someone with no such history, not because of a psychological discount factor, but because the pleasure occurs within a diminished "register" of subjective value.

The essay then argues that if refractory asymmetry holds, the lexical threshold cannot function as a static, universal boundary. Instead, I propose a "dynamic lexical boundary" model: the point at which suffering acquires lexical priority shifts downward for populations or individuals with accumulated sub-threshold suffering, because the baseline commensurability required for aggregation has already been compromised. This is not merely an empirical claim about psychological resilience; it is a normative claim about the conditions under which cardinal comparison remains valid.

The essay concludes by examining whether this dynamic model collapses back into standard threshold utilitarianism with a variable parameter, or whether it introduces a genuinely distinct normative structure—one in which the incommensurability of pleasure and pain is not binary (above versus below the line) but gradient and path-dependent.

I would appreciate comments and suggestions on several specific points.

First, does the concept of refractory asymmetry genuinely distinguish psychological saturation from normative incommensurability, or does it conflate a descriptive claim about hedonic adaptation with a prescriptive claim about value comparison? Second, is the move to a dynamic lexical boundary internally coherent, or does it risk making the threshold either arbitrary (if it tracks individual histories too closely) or redundant (if it tracks only aggregate population-level effects)? Third, in what ways might a defender of the static-threshold view respond—particularly by arguing that normative aggregation should operate on idealized, counterfactual capacities rather than on actual, path-dependent subjective registers? Finally, are there obvious objections from within the negative-utilitarian tradition—perhaps from those who reject cardinal measurement altogether—that would undermine the premise that any form of commensurability is required below the threshold?

Please address the argument's accuracy with respect to standard formulations of lexical negative utilitarianism, the interest and originality of the proposed asymmetry, and whether the essay's framing successfully avoids treating pleasure and pain as simple mirror-image quantities without falling into an equally problematic dualism.
