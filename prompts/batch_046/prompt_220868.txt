I have come to accept a dissolution of the paradox of fiction that treats emotional responses to fictional entities as instances of decoupled modal tracking rather than belief-mediated affect: just as a physicist can compute the dynamics of a hypothetical system without ontological commitment to its actual existence, our affective engagement with fiction takes the represented scenario's internal premises as working assumptions, not as propositions believed about the world. On this account, what we call "pity for Anna Karenina" is not a contradictory emotional state—pity directed at a known non-existent—but a calibrated simulation that tracks the modal structure of the narrative without existential commitment. I want a rigorous assessment of what would genuinely count against this position: not merely restatements of the standard paradox, but specific empirical findings, phenomenological reports, or conceptual distinctions that would undermine the analogy between fictional affect and scientific counterfactual reasoning. Which predictions distinguish this decoupling account from rival theories—such as pretence or make-believe accounts—and have its defenders insulated it by qualification into unfalsifiability? In particular, I want to know whether committed defenders of this view would concede that reports of genuine grief over fictional deaths count against them, or whether they would systematically reinterpret such reports; and whether there are results in the cognitive science of emotion that either support or threaten the claim that affective processing can operate with suspended existential commitment. Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, include an honest evaluation of whether the view risks being protected by definitional moves rather than substantive claims, and say what it predicts that its rivals do not.
