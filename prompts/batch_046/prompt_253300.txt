I'm writing a feature on the resurgence of universal picture-protocols—climate-risk dashboards, maritime safety icons, and public-health signage—whose designers explicitly reject grammatical syntax in the manner of Otto Neurath's ISOTYPE. In researching these projects, I've noticed a recurring failure mode that seems adjacent to Neurath's well-known problems yet may be conceptually distinct, and I want to determine whether philosophers of language or design theorists have already given it a precise name.

Here is the pattern, observed across three recent cases: an epidemic-preparedness icon set, an international navigation-safety protocol, and an educational visualization project. The designers begin with a strict anti-syntactic rule: icons denote only objects or states, and any relation must be inferred by the viewer from pure adjacency or overlap, never from an encoded layout. Yet whenever they must represent a claim involving scope, negation, or counterfactual dependence—for instance, that a particular extreme weather event does not occur if a given emissions policy is adopted—they find that mere proximity is ambiguous. Does placing the event icon near but not inside the policy icon signal prevention, correlation, or simple co-occurrence? To resolve this, they introduce unstated spatial conventions—radial distance, containment borders, opacity gradients, layered planes—that function exactly as syntactic markers, even as the designers insist these are purely visual or aesthetic properties rather than grammar.

The result is a system that advertises itself as universally transparent, free of linguistic barriers, while actually requiring users to master an implicit spatial grammar that is never explicitly codified. The designers cite Neurath's vision of a picture language independent of syntax, yet they reproduce the very structural dependence on relational encoding that critics of ISOTYPE identified—only now it is hidden inside layout rules rather than printed symbols.

Does this specific configuration—where an anti-syntactic picture language reinvents relational syntax through unstated spatial conventions, particularly for modal and counterfactual claims—have an established term? Or is it best understood as a direct variant of the standard criticism that Neurath's system could not represent logical form? I am not asking for a bare label; I want the phenomenon carved precisely: what distinguishes it from ordinary design ambiguity, where its boundaries lie (for example, does a statistical chart with an implicit axis fall under it, or must there be an explicit denial of syntax?), and how it relates to the broader debate about whether picture languages can ever fully escape syntactic structure.
