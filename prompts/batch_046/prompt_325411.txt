In the course of drafting a novel about a logician who becomes quietly obsessed with the architecture of uncertainty, I have been trying to understand a result that keeps surfacing in the philosophical literature: David Lewis’s triviality argument concerning indicative conditionals. A colleague—someone who teaches formal logic but not philosophy—summarized it for me in the following terms. “Lewis demonstrated that if you assign probabilities to indicative conditionals in the natural way—so that the probability of ‘if A then C’ equals the conditional probability of C given A—then the only consistent assignments are trivial ones, where every conditional receives either probability zero or one, or where the probabilities of the antecedent and consequent become independent. The argument turns on a hidden contradiction with the law of total probability once you allow nested conditionals or disjunctions in the antecedent. From this, Lewis concluded that indicative conditionals are not genuine propositions that can be assigned probabilities at all; rather, they function as epistemic rules for revising credence, which is why they feel like assertions of connection rather than reports of correlation.”

I am inclined to think this captures the spirit of the result, but I worry it conflates Lewis’s own conclusion with the later suppositional theories of Adams and others, and that it perhaps overstates the collapse—presenting a modest semantic constraint as an outright refutation of the propositional view. Could you audit this summary? Please separate what is accurate about the probabilistic constraint and the collapse result, what is a standard pedagogical simplification, and what a defender of the propositional view of conditionals would say is misrepresented—particularly regarding whether Lewis intended to show that a specific probabilistic semantics fails, or that indicative conditionals are irreducibly non-propositional. Also, does the claim that the contradiction specifically requires nested conditionals or disjunctions in the antecedent survive correction, or is that an embellishment added by popularizers?
