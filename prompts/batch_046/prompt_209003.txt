In a graduate seminar I am running on the moral status of entities with ambiguous phenomenal boundaries, we have spent months circling the same problem: if sentience is genuinely a matter of degree—running from the putative micro-experiences of insects, through mammalian affect, down to the null case of a thermostat—then moral status should presumably scale with it, yet every attempt to formalize that scaling collapses into either an implausibly smooth gradient that grants negligible but real rights to thermostats by error, or an arbitrary threshold that reinstates human exceptionalism by fiat. The empirical literature on insect nociception and integrative information theory has not moved anyone, because both the gradualists and the thresholdists accept the same neural data and simply draw opposite normative lines through it.

The stall feels deeper than a factual dispute. The gradualists talk about more or less moral considerability, treating status as a scalar weight that can be summed across populations; the thresholdists talk about being within the moral community or not, treating status as a binary gate that activates full protections. My working hypothesis is that this is partly verbal—degree means quantitative weight to one side and qualitative kind to the other—but partly substantive: they differ on whether moral comparability across species requires a shared dimension of evaluation. I recently sketched an alternative that complicates both views: rather than placing entities on a single sentience axis, we might model moral status as a vector across at least three independent dimensions—affective valence capacity, cognitive integration, and temporal continuity—so that an insect scores high on affective capacity but near-zero on temporal continuity, yielding not a lower human-like status but a non-comparable moral profile. If this is right, then both camps are wrong to assume unidimensional scaling, but I am unsure whether introducing multidimensionality dissolves the disagreement or just relocates it to a dispute about which dimensions are morally relevant and whether incommensurable profiles can still ground comparative obligations.

Given this, I want a committed diagnosis rather than a survey of options: is the impasse primarily a hidden disagreement about the structure of moral comparison rather than about sentience itself? Would progress require a formal framework for comparing incommensurable moral profiles, or is the very idea of scaling moral status—whether scalar or vector—misguided because moral status is essentially categorical? And does my vector proposal actually break the stalemate, or does it smuggle in the same threshold intuition by letting us declare certain dimensions necessary for full membership? Take a definite position on what kind of disagreement this is and what, specifically, would need to be shown—conceptually or empirically—to move the debate forward.
