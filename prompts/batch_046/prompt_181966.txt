Here are notes for an essay I am drafting on the defense of unrealistic evaluation benchmarks in artificial intelligence, using Milton Friedman’s methodological argument that unrealistic assumptions do not matter if a model’s predictions are accurate — the so-called F-twist. I want the essay to stay within philosophy of science rather than becoming a technical AI paper, but I also want to avoid saying something that any philosopher of science has said a hundred times.

The core idea is this. In economics, Friedman argued that the realism of a model’s premises is irrelevant compared to the accuracy of its predictions. Critics often reply that predictive success is not enough if the model misrepresents causal mechanisms. Now consider how large language models are evaluated: we test them on decontextualized logic puzzles, synthetic mathematics, and contrived reasoning tasks that bear little resemblance to real-world language use. Defenders of these benchmarks implicitly invoke an F-twist: the tests are unrealistic, but if high scores predict useful downstream behavior, the unrealism is harmless.

My essay argues that this analogy mostly fails, but for a reason that is rarely noticed. In Friedman’s original case, the predictions are independent empirical observations — market prices, employment levels — against which the model is checked. In AI evaluation, however, the benchmark score is not an independent prediction about the world; it is an internal metric that is often constructed to align with the very capabilities it claims to measure. This creates a circularity that Friedman’s framework does not accommodate. Moreover, Friedman coupled his F-twist with a form of instrumentalism about scientific theories. If one rejects instrumentalism for AI — for instance, if one thinks we should care about whether the model’s internal representations correspond to genuine reasoning processes — then the defense of unrealistic benchmarks collapses, not because the benchmarks fail to predict, but because prediction is no longer the only thing that matters.

I am considering adding a further twist: that some recent evaluation protocols try to escape circularity by using hidden downstream tasks that are not part of the training distribution. Even here, I want to argue, the F-twist is only partially restored, because the selection of hidden tasks still presupposes a normative judgment about which real-world behaviors count as successes.

2. Is the circularity objection in the comparison with economics genuinely damaging, or can a defender of benchmarks reply that benchmark scores are still empirically observed outputs of the system, analogous to observed market behavior?

3. Have I missed a stronger disanalogy — for example, regarding the difference between a theoretical model and an artifact being tested?

4. Is the argument interesting, or does it merely apply a familiar philosophy-of-science distinction to AI evaluation without generating new insight?
