Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers of mind—Dr. Aisha Rahman, who defends an integrated-information account of consciousness unity, and Dr. Thomas Vance, who defends a phenomenal-boundary account—discussing the hypothetical case of “Patient J,” a split-brain subject who has undergone a partial callosotomy followed by an experimental neurofeedback protocol that permits voluntary “cross-hemispheric attention” without restoring full callosal transfer. Patient J reports two distinct experiential modes: in one, she describes two independent streams of intention, each localized to one side; in the other, she describes a “merged perspective” in which she feels like a single subject with two simultaneous viewpoints, neither experienced as an external object.

The dialogue should be genuine back-and-forth: each philosopher must respond directly to the other’s points rather than talking past them. They should address whether Patient J’s first-person reports carry direct evidential weight or are likely confabulated; whether the “merged perspective” restores the kind of unity that was lost or reveals that unity was always a matter of functional coupling rather than phenomenal singularity; and whether split-brain cases ever genuinely threatened the unity of consciousness, or whether that threat only arises on the assumption of a single central observer that both reject but reject for different reasons. Neither character should be a strawman. The exchange should make clear whether their disagreement is ultimately empirical—about what neurofeedback protocols could in principle reveal—or conceptual—about what conditions must be met for one subject rather than two to exist.
