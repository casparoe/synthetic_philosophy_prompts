I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, writing on the norms governing rational belief revision. I have been following what is often discussed as the dogmatism paradox: the apparent tension between the normative requirement that rational agents should remain open to revising any empirical belief in light of counter-evidence, and the formal result that belief-revision frameworks satisfying standard consistency and closure conditions tend, in the limit, to generate beliefs that are structurally immune to further revision. For decades, the debate has seemed stalled. On one side, proponents of open-mindedness maintain that rationality requires non-extremal priors across all empirical domains and that any limit-immune belief represents a failure of epistemic humility. On the other side, defenders of structural rationality argue that rationality governs the dynamics of belief change—how an agent should move from one belief state to another—rather than imposing a static distribution of confidence, and that immunity at the limit is a feature of coherent belief management rather than a cognitive vice.

I am trying to determine whether this impasse is substantive, verbal, or a case of hidden divergence in the questions being asked. Specifically, I wonder whether much of the disagreement stems from a failure to distinguish diachronic dogmatism—an agent’s refusal to update over time in response to evidence—from synchronic framework-dogmatism, the formal property that certain beliefs become unrevisable within a given logical system. If these are separated, does the paradox dissolve, or does it re-emerge at the meta-level when we ask whether the revision framework itself can be rationally altered?

Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually commits to. Do they differ about empirical claims regarding how scientists or ordinary reasoners actually behave; about the correct formal model of belief change; about which cases should serve as data for a normative theory; or about starting intuitions—such as whether universal revisability is a non-negotiable condition on rationality—that neither camp can argue for from shared premises? Are they in fact answering different questions, one about the psychology of inquiry and one about the logic of belief sets, under the single heading of ‘rational belief revision’? And might a hidden empirical question—perhaps about whether human cognition can represent extremal priors without practical distortion—be doing the work?

If you think the debate is verbal or misdirected, say so, and propose the distinction or reformulation that would make progress possible. If you think it is substantive but hard, identify what kind of result—formal, empirical, or conceptual—would genuinely move it forward. Or, if you believe no progress should be expected, explain why: is the question ill-posed, do the sides share no common standard of success, or does the disagreement ultimately rest on normative commitments that are not subject to further rational adjudication?
