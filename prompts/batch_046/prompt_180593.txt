In my philosophy of science seminar we have been stuck for months on the rationality of belief revision when the evidence itself is graded rather than categorical—specifically, whether Jeffrey conditionalization is mandatory whenever an observation fails to determine a unique proposition. One camp holds that if you learn your thermometer reads somewhere between 68 and 72 degrees, you must redistribute credence inside that range according to Jeffrey’s rule, preserving the prior ratios within each cell of the evidential partition; they maintain that any other procedure violates the principle that rational updating should affect only beliefs about what was actually observed and should leave internal structural relations untouched. The opposing camp insists that the correct response is either to apply standard Bayes conditioning after arbitrarily sharpening the evidence into a definite proposition, or to treat the 68–72 range through a continuous likelihood function that relates the data to hypotheses about the true temperature without committing to any internal partition; they argue that Jeffrey’s rule illegitimately reifies a representational choice—how finely we carve up the possibility space—into a normative constraint on belief. We have worked through every standard illustration—the urn with unknown color proportions, the noisy thermometer, and even a climate-model ensemble that outputs a distribution over warming scenarios rather than a point prediction—yet neither side has conceded an inch. My working hypothesis is that this is not genuinely a dispute about the axioms of probability, but rather a disagreement about whether “the evidence” carries an intrinsic logical structure (a partition) that binds rational agents, or whether that structure is always an artifact of the agent’s descriptive framework. Is that diagnosis correct? More precisely, is the stall caused by a hidden normative difference about what counts as a reason for belief change, by a verbal ambiguity in what we call “uncertain evidence” versus “evidence with uncertain content,” or by the fact that the two sides are solving different problems—one concerned with preserving consistency with one’s prior representational scheme, the other concerned with accommodating external measurement error? And if the disagreement is substantive rather than ill-posed, what would actually move it: a formal result showing that one updating rule uniquely maximizes a specified epistemic utility, an empirical study of how human reasoners actually revise beliefs in graded-evidence tasks, or a conceptual distinction that shows the two rules apply to non-overlapping domains of inquiry?
