I'm currently taking a course on intergenerational justice and climate ethics, and I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in the assigned readings and in seminar discussions that I do not know how to classify. The pattern typically appears in defenses of aggressive emissions reduction. The speaker begins by attributing specific projected harms—crop failures in particular regions, coastal displacement, excess mortality from heat—to identifiable future cohorts, saying that we have a direct duty not to inflict these conditions on the people who will live in 2080 or on our grandchildren's generation. This frames the obligation as person-affecting: we are harming particular future individuals.

Then, when an interlocutor raises the non-identity problem—that our mitigation choices affect conception, birth, and migration patterns, so no single group of people exists under both the high-emission and low-emission pathways—the speaker does not abandon the direct-harm framing. Instead, they pivot to an impersonal justification, saying the real wrong is degrading the institutional or environmental conditions that any future people will inherit, or violating a threshold of intergenerational decency. But they immediately revert to citing the specific suffering of the 2080 cohort under high emissions as the decisive reason that the threshold has been breached, comparing welfare levels across the two pathways as if they applied to the same people.

What strikes me is that this is not merely equivocation or a motte-and-bailey retreat. Both frames are defended sincerely in different parts of the literature, and the speaker often acknowledges both. The distinctive move is the seamless oscillation within a single argument: the normative urgency is borrowed from the image of harming specific future people, while the logical defense against identity-sensitive objections is borrowed from an impersonal or structural principle. The argument thereby seems to gain the emotional and moral force of person-affecting reasoning together with the robustness of impersonal evaluation, without acknowledging that the two rest on incompatible baseline comparisons.

I have started thinking of this as a kind of reference-class drift in intergenerational reasoning, but I am unsure whether that captures it. Is there an established concept—perhaps in population ethics, climate ethics, or the theory of intergenerational equity—that names exactly this oscillation? If there is no established term, can the phenomenon be defined with precise boundaries that distinguish it from ordinary equivocation, from the non-identity problem itself, and from legitimate uses of representative agents or social welfare functions? I am particularly interested in whether the pattern depends essentially on identity-sensitive policies, or whether structurally analogous moves occur in other intergenerational contexts, such as resource depletion or genetic-engineering policies that affect who comes to exist. What matters to me is not a bare label but the exact carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
