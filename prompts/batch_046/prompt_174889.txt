I have been examining the architecture of philosophical intuition pumps, especially those deployed in debates about moral responsibility and mental causation, and I have observed a recurring structural pattern for which I lack a precise label. The pattern operates as follows. The author presents an action performed by an agent and offers two competing descriptions of that same event: one that highlights the agent's reasons, intentions, and institutional or social embeddedness, and another that highlights only the physical and causal antecedents. The reader is then invited to judge whether the second description captures everything that matters about the action. The critical feature is the selection of domain: the scenario is always drawn from a context—such as addiction, compulsive behavior, or automated routine—where the mechanistic redescription already feels alienating or dehumanizing. Parallel domains in which causal determination coexists comfortably with robust agency—master-level chess play, artistic composition under constraint, or the execution of professional duty—are systematically excluded as controls. The resulting intuition that the mechanistic account is missing something essential is then generalized into a metaphysical claim about the irreducibility of agency or consciousness, despite the possibility that what is missing is not a metaphysical property but precisely the normative and social framing that the pump has stripped away. I have noticed analogues of this structure in arguments about free will, in certain presentations of the explanatory gap, and in mechanistic accounts of practical reasoning. Does this specific configuration—the strategic pairing of a mechanistic redescription with a pre-selected, agency-depleted domain, followed by generalization—have an established name in the literature on thought experiments or philosophical methodology? How does it differ from a generic framing effect, from an omitted-variable fallacy in reasoning, or from the context-sensitivity that psychologists document in moral judgment? If the phenomenon is not separately catalogued, what would be the appropriate way to delineate it—particularly with respect to whether the omitted framing must be normative, or whether purely descriptive contextual details can play the same role?
