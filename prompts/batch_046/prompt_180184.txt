I'm drafting a paper for my poli sci seminar on why populist leaders still claim to speak for "the real people" even after they lose elections, and my roommate tried to summarize the academic view over coffee. He said: "Populism is basically just majortarianism with extra steps — it's the belief that whatever the majority wants is automatically right, and if courts or journalists block that, they're enemies of democracy. Populists want pure direct rule, like a permanent referendum, and by 'the will of the people' they just mean whatever the polls say. They say only they can read those polls right, so anyone who disagrees is treasonous. That's why they hate institutions — because real democracy should have no middlemen."

Something about that feels off, maybe too neat, but I can't put my finger on it. Is he conflating populism with simple majoritarianism? I keep hearing there's an anti-pluarlist element about the people being morally pure and homogeneous, not just a numerical majority, and that populists claim a monopoly on representing that unified will rather than just wanting direct democracy. Does his summary miss that distinction, and does his conclusion — that populists basically hate democracy — actually follow once you correct the parts that are wrong? I want to know which pieces are roughly right, which are common simplifications, and whether the accurate parts really support the idea that populism is anti-democratic in the way he thinks.
