Explain why he regards exchange mediated by money — particularly retail trade — as an instance of unnatural wealth-getting, and assess whether this critique necessarily depends on his claim that money is by nature barren, that is, atokos.

One student submitted the answer below.

Aristotle distinguishes oikonomikē, the natural management of household goods up to a necessary limit, from chrēmatistikē, the art of acquiring wealth without natural boundary. In Politics I he states that money (nomisma) was invented to serve exchange, yet in retail trade (kapēlikē) it is treated as an end in itself rather than a means. This reversal makes retail trade unnatural. Moreover, money is by nature barren (atokos): unlike living things or crops, it cannot reproduce. Because of this barrenness, Aristotle condemns every form of exchange for profit, including barter, whenever accumulation exceeds immediate household need. His rejection of usury (tokos) follows from exactly the same premise — if money cannot breed, then to demand interest is to ask a barren thing to reproduce. Hence the premise that money is atokos is the fundamental ground for both his critique of retail trade and his critique of lending at interest.
