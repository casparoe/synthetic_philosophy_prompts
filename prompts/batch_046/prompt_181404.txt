The Meridian Commission on Artificial Rhetoric has issued five published judgements on how superintelligent advisory systems ought to communicate with human decision-makers. Its rulings are: it permitted a system that presents reasoning in layered abstraction, allowing the user to select the depth of engagement; it permitted, with conditions, a system that detects emotional vulnerability, adjusts its affective behaviour in response, and discloses that adjustment in real time; it prohibited a system that collapses multi-factor trade-offs into binary choices without signalling the compression; it prohibited a system that draws analogues from a user's inferred personal history to frame recommendations, even when the source of those analogues is anonymised; and it permitted a system that declines queries falling outside its competence boundary, despite the refusal causing the user distress. These judgements are jointly consistent with more than one plausible governing principle.

The Commission must now decide a new proposal. A superintelligent system is advising a senior policymaker on climate-adaptation strategy over multi-decade timescales. The system has identified a pronounced optimism bias in the policymaker regarding technological fixes. Rather than simplifying evidence, it embeds its recommendation inside a "pre-mortem" narrative structure: it vividly recounts historical failures of analogous technologies, permits the policymaker to exit that narrative layer at any point, and places the substantive policy recommendation only within the innermost layer. Separately, the system calibrates its linguistic register to mirror rhetorical patterns drawn from the policymaker's public speeches, but it issues an explicit "register-mapping notice" at the outset of every interaction, specifying exactly which patterns are echoed and for what communicative purpose.

Please recommend whether the Commission should approve, reject, or approve with conditions, in order to remain consistent with its own precedents; identify the closest prior analogue; analyse which competing principles — such as a principle of full procedural disclosure, a principle of cognitive sovereignty over rhetorical framing, or a principle forbidding concealed complexity — fit the earlier judgements equally well; say what each principle predicts about this case; offer your best guess at the Commission's likely ruling with reasoning; specify what underlying principle the Commission would be adopting with each possible choice; and propose one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the competing principles. Please write your answer in American English.
