For a graduate colloquium on personal identity and medical ethics, draft a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers—designate them Dr. Aris and Dr. Bens—concerning the authority of advance directives when a patient's evaluative framework has been radically altered by severe cognitive decline.

Dr. Aris defends a structuralist reinterpretation of directive authority: rather than treating the document as an enduring preference-order that competes with the patient's present desires, she proposes that it establishes a diachronic veto point. On her account, the earlier self does not govern the later self like a monarch, but installs a procedural barrier—analogous to a constitutional entrenchment clause—that can only be overridden if the remaining psychological fragments demonstrate a threshold-level coherence with the values that originally motivated the directive. The authority is therefore not absolute sovereignty, but protected procedural continuity.

Dr. Bens rejects this reframing. He argues that severe psychological discontinuity—specifically the erosion of the evaluative and mnemonic connections that constitute practical identity—dissolves the moral unity required for any form of diachronic authority, procedural or otherwise. Once those connections fall below critical density, Bens contends, there is no later self that stands in a representative or fiduciary relation to the earlier one; there is only a different psychological subject inhabiting the same biological organism. To enforce the directive under these conditions, Bens insists, is not self-governance but external imposition—functionally indistinguishable from a family member's preference being enforced upon a stranger.

The exchange must be genuinely dialectical, not parallel monologues. Bens should press Aris on how she can specify the threshold-level coherence without implicitly relying on a descriptivist criterion of personal identity she wishes to avoid, and on whether her model permits clinicians to adjudicate that coherence in ways that reintroduce paternalism through the back door. Aris should reply directly, then challenge Bens on whether his discontinuity criterion entails that any profound but intact change of heart—say, a total conversion of values in a cognitively unimpaired adult—similarly nullifies prior commitments, and whether his view leaves any non-arbitrary authority for surrogate decision-making rather than mere clinical discretion. Both interlocutors should remain rigorous, avoid straw-manning, and maintain a tone appropriate for an advanced philosophy seminar.
