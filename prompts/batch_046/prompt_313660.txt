hey, i have this argument for my study group about the St. Petersburg paradox and it feels like it proves way too much. (1) the classic game has infinite expected value, so any rational agent should pay any finite price to play. (2) now imagine a "shadow game" where there's a hidden 1-in-1000 chance the casino burns down before paying, but otherwise its identical. the expectation is now (999/1000)·∞, which is still infinite. (3) you can stack as many independent shadow risks as you want — meteor strike, dealer heart attack, coin corosion — each with arbitrarily small probability, and as long as at least one path remains open to the infinite payout branch, the expectation doesn't drop to a real number. (4) so for literally any real-world bet that has a nonzero, however remote, path to unbounded payoff, rationality requires preferring it to every safe, bounded option. (5) that means i should empty my savings into every long-shot stock, every lottery, every speculative crypto token, because each has some implausible path to infinite returns. (6) nobody actually thinks that's required. (7) therefore the move in (2) — that scaling infinity by a factor less than 1 still leaves it infinite and therefore still dominates — must be wrong, or else expected utility maximization collapses.

i'd like to keep (1) and (6). where does the chain actually break? is it the arithmetic in (2)-(3), or the jump from infinite expectation to "must prefer" in (4)? and if i reject the arithmetic, what am i committing to about how probabilities interact with unbounded values?
