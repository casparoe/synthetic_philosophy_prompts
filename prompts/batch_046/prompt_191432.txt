There's this wierd split in eldercare policy that I've been noticing. On one side you've got these functional assessment tools — ADL and IADL checklists — that determine funding based on how independant a patient is. The more you can do alone, the lower your care tier, and facilities actually get penalized if residents don't "progress" toward self-sufficiency. On the other side, the same state agencies mandate relationship-centered care training for staff, which is all about how care is inherently relational and that needing others isn't a deficit. So the institution is literally scoring people on atomistic autonomy while preaching interdependence. What philosophical commitments about autonomy, dependency, and moral value does that practice presuppose? Can you reconstruct the implicit theory that makes both sides consistent, or is it just incoherent? And if we replaced whatever hidden view is there with a genuinely care-ethical framework — where vulnerability and asymmetrical care relationships are central — what would have to change in how these assessments are designed and used?
