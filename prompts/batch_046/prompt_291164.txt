Below is a draft outline and partial argument for an essay tentatively titled “Axiological Drift and the Rationality of Moral Enhancement.” The essay takes L.A. Paul’s well-known framework for transformative experience—centered on epistemic opacity, the inability to know what an experience will be like prior to undergoing it—and argues that it neglects a structurally distinct problem I call axiological drift: the transformation alters not merely what the subject knows, but the very evaluative standards by which she would judge whether the transformation was worth it.

The essay is divided into three parts. First, I distinguish epistemic opacity (ignorance of the phenomenal or qualitative character of being enhanced) from axiological drift (the post-transformation revision of one’s preference ordering, temporal discount rate, and weighting of personal projects against collective goods). I illustrate the distinction with a hypothetical but carefully specified case: a safe, in-principle-reversible neurochemical intervention that reliably strengthens altruistic motivation but gradually shifts the subject’s axiological framework so that she comes to regard her pre-intervention life-plan as misguided. Second, I argue that standard decision-theoretic treatments fail here not because information is missing, but because the utility function is endogenous to the choice itself. I propose a “stability constraint” on rational transformative choice: if undergoing a change makes it conceptually impossible for the pre-choice agent to recognize the post-choice evaluation as authoritative without circular appeal to the very values being assessed, then rational choice requires either refusing the transformation or accepting that post-choice preferences carry no special normative weight—an uncomfortable dilemma that Paul’s framework does not capture. Third, I consider the objection that axiological drift is merely a dramatic instance of ordinary preference change, which rational agents accommodate routinely. I reply that ordinary maturation allows for retrospective endorsement using stable higher-order values, whereas transformative drift undermines the continuity required for such endorsement; the direction of evaluative authority is reversed.

The draft currently runs to about 1,200 words of outline and argumentative prose, with the formal machinery left informal.

I would welcome focused comments on four points. (1) Is the distinction between epistemic opacity and axiological drift genuinely novel, or does it collapse into familiar concerns about adaptive preference formation and value change under coercion? (2) Does the stability constraint impose an implausibly conservative burden on rational agency—would it rule out benign transformations such as deep religious conversion or long-term moral development? (3) In the decision-theoretic section I gesture at endogenous utility functions without formalizing them; is the informal treatment sufficient for the conceptual claim, or does the argument require an explicit dynamic-choice model to avoid being hand-wavy? (4) The hypothetical moral-enhancement case is speculative by design; does this undermine the essay’s force, or is the speculative framing appropriate given that the target is a structural feature of rational choice rather than an empirical prediction?
