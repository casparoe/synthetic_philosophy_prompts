I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop titled Causation Across Disciplines, held at a university venue. I want the remarks to be structured, not decorated, by Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism: the view that what we call natural causation is in reality only a habitual conjunction (ʿāda) instituted by divine will, and that no created thing possesses independent causal efficacy. Specifically, I want to use Al-Ghazālī’s modal distinction—between what is impossible in itself, such as a triangle being circular, and what is merely not impossible in itself to be separated, such as fire failing to burn cotton—as the conceptual spine of the address. At the same time, I must not say anything that an informed occasionalist would regard as false—for example, attributing autonomous causal power to nature—nor anything that renders scientific inquiry pointless, since Al-Ghazālī himself maintained that investigating habitual patterns is rational and obligatory.
