During a graduate seminar on political morality, two participants became stuck in an argument about whether the problem of dirty hands operates differently in coalition governments than in unitary executives. The first maintained that coalitions actually dissolve dirty hands: because no single party controls the full programme, each partner can plausibly attribute any morally questionable compromise to the demands of the others, so individual moral exposure is reduced rather than shared. The second insisted this gets the logic backwards: coalitions multiply dirty hands, since every partner must actively endorse the joint governing agreement, and the capacity to shift blame is merely a psychological comfort, not a moral escape route. The first then introduced a distinction between 'executive dirt' — the direct ordering of a wrongful act — and 'legislative dirt' — voting for a legislative package that one knows contains an unjust element — arguing that coalition partners typically incur only the latter, which is structurally different. The second rejected the distinction outright, contending that to vote for a package one knows includes injustice is itself to order that injustice through one's representative act, so the structural difference collapses. They turned to a bystander and asked for an adjudication. Please examine their exchange move by move: evaluate the claim that coalitions dissolve dirty hands, the counter-claim that they multiply them, the executive/legislative dirt distinction, and the argument that representative endorsement collapses that distinction. For each move, indicate who has the stronger point and why. Then say what both interlocutors are missing about collective moral agency in multi-party government, and whether either account can accommodate the intuition that some institutional roles carry heavier moral exposure than others without reducing that exposure to a mere feeling of guilt.
