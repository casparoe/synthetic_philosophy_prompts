As a retired experimental physicist now serving on the curation board of a natural-history archive, I regularly face a practical classification problem that has begun to feel like an applied version of Leibniz’s principle. Our database catalogs physical specimens—chiefly meteorite fragments and synthetic crystal samples. When two entries register identical intrinsic measurements (elemental composition, isotopic ratios, crystal lattice parameters, mass within measurement error), we must decide whether to merge them or preserve them as distinct records. I want this translated into a reusable, fifteen-minute decision procedure that an archivist can run without philosophical training, using only the fields in our system.

The procedure should operationalize the identity of indiscernibles, but it must explicitly accommodate the challenge posed by the symmetric-universe spheres scenario—where two objects share every intrinsic property and differ only in mutual spatial or temporal relation. To keep the translation honest, it must separate archive-constitutive relational properties (provenance chain, documented origin event, synthetic batch identifier) from archive-accidental ones (current shelf coordinates, climate-zone storage location, order of data entry). It should take the form of an ordered flowchart in words: first test for intrinsic identity, then for relational and historical divergence, then apply a symmetry-breaking condition, and finally reach a signed judgment node.

Please construct the following:

1. The procedure itself, with ordered steps and explicit decision nodes. Mark which steps are mechanical checks against database fields and which are irreducible judgment calls that the archivist must document and sign off on.

2. Guidance on how to record the judgment calls, including a standard exception-protocol form for cases where scientific practice demands keeping two records separate (or merging them) despite what the indiscernibility test suggests.

3. Three worked applications:
   (a) Two synthetic crystals with identical lattice structures but different documented synthetic-batch origins;
   (b) Two meteorite fragments with identical isotopic profiles and no documented historical divergence except that they are stored in different climate-controlled zones;
   (c) One hard case where the procedure yields a result the archive’s scientific users will reject—either a forced merge that destroys useful provenance separation, or a forced separation that duplicates a genuinely single object—together with the correct use of the exception protocol.

4. An explicit statement of what the procedure cannot determine: whether relational differences individuate objects in nature or only in our cataloging scheme, and how that limitation should be communicated to archive users without invoking metaphysical debates about universals or possible worlds.

The deliverable should be a practical consistency tool for record-keeping, not a defense or refutation of Leibniz’s law. It must not ask the archivist to consult philosophical texts, and it should be frank about where simplification causes the procedure to misfire.
