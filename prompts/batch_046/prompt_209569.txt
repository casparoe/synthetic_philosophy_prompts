I'm developing a framework I call normative architecture—the structural relations among our first-order moral judgments, our meta-level commitments about how those judgments are formed, and the corrective procedures we apply when we suspect psychological distortion. For an upcoming seminar, I have tried to formalize my own position, but I worry I am smuggling in assumptions from incompatible traditions without noticing. Here is where I have landed, roughly.

First, I am a moral anti-realist in the expressivist tradition: I take moral judgments to function primarily as expressions of commitment and tools for coordinating attitudes, rather than as representations of mind-independent moral facts. Second, I nevertheless maintain that there is a rigorous, objective distinction between a cognitive bias—a systematic deviation in belief-formation caused by an irrelevant psychological or environmental feature—and a legitimate preference, understood as a weighting of values that survives full reflective scrutiny. Third, drawing on evolutionary moral psychology, I hold that judgments in the domain of purity, bodily disgust, and incest-avoidance are heavily contaminated by pathogen-avoidance heuristics and should therefore be treated as biased inputs rather than as data for reflective equilibrium. Fourth, I take reflective equilibrium—both narrow coherence among particular judgments and wide coherence with background theories—to be the correct normative method for ethics, but I interpret it non-representationally: coherence is a pragmatic virtue of a stable normative system, not convergence on an external moral reality. Fifth, I believe that identifying any judgment as "biased" requires an independent standard of correctness, which in the moral domain must be supplied by our all-things-considered preferences after full reflective correction has been applied.

My concern is whether these five commitments form a stable package. Specifically, I am uneasy about whether (2) can survive (1): if there are no mind-independent moral facts, in what sense is the bias-preference distinction objective rather than merely another expression of my own corrected preferences? At the same time, (5) seems to presuppose that there is a "fully corrected" endpoint functioning as a criterion of correctness, which looks suspiciously like the representationalist picture I rejected in (1). Meanwhile, (3) and (4) may pull in opposite directions: if reflective equilibrium is interpreted purely non-representationally, on what grounds do we privilege evolutionary-debiased judgments over coherence-preserving ones—why should pathogen-avoidance mechanisms be discounted rather than integrated into the equilibrium? There may also be an apparent tension between (1) and (4) that I am overestimating. I want an audit, not a general lecture: go through these commitments pair by pair, rank the tensions by severity, identify which ones are genuine incompatibilities and which dissolve on closer inspection, and for each real tension specify the least costly revision—what exactly would have to be modified or abandoned to restore consistency, and what that costs the overall framework. Please justify your grading with point-by-point comments rather than a broad impression.
