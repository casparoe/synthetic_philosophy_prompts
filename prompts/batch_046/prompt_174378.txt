I'm serving as a teaching assistant for an advanced seminar on nineteenth-century critiques of modernity, and I'm working through a stack of final exams that asked students to evaluate an extension of Kierkegaard's analysis in his 1846 review-essay The Present Age. In that text, Kierkegaard argues that the press produces an abstract entity he calls the public—a phantom composed of everyone in general and no one in particular—by substituting reflective opinion-formation for passionate, committed action. To push students beyond standard summaries, the exam introduced a conceptual distinction not usually foregrounded in introductory accounts: horizontal leveling, in which qualitative differences among individuals are flattened into quantitative equivalence, and vertical leveling, in which the hierarchy between immediate commitment and detached observation collapses so that every position occupies the same reflective plane.

The exam question read as follows. Some interpreters extend Kierkegaard's critique to claim that any medium accelerating the circulation of opinion necessarily deepens both forms of leveling, since speed forces abstraction from concrete situations. Assess this claim. In your answer, address three points: first, whether anonymity is a necessary condition for the public's existence or merely a facilitating feature; second, whether reflection is causally produced by the technological form of the press or only contingently hosted by it; and third, whether a hypothetical technology that restored embodied, face-to-face interaction but retained mass scalability—call it synaptic assembly—would dissolve the public or merely transform its mode of leveling.

STUDENT ANSWER: Kierkegaard's central claim is that newspapers create the public by making opinion anonymous. Because readers never encounter the author and never have to act on what they read, reflection replaces passion. This explains why the public is phantom-like: it possesses no concrete body and no unified will. The student correctly notes that leveling occurs when qualitative distinctions—such as the difference between a genuinely committed stance and a casually held view—are treated as quantitatively equivalent, so that every opinion counts the same regardless of its depth. On the first point, the student argues that anonymity is not merely facilitating but constitutive: without it there is no public, only a crowd. On the second point, the student asserts that the physical properties of print—its reproducibility and velocity—are what causally generate reflection, which means technology functions as the engine of leveling rather than as a neutral vehicle. Applying this logic to contemporary media, the student concludes that faster digital platforms must deepen leveling for exactly the same reason. On the third point, the student claims that synaptic assembly, because it restores embodied presence and eliminates anonymity, would necessarily dissolve the public and restore passionate commitment, since leveling requires absence. The answer concludes that Kierkegaard's analysis therefore supports a form of technological determinism: alter the medium's speed and its anonymity, and you alter the social form; the only escape from leveling is a technology that slows reflection and restores direct presence.

Identify where the student accurately captures Kierkegaard's visible claims—particularly the phantom-like abstraction of the public and the flattening of qualitative distinction—while also catching any subtle conflations, misattributed causal steps, or inferences that do not actually follow. I do not need references, bibliographies, or suggestions for further reading; I need only a precise assessment of the argument as it stands.
