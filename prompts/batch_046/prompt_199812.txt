Having retired from field entomology and taken up philosophy of action only in recent years, I am preparing a formal handout for a small seminar on Sphex ichneumoneus and the problem of mechanical rigidity, behavioural flexibility, and free will. Rather than reproducing the familiar taxonomy of compatibilism, libertarianism, and hard determinism, I should like a diagnostic decision tree built from three concrete splitting claims. First, is the wasp's stereotyped behaviour best explained by a failure of representational decoupling, or by an absence of what I shall call predictive entropy modulation — that is, the capacity to suppress a pre-potent motor programme when sensory feedback diverges from an expected statistical profile? Second, ought evolutionary stability under ecological stochasticity to count as a normative criterion for attributing behavioural flexibility, or is flexibility purely an architectural feature of the nervous system, independent of adaptive success? Third, does human free will require the representational buffer usually invoked by philosophers of mind, or can it be grounded in predictive entropy modulation alone, without full representational decoupling? Each branch should be labelled by the claim that divides it. Each leaf should carry either the name of a recognised position (for instance, views in the tradition of Hofstadter, Dennett, or contemporary enactivists) or be marked unnamed, together with one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Combinations that appear unoccupied should be noted and briefly diagnosed — whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I do not require bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the tree should simply distinguish the actual views of named thinkers without copying standard textbook classifications, and it should be expressed in formal British English.
