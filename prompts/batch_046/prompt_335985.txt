I take the view that in any normal-form game—whether finite or with a continuum of pure strategies—iterated elimination of strictly dominated mixed strategies yields a rationalizable set that is invariant under the order of elimination, even when domination is evaluated only against subsets of the opponent's strategy simplex that carry full measure. That is, simultaneous transfinite elimination and sequential player-by-player elimination converge to the same set of surviving strategy profiles. Present the position straight, then ask: what would have to be true for this invariance to collapse? Specify the measure-theoretic or topological pathologies—such as non-measurable best-response correspondences, strategies that become undominated only after the removal of a null set of rivals, or discontinuities in the payoff structure that allow resurrection of previously eliminated mixtures—that could make the elimination path depend on ordering. Distinguish failures that are merely logically conceivable from ones that could actually be instantiated in well-behaved economic or strategic models. Finally, assess whether the view can be saved by restricting attention to games with continuous payoffs and convex strategy spaces, and whether such a restriction protects the claim at the cost of rendering it trivial or unfalsifiable in practice.
