In philosophy of science, the debate over reductionism versus emergence in chemistry and biology has been stalled for decades despite broad agreement on the underlying science: quantum mechanics governs chemical bonding, evolutionary dynamics are physically instantiated, and in practice we rarely derive higher-level regularities from lower-level laws alone. Reductionists maintain that this practical failure is merely epistemic and that all causal powers remain physical; emergentists maintain that genuinely new properties or laws appear at higher organizational levels. My working hypothesis is that part of the stall comes from sliding between two patterns that both sides call emergence—one where higher-level structure merely constrains lower-level dynamics without introducing novel forces, which I will call structural-constraint emergence, and one where the higher level acquires genuinely autonomous causal capacities, which I will call dynamical-autonomy emergence. Reductionists typically grant the first and reject the second, while emergentists often use examples of the first to argue for the second. Is this disagreement primarily ontological, methodological, or verbal—are the sides answering different questions about scientific completeness versus disciplinary autonomy? Or is there a hidden empirical or formal issue—perhaps about the nature of causal powers or about derivability conditions—doing the real work? Given your diagnosis, what distinction, result, or change of framing could actually move the debate, or is this an unavoidable impasse rooted in bedrock intuitions about what counts as a satisfactory explanation?
