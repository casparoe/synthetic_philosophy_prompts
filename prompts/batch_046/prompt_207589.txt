Before responding, take a moment to recall the logical architecture of your defense: values must demonstrate both meaning-adequacy and causal adequacy, yet without being treated as physical efficient causes. Consider whether a reinterpretation of your thesis can preserve both conditions or whether it collapses one into the other.

My opening move: I want to test your thesis against what I will call selective filtration. On this view, Calvinist asceticism does not operate as a direct psychological motor—no entrepreneur accumulates capital because he feels the ethic pushing him—but rather functions as a cultural sieve: it eliminates institutional and behavioral forms incompatible with rationalized calculation (monastic charity, festive expenditure, status consumption), leaving only the austere, methodical form intact. The values are not motors but filters; they shape capitalism not by causing specific actions, but by selecting which actions survive culturally. This seems to save your claim that culture is indispensable—without the filter, the economic form would differ—while denying that anyone ever acts because of the religious meaning-connection you insisted upon. Does this filtration model preserve your argument, or does it betray its heart by stripping away the Sinnzusammenhang that you held was indispensable for historical explanation? And if the filter does all the explanatory work, have we not replaced your cultural causation with a functionalist mechanism that renders values causally redundant once the selection environment is fully described?
