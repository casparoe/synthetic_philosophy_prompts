A colleague on my university’s genocide-studies curriculum committee has consistently ruled on whether historical events may be taught within comparative modules that include the Holocaust. Her record is as follows: she approved including the Armenian genocide only in a separate “premodern genocide” unit, arguing that its organizational logic differs in kind; she approved including the Gulag system in the same comparative unit as the Holocaust, citing shared modern bureaucratic rationalization, statistical administration, and the division of labor that distances perpetrators from victims; she rejected including the Rwandan genocide in that unit, holding that its face-to-face, locally organized violence lacks the modern bureaucratic distancing she considers essential to the comparison; she rejected including the transatlantic slave trade in any genocide-comparison module, on the grounds that its driving logic was economic exploitation rather than ideological elimination, regardless of mortality scale; and she approved including the atomic bombing of Hiroshima only in a separate “technological distancing” unit, not in the genocide-comparison unit, distinguishing bureaucratic-technical execution from genocidal intent. The committee must now decide whether to include the 1943 Bengal famine—where British colonial administrators used census data and railway logistics to redirect grain reserves away from starving districts, producing mass mortality estimated in the millions—within the comparative genocide framework, or whether it belongs elsewhere entirely. Predict her verdict on this new case, articulate the two or three competing rules about modern bureaucratic form, ideological intent, and mortality scale that all fit her prior rulings equally well, state which rule she is most likely running and why, and specify the organizational features of one further hypothetical case that would best discriminate among those competing rules.
