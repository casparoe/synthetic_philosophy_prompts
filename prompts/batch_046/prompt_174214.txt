I just finished a long argument with a close friend about Keynes's account of probability and uncertainty, specifically about whether his notion of the weight of an argument can be fully absorbed into modern Bayesian or imprecise-probability frameworks. To make the disagreement concrete, we kept returning to a paired case: two climate scientists assessing the same regional flood-risk model, one with extensive local frequency records but little grasp of the underlying hydrology, the other with a deep causal model of watershed dynamics but almost no local measurements. We wanted to know whether Keynes's distinction between numerical probability and weight actually does normative work that a formal model cannot replicate. Here is the exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can manage without signaling which position was mine.

(1) My friend opened by saying that Keynes's distinction between risk and uncertainty is essentially the Knightian or Ellsberg-style distinction updated: uncertainty just means we lack a well-defined sample space or a precise prior, and weight is nothing more than the precision, confidence, or tightness attached to a subjective prior. If the first scientist has more data, her prior is tighter; if the second has less, his is wider. That, he said, is the whole story.

(2) I replied that Keynes meant something stronger. In his Treatise on Probability he distinguishes atomic relations, where evidence decomposes into independent items whose relevance is stable, from organic relations, where the relevance of any piece of evidence depends on the whole structure of the argument. Weight is not precision; it is the absolute amount of relevant evidence supporting a belief, and it can remain high even when the assigned probability is near one-half. More importantly, Keynes allows that some probabilities are genuinely non-comparable—not merely unknown in their ordering, but lacking any ordering relation at all—whereas any Bayesian framework, even an imprecise one, requires at least a partial order over credal states.

(3) He countered that organic is only a description of our present epistemic situation, not a feature of rational belief itself. Once you formalize the causal structure—say, with structural equations or a graphical model—the interdependencies become explicit, and every belief can be assigned a range or a point within the model. The appearance of non-comparability is just a failure to specify the state space precisely enough. Keynes's language is heuristic, not a claim about the logical limits of formalization.

(4) I pushed back with the concrete case. The first scientist can assign a sharp point probability to a hundred-year flood event because she possesses a long frequency record, yet in Keynes's terms her weight is low: her model is theoretically shallow, so she does not understand why the record behaves as it does. The second scientist possesses almost no frequency data, so he cannot rationally assign a sharp number, but his theoretical weight is high because his causal understanding is deep. Keynes's normative claim, I argued, is that when weight is very low, assigning any specific numerical value—sharp or bounded within a range—is rationally misleading, not merely imprecise. A Bayesian can represent both scientists with different priors, but Keynes is saying that the first scientist's sharp number misrepresents the rational status of her belief.

(5) My friend said that this normative claim is fully compatible with imprecise Bayesianism. A wide credal set is exactly the formal representation of do not assign a specific number. The second scientist's belief state is just the set of probabilities consistent with his structural model. There is no need for a separate dimension of weight; the width of the credal set together with the structure of the model does all the work. If the first scientist's belief should not be sharp, that is captured by assigning her a wide set constrained only weakly by her shallow theory.

(6) I then offered what I took to be the irreducible residue. Keynes suggests that for genuinely organic relations, not only are point values inappropriate, but even a partial ordering among belief states may fail—there may be no fact of the matter about whether one belief state is more or less probable than another, not because we are ignorant of the comparison, but because the evidential relations are not quantifiable along that dimension at all. If that is correct, then there exists a form of rational belief—perhaps exactly the second scientist's deep but data-poor state—that is not representable as any set of probability functions over a shared state space. Either this points to a profound limit on formal epistemology, or it is a confusion that arises from mistaking theoretical complexity for logical structure.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of (1) through (6), rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then tell me whether both of us missed something important—perhaps about how Keynes relates weight to practical action or decision-making under uncertainty, or about whether recent work in formal epistemology has already dissolved the problem without our noticing. Finally, be frank: does the idea in (6) that organic relations may resist even partial ordering point to a genuine gap in Bayesian and imprecise-probability frameworks, or is it best understood as a formalization difficulty that further modeling eventually resolves? I want an honest assessment of the philosophical substance, not a diplomatic split.
