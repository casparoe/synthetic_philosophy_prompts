I'm working through a comparative essay on institutional decay and have been cataloguing the analogical judgments of a particular historian of the late Roman Republic—let's call her the analyst—who compares Roman pathologies to contemporary democratic backsliding. Her track record is as follows. She accepts the analogy between the accumulation of extraordinary military commands with extended proconsular imperium and modern executives who institutionalize permanent emergency security powers. She rejects the analogy between senatorial obstruction driven by private wealth concentration and modern legislative gridlock driven by partisan polarization, insisting that patron-client militarization and party discipline are structurally incomparable. She accepts the analogy between the erosion of collegial magistracies through repeated prorogation and modern normalization of executive decrees that bypass ordinary legislative quorum rules. She rejects the analogy between popularis land redistribution and modern universal basic income experiments, on the grounds that the Roman mechanism involved direct militarized clientelism absent in bureaucratic welfare states. Finally, she grants only a weak analogy between the partisan packing of the quaestiones perpetuae and modern court-packing, emphasizing that Roman judges served without tenure and were therefore exposed to immediate physical retaliation in ways that modern judges are not.

The next case concerns the Roman censura morum: censors, elected to a separate magistracy, could strike senators from the roll for moral turpitude—bankruptcy, excessive luxury, failure to observe religious rites—without any criminal trial, relying on unwritten aristocratic norms rather than codified law. The proposed modern parallel is the emerging model of independent parliamentary ethics boards in presidential systems, bodies whose members are appointed rather than elected and who are empowered to suspend sitting legislators for violations of 'constitutional morality' or 'democratic values' that are deliberately left uncodified, without requiring prior judicial conviction.

Given this record, I want you to do four things. First, predict whether the analyst should judge this analogy strong, weak, or rejected, and justify your prediction. Second, articulate at least two competing interpretive rules that could consistently explain her earlier five verdicts—one that might stress formal institutional structure, another that might stress the presence or absence of direct patron-client coercion, or another principle of your own identification. Third, show what each of those rules predicts about the censure-to-ethics-board case, then state which rule you think she is actually running and why, together with your best-guess verdict. Fourth, propose one further analogical case—specifying both the Roman institution or practice and the modern democratic feature—that would sharply distinguish between the two rules you have identified.
