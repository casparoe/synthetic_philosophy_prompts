I'm preparing an application for a six-month graduate research fellowship in political philosophy, and I want to develop a proposal that does not simply rehearse the familiar debate over whether the left-right spectrum still describes contemporary politics. Instead, I want to ask whether the distinction is held together by something deeper than shared policy preferences—specifically, by opposing answers to what I will call the problem of residual social obligation: the obligations that remain binding when formal institutions, contracts, or markets fail to fully determine outcomes in domains like environmental risk, intergenerational debt, and technological displacement. My half-formed intuition is that left and right are not best understood as positions on a single axis about state intervention, but as two different integration strategies—one expanding the scope of recognized obligation, the other localizing and restricting it—and that testing whether these strategies cohere across cases will reveal whether the distinction tracks a real philosophical principle or is merely a historical cluster of unrelated stances.

The existing literature leaves this open because critics in the end-of-ideology tradition typically show that self-identified left and right disagree among themselves on specific issues, but they do not ask whether a structural logic explains why particular disagreements travel together. Conversely, defenders of the spectrum usually defend it on empirical grounds—voting patterns, survey data, or institutional alignments—without providing a conceptual principle that makes the clustering intelligible rather than accidental. My approach would therefore be philosophical rather than sociological: I would construct an analytical framework distinguishing three separable components of any response to residual obligation—an epistemic component (how we come to know that an obligation exists), a normative component (to whom it is owed and with what weight), and an institutional component (whether it should be enforced through centralized or distributed mechanisms)—and then examine whether standard left-right positions map consistently onto these components across the three case domains. I would proceed month by month: the first two months refining the framework and identifying precisely where conventional left-right classifications break down within each case; the next two months analyzing whether those breakdowns are random or systematically correlated with one of the three components; and the final two months determining whether a reconstructed, philosophically defensible distinction survives this decomposition, or whether it fragments into local, incommensurable stances.

What I need is a polished, citation-free two-page proposal that states the precise research question—does the left-right distinction track a single underlying principle of social integration, or does it dissolve once we separate epistemic, normative, and institutional commitments?—explains why standard empirical and ideological defenses fail to settle it, details the method of conceptual decomposition plus cross-case consistency testing, specifies what counts as success (a reconstructed distinction that survives decomposition with predictive coherence across cases) versus failure (the distinction collapses into unrelated contextual judgments), and includes an honest scope assessment: this is tractable in six months for a philosophical argument of roughly six thousand words, but it will not yield predictive political science, and if the three domains turn out to be genuinely incommensurable the project will conclude with a negative result that is still philosophically useful. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus; I will supply references myself, and I want the proposal to stand entirely on its conceptual architecture.
