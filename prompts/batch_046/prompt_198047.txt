I am a novelist who has spent the last fifteen years constructing a long-form fiction in which the laws of nature themselves function as a distributed, non-human narrative consciousness—not merely as metaphor, but as an ontological premise—so I have developed a working fluency in the vocabulary of narrative focalization, the distinction between foreground entities endowed with full interiority and background elements treated as mere setting, and the ethical obligations an author implicitly assumes toward characters whose subjective experience is acknowledged versus those rendered as inert scenery. With that framework in mind, I want you to take the core ethical argument arising from contemporary panpsychism about suffering in fundamental physics—the claim that if minimal sentience or proto-experiential qualities pervade physical reality at the level of quantum fields, elementary particles, or continuous spacetime, then the aggregate moral weight of suffering or disvalue in the universe vastly exceeds anything human-scale ethics is equipped to measure—and translate it systematically into my narrative vocabulary. For each premise—physical ubiquity, minimal or non-cognitive sentience, and the aggregation of moral significance—state what maps cleanly onto narrative architecture, what must be distorted to fit, and what possesses no analogue at all, paying particular attention to whether the notion of non-intentional, non-culpable suffering can survive translation without smuggling in metaphysical commitments foreign to fiction.

Then, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, report whether the untranslatable residue points to a genuine structural feature that narrative ontology suppresses—suggesting that panpsychism reveals something physics actually demands—or whether it exposes a category error in which human psychological concepts are projected onto non-human substrates. Please compose your answer as though writing a formal letter, entirely in American English, and keep the reasoning rigorous enough that I could use it as a conceptual anchor for the next volume of my project.
