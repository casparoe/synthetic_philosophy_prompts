As a retired experimental biologist recently working through the philosophy of action, I now serve on a regional review board that approves or rejects intervention plans for convicted offenders with documented executive-function deficits; we handle roughly a dozen files monthly, each must be decided in under thirty minutes without legal counsel present, and I must personally sign every recommendation. I need a reusable, non-technical decision procedure—ordered questions, sufficient-answer criteria, explicit stopping rules, and clearly marked judgment-calls—grounded in Daniel Dennett’s compatibilist defense of just deserts, which ties moral responsibility to a kind of cognitive “elbow room” and appropriate reactive attitudes, and in Gregg Caruso’s free-will skepticism, which rejects retributive desert and justifies coercive intervention solely on a public-health quarantine model. To make this usable with the clinical files I actually see, the procedure must first run a “capacity audit” that distinguishes localized prefrontal impairment from global cognitive closure—a distinction neither philosopher develops in detail but that constantly arises in practice—then apply Dennett’s criteria for responsible agency and Caruso’s criteria for containment-worthy dangerousness in sequence.

Please specify: (1) the factual questions to ask of each file, in order; (2) what counts as a sufficient answer to each; (3) the exact point where the procedure yields a clear “approve moral-repair track” or “approve containment-only” recommendation; (4) the precise conditions under which it must halt and hand the decision to human value-judgment rather than yielding an answer; and (5) the categories of case it is structurally likely to misclassify. Then apply it to three worked cases: (a) an offender with localized working-memory deficits who committed calculated financial fraud; (b) an offender with global impulsivity and no record of reason-responsive behavior who caused serious bodily harm; and (c) one boundary case of your own design where the procedure produces a recommendation I will likely find unwelcome—explain whether that result reveals a flaw in the procedure or in my intuitions. Conclude by stating plainly what this procedure cannot do, especially regarding predictive risk assessment and the moral weight of suffering, and keep everything formal, without bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading.
