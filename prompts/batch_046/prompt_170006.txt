I've been thinking about the language of thought hypothesis—the idea that thinking involves manipulating mental symbols with something like grammar, not just shuffling images or feelings. I don't have a formal philosophy background, but I find the claim compelling, and recently I got into a long discussion with two friends about whether a particular kind of artificial mind could count as having a language of thought at all.

We were imagining a system that doesn't use discrete symbols or words of any kind. Instead, it represents properties and relations—redness, cubeness, spatial proximity—as continuous points in a high-dimensional geometric space. When it needs to represent something complex, like a red cube near a blue sphere, it doesn't concatenate symbols. Instead, it applies what we called a "binding rotation": it takes the vector for redness, the vector for cubeness, and the relation vector, and transforms them together into a single new point that encodes the whole scene. One friend argued that this is still compositional in exactly the way language-of-thought theorists care about, because the representation of the whole is systematically built from representations of the parts; it's just that the combination is geometric rather than syntactic. The other friend insisted that this misses the core point: compositionality in a language requires that the constituent representations remain intact and available for other combinations, like how "red cube" and "blue sphere" can be rearranged by rules. In the geometric system, she said, the original feature-vectors get blended and distorted during the binding rotation, so you can't recover the pure "red" vector from the scene-vector; the parts lose their identity, which is precisely why it's not a language. The first friend replied that human neural representations might work in the same blended, distributed way, so demanding discrete, rearrangeable syntax just assumes cognition is computer-like rather than brain-like. They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't decide.

Can you walk through their exchange point by point and say who is right about each specific move? In particular, is the geometric-binding system genuinely compositional without being syntactic, and does that challenge the language of thought hypothesis itself, or does it merely show that some other kind of representation is possible? Also, is there something important both sides are missing about whether blended representations can still support the systematicity that the hypothesis was originally meant to explain?
