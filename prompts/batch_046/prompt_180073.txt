Write the text of the opening remarks for a retirement symposium honoring a senior administrator who spent thirty years attempting to formalize university governance through axiomatic planning models. The audience is split: half are mathematicians and philosophers of science familiar with Thomas Hobbes's conviction that geometry supplies the only genuinely demonstrative science and with his subsequent public failure in geometric practice, particularly the dispute over squaring the circle with John Wallis; the other half are family, staff, and donors with no background in either. The philosophical idea must do structural work, not decorative work. Use Hobbes's geometric ideal—that civil philosophy should achieve the same certainty as the demonstration of magnitudes—to explain why the honoree's formal approach to institutional design was intellectually serious rather than merely bureaucratic. Then use Hobbes's actual geometric failure, and Wallis's correction of it, to frame a specific limitation of the career being honored: that planning can aspire to Euclidean clarity in its first principles without ever reaching the closure of a proof, and that recognizing this gap is part of the rigor rather than a confession of defeat. The address should therefore avoid both technical lecture and generic eulogy; it must make the comparison intelligible to non-specialists without letting specialists treat it as a shallow analogy, and it should honor persistent aspiration rather than claiming a success that never arrived.
