I'm preparing the framework for my graduate seminar on logical epistemology and want to audit my commitments before committing them to print. I hold that (a) Carroll's tortoise demonstrates a vicious regress specifically at the token level of applying modus ponens, not a benign regress at the type level; (b) consequently, logical rules must be treated as primitive with respect to derivation, not reducible to more fundamental cognitive or metaphysical principles; (c) nevertheless, Quine's claim that logic is empirically revisable remains defensible, since a primitive rule might still be abandoned if it systematically conflicts with the constitutive norms that govern rational discourse; (d) however, revising a logical framework is structurally unlike revising an empirical hypothesis, because the former alters the conditions under which revision itself is evaluated, while the latter operates within those conditions; (e) accepting logical rules as primitive does not, on my view, entail rationalist intuitionism, because primitive acceptance can be understood as pragmatic and fallibilist rather than as an appeal to self-evidence; (f) finally, I maintain that the tortoise problem is distinctive to deductive inference and does not extend to inductive or abductive reasoning, since only deduction involves the explicit application of an inference rule in the manner Carroll describes. Do these six commitments form a jointly tenable package? Specifically, I am concerned that (c) and (d) pull in opposite directions regarding whether primitive rules are genuinely revisable in Quine's sense, and that (f) may not survive scrutiny if abductive reasoning also involves implicit rule-application. At the same time, I suspect that the tension between (e) and (b) is merely apparent, since fallibilism about our acceptance of rules is compatible with their being primitive with respect to derivation. Rank all tensions by severity, distinguish the genuine conflicts from the merely apparent ones, and specify the least costly revision—whether that requires weakening (c), qualifying (d), or abandoning (f)—together with what that revision costs the overall position.
