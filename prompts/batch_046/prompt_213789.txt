I teach a postgraduate philosophy of mind seminar with about fourteen students, and I have one ninety-minute session to work with. We have been discussing the thought experiment from The Mind's I, in which Einstein’s brain is replaced, neuron by neuron, by a book describing each state transition, and I want a structured classroom simulation that makes the group feel—rather than merely argue about—the precise point at which perfect functional replication ceases to carry the presence of a conscious subject. Design an activity with clear phases, roles, and physical materials: in the first phase, six students form a live “neural network,” passing handwritten messages according to hidden coordination rules that collectively produce answers in a recognisably Einsteinian voice, while the rest observe; in the second phase, without prior warning, every live node is replaced by an index card bearing the exact same rule, and the cards are bound into a single physical book that one designated reader must consult aloud to answer identical questions from a questioner. Specify the message-passing rules, the room layout, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the observer task; then provide a debrief that maps each phase onto the book-replacement scenario, directs attention to what changed in the room’s interactional dynamics rather than in the logical output, and is frank about which feature of phenomenal presence—perhaps the binding of experience or an irreducible first-person point of view—the activity can only gesture toward and must be supplied by theoretical discussion afterwards.

Please include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time you run it, such as observers instinctively attributing consciousness to the card-reader out of ordinary social reflex, and advise how the facilitator should handle that without breaking the simulation’s effect. The deliverable should avoid any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, should not depend on highly specialised theories that a small language model would not have properly memorised, and should be written in British English.
