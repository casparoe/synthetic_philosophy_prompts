I am preparing a contribution for an interdisciplinary workshop on strategic reasoning in artificial multi-agent systems, and I would like you to construct a rigorous argument for the following position. In mechanism-design settings where participants are algorithmic agents whose source code is observable to other nodes—so that an agent's decision procedure is part of the strategic environment rather than hidden private information—program equilibrium ought to be adopted as the normative benchmark in place of both Bayesian-Nash equilibrium and subgame-perfect equilibrium. Please argue that the familiar objection—that program equilibrium becomes trivial or collapses once agents are permitted to rewrite their own code—can be dissolved by drawing a sharp distinction between syntactic self-modification (altering the literal instructions of one's programme) and semantic commitment (binding one's future reasoning architecture through publicly verifiable constraints). From there, defend the claim that this distinction allows program equilibrium to sustain genuinely non-trivial strategic interaction even under conditions of bounded transparency, because internalising the agent's reasoning procedure into the game form transforms the problem of common-knowledge rationality into a problem of verifiable architectural consistency. Your argument should explain why this shift does not merely reproduce the circularities associated with traditional equilibrium concepts, but instead offers a more defensible foundation for evaluating mechanism performance when agents possess only bounded strategic sophistication. Please present the argument in British English, with premises and inferences clearly articulated, and avoid appeals to specific bibliographic sources since the workshop emphasises conceptual analysis over citation.
