After retiring from thirty years of laboratory work in condensed-matter physics, I have recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of science and mind, and the course instructor has asked me to assist in grading a midterm examination on perception and cognition. I have composed the examination question below myself, and I now present one student response that strikes me as competent on first reading yet possibly flawed in ways a hasty grader might miss; I do not wish to impose my own tentative judgment, and I ask for your independent, point-by-point assessment.

The examination question was as follows: Some philosophers of science claim that expert laboratory observation is a paradigm case of cognitive penetration—the trained researcher literally sees structures that the novice merely interprets. Drawing on Fodor’s thesis of informational encapsulation, explain whether perceptual learning in scientific expertise supports the claim that central cognitive states penetrate perceptual experience. In your answer, distinguish carefully between (a) intra-modular calibration, in which the perceptual system adjusts its own parameter settings through statistical exposure, and (b) cross-domain penetration, in which a specific belief formed in central cognition alters the content of a particular perceptual episode. Evaluate whether ambiguous figures—such as the duck-rabbit—provide evidence for (b) or only for (a).

The student answered: Fodor’s modularity thesis holds that perceptual systems are domain-specific, fast, and informationally encapsulated, meaning that central beliefs and desires do not have direct access to the computations within a perceptual module. Pylyshyn appeals to ambiguous figures—such as the duck-rabbit—to show that cognition penetrates perception: because the retinal image remains constant while the experienced image shifts, a change in central belief must be altering the perceptual content itself. In scientific expertise, a trained microscopist genuinely sees cellular structures that appear as undifferentiated gray shapes to a novice; this is not a matter of post-perceptual interpretation but of cross-domain penetration, since the microscopist’s theoretical knowledge of biology reaches into the visual system and restructures its outputs. Therefore, informational encapsulation is false. Fodor can respond only by denying that perceptual learning occurs at all, which is implausible given the evidence from expert vision, or by abandoning modularity for human perception. The distinction between intra-modular calibration and cross-domain penetration is useful but ultimately collapses in practice, because any calibration that depends on conceptual training involves the very cognitive states that modularity was meant to exclude.

Please assign a specific grade—whether on a conventional letter scale or a percentage—and justify it with precise, defensible comments that address each substantive move. I am particularly interested in whether the student has correctly applied the distinction between calibration and penetration, whether any philosophical view has been misattributed, and whether the concluding inference about Fodor’s available responses actually follows from the preceding premises. Treat your reply as an instructor’s grading memo: I need concrete remarks I can return to the student without ambiguity.
