I was covering a recent colloquium on metaphysics for my publication and found myself drawn into a dinner debate that has stayed with me. The conversation turned to Max Black’s well-known thought experiment involving two identical spheres in an otherwise empty universe, traditionally deployed to challenge Leibniz’s principle of the identity of indiscernibles. My interlocutors—two philosophers of physics I will call Elena and Marcus—disagreed sharply about what the case actually establishes, and they introduced a topological twist I had not encountered in the standard literature.

Elena argued that Black’s spheres fail to refute the principle because spatial separation is not an intrinsic property but a relational one; once we allow that each sphere bears distinct relations—being one mile from the other sphere, being situated at one location rather than another—the principle is satisfied, not violated. She insisted that critics of Leibniz confuse the absence of qualitative difference with the absence of all difference whatsoever. Marcus countered that this defense renders the principle vacuous: if we are permitted to invoke relational properties that essentially presuppose the very distinctness we are trying to explain, then the principle becomes a tautology rather than a substantive constraint. He added that in a genuinely symmetric scenario, such relational distinctions collapse. At this point Elena proposed a refined variant: imagine not flat space but a universe with the global topology of a closed, non-orientable three-manifold—conceptually similar to a Klein bottle—where the two spheres sit at antipodal positions. In such a space, directional relations such as being north of or to the left of are globally inconsistent, so there is no stable relational property that attaches to one sphere but not the other. Even distance relations become symmetric in a way that eliminates directional asymmetry. Elena claimed that in this setting, the spheres are genuinely indiscernible by both intrinsic and relational properties, which means the principle of identity of indiscernibles actually forces us to conclude they are one and the same object—despite our intuition that there are clearly two. Marcus replied that this result is not a vindication of the principle but a reductio ad absurdum of the scenario: the proper conclusion is that such a symmetric, two-object universe is metaphysically impossible, not that the spheres merge into identity. He further noted that Elena was smuggling in an implicit reference frame by assuming the spheres can be placed at antipodal points, which already distinguishes them via their respective positions in the topological structure.

Can you walk through their exchange point by point and say who is right about each specific claim—particularly regarding whether relational properties save the principle, whether the non-orientable variant genuinely eliminates all distinctions, and whether Marcus’s impossibility reply succeeds? I am also curious what both of them are missing: is there a coherent formulation of the principle that survives Black’s original case without collapsing into tautology, and does the topological twist actually strengthen the anti-Leibnizian argument rather than Elena’s defense?
