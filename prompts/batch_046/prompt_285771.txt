hey, I'm writing a piece on marriage law and I keep hitting this conceptual wall. So same-sex marriage is legal in a bunch of countries now, which seemed to kill the "marriage is just for reproduction" line. But the state still only lets exactly two people do it, and it still hands married couples this automatic bundle of rights—spousal priviledge in court, forced inheritance shares you can't waive with a will, tax breaks that don't apply to siblings running a farm together. So what view of what marriage is *for* is actually smuggled into that half-reformed setup? Does allowing gay couples but banning triads and platonic cohabitants presuppose that marriage is essentially a romantic dyad with built-in caregiving duties? Or is it just a status the state assigns to regulate intimacy? And if the real point is protecting dependants, why do we care about a spouse's welfare but not a live-in nurse's? I'm trying to see whether the practice is committed to one coherent theory, or if it's just historical leftovers that don't hang together—like we updated the gender part but left the dyadic and automatic-rights parts untouched because no one wanted to touch them.
