I am preparing a presentation for my metaphysics seminar on vagueness, specifically on whether epistemicism or supervaluationism offers the better account of borderline cases. To move beyond standard sorites examples, I want to examine Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982), particularly the director's cut with its unicorn dream sequence and the origami unicorn left by Gaff. The film constructs a world in which the boundary between human and replicant is administratively treated as sharp—the Voight-Kampff empathy test is meant to draw a precise line—yet practically dissolves: Rachael requires an unusually high number of questions before the test registers a response, Roy Batty demonstrates more compassion and existential reflection than many human characters, and Deckard's own status is left deliberately ambiguous, especially given the implication that he may possess implanted memories symbolized by the unicorn vision.

Read the film through the lens of this philosophical debate. Does Blade Runner present the human-replicant distinction as a case of epistemic opacity, where there is a hidden sharp biological or ontological fact about Deckard that the characters and audience simply lack access to? Or does it present the distinction as genuinely semantically indeterminate, such that there is no fact of the matter about whether Deckard counts as human, and assertions about his status are neither true nor false but suffer from a failure of bivalence? Focus your analysis on the unicorn sequence and the tears in rain scene, asking in each case which philosophical reading is more natural and where the film actively resists that reading.

I also want you to consider a more niche possibility that standard treatments of vagueness rarely address: that the moral and aesthetic urgency of the film depends not on resolving the boundary but on its irreducible fuzziness. If the value of Roy Batty's final monologue lies precisely in the fact that his mortality and memory do not depend on a clear categorical answer to whether he is human, then does the film suggest that vagueness is not merely a semantic or epistemic problem but an ethical resource? Give me a reading that is faithful in both directions—do not reduce the film to an illustration of either philosophical position, and do not distort the theories to accommodate the plot. If the work genuinely adds something to the debate, such as the idea that practical recognition can outrun categorical determination, say so explicitly and explain what argument alone misses.
