I'm a phd student in philopsophy of law consulting with a state capital-case review board. They meet monthly, get 90 minutes per file, and the panel is two prosecutors, one public defender, and a retired judge—no philosophers in the room, and both the chair and the DA have to sign whatever recommendation comes out. They need a reusable decision procedure for whether to file a death-notice motion, built out of the three things everyone talks about: retributive proportionality, actual deterrence, and the risk we'll execute someone innocent.

What I need is an ordered checklist they can run without me: the questions in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to pass each gate, the exact points where the procedure stops with a hard no, and the spots where it has to hand off to a value judgment because facts run out. I also want you to flag where the procedure will misfire—where it hides judgment behind fake precision, or where two board members can run the same file and come out different because they dont share a baseline.

Ground it in something like an "irreversibility symmetry" condition for retribution—basically, the punishment is irreversible so the culpability finding has to be irreversible in the same register—and treat deterrence as marginal case-specific deterrence, not general population stats, since thats all they actually have data for. Keep it away from bibliographies or reading lists; they dont have library time.

Then apply it to three files:

1) A contract murder with DNA, three eyewitness IDs, and a prior homicide conviction—this should sail through, but I want the procedure to flag exactly why it still might feel wrong to recommend death.

2) A domestic killing where the defendent has documented severe mental illness and the physical case rests on contested blood-spatter interpretation—this should hit a hard stop on wrongful-execution risk, but I want to see if the retributive filter also forces a downgrade or just stays silent.

3) A mass-casualty shooting with overwhelming forensic and video evidence, but the shooter was 19 and theres credible developmental neuroscience on adolescent decision-making. This is the case where I think the procedure will recieve a "do not file" recommendation because culpability doesnt meet the irreversibility threshold, and I know the board will hate that answer—show me how the procedure delivers it anyway and where it admits it cant settle the moral weight.

Finally, be honest about what the procedure cant do: where it needs numbers they wont have, where it collapses if they disagree on prior probabilities, and what happens when a gate is technically passed but the board's gut says no.
