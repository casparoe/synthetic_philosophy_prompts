Having spent decades in experimental physics, I have recently turned to philosophy and am now working through Aristotle's argument about future contingents—the so-called sea-battle problem. Rather than asking for historical exegesis, I would like you to describe the most important implications of accepting Aristotle's core claim—that not every future-tensed statement is now determinately true or false—for the logic and practice of natural science. Specifically, does this logical openness bear on statistical forecasting, on how we interpret quantum probabilities, or on the difference between deterministic dynamical laws and genuinely open-ended evolutionary processes? Or is the difficulty purely logical, with no consequence for scientific reasoning? I am particularly interested in whether accepting this view should lead a scientist to distinguish predictive determinism—the idea that laws fix outcomes—from logical determinism—the idea that truth-values are already fixed—and whether that distinction can be maintained in actual scientific practice.
