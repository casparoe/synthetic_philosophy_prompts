I was discussing with a friend last night—he is a mathematician with a taste for structural realism, I am a philosopher of physics still unsettled by Einstein’s hole argument—whether the points of spacetime possess any identity independent of the fields that inhabit them. We argued for hours about the scenario in which a smooth diffeomorphism is applied that leaves every physical quantity unchanged outside a bounded region, the “hole,” yet permutes the points inside so that the metric is dragged along with them, yielding two mathematically distinct solutions for precisely the same initial conditions. My friend maintained that this is only a redundancy in representation: the points are mere labels, and physics should be invariant under their reassignment. I kept returning to a more disquieting thought—if every intrinsic and relational property inside the hole is preserved across the two descriptions, then there is nothing, not even a hidden essence, to distinguish one point from its image; the hole is not missing an object, but missing the very relation that would make identity determinate. We parted without agreement, and I have been turning over in my mind what it would feel like to encounter this not as an equation but as a lived dilemma.

Could you write a short story that dramatizes this tension? I imagine a cartographer named Elian who works in an archive that preserves every physically possible universe as a bound volume, indexed not only by its fields and geometries but by the identities of the manifold points that bear them. She discovers two volumes describing worlds that are indistinguishable outside a spherical region—the hole—but inside that region the archival index assigns different point-identities to locations that share every measurable property: the same curvature, the same energy distribution, the same causal structure. The archive’s governing principle holds that if two volumes differ only by such an internal permutation, they record a single world with redundant labeling; yet Elian notices that the index itself treats the permutations as generating distinct histories, as though the future of the region is fixed relative to one labeling but not to the other. She must decide whether the archive should collapse the pair into one entry, thereby accepting that determinism holds but point-identity is ontologically empty, or preserve both, thereby conceding that the same past genuinely permits multiple futures not because information is lacking but because the present lacks the structure to say “this point, not that one.” The story should explore what it means for identity to be underdetermined by physics, and whether the failure of determinism inside the hole is a failure of prediction or a revelation that prediction was never about points at all, but only about patterns that have no one to belong to. Please keep the conceptual core precise—the problem is not hidden variables or epistemic limitation, but the possibility that general covariance strips the manifold of any feature that could anchor a unique correspondence—while allowing the human drama to center on Elian’s growing suspicion that she herself, in trying to adjudicate the volumes, is imposing an identity that the physics refuses to ratify.
