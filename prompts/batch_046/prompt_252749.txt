As a member of my university's Digital Ethics Board, I am drafting the opinion we will circulate before Thursday's meeting on a pilot proposal from the Graduate School of Public Administration titled "Civic Data Reciprocity." The program requires students to maintain an encrypted, purpose-limited ledger documenting every data request they file with state agencies, reflecting German data-protection norms of transparency and minimization; simultaneously, it requires them to publish an anonymized "civic contribution index" calculated from peer-reviewed service hours and community ratings, adapting the evaluative logic of Chinese social-credit systems but confining it to non-financial civic behavior. Our American partner institutions have insisted that all ledger metadata remain open-access and machine-readable to prevent bureaucratic corruption, invoking norms of radical governmental openness rather than individual privacy. I was discussing this with a colleague in the faculty lounge after our comparative governance seminar, and she kept insisting that democratic citizenship depends on strategic opacity—the deliberate withholding of certain information from the state—rather than on achieving either total transparency or total exposure. I need our memo to recommend approval, rejection, or conditional approval with a concrete timeline, and it must engage seriously with the argument that citizens should sometimes hide information from the state.

Please present your response as a numbered list of exactly five items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The first item should state the board's recommendation and any conditions; the second and third should address how German minimization norms and the American-Chinese transparency models, respectively, relate to strategic hiding; the fourth should present the strongest consideration on the side of openness or exposure; and the fifth should specify the concrete course of action and its implementation deadline.
