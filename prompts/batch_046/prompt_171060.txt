I am preparing the final examination for a graduate seminar in philosophy of physics and metaphysics. The exam lasts three hours and contains two essay questions; each should be answered in roughly 90 minutes and about 800 words, with 30 marks available per question. I want one essay question on Kurt Gödel’s argument that rotating universes containing closed timelike curves undermine the objective reality of temporal succession. The question must not be answerable by summarizing Gödel’s 1949 essay; instead, students must reason through a modified scenario that I will call local temporal isolation. In this hypothetical, an observer in a Gödel-type universe can physically traverse a closed timelike curve, but a governing physical law ensures that no records, memories, or causal traces of the loop survive—neither in the observer’s mind nor in the surrounding environment.

The exam question should ask students to determine whether this modified scenario still supports Gödel’s idealist conclusion that objective time is unreal, or whether it severs the link between closed timelike curves and the unreality of temporal passage. Specifically, the prompt should require students to: (i) reconstruct Gödel’s reasoning from global rotation and the existence of closed timelike curves to the claim that objective temporal succession is not real; (ii) analyze whether the metaphysical threat depends on the loop being causally or epistemically accessible, or merely physically traversable; (iii) evaluate whether local temporal isolation saves objective time or merely conceals the loop without resolving the underlying tension; and (iv) present and respond to at least one serious objection—such as the charge that Gödel commits a category error by inferring a general metaphysical conclusion about time from a particular cosmological model.

Please provide: (1) the exam question, clearly worded for students and without any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; (2) a model answer of approximately 600–700 words that demonstrates the required conceptual reasoning; and (3) a marking scheme that distinguishes an A-range answer (26–30 marks) from a B-range answer (22–25 marks) by naming the specific moves an excellent answer must contain—such as correctly distinguishing physical traversability from epistemic accessibility, explaining why the absence of retained traces might or might not undermine Gödel’s inference, and engaging the category-error objection with precision. The marking scheme should also list the common errors or omissions that should cost marks, for example conflating local temporal isolation with the absence of a curve altogether, treating Gödel’s conclusion as purely physical rather than metaphysical, or failing to take a clear position on whether the modified scenario supports or undermines the idealist claim. All references to Gödel’s position should be paraphrased; do not include any verbatim quotations from his texts.
