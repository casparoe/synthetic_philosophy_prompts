For my dissertation in jurisprudence, I am examining an institutional design visible in several post-authoritarian constitutional orders: courts exercise anticipatory constitutional review, issuing binding advisory opinions on draft legislation before enactment and assessing its compatibility with an unwritten “fundamental values” framework that the court itself has gradually articulated. The courts insist this is purely interpretive, not legislative, and that they remain bound by the positive constitutional text. At the same time, these courts have invalidated constitutional amendments that would have abolished judicial review altogether, reasoning that an “essential core” of the constitutional order is immune to amendment even where the text contains no eternity clause or explicit entrenchment mechanism. I want to determine what philosophy of law this double practice presupposes.

Does the combination of pre-enactment advisory review based on unwritten values and amendment-blocking necessarily commit these institutions to a Fuller-style view that law possesses an internal morality and that norms violating it fail to be law? Or can a Hartian positivist reconstruct both moves by treating the “fundamental values” and the “essential core” as especially entrenched conventional rules recognized by legal officials? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires philosophically from what it merely accommodates, and say whether the two judicial moves are consistent with each other under each reconstruction. Also identify where the institutional pattern is genuinely philosophically underdetermined—where a moral-realist natural-law reading, a conventionalist-positivist reading, and an intermediate position might all fit equally well.

Please answer in American English. In your response, please state explicitly what argument or evidence would change your assessment—for instance, whether empirical demonstration that the courts’ “essential core” reasoning is purely strategic rhetoric with no genuine normative commitment, or a precise sociological account of the rule of recognition in these jurisdictions, would cause you to revise which theory the practice presupposes. Please also flag the specific point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
