After receiving a diagnosis that clinicians disagree on—specifically, a disorder characterized by severe post-exertional symptom exacerbation and functional impairment despite the absence of consistent, objective biomarker abnormalities—I have become preoccupied with whether medicine can define health and disease without smuggling in value judgments. Write an essay on the dispute between naturalist and normativist philosophies of medicine. Examine Christopher Boorse’s biostatistical account, which defines disease as a statistically subnormal interference with species-typical biological functioning, alongside normativist alternatives such as Lennart Nordenfelt’s holistic theory and Jerome Wakefield’s harmful dysfunction analysis, both of which maintain that disease judgments necessarily involve evaluations of harm, disability, or deviation from a valued state of being. In particular, consider whether accepting normativism collapses the boundary between genuine pathology and socially disvalued biological variation, thereby threatening the legitimacy of conditions like mine that rely heavily on first-person reports of dysfunction in the absence of clear third-person markers. Conversely, assess whether naturalism can genuinely avoid normative commitments when explaining why identical biological states—such as particular inflammatory profiles—are treated as pathogenic in some social contexts and benign in others. Do not end non-committally: defend either a refined naturalism that accommodates contextual variation without normative foundations, or a constrained normativism that prevents diagnostic injustice by grounding authority in procedural rather than substantive value judgments. In constructing your argument, evaluate whether introducing an epistemic privilege clause—giving primary definitional weight to the affected individual’s reported functional impairment whenever objective biological metrics are indeterminate—strengthens or undermines the position you defend.
