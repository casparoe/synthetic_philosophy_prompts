So my roommates Sam and Jordan got into it last night over that Browning vs Goldhagen thing — you know, whether the guys in Reserve Police Battalion 101 killed because they were ordinary men in a bad situation or because they were already steeped in that eliminationist antisemitism Goldhagen talks about.

Sam was arguing the Browning side. He said the key thing is that Trapp offered them the chance to step out at Józefów and around a dozen actually did, and nobody punished them. To Sam that kills the culture argument — if deep-seated hatred was driving them, why would refusal be tolerated? He also pointed out they were middle-aged reservists with families, not ideologicial hardliners, and that peer presure and incremental escalation explain the brutality way better than some special German mindset.

Jordan came back saying that actually proves Goldhagen right. Only a tiny fraction stepped out despite explicit permission — that means the vast majority were willing even without coercion. She said Trapp offered the out precisely because he knew some might waver, but the culture did the heavy lifting so he barely needed force. Jordan also said the fact that refusal was tied to older guys or people with prior Jewish business contacts doesn't disprove culture; it just shows you need unusually strong countervailing ties to overcome the default.

Then Sam said if culture is the default, why was there any refusal at all? And why did the ones who refused face zero social sanction? Jordan said Goldhagen never claims 100% uniformity — cultures have distributions. But Sam pushed harder: he said Browning's point is that situational factors explain both the killers and the refusers — those who stepped out were the ones who hadn't yet bonded with the group or had social reasons to resist. If culture were primary, refusal should cluster by ideological dissent, not by social position.

Jordan answered that that's a false dichotomy — social position and cultural belief interact. But then she added this other thing: she thinks people misread Browning because he admits the violence got worse over time, and that desensitization happened. Jordan said that's not just 'situation,' that's the culture giving them permission to express what was already latent. Sam got annoyed and said by that logic every atrocity is culture-specific, which makes the explanation circular. He threw out a hypothetical: if another police unit in Romania had a 40% opt-out rate under similar orders, wouldn't that show the German case was about local command climate, not national culture?

Jordan said no, that'd just show Romanian culture was different, and besides, comparative studies actually show German police had unusually high compliance. Sam said that's cherry-picking — you can't claim culture explains high compliance and also explain away low compliance in other units.

They asked me to settle it. Can you go through each move, rate who's closer to right, and say what both of them are missing? Don't just pick a winner — I want to know where each argument actually lands, and whether there's a bascially different framing they haven't considered.
