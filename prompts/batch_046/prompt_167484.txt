I have been trying to work out an idea I encountered about identity and harm: if changing the conditions under which people come into existence means the particular individuals who are born are different ones, then you have not harmed anyone specific by making those conditions worse, since those exact people would never have existed otherwise. That reasoning seems clear enough for an individual deciding when to start a family, but I have begun applying it to large-scale institutional choices—say, a society deciding between a rapid-transition energy plan and a gradual one, where each path produces a different population centuries later through different economic patterns, migration flows, and settlement timelines. If the rapid plan creates serious hardship for the people alive in 2200, but those precise people only exist because the rapid plan was chosen, then it seems mistaken to say the plan “harmed” them. By the same token, comparing future welfare across the two plans looks incoherent, since there is no shared set of future individuals whose interests can be weighed against each other. That would imply that the common argument that we owe future generations a stable climate and a livable world rests on a category error: we cannot wrong people whose very existence is contingent on our policy choices. Is that extension of the reasoning valid, or does it depend on a hidden confusion about how collective, long-horizon institutional decisions relate to individual reproductive ones?
