Two friends, Ravi and Lena, have been arguing about whether the scale argument for insect welfare succeeds. Ravi notes that there may be ten quintillion insects alive at any time; even if the credence that any particular insect is sentient is very low, multiplying that epistemic probability by the population yields an enormous expected number of conscious beings, far outnumbering all vertebrates. He concludes that reducing insect harm ought therefore to be a major moral priority.

Lena answers in three moves. First, she claims Ravi is committing a category error: "probability of sentience" measures our ignorance rather than an objective frequency in nature, and one cannot generate moral obligations by multiplying ignorance by headcount. Second, Ravi replies that decision theory standardly employs subjective probabilities under uncertainty, and that the deeper issue is ontological — whether insects are the right kind of entities to bear moral status at all. Third, Lena introduces a specific conceptual claim: for an entity to be genuinely harmed, there must exist a unitary phenomenal subject, a single integrated field of experience. Insects possess distributed ganglia and lack the global neural integration seen in mammals; consequently, an insect is not one potential victim, but either no unified subject or an indeterminate cluster of micro-subjects. She argues that the scale argument tacitly assumes a one-to-one correspondence between organisms and subjects, and that without determinacy about how many subjects inhabit one body, there is no well-defined "expected harm" to compute.

Ravi counters in two moves. First, he says Lena's "unitary subject" criterion is arbitrary: applied consistently, it might withhold moral status from human infants, patients under deep anaesthesia, or split-brain individuals, which seems implausible. Second, he contends that even if Lena is correct and insects contain multiple micro-subjects rather than none, the scale argument is strengthened rather than undermined, since the number of potential subjects per organism would then increase.

Neither will budge. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: rate each of Ravi's and Lena's moves from clearly mistaken to clearly sound, say where each party is missing an important distinction, and indicate whether the scale argument can survive the loss of organism-level unity. In particular, address whether Ravi's decision-theoretic defence genuinely answers Lena's ontological objection, and identify the deeper conceptual tension both are overlooking.
