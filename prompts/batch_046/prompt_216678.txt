My mother has recently been diagnosed with a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, and I have been trying to make sense of the clinical language that frames her condition as discrete “hardware failure” rather than a gradual statistical degradation. This has led me back to Von Neumann’s late argument that the brain is not essentially a digital, binary-state machine but a statistical, analog-error-correcting system that computes through noisy thresholds and massive redundancy. That view seems largely ignored in contemporary debates about whole-brain emulation and synthetic neural replacement, where the brain is often treated as a precise circuit diagram that could in principle be copied state-for-state. Given this tension, please describe the most important implications of Von Neumann’s statistical-analog model for three issues: first, whether digital whole-brain emulation is conceptually coherent, or whether it necessarily strips away the brain’s essential error-tolerant architecture; second, whether understanding dementia as statistical degradation rather than discrete hardware breakdown changes how we should think about personal identity and the continuity of self as cognition slowly dissolves; and third, whether his view implies that any genuinely equivalent artificial cognition must itself be analog and noisy rather than deterministically precise, and what that means for the ethics of substituting synthetic substrates for failing neural tissue. I am not looking for a literature review, but for a focused assessment of what follows if we take his model seriously in these specific contexts.
