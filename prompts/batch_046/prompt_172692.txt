I spent much of last night in a prolonged, circular argument with a close friend about whether a nation can remain guilty for atrocities committed decades before most of its present citizens were born, and we kept returning to Jaspers’s postwar framework of criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt without ever agreeing on where that framework permits inheritance and where it closes the door. To settle my own grasp of the distinctions, I have drafted the following graduate seminar exam question, along with a student answer that seems to me largely competent yet possibly conceals one or two subtle missteps—the very ones my friend claimed were present and I kept missing. I ask you to grade this answer as a dispassionate instructor or TA would, without indicating whether you share the student’s conclusions.

The exam question was: Drawing on Jaspers’s postwar analysis of collective guilt, distinguish the four categories he identifies—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—and explain whether, on his view, any form of guilt can be meaningfully attributed to a nation or to generations born long after the events in question. Address specifically whether political liability extends to descendants who never held citizenship in the offending state, and whether metaphysical solidarity can be inherited across generations. Then evaluate the following proposal: a recent commentator has introduced the concept of “institutional memory guilt” (institutionelle Erinnerungsschuld), said to attach not to individual persons but to the enduring structures—archives, monuments, school curricula—through which a society preserves its past. On this account, descendants inherit the guilt structurally, not personally, by maintaining those institutions. Assess whether this proposed fifth category is compatible with Jaspers’s framework, paying close attention to how he differentiates personal guilt from collective responsibility.

The student’s answer reads as follows. Jaspers distinguishes four kinds of guilt. Criminal guilt applies to individuals who violated laws and is adjudicated by courts. Political guilt attaches to citizens as members of a state; since the state committed wrongs, its citizens share in a collective liability for the political consequences, regardless of individual involvement. Moral guilt is strictly internal—an affair of individual conscience and self-judgment that can never be distributed across a group. Metaphysical guilt expresses the solidarity of all human beings: if injustice is tolerated anywhere, all share in the failure, and this burden is inherited by subsequent generations who remain part of the same national community that once failed. Consequently, a nation cannot be criminally guilty in the collective sense, because courts judge persons, not abstractions. Political guilt is genuinely collective, but it belongs only to those who were actually citizens of the state in question; descendants born afterward do not carry it, since they were never members of that political body. Moral guilt is non-transferable by definition. Metaphysical guilt, however, does pass across generations, not through legal or political membership, but through the continuity of the national community as a human collective that remains bound to its past failures. Regarding the proposed fifth category, “institutional memory guilt,” which attaches to archives, monuments, and curricula rather than to persons, this extension should be rejected. Jaspers’s framework already accommodates collective dimensions through political guilt, and he maintains a rigorous distinction between Schuld—proper guilt, which is always personal and applies only in the criminal and moral registers—and Verantwortung, the structural responsibility that covers political and institutional continuity. The proposal therefore confuses an administrative inheritance of records with the kind of personal liability that Jaspers restricts to individual conscience.
