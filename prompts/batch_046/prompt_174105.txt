I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether honoring sunk costs is ever rational, and I have tried to formalize our exchange into a chain that now strikes me as suspicious: it seems to prove that rational agents cannot have enduring commitments at all. Here is the reconstruction. (1) Rational choice at any time t depends solely on the causal consequences of actions available at t, weighted by their probabilities and values; past expenditures are causally inert with respect to present options. (2) A sunk cost is precisely an expenditure that cannot be recovered or altered by present choice, so it carries zero weight in the forward-looking calculus. (3) Therefore, if a decision is influenced by a sunk cost—say, finishing a failing research program because “I have already invested a decade in it”—that influence is an irrational deviation from the forward-looking calculus. (4) But this applies not only to wasteful escalation but also to any commitment whose stability depends on past investment: remaining in a long marriage partly because of shared history, continuing an artistic practice partly because of years of embodied training, or honoring a political promise partly because of past solidarity. (5) In each case, the agent gives normative weight to past expenditure (time, loyalty, effort) that does not change the marginal utility of the future options taken in isolation. (6) So if (1)–(3) are correct, then all such commitments are irrational; a rational agent must treat every decision point as a fresh optimization with no diachronic constraint linking present choice to past investment. (7) Yet that conclusion seems to destroy the possibility of long-term projects, trust, or narrative identity—things that appear not merely permissible but central to rational agency. (8) So either (1) is false, or there is a hidden distinction between “wasteful escalation” and “honoring commitment” that (4) illegitimately collapses.

My friend insists the flaw lies in (4): he says commitments are sustained not by sunk costs but by independent future values (love, artistic fulfillment, integrity), and that mentioning past investment is merely rhetorical packaging for those forward-looking reasons. I want to keep (1) if I can, because abandoning pure forward-looking rationality sounds like opening the door to superstition or self-deception. But I am unsure whether (4) really commits the fallacy of treating commitment-reasons as sunk-cost reasons, or whether there is a more subtle equivocation in (3) between causal influence and normative justification—perhaps past investment can alter the strategic structure of future options through commitment-device effects without entering the utility calculus as a direct weight. Can you tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs? If the argument is actually valid and the real question is only which plausible premise is least costly to abandon, say so explicitly: I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (6) or (7), but I need to know exactly what I am paying—conceptually and in terms of diachronic coherence—to do that.
