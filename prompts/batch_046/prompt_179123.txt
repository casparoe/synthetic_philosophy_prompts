I have been working on a short essay about Samuel Scheffler's conjecture regarding the collective afterlife—the view that human extinction would be bad for present people in ways that outstrip the deaths it involves, because so many of our projects derive their significance from an assumed continuity of human life. Rather than simply rehearsing this, I want to examine a specific extension: that the harm of extinction lies not only in the loss of future welfare or in psychological disappointment, but in the dissolution of what I call intergenerational practice-structures—activities like long-term scientific inquiry, cumulative artistic tradition, or moral reform that are intelligible only as contributions to an unfinished collective project. On this view, if humanity were to end, present agents would not merely lose beneficiaries; they would be retroactively transformed from participants in an open-ended narrative into performers of isolated, terminal gestures.

My concern is that this distinction may not hold up. Critics might argue that the narrative frame I describe is just a phenomenological gloss on the expectation that future people will exist and flourish, which would reduce my claim to the familiar future-welfare argument rather than establishing a distinct axiological category. I am also unsure whether this framework implicitly privileges human-specific, historically extended goods without justifying that privilege, and whether it can accommodate apparently atemporal achievements—certain mathematical insights or contemplative states—that seem to retain their value regardless of posterity. Could you comment on the coherence of this extension, particularly on whether intergenerational practice-structures constitute a genuinely separate source of value? I would especially value your assessment of whether the argument avoids collapsing into utilitarian concerns about future happiness, whether it unfairly marginalizes non-historical goods, and whether there is an undefended psychological premise about how agents conceptualize their own activities.
