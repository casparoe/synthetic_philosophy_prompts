As a novelist preparing a historical narrative set among the Jesuit theologians of Salamanca in the 1580s, I have been studying Luis de Molina's doctrine of middle knowledge—scientia media—to understand how a thinker of that period could reconcile exhaustive divine foreknowledge with genuinely free human agency. I have encountered a passage, paraphrased from Molina's Concordia, in which he maintains that God possesses, prior to any creative decree and independent of causal determination, knowledge not only of what free creatures actually do, but of what they would freely do in circumstances that never come to pass. These counterfactuals, he claims, are contingent truths—true without being necessary—yet they are not made true by God's will, since they belong to the second logical moment of divine knowledge, between natural knowledge and free knowledge.

My difficulty is conceptual rather than textual. Molina's architecture distinguishes three moments: natural knowledge of necessary truths; middle knowledge of counterfactuals of freedom; and free knowledge of the actual world God chooses to create. But I am unsure whether this ordering genuinely preserves libertarian freedom or merely relocates determinism across modal space. If every counterfactual circumstance carries a fixed truth-value about what the creature would freely choose, and if God surveys these values before actualizing any world, then the creature's freedom in those unactualized branches appears to be a deterministic feature of the circumstance rather than an open possibility. In other words, has Molina constructed a framework in which freedom is observed but never genuinely open?

This concern is sharpened by an analogy drawn from narrative craft. In constructing a novel, I treat my characters' choices as branching across counterfactual scenarios—some realized in the plot, others left as unrealized possibilities—yet I, as author, determine which branch is actualized. Molina seems to place God in an analogous position: surveying all possible worlds through middle knowledge, then selecting one through free knowledge. But this raises a question about modal determinacy. Does Molina require that every branch have a determinate truth-value prior to God's creative decision, in the way a novelist requires internal consistency among plot branches? If so, does this import a kind of modal realism or narrative determinism foreign to Molina's own metaphysics? Or does he maintain that the counterfactual truths depend on the creature's free will in such a way that they are not fixed independently of it, even for God?

Please help me understand the passage and its conceptual framework. Specifically, explain how Molina avoids collapsing middle knowledge into either Thomist foreknowledge—which seems to make the future necessary by grounding it in God's eternal present—or into an arbitrary voluntarism in which God simply decides what the counterfactuals are. Address whether my analogy to narrative branching illuminates or distorts Molina's position, particularly regarding whether counterfactual freedom requires an indeterminacy that is compatible with being known. Finally, please indicate explicitly what argument or textual discovery would cause you to revise your reading: for instance, whether a closer examination of Molina's distinction among the three moments of knowledge, or a Thomist objection that middle knowledge presupposes the very freedom it attempts to preserve, would overturn your interpretation, and point out where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
