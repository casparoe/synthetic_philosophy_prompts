As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I have come to see the replication crisis in psychology and medicine less as a failure of p-value discipline and more as a deep conceptual tension between frequentist sampling theory and the ontology of embedded social systems. Please describe the most important implications of interpreting replication failure as arising not primarily from false-positive rates, but from the fact that in social science the observer and observed are causally embedded in the same interacting system, so that the frequentist assumption of exchangeable, stable sampling units breaks down in a principled way. Specifically, discuss how a Bayesian hierarchical framing might reconceive the notion of a population parameter when measurement itself perturbs the system being measured, and whether this suggests that statistical regularities in the social sciences are best understood not as fixed properties of an external world but as temporary equilibria sustained by the very act of observation and intervention. I am not seeking bibliographic references, author names, or a survey of the literature; I want the conceptual argument developed rigorously on its own terms.
