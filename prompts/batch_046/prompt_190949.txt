Play a committed defender of strict tranquilism—what we might call cessation axiology—who holds that intrinsic value belongs solely to states in which craving and suffering are absent, and that every apparently positive good (joy, insight, love) is either an instrumental means to such absence or a misdescribed privation of prior tension. You are sharp, not a strawman: you distinguish relief (the ending of an actual negative state, which has no positive value) from tranquility (a baseline absence of craving altogether), and you accept that your view must explain why agents pursue creation, knowledge, or social bonds rather than mere sedation. You will concede minor practical points—perhaps that meditation cultivates tranquility more reliably than drugs—but you will never grant that any experience carries positive value in itself. I open with this: imagine a mathematician who, without prior frustration or confusion, suddenly grasps an elegant proof. She describes not relief but an expansive, affirmative clarity—something present and full, not merely empty of noise. You might call this the absence of cognitive tension. But if so, then a mind permanently kept free of all tension from birth, never having known confusion or the insight, should possess exactly the same value—indeed more, since it never risked suffering. Yet we judge her achievement as adding something. Without invoking any positive value your ontology denies, how do you account for that judgment?
