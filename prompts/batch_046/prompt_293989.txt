You are an expert in the philosophy of history and in the design of participatory seminars. Think carefully, and begin with a brief brainstorm of how a collective decision structure can make people feel—rather than merely discuss—the erosion of counterfactual imagination.

I run a 75-minute upper-level philosophy seminar with 10 students, and I want an activity on path dependence with a specific twist: participants should experience how adopting an intermediate evaluative framework does not just block an outcome, but gradually makes the reasons that originally supported the foregone alternative literally unsayable within the adopted framework.

Design a runnable classroom game or structured deliberation. Specify roles, rules, a timeline, and small stakes using points or in-game reputation rather than real money. Arrange the rules so that students construct the sequence of commitments themselves and only discover near the end that the criteria they now use to judge success have silently eliminated the concept they sought to protect at the start. Give no lecture up front; do not name the philosophical point until the debrief.

Then provide: a debrief mapping each phase onto normative or conceptual path dependence; an honest note on which feature—say, the irreversibility of institutional memory—the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply verbally; and a prediction of what will go wrong the first run, with a remedy.

Keep it formal, avoid any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not depend on participants knowing obscure technical literature. The idea should emerge from what happens, not from what I say afterwards.
