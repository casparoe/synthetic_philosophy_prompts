I'm preparing a ninety-minute session for a graduate seminar on American pragmatism—twelve students—who have read Dewey's The Public and Its Problems and Democracy and Education but keep reducing his experimentalism to familiar slogans about participatory voting or civic education. I want them to feel, rather than hear, the specific claim that democracy is a mode of cooperative social inquiry in which a "public" is not a pre-given demographic group but an emergent body constituted by recognizing the indirect, often invisible consequences of joint action. To that end, I'm looking for a structured simulation or deliberation with assigned roles and small real stakes—perhaps points, privileges, or binding recommendations for a fictional but realistic local case—so that participants must make collective choices while blind to who is actually affected.

Please design an activity centered on what we might call an epistemic boundary problem: the rules should allow participants to see and vote on direct, local effects of a shared decision (for example, allocating a community resource or setting a local policy), but the indirect consequences should fall on a second group that is initially hidden from them. Only after one or two rounds of binding choice should the hidden affected parties be revealed, forcing the group to reconstruct not just the decision but the very boundary of who counts as the democratic public for this issue. The debrief should map specific moments—initial confidence in the vote, the shock of revelation, the attempt to revise the decision—onto Dewey's distinctions between direct and indirect consequences, the formation of a public through inquiry rather than prior identity, and the experimental reconstruction of shared conditions. Include a timeline, the roles or rules that create the coordination failure, what will predictably go wrong when the activity runs, and how to handle it; also be frank about which feature of Dewey's view—say, the long temporal continuity of inquiry or the analogical role of scientific method in social problem-solving—the simulation cannot reproduce and you must supply in closing remarks. The group should come away seeing that democratic failure, on this reading, is often not disagreement among visible stakeholders but a failure to perceive the hidden scope of joint action in the first place.
