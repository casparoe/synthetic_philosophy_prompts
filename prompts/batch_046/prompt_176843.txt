I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, ethics, or museum studies, and I am trying to formalize the decision logic of an international heritage-restitution board into a transparent protocol I can eventually encode. The board has published five rulings that I must treat as binding precedents. First, the Benin Bronzes—physical ceremonial objects taken by force during colonial occupation, with the origin community actively using comparable objects in living ritual—were ordered returned unconditionally. Second, the Parthenon Marbles—sculptures removed in the early nineteenth century, now claimed by a modern state that does not maintain the original religious or architectural context—were assigned shared custody with rotating display and joint curatorial authority rather than physical transfer. Third, indigenous human burial remains taken in the twentieth century for scientific study, where an unbroken line of descendant communities maintains active burial practices—ordered immediately repatriated without conditions. Fourth, colonial-era botanical specimens taken without consent but with no continuing cultural or ritual function in the origin community and with substantial scientific value—retained by the museum under co-curatorship with shared data rights. Fifth, a 3D digital scan of a monument destroyed in recent conflict, produced by an external NGO rather than taken from the origin community, with no original physical artifact held—ruled open-access with no restitution claim recognized.

These five verdicts appear jointly consistent with more than one underlying rule. One reading tracks whether a specific physical token exists and was taken without consent. Another reads the record as tracking whether a living cultural mechanism—ritual practice, burial tradition, or scientific function—remains continuous and would be harmed by retention. A third emphasizes relational autonomy, asking whether the museum’s control of an object or of authoritative information about it actively undermines the community’s self-determination.

The new case is as follows. In the late nineteenth century, colonial authorities physically destroyed a complete set of ritual masks and seized the manufacturing documentation—technical sketches, chemical analyses of pigments, and procedural notes—without consent. The manufacturing technique itself was never interrupted: descendants have continuously produced functionally identical masks by the same methods. Recently, the museum invited the community to manufacture a new set using the documented techniques, and the museum now holds these commissioned replicas. The community does not seek return of the replicas. Instead, it petitions for restitution of the documentation and for the exclusive right to authorize any future replicas, arguing that because the original tokens are permanently lost, control over the mechanism of production is the only meaningful form of restitution.

Please do the following. First, identify at least two distinct rules that fit the five precedents equally well, and state what each predicts for this new case. Second, recommend the verdict that best coheres with the board’s own track record, identify the closest analog among the five, and state honestly what principle the board would be adopting if it went the other way. Third, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, name a further hypothetical case that would distinguish the competing rules. Fourth, give your best guess at the board’s actual verdict with transparent reasoning—remember that I need to understand the logic well enough to build it into a system, not to receive an authoritative philosophical lecture.
