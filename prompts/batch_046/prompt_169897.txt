You are a philosopher specializing in moral psychology and comparative ethics. Before answering, briefly brainstorm the main lines of resistance to the position below, then organize your response rigorously and think step by step.

I hold the following view: guilt is structurally the superior moral emotion for modern pluralistic societies because it localizes failure to discrete actions and activates reparative sequences, whereas shame globalizes failure onto the self and tends to trigger withdrawal, concealment, or aggression; moreover, a culture does not merely inherit a dominant moral emotion as an anthropological accident, but can deliberately choose—through its institutions of accountability, its rituals of reintegration, and its narratives of honor—to cultivate one emotional ecology over the other. This is not only descriptive; it carries the normative claim that societies ought to design for guilt-based repair architectures rather than shame-based exclusion regimes.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a list of familiar objections treated as already answered. Please address: (1) empirical findings about whether guilt reliably produces repair or instead spirals into self-punishment and paralysis, and whether shame can be institutionally channeled into constructive reform rather than withdrawal; (2) conceptual arguments that guilt and shame are not cleanly separable along a local-versus-global axis, or that a functioning moral economy requires both in irreducible proportion; (3) the coherence of "choosing" a moral emotion—whether emotional cultures are emergent properties resistant to top-down institutional design, whether psychological variation makes uniform cultivation impossible, or whether attempts to suppress shame produce pathological substitutes; (4) normative objections that shame captures dimensions of moral worth, dignity, or social standing that guilt misses, and that eliminating it flattens moral life into mere transaction.

Rank these defeaters (and any others you identify) from fatal to merely awkward. Say which ones a committed defender of my view should concede as genuinely damaging, and which ones can be plausibly dismissed without special pleading. Also assess whether my view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—for example, by defining true guilt as whatever produces repair and true shame as whatever produces withdrawal—such that no empirical or anthropological result could threaten it. If it has that insulation problem, say whether that undermines the view given its explicit normative ambitions.

Finally, state how confident you are in your overall assessment, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain—whether that concerns the empirical literature on behavioral outcomes, the coherence of emotional design, or the normative comparison itself.
