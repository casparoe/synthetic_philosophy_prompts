The curators want visitors to tweak tiny variables—like whether a blacksmith had enough iron nails in 1847, or if a messenger horse threw a shoe—to run simulations showing how those micro-events either cascade into the big 1852 fire and rail rerouting, or don't. They say it's about teaching chaos theory and historical contingency; half the board loves it, the other half thinks its pseudohistorical storytelling that confuses deterministic chaos in weather models with agent-driven social change, and they're worried we're funding just-so stories with a physics gloss.

We vote Thursday and I need to circulate a short written opinion recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions—something concrete with a time frame if it applies. The memo should take the strongest objection seriously: specifically the worry that applying Lorenz-style sensitive dependence to social history commits a category mistake, since human agents adapt and correct errors in ways atmospheric particles can't, so the 'for want of a nail' framing might be ontologically misleading even when it's narratively catchy. Don't just restate our grant guidelines about 'educational value'; actually engage whether the exhibit's philosophical framing holds up, especially the distinction between structural contingency and what the proposal calls 'path-dependent institutional drift.' Also, I'm drafting this without web access, so please don't ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations—just give me the reasoning and a clear bottom line I can defend in the meeting.
