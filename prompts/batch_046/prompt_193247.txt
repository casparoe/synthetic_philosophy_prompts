I am preparing the syllabus for a graduate seminar on free will and penal theory, and a colleague circulated an informal summary of the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on just deserts and punishment. Before I assign the original essays, I want to verify whether this characterization is fair enough to present to the class. The summary claims that both philosophers accept determinism but diverge sharply on blame: Dennett is said to defend a position called "compatibilist desert," according to which causal determination is compatible with robust retributive punishment so long as the agent exercises diachronic, self-forming control over her character; Caruso is said to reject all forms of desert, arguing that because determinism eliminates ultimate authorship, offenders should be treated on a public-health model—deterrence and rehabilitation only—much as we quarantine carriers of a dangerous pathogen. The summary concludes that Dennett therefore saves punishment by redefining freedom downward, while Caruso eliminates moral blame altogether and reduces the criminal to a risk profile.

I am particularly concerned about three possible distortions. First, does the summary conflate Dennett's defense of moral responsibility with an endorsement of retributive justice, or does Dennett in fact reject basic desert and support only forward-looking justifications? Second, does Caruso really reject every reactive attitude, or does his skepticism target only a specific metaphysical conception of basic desert while leaving room for non-retributive responses? Third, the summary introduces a distinction between diachronic agency and synchronic choice that seems to do important work for Dennett, yet it never explains why synchronic choice is not also required for punishment; is this omission a subtle misrepresentation of Dennett's view, or a genuine gap in his account? Additionally, the summary implies that if we discovered a reliable neural predictor of criminal behavior—say, a detectable 30-second pre-conscious signature—this empirical finding would strengthen Caruso's case by showing that actions are mechanistically determined, but I wonder whether such a discovery would actually affect the philosophical debate about desert at all, or whether it merely changes the epistemic conditions without touching the metaphysics. Could you audit the summary point by point, indicating which claims are accurate, which are subtle misrepresentations—whether by conflation, overstatement, or the importation of an unstated empirical premise—and whether its final comparison of the two philosophers survives correction? I am not asking for a defense of either view, but for an accuracy audit that my students could rely on.
