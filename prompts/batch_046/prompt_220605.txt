Following a lengthy argument with a friend about whether social construction names a single mechanism or a family of unrelated phenomena, I have tried to articulate my own verdicts across several cases. I judge that race is socially constructed: its categories have shifted historically, they do not map onto stable biological divisions, and they acquire causal efficacy primarily through institutional enforcement rather than natural kindhood. I also judge that gender is socially constructed, but in a different register—the binary is institutionally enforced, yet gendered phenomenology does not seem reducible to social role, so treating gender as purely constructed feels incomplete. Money, I think, is constituted by collective intentionality: paper currency has value because a community treats it as having value. By contrast, cryptocurrency lacks comparable institutional backing; its value appears sustained by speculative belief and network effects, making it more like a collectively maintained fiction than a robust social fact. When botanical knowledge leads a community to distinguish elms from beeches, I take this as refining an existing kind rather than reconstructing it. But when a corporation invents a role—say, Chief Vibe Officer—and embeds it in a hierarchy with real decision-making authority, that kind seems generated ex nihilo in a way racial categories are not. Is there one coherent principle—perhaps involving collective intentionality, institutional backing, or causal history—that unifies these five verdicts? If the principle requires me to abandon one judgment, which should it be? And what would it predict about a novel case: AI-driven behavioral classifiers that generate personality kinds, which users subsequently internalize and perform, thereby making the classification self-fulfilling?
