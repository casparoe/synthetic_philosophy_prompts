I am a software engineer without formal philosophical training. At a discussion group, two other participants—neither of whom is me—debated the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism. I am recounting their exchange without taking sides. Please adjudicate it move by move.

The discussion proceeded in four moves.

Move 1: The first speaker argued that the objection is essentially the moral equivalent of process isolation in an operating system. Each person is an independent process with its own protected memory space; utilitarianism wrongly permits terminating one process if that improves aggregate cluster performance. Treating one individual’s severe harm as canceled out by distributed minor benefits to others, he said, is analogous to a scheduler sacrificing a single server for system-wide throughput.

Move 2: The second speaker replied that this mischaracterizes the aggregation. Utilitarianism assigns equal weight to each person’s welfare; it does not merge subjects into a collective entity. She compared it to a load balancer that gives every server equal priority—the arithmetic is collective, but the entities remain fully separate. The objection, she argued, confuses being affected by a global decision with being ontologically fused into a shared account.

Move 3: The first speaker pushed back that the load-balancer analogy actually strengthens his point. A scheduler can indeed shut down one server to preserve ten others, treating that server’s integrity as subordinate to total throughput. The separateness of persons, he said, requires moral boundaries to function like strict process isolation: you cannot justify harming one individual by citing benefits to others, regardless of weighting, because welfare is not deposited into a shared pool.

Move 4: The second speaker answered that this makes the objection self-defeating. Any collective practice—vaccination mandates, emergency triage, progressive taxation—imposes burdens on some for benefits to others. If separateness forbids all cross-person trade-offs, it rules out standard moral arrangements. She then introduced a sharper framing: consider distributed agents that share hidden-layer representations but maintain separate reward signals and action spaces. If two agents have partially overlapping internal states yet distinct outputs, does optimizing globally across both violate separateness? Or does the shared substrate justify cross-agent aggregation? She argued that unless the first speaker can specify where the boundary lies in such partially merged architectures, his appeal to process isolation relies on an all-or-nothing view of personal identity that does not match actual cognitive structures.

Please rate each of the four moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then identify, for the core philosophical issue, which speaker is closer to being right and on which specific point. Finally, note what—if anything—both parties are missing or failing to distinguish clearly.
