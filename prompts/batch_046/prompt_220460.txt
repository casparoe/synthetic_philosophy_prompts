Adam Smith defines sympathy not as pity but as an imaginative exchange in which we place ourselves in another's situation and experience a corresponding emotion; this differs from compassion because it applies equally to joy and to sorrow. The impartial spectator is an internalized observer—Smith describes him as the man within the breast—who approves or disapproves of our conduct by comparing our feelings to what a neutral, well-informed party would feel in the same circumstances. Regarding active and passive sentiments: for passive sentiments like grief, the spectator checks only whether the intensity of our feeling is proportionate to the cause, so that excessive mourning is improper. On the role of self-love, Smith clearly rejects the view that self-interest corrupts moral judgment. Self-love is the fundamental drive that leads us to seek social approval; without it, we would not care about the spectator's judgment, and sympathy would remain a private feeling with no public force. Thus self-love and sympathy operate as complementary mechanisms: sympathy provides the content of moral norms, while self-love supplies the motivation to adhere to them.
