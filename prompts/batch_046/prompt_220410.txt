You serve as the chair of a university interdisciplinary research funding committee. A proposal has reached your desk requesting a five-year grant to establish a Many-Sided Inquiry Lab, led jointly by a philosopher of science, a climate modeler, and a cognitive neuroscientist. The investigators propose to adopt Jain anekāntavāda as an explicit methodological framework. Under their protocol, every funded project must simultaneously maintain at least three formally contradictory theoretical models of its target phenomenon—treating each as a partial truth (naya) rather than resolving them into synthesis—while using syādvāda (conditional, qualified predication) to withhold absolute commitment to any single account. They argue that reality is multifaceted (anekānta) and that forcing premature coherence suppresses legitimate perspectives, but they do not seek exemption from peer review; instead, they ask that reviewers evaluate whether contradictory models are maintained with rigor rather than whether they have been eliminated.

Half the committee sees this as a necessary corrective to scientific imperialism; the other half regards it as an abandonment of parsimony and falsifiability, reducing research to curated disagreement. Your task is to draft the committee's formal decision memo—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—with a clear, actionable bottom line and reasoning the investigators could actually respond to. The memo should treat anekāntavāda with philosophical precision: explain exactly what it commits the lab to, distinguish it from mere relativism or contradiction for its own sake, and present honestly the strongest consideration on the side that loses your recommendation. Write in polished American English. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or references to specific texts.
