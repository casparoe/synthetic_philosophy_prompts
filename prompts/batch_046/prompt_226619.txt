Path dependence is often glossed as historical lock-in, where an early contingent event rigidly constrains all subsequent development along a single trajectory. If that gloss is accurate, then any macro-historical outcome that eventually arrives across societies with radically different initial conditions cannot be genuinely path-dependent, since its occurrence was overdetermined rather than locked in by any particular branch. Industrialization, for example, emerged in Britain through enclosure and coal, in Japan through state-led modernization after the Meiji Restoration, and in the United States through frontier expansion and capital accumulation; because the endpoint was reached via such divergent institutional routes, it seems to carry a near-zero counterfactual divergence coefficient—flipping any single early event does not appear to redirect the macro-outcome. By contrast, the QWERTY keyboard layout persists despite being ergonomically inferior, and its survival looks highly sensitive to the precise commercial timing of Remington's adoption, suggesting a high divergence coefficient. This yields an uncomfortable asymmetry: path dependence seems to apply only to arbitrary conventions lacking functional importance, while structurally transformative processes appear immune to it. Is path dependence therefore just a taxonomy of trivial historical accidents? Or is there a principled distinction—perhaps drawing on institutional complementarity, network externalities, or increasing returns—that can explain why some domains exhibit deep lock-in even when functionally equivalent alternatives exist, without requiring that every macro-outcome be predetermined? And if such a distinction exists, how should it guide our judgment about whether democratic consolidation in post-authoritarian regimes is genuinely path-dependent, or merely converging on an inevitable attractor?
