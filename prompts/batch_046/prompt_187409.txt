I've been trying to formalize what it takes for a long-run predictive theory to be genuinely falsified rather than merely postponed, using Malthus as the canonical case. Here is the argument my study group walked into, and it seems to collapse into an immunization strategy that proves far too much.

(1) Malthus's core claim is structural: population tends toward geometric growth, subsistence toward arithmetic growth, so positive checks must eventually operate if growth outruns food supply.
(2) From 1800 through the present, global population grew roughly exponentially, yet per-capita food availability also rose, because technological change consistently outpaced Malthusian pressure.
(3) So by any reasonable verification horizon, the predicted positive checks have not occurred, which should count as clear falsification.
(4) But defenders rescue the theory with a right-but-early move: the mechanism has not been eliminated, only masked by temporary technological buffers that must eventually saturate.
(5) If that rescue is legitimate, then for any long-run theory predicting outcome O by horizon H, a failure at H can always be explained by invoking temporary countervailing forces and pushing H further out.
(6) Therefore no long-run predictive theory can ever be conclusively falsified; every apparent counterexample is reducible to a verification delay.
(7) Yet that proves too much — we want to maintain that some long-range research programs are genuinely dead without being able to claim they were merely delayed.

I want to keep (1) intact and preserve the intuition behind (4), that a mechanism can outlast temporary buffers. But I cannot locate the exact step that turns that local, mechanistic rescue into a universal license to immunize any theory. Where does the argument slip, and what would rejecting that step cost for keeping Malthus defensible without opening the floodgates?
