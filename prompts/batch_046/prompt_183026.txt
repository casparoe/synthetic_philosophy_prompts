Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, rhyme scheme A B A, with two refrains that alternate through the stanzas and finally converge in the closing couplet—on moral progress understood as an expanding circle of ethical concern, but frame the theme not as triumphant universalism but as an unresolved conceptual tension. Use one refrain, appearing at lines 1, 6, 12, and 18, to articulate the inclusion thesis: that the capacity to suffer or to hold interests generates moral standing regardless of species, nation, or generation, and that any boundary drawn around the circle is therefore arbitrary. Use the second refrain, appearing at lines 3, 9, 15, and 19, to articulate the dilution or weight thesis: that as the circle widens to include more beings, the intensity and action-guiding force of obligation to each included being must diminish, and that without some unstated limit the view risks collapsing into an undifferentiated sympathy that cannot direct actual choice. Between these refrains, build concrete imagery rather than abstract moral vocabulary: a boundary line redrawn in wet sand that the tide erases farther out; ripples in water whose outer rings carry less energy; a census that grows without ever closing. The form should do the philosophical work—the refrains must keep returning and passing each other without synthesis, and the final quatrain should place them side by side without resolving them, leaving the reader with the same tension that critics raise when they ask whether expanding-circle ethics secretly relies on an unstated boundary or dissolves into demandingness, especially once the possibility of infinitely many moral patients is taken seriously. Make the framing precise enough that someone familiar with Peter Singer's expanding-circle argument, with the standard objection about moral dilution and boundary arbitrariness, and with the problem of infinite moral patients recognizes the conceptual stakes rather than merely the label. After the poem, provide a brief reflective note that states how confident you are in the framing—particularly whether the dilution objection genuinely undermines the expanding-circle view or only complicates it—singles out the single point about which you are least certain, and indicates where the poem takes liberties with the philosophical position or relies on a contested interpretation of it.
