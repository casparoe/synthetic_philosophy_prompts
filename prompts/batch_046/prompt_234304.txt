I hold that the silences in official archives are not passive omissions but structurally productive forces: they do not merely fail to record subaltern voices, but actively generate the epistemic conditions under which those voices become historically unintelligible on their own terms, so that any counter-archival attempt at recovery necessarily translates them into the conceptual framework of the archive itself. What would genuinely count against this? I want the defeaters mapped by decisiveness: empirical cases in which recovery of a silenced voice occurs without conceptual distortion; theoretical arguments that oral, performative, or material traditions can maintain epistemic independence from textual framing; methodological results from ethnography or archaeology showing non-archival preservation maintaining original conceptual structures intact; and any formal result showing that structural absence cannot exercise this kind of productive, framing effect. For each category, say whether a committed defender of the archival-productivity view would concede damage or dismiss it as a category error, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. I also want to know whether the position makes any risky empirical prediction that distinguishes it from the weaker claim that archives are merely biased and incomplete, or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if the latter, whether that insulation is a vice for a thesis about historical knowledge or an unavoidable feature of it.
