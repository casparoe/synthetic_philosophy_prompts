I'm reconstructing Berkeley's economic philosophy for a chapter on early modern theories of value, and I've settled on an interpretation of his monetary writings—the Querist essays and related papers on Irish reform—that I want to stress-test for internal coherence before I finalize the argument. My current reading holds four commitments: first, that Berkeley regards money as nothing but a conventional counter or ticket, lacking intrinsic value and functioning solely to circulate real goods and labor; second, that he therefore assigns the state a monopoly over currency issuance, with paper notes secured against tangible national assets rather than left to private banking, in order to preserve public confidence; third, that because value is purely conventional, the state may expand the money supply in proportion to economic growth without risk of inflation, provided the legal-tender framework is maintained; and fourth, that individual hoarding of coin is economically harmless and morally permissible, since withdrawing tokens from circulation does not diminish the underlying real wealth of goods and labor.

Before I defend this package, I need to know whether it actually hangs together, or whether I'm importing modern state-theoretic assumptions that Berkeley wouldn't recognize. In particular, is there a genuine tension between the state-monopoly claim in the second commitment and the hoarding-permission claim in the fourth—if circulation is a public good secured by state control, why should private withdrawal be neutral? Or is the conflict between the first and second commitments—if money is purely conventional, why does it require tangible asset-backing—only apparent, dissolving once we see that convention for Berkeley still requires institutional trust anchored in real property? More seriously, does the third commitment misread his actual anxiety about over-issuance, and if so, which of the four is the least defensible on Berkeley's own terms? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, identify the apparent conflict that resolves on closer inspection, and specify the least costly revision—whether giving up the hoarding claim, qualifying the inflation immunity thesis, or reconceiving the state-back relation.
