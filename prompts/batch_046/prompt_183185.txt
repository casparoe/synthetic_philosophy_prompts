I am a student in an advanced undergraduate course on normative ethics, and we have spent the past several weeks on the doctrine of double effect and its relationship to trolley-style dilemmas. After working through the standard literature, I find myself holding a cluster of intuitive verdicts that do not obviously cohere, and I want to know whether reflective equilibrium is achievable here or whether one of my judgments must be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. In the standard switch scenario—diverting a runaway trolley onto a side track where one person will be killed rather than five—I judge the action permissible. In the footbridge scenario—pushing a large stranger onto the tracks to stop the trolley—I judge it impermissible. In the loop variant, where diverting the trolley onto a loop track kills one person but that same person's body stops the trolley from returning to kill the five, I also judge the diversion permissible. Killing one healthy patient to harvest organs for five dying patients strikes me as impermissible, despite the identical numerical trade-off with the switch case.

I have also been considering institutional analogues, and my verdicts there seem to pull in opposite directions. First, a public-health authority approves a drug that saves thousands but will cause a fatal adverse reaction in roughly 0.01% of users, distributed randomly; the harm is foreseen but neither intended nor causally instrumental to the therapeutic benefit. I judge this permissible. Second, a municipal traffic redesign reduces city-wide fatalities by removing pedestrian crossings in a specific low-income district; the increased deaths there are statistically predictable, but they are not the mechanism by which overall fatalities drop—they are a distributive side-effect of the system's efficiency. Despite the structural parallel to the drug case—foreseen harm, unintended, not causally used—I find this redesign impermissible. Third, a rapid carbon-tax policy causes severe economic harm and some mortality in coal-dependent regions; I judge the policy permissible only if that suffering is genuinely a side-effect of emission reduction, not if the suffering itself functions as the causal lever that drives the transition.

My request is for a small set of precisely stated candidate principles—perhaps refined versions of double effect with explicit conditions on causal structure, intentional object, and distributive pattern—that can accommodate as many of these verdicts as possible. For each principle, identify the clause that explains each case, state its prediction for an unconsidered scenario—specifically, an autonomous-vehicle crash-avoidance algorithm that minimizes total casualties by exploiting a known demographic correlation in impact severity (not by targeting anyone, but by treating the correlation as part of its optimization function)—and state honestly which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate. If saving every verdict requires an ad hoc complication, say so and defend the simpler principle that forces me to drop one intuition, explaining whether the loop case should collapse into the footbridge case, whether my institutional judgments undermine the personal-action distinction, or whether my discomfort with the traffic redesign reveals an unstated fairness condition that should be built into the principle rather than treated as an outlier. I do not need citations or a bibliography; I need the principle itself, its costs, and its verdict on the algorithmic case.
