A comparative political theorist has offered the following judgments on whether authoritarian regimes can claim legitimacy through prosperity and social order alone. Singapore’s one-party state, with broad-based housing and education delivery, low corruption, and a rule-bound bureaucracy but limited electoral competition, succeeds in its performance-justification claim. China’s post-reform developmental state succeeds as well: mass poverty reduction, infrastructural order, and bureaucratic adaptation justify the absence of multiparty politics, though she notes the fragility introduced by personalized leadership. By contrast, a resource-rich monarchy that delivers high GDP per capita and internal security but concentrates wealth in a narrow citizenry and employs no institutionalized welfare delivery fails the claim. So too does a hypothetical benevolent autocracy that achieves rapid industrial growth and perfect internal security through a hereditary ruler with no bureaucratic or legal constraints on arbitrary power and no formal succession mechanism.

Next case: a centralized state achieves rapid, broad-based industrialization and universal basic services through a technically competent, meritocratic bureaucracy. It maintains an independent judiciary for commercial and civil disputes and has formal, if non-electoral, leadership succession rules. Internal order, however, depends substantially on predictive digital surveillance and preemptive policing. Moreover, roughly seventy percent of state revenue derives from a single state-controlled mineral export, and independent labor organizing remains prohibited.

Predict whether she will say this regime’s performance-justification claim succeeds. Identify the two or three implicit rules that best fit her earlier verdicts—distinguishing, for instance, between distributive universalism, institutional constraint on arbitrary power, and productive-developmental path dependence—and say which rule she is most likely running. Then specify the single further case that would best distinguish between the leading candidates.
