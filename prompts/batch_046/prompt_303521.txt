Construct a decision scenario engineered to isolate whether special moral claims among compatriots rest on shared institutional membership rather than on shared national identity, with the two bases coming apart in a realistic, ordinary-life setting. The protagonist should hold dual status—naturalized institutional citizen of state A, but born and culturally formed in state B—facing a choice to aid either a fellow institutional citizen of A with whom she shares no cultural or linguistic background, or a fellow cultural national of B with whom she shares no institutional ties. Both potential beneficiaries must be equally distant strangers, the costs and risks to the protagonist identical, and there must be no strategic, reciprocal, or long-term advantage attached to either rescue. Present the case in a single paragraph. Then demonstrate that the scenario delivers the specified verdicts: (1) an institutionalist partialist favors the A-citizen, (2) an identity-based partialist favors the B-national, and (3) a strict cosmopolitan finds no differential obligation. For each, identify the precise feature of the case to which the reasoning appeals. Next, state the structural variable that drives the divergence between (1) and (2), and show what occurs if it is removed—whether by making both candidates share institutions and culture, or share neither—specifying whether the disagreement collapses into a standard cosmopolitan allocation problem or leaves a residual, non-collapsing dispute. Finally, explain how a cosmopolitan defender would attack the framing of the scenario, and assess whether that attack succeeds given the stipulation that institutional and cultural membership are genuinely non-overlapping here.
