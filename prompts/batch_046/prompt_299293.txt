I facilitate a graduate workshop on epistemic norms and practical reasoning—sixteen participants, one ninety-minute session, with no prior assigned readings. I want the group to experience the conflict between inside-view reasoning, in which we construct a rich causal narrative around a specific case, and outside-view reasoning, in which we defer to reference-class statistics, and to feel why adopting epistemic modesty—privileging the base rate—can seem like an interpretive betrayal rather than a simple correction. Design a structured in-class simulation with explicit rules and assigned roles: first, divide participants into small committees and provide each with a dense, fictionalized dossier describing one specific decision scenario (for example, an unconventional interdisciplinary research proposal or a mid-career professional transition) that includes personal histories, mitigating circumstances, and contextual details they must collectively defend; then require each committee to render a binding predictive judgment, but only after they have been given a stark statistical summary of outcomes across one hundred structurally similar past cases. The activity should be arranged so that participants build the inside narrative collaboratively—perhaps by assigning each student one piece of contextual evidence they must argue for—before being forced to vote using only the outside-view data, with rules that penalize or socially cost any explicit reference back to the narrative details they just assembled. Specify materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff or reputation mechanism (not real money) that makes the constraint feel genuinely costly.

The debrief should map concrete moments in the simulation onto the conceptual distinction: identify exactly when inside-view reasoning entered, when the statistical frame was imposed, and where participants tried to smuggle narrative considerations into the base-rate judgment. Predict what will go wrong the first time you run this—likely that students will reinterpret the statistical category to fit their specific case, or that the committee will split into factions defending the narrative versus the numbers—and suggest how to manage that dynamic without resolving it prematurely. Be explicit about which aspect of epistemic modesty this exercise cannot reproduce—perhaps the long-term calibration benefits of statistical deference, or the social signaling involved in publicly abandoning one's own reasoning—and state how you will supply that missing element verbally after the simulation ends. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; also avoid relying on well-known canonical illustrations such as the planning fallacy or standard textbook formulations, since the point is that the tension should arise from the activity's own invented case and its paired statistical counterpart.
