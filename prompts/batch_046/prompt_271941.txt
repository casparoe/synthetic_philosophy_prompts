I just spent the better part of last night arguing with a close friend about whether it is even coherent to say that “Germany caused World War I,” and we kept circling back to the Fischer controversy without ever resolving the underlying conceptual problem. My friend kept insisting that Fritz Fischer’s archival findings about the 1914 decision-making chain and the so-called blank check to Austria-Hungary only establish structural causation—systemic pressure, alliance dynamics, bureaucratic momentum—while historians write about them as if they prove intentional, state-level guilt comparable to blaming an individual for a premeditated act. I think she is conflating two different questions—who triggered the crisis and what it means to hold a collective entity responsible—but I want an exam that forces students to see the distinction for themselves rather than recite a summary of “Fischer said Germany sought world power and other historians disagreed.”

Please design a final-exam essay prompt for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in modern European history or political philosophy. The exam should be completed in ninety minutes, with an expected length of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, and it should carry forty percent of the final course grade. The prompt must not be answerable by narrative summary alone; it should require students to do conceptual work on what “holding a nation responsible” actually entails, using Fischer’s well-known thesis about German war aims and the 1914 crisis as the concrete case.

Specifically, inject the following framing device: present a hypothetical 2025 international tribunal convened to determine whether to issue a formal “historical responsibility” declaration against the German Empire for the outbreak of the war. Ask the students to advise the tribunal on which concept of collective blame—intentional collective agency, distributive structural liability, or some hybrid—is appropriate to the evidence, and on whether Fischer’s specific findings about the September Program and the Kaiserreich’s internal decision-making actually support that concept. The question should require them to distinguish causal contribution from moral attribution, to explain why alliance-structure counterfactuals matter for one form of blame but not the other, and to identify what is lost if we collapse “responsibility” into mere historical causation.

Together with the exam question, provide a model answer that names the precise conceptual moves a top response must make: for example, separating agent-level intentionality from system-level outcomes; applying that separation to the archival record Fischer cites; explaining why treating the Empire as a unitary agent either overstates or understates the moral claim; and clarifying what a tribunal declaration actually does that a causal explanation does not. Also provide a detailed marking scheme that specifies the marks available and the exact errors that should cost points—such as treating Fischer’s argument as purely about individual blame rather than state-level intentionality; ignoring the distinction between triggering a crisis and bearing responsibility for it; relying on an undefined notion of “national character”; or failing to address the tribunal framing altogether.

Do not ask students to provide bibliographies, reading lists, or specific page citations; the exam is closed-book, and answers should not depend on guessing at obscure sub-debates or minor historiographical figures. Keep the prompt grounded in ideas that a well-prepared senior should have encountered, but force them to navigate the crux my friend and I could not resolve: whether Fischer’s evidence licenses the kind of blame we actually mean when we say a nation is responsible for a war. Write in formal, polished American English.
