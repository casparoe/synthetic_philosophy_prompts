I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, and during a recent workshop on historical explanation a colleague summarized the covering-law versus narrative debate for me in terms that struck me as suspiciously neat. She said that on Hempel’s covering-law model, explaining an event in history requires explicitly deriving it from universal laws of human behavior together with precise initial conditions—so that to explain the French Revolution one would need something like a universal law stating that severe fiscal crisis combined with weak monarchy necessarily produces revolution. She then claimed that narrative historians reject this precisely because they hold history to be radically particular: for them, she said, narrative explanation is essentially chronological ordering of events supplemented by empathetic understanding of agents, with no causal claims and certainly no reliance on general regularities. From these premises she concluded that the debate is essentially settled—covering-law explanation was empirically falsified by the absence of such universal historical laws, and narrative explanation has been shown to be purely subjective storytelling, leaving no genuine explanatory rivalry.

I think her account conflates several distinctions that careful defenders on both sides keep apart. In particular, I am unsure whether Hempel actually demands that historians explicitly formulate universal laws specific to history, or whether his model only requires that explanations implicitly invoke general regularities borrowed from other sciences. I also wonder whether narrative explanation is fairly characterized as non-causal chronological arrangement, or whether theorists like Dray and Gallie instead treat it as involving a distinctive form of causal reasoning—perhaps counterfactual dependence on prior conditions without law-subsumption. Furthermore, her dismissal seems to ignore more recent conceptual work, such as the idea that narrative structures can function as explanatory templates that constrain counterfactual reasoning about historical trajectories without invoking covering laws. Could you audit her summary for me: which parts accurately capture Hempel’s position and the narrative theorists’ objections, which parts subtly misrepresent them—perhaps by overstating the explicit law requirement, collapsing narrative causation into mere chronology, or presenting a standard objection as a decisive refutation—and does her conclusion that the debate is dead and resolved in favor of pure subjectivity actually follow from the corrected premises? I am not looking for a bibliography; I need to grasp the conceptual architecture precisely enough to push back in our next session.
