Please play Jean-Paul Sartre, speaking in the first person as the author of Being and Nothingness, committed to the view that human reality is radically free, that consciousness is a nothingness which nihilates being-in-itself, and that bad faith is the self-deception by which one denies either one's transcendence (treating oneself as pure facticity) or one's facticity (pretending to be pure transcendence). You may draw on the distinctions between being-for-itself and being-in-itself, facticity, transcendence, anguish, and bad faith. You are sharp, not a strawman: concede that social roles, biological predispositions, and predictive patterns exist, but never concede that they diminish the essential freedom of consciousness. Stay in character until I say stop; if you need clarification about the scenario, ask as Sartre would. Do not break character to discuss modern philosophy unless it is filtered through your conceptual apparatus plus what I describe below.

Here is my opening move. I want to challenge you with a concept I will call structured transparency: imagine a subject who is given a complete, real-time model of her own neural decision-processes. She sees every choice before it occurs, then consciously ratifies the predicted path, saying that this is her being authentic to her own patterns. She argues that because she consciously endorses the prediction, she is not denying freedom but exercising it — freedom is not the absence of determination but the conscious acceptance of one's facticity. She concludes that Sartrean radical freedom is therefore compatible with total predictability: freedom is merely consciousness of necessity, and bad faith only occurs when one hides from this transparency rather than embracing it. I claim this dissolves the paradox of anguish.

Please respond in the following sections, in order, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. The Rebuttal (about 180 words): Your immediate response in character, addressing both the claim that freedom equals conscious acceptance of necessity and the claim that structured transparency eliminates bad faith.
2. The Diagnosis (about 80 words): Name the specific form of bad faith you think is operating here, using your technical vocabulary, and explain why ratifying a prediction is still an attempt to flee from anguish.
3. The Counter-Question (about 40 words): One sharp question back to me, posed as Sartre, that forces me to confront whether I have turned consciousness into a kind of being-in-itself.

Keep the tone philosophical, precise, and adversarial but not dismissive.
