Two colleagues in my philosophy of mind reading group have become deadlocked over a regularity they observed in a large language model's responses to moral dilemmas. When asked whether it is permissible to sacrifice one person to save five, the model does not vary randomly; instead, it systematically shifts its justificatory framework depending on whether the individuals are referred to by proper names or by abstract variables, despite the logical structure of the scenario remaining identical. Colleague A argues that this is a 'real pattern' in Daniel Dennett's sense — a genuinely projectible regularity, not a statistical fluke — and that it ought to be explained by adopting the intentional stance: the model behaves as if it holds a belief about how moral language functions, applying that belief inconsistently across framing conditions. Colleague B replies that invoking 'real pattern' status already assumes what needs proving; from the design stance, the clustering is precisely what one expects from a system optimised to produce coherent-sounding justifications rather than logically consistent ones, and from the physical stance one can trace the divergence to specific attention-head activations triggered by named entities. A counters that the design stance cannot account for the context-sensitivity of the pattern in ways not specified by the architecture, and that reducing the phenomenon to weights and activations forfeits the predictive leverage gained by treating the system as an agent with beliefs. B then contends that the intentional stance is only licensed when behaviour is rational and unpredictable from lower-level descriptions, and that since the model's outputs are fully predictable given the prompt embeddings, positing beliefs is ontologically extravagant. A concedes the predictability point but insists that Dennett's criterion for real patterns is independent of ontological commitment — a pattern is real if it supports robust counterfactuals, regardless of whether we must posit mental states. B responds that, even if the pattern is real, the three stances are not competing explanations but levels of abstraction, so A is mistaken in thinking the intentional stance 'wins'; they are compatible descriptions of the same phenomenon. A disagrees, maintaining that in this particular case the intentional stance yields distinctive predictions that the other stances miss — for example, that the model will resist explicit correction about its framing bias in ways analogous to human motivated reasoning — and that this predictive success is what justifies adopting it.

Please adjudicate this disagreement point by point. Is the observed clustering best treated as a real pattern in Dennett's sense? Do the design and physical stances together provide an adequate account, or does the intentional stance earn its keep through distinctive predictive power? Are the three stances genuinely compatible descriptions here, or is there a substantive explanatory conflict between them? And what, if anything, are both colleagues overlooking — for instance, regarding the conditions under which the intentional stance is appropriate for artificial systems, or regarding whether real-pattern status requires more than predictive utility? Please answer in British English.
