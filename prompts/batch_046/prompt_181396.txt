I've recently become the primary caregiver for my mother, who is in the middle stages of dementia, and at the same time I've been asked to advise a university committee on ethical frameworks for distributed autonomous decision systems. The overlap has made me unusually reflective about my own moral judgments, because I find myself applying what feels like the same kind of discernment in both domains—yet the judgments resist any principle I try to formulate. I'd like to understand whether I'm implicitly a moral particularist, or whether there's a defensible generalist account that can accommodate my verdicts without being contrived.

Here are my gut reactions to specific cases, all of which feel stable except the last one. In caregiving: breaking a scheduled visit to sit with my mother in order to stop a stranger from being seriously harmed feels permissible, but breaking the identical visit to attend a pleasant social event feels clearly wrong—though both involve the same broken commitment and roughly the same distress to her. Telling my mother that her favorite sweater was ruined by my own carelessness rather than by the laundry service feels wrong in a way that telling her a distant relative died peacefully rather than violently does not, even though both are falsehoods told to prevent suffering. In my advisory work: recommending that one autonomous node maintain a local protocol commitment despite a global efficiency gain feels right, while recommending the structurally identical override for another node feels corrupt—yet I can point to no architectural feature that distinguishes them except what I can only describe as the moral texture of the commitment. Telling a dying acquaintance that their estranged child called, when the child did not, feels gravely wrong; telling the same person that an unpublished manuscript they valued was praised by critics, when it was not, feels compassionate and acceptable. But here is the case I keep returning to: if my mother, during one of her rare lucid intervals, asks whether her deceased husband—who died five years ago—is still alive, is telling her he is just away on a trip a permissible act of protection, or is it a betrayal of the only moments when she can still grasp reality?

Is there a coherent moral principle—perhaps one that appeals to the preservation of relational integrity, to the distinction between harm and offense, or to some other feature—that captures all of these verdicts? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which of my judgments it forces me to abandon, and tell me what it predicts for the lucid-interval case and for a parallel case in distributed systems: whether a node should be permitted to maintain a false but stabilizing representation of a deprecated partner node's status. If no single principle works, please present the two or three most plausible candidates, explain what each saves and what each costs, and advise me—though I recognize this is philosophical, not clinical—on whether particularism is the more honest stance for someone in my position. Please answer in American English.
