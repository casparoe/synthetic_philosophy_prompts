I want to set out the criteria of adequacy for any theory that claims to extend Confucian role ethics—where moral identity is constituted by relational roles (father-son, ruler-subject, elder-younger friend) cultivated through ritual propriety (li) and reciprocal extension (shu)—to what we might call synthetic relationality: sustained social bonds mediated not by face-to-face ritual but by algorithmic curation and predictive modelling, in which the interlocutor is partly constituted by a recommendation architecture rather than by an embodied presence. Before I evaluate any particular proposal, write the desiderata: what must such a theory deliver? For each requirement, supply the case or conceptual pressure that motivates it—e.g., whether it must preserve the asymmetry of filial obligation even when the caregiver is a synthetic agent; whether it can account for moral failure when ritual propriety is replaced by interface design; whether it distinguishes derivative role-playing (adopting a persona) from genuine role-constitution; and whether it can accommodate multi-layered roles (one agent simultaneously occupying positions in a kinship network and in an algorithmically constituted community) without collapsing into contractual individualism. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable—particularly between the demand for embodied ritual and the demand for scalability across mediated networks. Then, briefly, indicate how classical role ethics, a modern relational-contractual hybrid, and a functionalist interpretation of li might fare against the list.
