After a long and somewhat circular argument with a close friend about whether gradual brain replacement actually preserves the person, I want a precisely engineered thought experiment that forces our disagreement into the open rather than letting it hide in ambiguous intuitions. Please construct a realistic scenario—no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, no demons, and suitable for a reader with only basic familiarity with philosophy of mind—that meets the following specifications.

The setting should be a near-future clinical protocol in which a patient with a progressive neurodegenerative condition undergoes replacement of brain tissue with synthetic equivalents over fifteen years, one synaptic cluster at a time, with continuous consciousness and no detectable lapse in mental life. The critical twist is that each synthetic cluster is implemented on a different substrate architecture: first on silicon spiking-neuron analogues, then on photonic crossbars, then on molecular-scale quantum-dot arrays. Every transition preserves all behavioral outputs, all reported phenomenal qualities, and all memory-access patterns, but alters the underlying causal dynamics of information processing. Meanwhile, the original biological tissue is not destroyed but is cryopreserved in a separate facility, kept metabolically alive but permanently disconnected from behavior. Present the scenario in a single paragraph.

The case must be designed so that three specified views reach exactly these verdicts:
1. A psychological-continuity theorist—who takes personal identity to depend on the uninterrupted flow of consciousness, memory, and psychological traits—must conclude that the patient survives and is identical to the entity that eventually wakes in the fully synthetic skull.
2. An animalist—who takes identity to depend on the continued existence of the living human organism—must conclude that the original patient died when the first biological cluster was removed and replaced, and that the synthetic entity is numerically distinct regardless of mental similarity.
3. A “causal-architecture essentialist”—a view you should treat as genuinely available even if rarely named in standard texts—who holds that personal identity requires not merely psychological continuity but preservation of the specific causal-functional architecture that originally realized that psychology. This view must conclude that identity is lost not at the first substrate change, but specifically at the first architectural transition (from silicon spiking to photonic crossbars), because although mental life never breaks, the underlying causal dynamics that constituted the original person’s mental economy have been replaced.

After the scenario, walk through the reasoning for each position, showing exactly how each reaches its verdict from the case details. Then identify the structural feature that does the real philosophical work—whether it is the substrate change, the architectural transition, or the preservation of psychological flow—and explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed (for example, if all replacements use the same silicon architecture throughout). Finally, include an honest assessment of how a defender of the view that comes out worst in your design—most likely the causal-architecture essentialist—would attack the scenario, and whether that attack collapses the distinction you have drawn.

Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, named texts, or reading lists; work only with the concepts as defined above. Keep the deliverable free of appeals to omniscient observers or technological devices that violate present-day neuroscience in principle.
