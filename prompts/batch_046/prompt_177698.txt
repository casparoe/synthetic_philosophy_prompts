As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I am assembling material for a feature on how illusionist accounts of consciousness reshape public rituals. Please draft the opening remarks for a retirement symposium honoring Dr. Elena Vasquez, a neuroscientist whose career rested on the claim that phenomenal consciousness—what philosophers often call subjective feel or what-it-is-likeness—is not a direct feature of neural activity but a representational fiction generated by introspective prediction errors, structurally analogous to the refrigerator-light illusion applied to the hierarchical self-model rather than to awareness as a whole. The audience is split: her lab colleagues understand predictive processing and representationalist frameworks, while her family and non-academic guests do not. The remarks must do real philosophical work: they should honor Vasquez’s legacy without invoking any literal inner theater, private qualia, or post-mortem experiential presence she would reject as false, and they must avoid standard consolation tropes that depend on phenomenal transparency—the assumption that we enjoy unmediated access to our own mental states. Instead, use her own framework to construct something genuinely comforting about recognition, the continuity of influence, and the intellectual value of dissolving a pervasive illusion. The language should be precise enough that specialists do not wince, yet fully accessible to listeners without philosophical training. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific papers, since this is intended for oral delivery to a live room.
