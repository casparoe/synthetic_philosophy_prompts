Consider the absent-minded driver problem: a driver must take the second of two identical highway exits to reach his destination; exiting at the first yields payoff 0, at the second yields 4, and missing both yields 1. Because he is absent-minded, he cannot distinguish whether he is at the first or second exit.

Here is an argument my study group built that seems to show rational agents with imperfect recall can never adapt in the middle of a sequence.

(1) At either exit, the driver occupies exactly the same information set: identical beliefs, identical desires, and the same available actions.
(2) Rational choice requires that decision rules map information sets to actions; subjectively identical inputs must yield identical outputs.
(3) Therefore whatever action the driver takes at the first exit, he must take at the second.
(4) The best strategy consistent with (3) is an ex-ante probabilistic plan—exiting with some weight p—which yields expected payoff 4/3, strictly better than always continuing (payoff 1).
(5) Now generalize: whenever an agent will forget an intermediate distinction, every node that shares that forgotten feature lies in the same information set.
(6) By (2), the agent must apply the same action at all such nodes, so conditioning on the forgotten intermediate outcome is rationally prohibited.
(7) Hence rational agents with bounded memory are committed entirely to ex-ante lotteries over complete histories; they can never revise, respond to surprises, or learn mid-course.
(8) This proves too much: bounded rationality is clearly not total paralysis, and we routinely adapt despite forgetting details.
(9) So (2) must be false, and rational choice can depend on something outside the current information set—perhaps a plan adopted earlier that the agent continues to endorse even when she no longer remembers its origin.

But I find (2) hard to abandon: without it, how can an agent justify treating two subjectively indistinguishable situations differently? If I keep (2) but block the generalization in (6), I seem to draw an ad hoc line between the driver case and ordinary adaptive planning. If instead I reject (7), I need to show that ex-ante commitment does not actually block all mid-course revision, yet I do not see how that works.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked plausible, and what each of the three alternatives—giving up (2), blocking (6), or weakening (7)—costs. In particular, does rejecting (2) force us to accept that rational agents can act arbitrarily differently in subjectively identical states, and does keeping (2) require accepting that all adaptation with imperfect recall is just the execution of a pre-set lottery?
