I'm a journeyman in a traditional ceramics collective that has preserved the same apprenticeship ritual for over two centuries: at the end of a novice's first year, the master deliberately fashions a visibly flawed vessel, fires it, and then smashes it before the assembled workshop while declaring that imperfection is intolerable, expecting the novice to remain silent and bow. The collective has long treated this not as cruelty but as a sacred transmission of humility and exacting standards. Recently, several younger members have begun refusing to participate, arguing that the ritual causes lasting anxiety and selects for endurance of humiliation rather than for genuine craft ability. At our upcoming annual assembly I have been asked to take a public position, and I am genuinely unsure what kind of case I should make.

I have sketched three possible approaches, each feeling partially right and partially inadequate. First, I could present a direct moral argument: that the ritual inflicts unnecessary psychological harm, violates the dignity of the apprentice, and serves no defensible pedagogical purpose. Second, I could frame it in terms of the collective's economic survival, noting that we are losing promising apprentices to less traditional studios and that modern clients increasingly view our culture as abusive, so that ending the ritual would be a strategic investment in reputation and retention. Third, I could invoke the collective's own traditional language of honor and mastery, arguing that true mastery is demonstrated through patient correction and that the public destruction of a novice's work is actually a corruption of our founding ethos, turning honor into domination. What confuses me is that the moral argument is immediately dismissed by older masters as an import from outside ethics, the economic argument is heard but seems to miss the internal meaning of the practice, and the honor argument resonates yet risks being co-opted—if honor is defined solely by those in power, then I may be losing a rhetorical contest rather than transforming anything.

This dilemma made me think of a thesis I encountered recently: that major social norms—such as dueling or footbinding—rarely collapse because opponents successfully prove them immoral; rather, they dissolve when the very people who uphold them come to see the practices as incompatible with the status and self-respect that the practices were originally meant to secure. If that framework is correct, I want to know whether I should therefore abandon the moral and economic arguments entirely and construct a pure appeal to honor, or whether a deliberately mixed strategy is more effective, and whether being transparent about my strategic motives undermines the appeal. More specifically, I want to understand what philosophical account of normative change predicts about my situation: will the ritual end because the masters' practical interests shift, because an internal contradiction in their honor code becomes visible to them, or because some combination makes the old practice gradually unthinkable? And if I must choose one line of argument to present at the assembly, which one is actually doing the work of moral transformation rather than merely winning a debate?
