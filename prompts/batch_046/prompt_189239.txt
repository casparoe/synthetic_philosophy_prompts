I'm on this zoning board in a old harbor town. A big developer wants to tear down the warehouse district for a data center—promises 500 jobs and a quick tax bump, done in 18 months. Some local geograhers and historians are pushing back: they say the weird canal angles and brick sizes trace back to 19th-century tidal farming and labor migration routes, and the land itself is filled marsh that still floods on a 30-year cycle tied to regional climate patterns. They argue treating this as jobs-vs-nostalgia ignores that geography and these slow social structures actually constrain what survives here long-term, while political votes and quick economic boosts are just surface events. The board wants a yes/no next month and I'm stuck—the fast-money argument sounds practical but feels like it's answering the wrong question. Am I missing something real about how long-term structures should weigh against immediate gains, or is this just romantic resistance?
