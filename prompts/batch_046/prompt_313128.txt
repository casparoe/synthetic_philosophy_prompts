Two people in my philosophy reading group, Jordan and Mira, have been arguing about whether the paradox of deontology is a genuine internal tension or a framing error imported from consequentialist intuition. Jordan claims deontology is consistent once we accept what he calls “domain-relative lexicality”: agent-relative prohibitions carry absolute weight, but only within the scope of actions an agent directly performs; outside that scope, only agent-neutral reasons apply. He says this dissolves the classic rescue paradox, because the agent is never required to kill—only permitted or not permitted to prevent deaths she does not cause—and there is no contradiction in allowing more deaths to avoid direct violation.

Mira replies that this merely relocates the paradox into a metaphysics of causation. She proposes a modified surgical case: a surgeon can save five patients only by harvesting organs from one healthy unconscious patient, and she can do so either by operating herself or by flipping a switch that activates an autonomous surgical robot she controls; the robot then performs every physical extraction without her further touch. On Jordan’s view, flipping the switch is forbidden direct action, but leaving the robot deactivated is permitted, even though both choices determine exactly who lives and dies. That, Mira argues, makes the moral fate of the five depend on the causal path rather than on any feature of the surgeon’s agency or intentions, which is a reductio.

Jordan answers that Mira smuggles in consequentialist premises: initiating the robot is not “determining outcomes” in the morally relevant sense, but initiating a causal chain in which the robot’s actions remain external to her agency. He adds that if the robot had been programmed years earlier by an unknown engineer, and the surgeon’s only option is to disable it before activation, then disabling is not “saving five” but “interfering with an independent process to prevent a harm she did not initiate”—permissible, not obligatory. The boundary, he insists, tracks direct authorship, not outcome prediction.

Mira pushes back: if disabling is permissible but not obligatory, then in the standard rescue case—where the surgeon could save five by not interfering with the robot’s default protocol—deontology permits her to do nothing while five die, solely to avoid being causally linked to one death. She asks whether Jordan really endorses that result, and notes that his boundary seems belief-dependent and technologically contingent: if the robot’s protocol is transparent and reversible, does the moral evaluation shift every time the surgeon updates her belief about whether she “directly” caused the outcome?

Please adjudicate move by move: rate Jordan’s initial claim about domain-relative lexicality, Mira’s robot-mediated case, Jordan’s reply about prior programming and causal authorship, and Mira’s final push about transparency and belief-dependence. For each, say whether it is clearly mistaken, clearly right, or partly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—specifically, whether there is a principled way to draw the direct/indirect boundary that avoids both consequentialist collapse and an implausible causal metaphysics. Take a definite position: either defend Jordan’s framework with a specific amendment, defend Mira’s critique with a specific refinement, or propose a third option that resolves the tension, and explain why.
