As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying moral philosophy in earnest, I wish to construct a conceptual map of corruption that does not depend on the usual encyclopedic labels. Please present your answer as continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold type, tables, or any other visual formatting, arranged as one might write in a thoughtful letter. I want a taxonomy, rendered entirely in narrative form, that sorts actual positions on corruption—particularly regarding clientelism, customary gift-giving, and the universality of Western institutional standards—by the answers their holders would give to concrete cases, not by the names they prefer. The root division should ask whether corruption is primarily a feature of discrete acts, of enduring relational structures, or of institutional functions. From there, branch through three diagnostic scenarios: first, a senior official in a non-Western administration who receives publicly acknowledged seasonal gifts from local merchants within long-standing reciprocal customs, with no direct quid pro quo for specific decisions, yet with durable social obligations; second, an identical exchange pattern hidden within a Western regulatory body and explicitly tied to licensing outcomes; third, a foreign aid program that channels resources through local intermediaries who, following community norms, distribute goods preferentially to kin, producing structural inequality but no private enrichment. Then apply two further distinctions drawn from my scientific background: whether judging the first scenario by Western legal norms alone commits what I call an indexical contamination error, that is, applying measurement instruments calibrated solely to one institutional environment onto a practice embedded in another; and whether an exchange becomes corrupt only upon crossing a reciprocity horizon, where mutual obligation transforms into structurally asymmetric dependency rather than remaining bounded social exchange. The taxonomy should terminate in at least these five leaves: strict universal legalism; cultural-contextual indexicalism; structural-functional institutionalism; relational reciprocity theory, which treats corruption as a breach of bounded mutual expectation rather than of formal rule; and the transparency-epistemic view, which holds that corruption is essentially a failure of observable accountability rather than of exchange itself. Wherever the logical space permits answer combinations that none of these five occupy, identify the empty branch and briefly diagnose whether it is unoccupied because incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where two of the named leaves cannot be separated by responses to these cases, state that limitation explicitly rather than inventing a spurious distinction. Do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke obscure named doctrines I am unlikely to have studied; and do not quote any text verbatim unless you can verify the exact wording from a source, in which case paraphrase without quotation marks instead. Build the instrument so that someone could determine which position they hold without knowing any philosophical labels in advance.
