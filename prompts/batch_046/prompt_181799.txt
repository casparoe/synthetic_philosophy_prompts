Here is an argument I have been trying to formalise about metaphysical grounding and fundamentality, and I am troubled because it seems to prove that no ontology can consistently claim to have reached the bottom. (1) Every fact is either fundamental or fully grounded in fundamental facts. (2) For any proposed ontology O, let C(O) be the claim that the entities listed in O constitute the complete set of fundamental entities — that is, there are no others whatsoever. (3) If O is correct, then C(O) is true; if C(O) is false, then there exists a fundamental entity outside O, contradicting O's completeness. (4) So any correct ontology entails its own completeness claim. (5) Now, is C(O) itself fundamental or grounded? (6) If C(O) is grounded, its grounds must be drawn from O, since only O's entities exist if O is complete. (7) But no internal arrangement of O's entities can entail the non-existence of entities outside O, for such outside entities, by definition, do not interact with or figure in O's internal structure. (8) Therefore C(O) cannot be grounded in O. (9) Nor can it be grounded in O together with some further fact, for any further fact either belongs to O (already considered) or lies outside O, which would make C(O) false. (10) Hence C(O) is ungrounded; by (1) it must be fundamental. (11) But C(O) is a boundary claim about O, not part of O's own furniture; treating it as fundamental means every ontology carries an inexplicable, sui generis fundamental fact about its own exhaustiveness. (12) Take two competing ontologies, O1 and O2, with disjoint fundamental entities. By (10), both C(O1) and C(O2) are fundamental, hence both true. (13) Yet they cannot both be true, since each asserts that its own entities alone exhaust reality. (14) Thus the very idea of a complete ontology collapses into contradiction. (15) The only apparent escape is to deny (1) and allow ungrounded but non-fundamental facts, but that seems to destroy the distinction grounding was introduced to make. I want to preserve both (1) and the meaningfulness of completeness claims, yet I cannot locate the slip. Is the error in (7), which treats completeness as if it needed internal proof rather than being entailed by exhaustion? Or is the mistake in (10), where an ungrounded meta-claim is illegitimately promoted to fundamentality? Or should I reject the idea that C(O) is a fact at all? If I must surrender one premise, I should least like to lose (1); please explain what giving up each alternative costs. Please reply in British English.
