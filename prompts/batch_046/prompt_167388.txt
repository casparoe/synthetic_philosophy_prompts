After a long and exhausting argument with a close friend about whether self-deception is even conceptually coherent, I have tried to formalize my own position to see whether it actually holds together. Here is the package as it stands: (1) I think self-deception is real and not reducible to mere ignorance or error, but only if we allow that the "deceiver" and the "deceived" can be different functional subsystems within a single, metaphysically unified agent—there is no literal split self, only differential access to evidence inside one cognitive architecture. (2) I am firmly committed to doxastic involuntarism: no one can directly intend to acquire or maintain a specific belief. (3) Nevertheless, I hold that self-deception involves a genuine form of intentionality—not at the level of belief-formation, but at the level of attention-management: the agent deliberately directs cognitive resources away from threatening evidence, knowing in some functional sense that the evidence is present. (4) I also maintain that clinical denial—say, a patient who systematically ignores clear symptoms—is a genuine case of self-deception rather than simple lack of knowledge, which implies the patient must have some epistemic access to the truth being suppressed, even if that access never crystallizes into explicit assent. (5) At the same time, I reject the idea that there is always a hidden "true belief" sitting in the unconscious waiting to be revealed; in many cases the suppressed content never takes the form of a belief at all, but rather of an unformulated recognition or an inhibited predictive expectation. (6) Finally, I want evolutionary explanations of self-deception—for instance, that it evolved primarily for social signaling—to be fully compatible with the above, because functional partitioning need not entail any conscious strategist inside the head.

My friend insisted that (3) collapses into a contradiction with (2), that (1) cannot be sustained without an ontological division of the self that I elsewhere reject, and that (4) and (5) are flatly incompatible—if there is no hidden belief, then there is nothing being deceived about. I think he is wrong on at least two counts, but I also suspect there is a genuine tension in my package that I am not seeing clearly. Can you audit these six commitments? For each pair that is genuinely in tension, rate the severity, say whether it is real or only apparent, identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency, and explain what that revision costs. For pairs that only look tense, show why they dissolve on inspection. Then tell me which single commitment I should be most willing to drop if I must choose one, and specify exactly where my friend is right to press me and where he is overreaching. Please be precise and move point by point rather than giving a general lecture.
