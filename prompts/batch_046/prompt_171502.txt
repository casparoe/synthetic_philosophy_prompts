Write an essay on the following controversy in the philosophy of history. Ranke's empirical aspiration to reconstruct the past as it actually was is commonly contrasted with E.H. Carr's claim that historical facts are always already interpreted through the historian's present-minded selection, and with Hayden White's stronger thesis that historical writing is essentially a literary operation governed by tropological patterns of emplotment rather than by evidential constraint. Your task is to evaluate whether a minimal, defensible notion of historical objectivity can be preserved by sharply distinguishing two normative dimensions—archival fidelity, understood as the accurate preservation and representation of source traces, and narrative coherence, understood as the causal-explanatory structure imposed upon those traces. Specifically, ask whether Carr's sociological critique and White's tropological analysis jointly undermine both dimensions, or whether archival fidelity can survive as an independent constraint even if narrative coherence is irreducibly constructive. Weigh the argument that intersubjective agreement among trained historians, of the kind Carr emphasizes, provides sufficient objectivity, against the counter-argument that such agreement merely reflects shared ideological emplotment of the kind White describes. Reach and defend a specific conclusion: either that a stripped-down objectivity grounded solely in archival fidelity remains viable despite the collapse of narrative objectivity, or that the distinction between fidelity and coherence itself collapses and historical writing cannot claim any special epistemic authority over fiction.
