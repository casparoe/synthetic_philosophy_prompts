At a department reading group, two graduate students—Mira and Jonas—fell into a sharp disagreement about Quine's gavagai case and what it actually threatens. Mira argued that the example demonstrates a deeper indeterminacy than is usually acknowledged: it is not merely that "rabbit" and "undetached rabbit part" are behaviorally equivalent translations of "gavagai," but that the very distinction between enduring objects and temporal stages is itself translation-indeterminate. If a speaker's ontology contains only four-dimensional "rabbit-stages," she claimed, the translator can systematically remap every compositional rule that applies to "rabbit" onto "stage" without any observational difference, so there is no fact of the matter about whether the native language refers to enduring rabbits at all. Jonas replied that this overreads Quine: the standard analytical hypotheses—charity, simplicity, and overall fit with the rest of the translated theory—break the symmetry just as they do in scientific theory choice, and a manual that posits enduring objects is generally more parsimonious than one that requires an ontology of instantaneous temporal slices plus a separate fusion relation. Mira countered that in this particular case both ontologies yield identical predictions for every possible utterance, including compositional ones, because any syntactic behavior of "gavagai" can be duplicated by a corresponding rule governing "gavagai-stage"; therefore the usual criteria of theory choice are genuinely tied and provide no leverage. Jonas then conceded that if the two frameworks are perfectly isomorphic in predictive and syntactic structure, then there may be no hidden fact to discover, but he insisted that this collapses Quine's skeptical point into a harmless metaphysical pluralism rather than a genuine failure of reference. Mira answered that Jonas had just conceded her central claim: if there is no fact of the matter even in principle—whether hidden or not—then reference is genuinely indeterminate, not merely epistemically opaque. Jonas pushed back that "indeterminate" is ambiguous between "we cannot know which ontology is correct" and "there is nothing there to be correct about," and that Quine's argument at best establishes the former, which is philosophically unremarkable. They turned to me and asked for a point-by-point adjudication: who is right about the scope of the indeterminacy, whether compositional rules can break the tie, whether perfect isomorphism undermines or confirms Quine's thesis, and whether Jonas's epistemic-versus-ontological distinction saves him.
