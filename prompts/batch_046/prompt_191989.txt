I serve as the community representative on the advisory board of the Riverside Municipal Museum, and I have no formal background in history or philosophy, though I have spent the past year reading widely on my own about how large-scale narratives of human development are constructed. Next month we must vote on a proposal for a permanent installation called The Human Arc, which would guide visitors from cosmic origins through local settlement using four sequential "thresholds" — physical, biological, cognitive, and agricultural — drawn mainly from Yuval Noah Harari and Jared Diamond, but revised to include David Graeber and David Wengrow's claim that farming and social hierarchy were contingent rather than inevitable. I am concerned that this design commits what I call "scale-mismatch liability": because the exhibit compresses 13.8 billion years into a single causal chain, it implicitly promises that the geographic and environmental forces shaping global agricultural transitions can also explain specific local events, such as why our own river valley was settled in the particular pattern it was, without actually delivering those explanations. The education director counters that without such a unifying arc, visitors receive only disconnected facts and have no emotional or intellectual reason to engage with local history at all. The board must choose by our March 15 meeting whether to approve the gallery as proposed, approve it only if the designers install explicit "contingency stations" at each threshold showing genuinely alternative paths that were available, or reject the proposal and fund a smaller, locally focused exhibit instead. Please draft the memo I should circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one of these three courses of action with a concrete timeline for implementation if approved, treat the education director's objection with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it as mere sentiment, and address whether my concern about scale-mismatch liability identifies a real philosophical flaw in grand-narrative exhibition design or reflects a lay misunderstanding I should correct.
