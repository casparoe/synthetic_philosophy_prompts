Following a long argument with a friend about whether constitutional monarchy is democratically redundant, I want to test a worry I could not shake. My friend argued that in both Japan and the UK the monarchy has been reduced to pure ceremony: Japan’s Emperor appoints the Prime Minister solely on the Diet’s designation and performs other state acts only with cabinet approval, while British royal assent is now never withheld in practice, so an elected ceremonial president could substitute without any structural loss. I countered that the hereditary office remains embedded in the legal architecture—the Crown persists as a corporation sole with continuous legal personality, and Japan’s Constitution assigns the Emperor formal acts in matters of state that are not merely decorative. Yet I think I may have fallen into a false analogy. By treating Japan’s cabinet-bound formalities and the British monarch’s theoretical reserve powers as comparable hidden checkpoints that could, in an extreme crisis, override popular sovereignty, am I wrongly attributing a discretionary veto to offices that actually possess none? Or is there a genuine structural distinction between a democratically accountable ceremonial president and a hereditary office that performs necessary constitutional functions—continuity of government, formal promulgation, appointment rituals—without ever holding personal power?
