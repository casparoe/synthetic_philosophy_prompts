I'm serving on the university's Constitutional Review Board — we mostly deal with governence deadlocks in student and faculty charters. The grad senate just submitted a proposal to amend their charter so future amendments only need simple majority instead of the current two-thirds. The twist is they want to ratify *this very change* by simple majority too, arguing the senate holds 'original constituent authority' that can't be bound by rules it made itself. Half the board thinks that's healthy democratic self-correction; the other half says it creates a logical loop, like a rule licensing its own suspension.

I need a memo for Thursday's meeting that reccomend whether we approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasoning the senate can actually reply to. Don't just cite Alf Ross or dump philosopher names on me; I want to know if there's a principled way to block the loop without just saying 'older rules always win.' My co-chair keeps asking about entrenchment clauses in real constitutions — address whether those analogies apply to a student charter, but keep the memo actionable. We're deciding if the vote stands, not writing a law review article.
