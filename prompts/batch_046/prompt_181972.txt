Set an exam for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar (final examination, 90 minutes). Write one essay question on whether self-deception is genuinely possible, designed so that it cannot be answered by merely summarising Davidson’s division-of-mind view, Mele’s motivated false-belief account, or Bach’s rationalisation theory. To achieve this, embed the following specific framing: present a scenario in which a senior researcher systematically downweights disconfirming data from her own experiments — because she needs the grant to renew — yet treats comparable anomalies in colleagues’ publications with complete epistemic rigour, and she never forms any explicit belief that she is biased. Ask candidates to use this case to adjudicate between two explanations: (i) an intentionalist, partitionist account that posits a functional division within the agent, and (ii) a single-agent “motivated reasoning” account that avoids paradox by treating the bias as ordinary irrationality. The question should require candidates to identify what extra condition — beyond false belief plus motivation — must hold for the researcher’s state to count as self-deception rather than mere epistemic negligence, and to assess whether specifying that condition inevitably reintroduces the very homuncular division the non-intentionalist sought to avoid.

Provide:
- The exam question itself, with clear instructions (answer in approximately 1,000–1,200 words; focus on argument, not exposition).
- A model answer of about 700 words that takes a definite position: for example, that self-deception is possible but only under a functional, non-ontological compartmentalisation that carries no commitment to sub-personal agents, and that the grant-renewal case meets the threshold only if we allow that the researcher can simultaneously entertain and suppress the same evidential standard at different functional levels.
- A marking scheme out of 20 marks, built around five must-hit points. For each point, specify: the precise philosophical move required; the marks (total 20); and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail it (e.g., describing Davidson and Mele without applying either to the scenario). The scheme should clearly distinguish an A answer (all five moves present with precise application to the researcher case) from a B answer (moves present but applied generically or missing the collapse-into-bias problem).

The difficulty must come from selecting the crux, not from obscurity: candidates should have to show they understand why the paradox of intentionality bites, not merely that it exists. Write in British English. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not include verbatim quotations from real texts — paraphrase any reference to Davidson, Mele, or Bach without quotation marks. If you mention specific works, verify titles or dates by search; otherwise frame the prompt so it does not depend on them.

Conclude your response with a single line summarising the core tension the marking scheme is designed to expose, enclosed as \answer{The exam tests whether functional compartmentalisation can save self-deception from paradox without collapsing it into ordinary bias}.
