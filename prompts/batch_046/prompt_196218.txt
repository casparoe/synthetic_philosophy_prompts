Here is an argument that turns Goodman's own machinery back on his solution, and I cannot locate the failure point. (1) Goodman resolves the emerald paradox by holding that 'green' is projectible and 'grue' is not because 'green' is deeply entrenched in our actual linguistic practice, whereas 'grue' lacks that history. (2) The judgment that a predicate is entrenched is itself an inductive generalization: we observe consistent past usage and project that consistency into our future classifications. (3) Define 'grentrenched': a predicate P is grentrenched just in case all observed uses of P have been consistent up to some time t, but P will be applied inconsistently thereafter. (4) For every observed case of 'green', the available evidence is identical for the hypotheses that it is entrenched and that it is grentrenched, since both match all past data exactly. (5) So by Goodman's own criterion, we have no rational basis for preferring the projection of consistency over the projection of grentrenched-ness. (6) Therefore the distinction between projectible and non-projectible predicates requires an inductive step that is vulnerable to the same paradox. (7) If every resolution must invoke a distinction that can itself be grue'd at the meta-level, then the paradox regenerates infinitely and induction is left permanently groundless. (8) But induction cannot be rationally treated as permanently groundless without self-defeat. Which step should be rejected? I would prefer to preserve (1) and (7), but I am willing to surrender either; identify the weakest link, explain why it initially looked sound, and say what abandoning it costs for Goodman's program.
