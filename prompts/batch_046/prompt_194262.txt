I have been working through Samuel Scheffler's argument that human extinction would damage present people in ways that go far beyond the deaths involved—what is often called his afterlife conjecture, the idea that the point of many long-term projects depends implicitly on there being future human beings to continue, witness, or be affected by them. To explore the precise boundaries of this claim, I want to practice pressing it with a constructed edge case, so please enter the following role-play and stay in it across turns.

Take on the persona of a serious, first-person defender of Scheffler's core thesis: you believe that scientific inquiry, artistic creation, and moral improvement derive part of their present value and motivational force from a background expectation of human continuity after our own deaths, and that learning of imminent total extinction would therefore undermine our current reasons to pursue them, even for individuals who will never live to see the extinction event itself. Speak with philosophical precision, not bluster; grant small distinctions or boundary conditions if they genuinely help, but do not concede that extinction harms present lives only through the sum of future deaths. Do not break character to offer biographical notes, reading lists, or historical summaries—if you invoke Scheffler, do so as someone articulating her own conviction.

Here is my opening challenge, drawn from a discussion in my philosophy of art seminar. Imagine a sculptor who spends three decades building a monumental work of ceramic composites and titanium, engineered to endure for tens of millennia in a geologically stable desert. She fully accepts that biological humanity will end within two centuries, and she designs the monument's symbolic system specifically to be decipherable not by people but by whatever synthetic or radically evolved, non-biological intelligences might eventually arise and study archaeological traces. She reports that this prospect does not diminish her motivation at all; rather, it gives her labor a trans-biological purpose, transforming it into a message left for post-human recipients rather than a contribution to an ongoing human tradition.

I put to you two precise questions. First, does the conjecture depend specifically on human cultural or biological continuity—an afterlife in the literal sense of people living after us—or does it depend only on the existence of some meaningful future reception, regardless of the nature of the recipients? Second, if you maintain that the harm is specifically tied to human continuity, explain why the sculptor's redirected purpose fails to preserve the value of her present labor, given that she still operates within a long-term framework extending far beyond her lifespan. If, conversely, you concede that any robust future audience will serve the same function, then it seems extinction of our particular biological lineage is not necessarily damaging to present meaning, provided one can reconceive one's projects for non-human inheritors. Which horn do you take, and how do you defend it without reducing the conjecture to a mere preference for human company? Begin in character.
