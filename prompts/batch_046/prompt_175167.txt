I work as a novelist, and my practical vocabulary runs to narrative arcs, focalization, unreliable narration, genre conventions, and the feedback loop between how a character is written and how a reader comes to inhabit that role. Take Hacking's analysis of looping effects in psychiatric classification—the way that labeling people as members of a kind can alter the kind's members, who then alter the kind itself—and restate it entirely within that narrative vocabulary. Treat the diagnostic category as a genre template, the clinician's assessment as an act of casting, and the patient's self-understanding as reader identification with an assigned role. Go through the core distinctions: between interactive and indifferent kinds, between the stability of the classification and the instability of the people it captures, and between the loop that reinforces the category and the loop that dissolves it. For each, report what survives translation cleanly, what must be distorted to fit narrative terms, and what has no expression at all in a framework built on fiction and reader response; then conclude with your view on whether the residue points to something psychiatric classification can do that narrative theory cannot, or to an ambiguity in Hacking's original account that becomes visible only once we try to tell it as a story.
