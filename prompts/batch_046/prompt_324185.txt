I work as a backend engineer with zero formal philosophy training, so when I started tinkering with prediction-market APIs I didn't expect to fall into a conceptual rabbit hole. There's this cluster of results — Milgrom-Stokey, Aumann's agreement theorem, hte no-trade literature — that basically says rational agents with shared priors shouldn't trade if they can observe each other's beliefs. At first it felt like a neat logic puzzle, but then I brought it up in our office reading group (mostly devs, one PM who reads philosophy for fun) and we got totally stuck. Half the group thinks the theorems prove real-world trading is mostly irrational noise, because if everyone were actually rational they'd just converge and stop. The other half says the models are too clean: they ignore risk preferences, liquidity needs, and the fact that human belief-updating is more like a buggy distributed system where nodes never reach true common knowledge. We all agree on the formal proofs. We agree people trade constantly. And yet after three meetings nobody's budged.

My gut feeling is that we're not really fighting about economics — we're fighting about what "agreement" even means, kind of like asking whether a blockchain reaches consensus when different nodes have slightly different ledgers because of latency or corruption. Is this stall actually a hidden empirical disagreement about how traders update beliefs? A verbal mix-up between "belief" and "preference"? Or are two different questions being asked under one name — one about normative rationality, one about how noisy algorithms (or markets) aggregate signals? I want a diagnosis, not a survey: is it bedrock intuition, a disguised formal gap, or an ill-posed question? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually move it — a sharper conceptual distinction, a formal result about noisy updates, a framing shift — or should we just accept we're looping like a consensus protocol with faulty nodes?
