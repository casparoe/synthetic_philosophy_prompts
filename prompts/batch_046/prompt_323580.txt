Two philosophers in my study group are stuck arguing about transformative experience and decision-making under radical ignorance.

(1) One claims that because you cannot know beforehand what it is like to become a parent, rational choice requires deferring to the testimony of those who have done it; otherwise you are deciding in complete ignorance.

(2) The other replies that if the experience is genuinely transformative, then others' reports are necessarily translated into your current conceptual scheme, which is precisely what the experience replaces; therefore testimony cannot bridge the gap and is useless for the decision.

(3) The first responds that this makes the choice irrational by definition, since no amount of evidence could ever help; yet people clearly make rational life choices, so the epistemic gap must be smaller than the second claims.

(4) The second counters that practical rationality does not require evidential completeness — it requires only coherence with your enduring values — and that the first is smuggling in an evidentialist model of practical reason that doesn't apply to life-shaping choices.

Please adjudicate moves 1 through 4: rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a one-sentence justification for each rating. Then state, in one sentence, what both sides miss about whether the pre-transformation self retains normative authority to bind the post-transformation self. Keep your response under 180 words; put the bottom line first — your four ratings, the missing point, and then the brief reasoning.
