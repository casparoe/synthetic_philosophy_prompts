I've been thinking through the induction problem that involves emeralds being green versus being grue — green before some arbitrary future date and blue after it. I understand that every emerald we've observed fits both descriptions, so past evidence alone can't tell us which color to expect tomorrow. The standard answer seems to be that green is simpler or more natural, but that explanation feels circular to me: we call green projectible because it seems simpler, yet its simplicity appears to come from the fact that we already treat it as the kind of property that carries forward. I want to understand, at a gut level, whether there's any non-circular way to privilege one predicate over another, or whether induction always depends on a prior framework that itself can't be justified by observation alone.

What makes this hard for me is that I see the same structure everywhere outside of science. For example, I assume a friend who has been generous in the past will stay generous, but I could invent a hidden predicate — generous-until-betrayed — that describes every past act just as well and predicts a sudden change. We dismiss that predicate not because the past rules it out, but because it feels contrived. So is the philosophical issue really about properties being real versus gerrymandered, or is it about the logic of confirmation being inseparable from the language we already speak? And if it's the latter, does that mean induction is just a matter of convention dressed up as reasoning?

I'd appreciate an intuitive explanation that walks through exactly where the difficulty lies: whether the problem is solved by appealing to natural kinds, whether it reveals a hidden circularity in scientific reasoning, or whether it points to something else about how human minds must organize experience before they can learn from it. I am especially interested in whether the social examples — predicting behavior with made-up cutoff predicates — illuminate the same conceptual knot, or whether they involve a different kind of mistake altogether.
