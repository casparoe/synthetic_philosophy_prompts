I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to formalize an applied ethical framework for engineering teams that draws on Confucian role ethics—specifically the idea that moral obligations are defined by relational roles (parent-child, mentor-junior, peer-peer) and mediated through ritual propriety (li) rather than by universal rules or pure consequentialist calculation. To check whether I'm applying it consistently, I've recorded four verdicts this framework has delivered on past dilemmas, but I suspect more than one principle could explain them.

The track record is as follows. First, a junior developer discovers a security flaw introduced by a senior's code; the framework requires private, hierarchical correction—approaching the senior with deference—before any external disclosure, even if delay increases risk. Second, when choosing between a product feature that maximizes individual user autonomy and one that strengthens elder-care family networks, the framework selects the latter, on the grounds that the parent-child role defines moral space more fundamentally than abstract rights. Third, an engineer declines video presence during a stakeholder meeting to care for an ill parent; the framework permits this breach of workplace ritual only if the absence is explicitly acknowledged and apologized for within the relational hierarchy—family obligation supersedes workplace li, yet relational awareness must be preserved. Fourth, the framework rejects public ranking of engineers by commit velocity, despite evidence that it improves output, because public comparison instrumentalizes peer relationships and violates the mutual-care dimension of the colleague role.

Here is the new case. A critical production bug requires immediate weekend deployment. One engineer volunteers, arguing that their role as a dependable teammate demands it, but requests that the company newsletter publicly celebrate their sacrifice as an exemplary fulfillment of professional loyalty. That same weekend is their parent's eightieth birthday, a major ritual milestone in the parent-child relationship. The engineer offers to split time, but either choice will cause serious failure in one role. The framework must advise whether to (a) accept the volunteer and grant public recognition, (b) reject the offer and insist on full attendance at the family ritual, or (c) permit the work only if any acknowledgment remains private.

Please predict what this framework should say for consistency, identify at least two distinct rules that could jointly generate the four prior verdicts but diverge on this case, state which rule best explains the track record and why, and specify one further hypothetical case that would tell those rules apart. In addition, please state how confident you are in your analysis overall, and single out the specific point—whether in interpreting the prior verdicts, constructing the candidate rules, or applying them to the new case—about which you are least certain.
