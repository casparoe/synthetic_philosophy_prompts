By this I mean a practice in which historians of the distant past construct several mutually inconsistent narrative reconstructions of a single event, then subject each to systematic simulated disruptions—fabricated losses of key testimony, algorithmic misattribution of material traces, and synthetic counter-evidence—retaining only the model that proves most resilient across these perturbations. Crucially, this method treats human testimony, material artefacts, and computational inference as epistemically equivalent nodes within a distributed evidential network, thereby dissolving the traditional hierarchy between primary and secondary sources. Given Herodotus's acceptance of multiplicity and his reliance upon oral tradition and visible monuments, and given Thucydides's insistence upon autopsy, precise chronology, and the exclusion of mythic embellishment, analyse whether each historian would regard this flattening of source authority and this reliance upon simulated rather than actual evidential disruption as a legitimate extension of critical inquiry, or as a corruption of the direct contact with the past that makes historical knowledge possible. In your answer, please distinguish carefully between their likely assessments of the method's epistemic reliability and their normative judgements about its compatibility with the historian's duty to preserve narrative coherence. Write your response in British English, maintaining a formal academic register throughout.
