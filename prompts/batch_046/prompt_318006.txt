I am revising my graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and I want an analysis that does not remain at the level of abstract taxonomy but actually tests Strawson’s framework against a concrete, well-documented episode. Take the 1966 University of Texas tower shooting by Charles Whitman, with particular attention to the reactions of survivors and victims’ families after the autopsy revealed a tumor pressing on his amygdala, and to the broader public’s continued insistence on moral blame despite that neuropathological finding. Analyze the case through Strawson’s distinction between the participant reactive attitudes—resentment, indignation, moral demand—and the objective attitude we adopt when we regard someone as lying outside the sphere of moral community. I want a disciplined mapping: specify who in this scenario are the bearers of reactive attitudes and who, if anyone, shifts to an objective stance; determine whether Whitman’s tumor and his prior psychiatric consultations function as excuses that preserve him within the moral community while diminishing blame, or as exemptions that license a clinical, detached posture toward him; and identify precisely what counts as the relevant evidence for each classification. Then run the analysis in the reverse direction: does the persistence of blame after the biological revelation confirm Strawson’s claim that reactive attitudes are largely insulated from deterministic or causal explanations, or does it instead suggest that neuropathology triggers the objective attitude in a way that ordinary social or psychological excuses do not—thereby exposing a gap or tension in Strawson’s binary schema? Be explicit about where the framework offers no clear verdict, and resist the temptation to force it into a definitive judgment on whether Whitman should have been treated as fully responsible, partially excused, or altogether exempt.
