As a retired molecular biologist who has lately turned to philosophy of science, I recently listened to two colleagues—Alistair, a philosopher of biology, and Beatrice, a synthetic biologist—debate the nature of biological function over coffee. Their exchange centred on a synthetic polypeptide, call it Scaffold-X, engineered de novo in a cell-free system to sequester cadmium ions; it has no evolutionary precursor and was never subjected to natural selection in any lineage.

Alistair began by insisting that biological function is fundamentally etiological. On his view, to say that something has a function is to say something about its selection history: the heart pumps blood because past variants that pumped better were selected. Since Scaffold-X was designed, not selected, Alistair argued it possesses no biological function at all—only a causal role, like a spanner tightening a bolt. Calling its ion-sequestration a 'function' is, he said, merely metaphorical borrowing from evolutionary biology.

Beatrice replied that this renders synthetic biology conceptually incoherent. In medicine and bioengineering, we routinely assign functions to designed molecules—insulin analogues, gene-editing guides—based on what they actually do within a system and what they are meant to maintain. For her, causal-role accounts capture this: a function is a contribution to some capacity of the containing system, determined by the current organisational context and design specifications. She added that malfunction is still possible: if Scaffold-X misfolds and fails to bind cadmium, that is not mere mechanical failure but malfunction relative to its designed role in the detoxification pathway.

Alistair countered that Beatrice had smuggled in normativity without grounding it. Without selection, there is no fact of the matter about what the molecule 'ought' to do; there is only what the engineer intended. A broken hammer is not malfunctioning in any biological sense—it is just a broken tool. He pressed her on exaptation: if a feather evolved for insulation and now aids flight, the etiological account can accommodate this by appealing to current selection pressures that stabilise the flight role. By contrast, he claimed, causal-role accounts struggle to say why the flight-contribution is the function rather than an accidental side-effect.

Beatrice answered that Alistair's appeal to current selection is ad hoc patching. The causal-role view distinguishes functional contribution from accidental effect by examining whether the activity is organisationally integrated—whether other parts of the system depend on it and whether its presence explains the system's maintenance. She also noted that Alistair's view renders it mysterious why we should care about the original selection history of a trait that has long since been co-opted: what explains current biological interest is present causal contribution, not ancient evolutionary origin.

Alistair then raised a final challenge: if causal-role is sufficient, then whenever a bacterial toxin accidentally kills a pathogen, we ought to say the toxin has the function of killing pathogens. Beatrice conceded that accidental correlations must be excluded, but maintained that this is done by testing for robustness and systemic dependence, not by consulting evolutionary archives.

They turned to me and asked me to referee. I could not. Please adjudicate their disagreement point by point: where is Alistair right, where is Beatrice right, and what, if anything, are they both overlooking? In particular, address whether malfunction genuinely requires an etiological history, whether exaptation poses a serious problem for either view, and whether there is a coherent middle position. Please write your answer in British English, with a formal and polished style appropriate for a philosophical discussion.
