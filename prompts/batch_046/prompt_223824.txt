I have drafted the following argument for a fiction workshop, and now suspect it proves too much, since its conclusion would seem to dissolve the moral authority of narrative itself. (1) Moral relativism holds that the truth of a moral judgment is fixed only relative to the normative framework of the person who makes it. (2) A novelist addressing a universal audience cannot presuppose that every reader shares her particular framework; she writes, as it were, from no framework at all. (3) Therefore, if relativism is true, any unqualified moral condemnation embedded in a narrative—say, that a character's cruelty is wrong without further qualification—lacks authoritative truth-value for readers outside her framework and collapses into mere autobiographical report for those inside it. (4) Yet the realist and tragic traditions clearly intend precisely such unqualified judgments: they ask us to regard Macbeth's ambition or Iago's malice as deficient regardless of the reader's cultural origin. (5) Consequently, either moral relativism is false, or every morally evaluative novel is conceptually incoherent. (6) The second disjunct is implausible; hence relativism must be rejected. Where does this reasoning slip? I should prefer to keep (4), but I want to know whether preserving it requires denying (2) by positing a diegetic moral framework internal to the work, or whether one must reject (1) more radically, and what either choice costs for relativism in ordinary non-fictional contexts.
