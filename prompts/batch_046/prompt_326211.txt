I've been working through Nozick's tracking conditions with a study partner, and we've hit on an argument that seems to force Nozick's theory into an impossible dilemma: either it validates full epistemic closure—including over skeptical hypotheses—or it makes ordinary deductive knowledge impossible. The reasoning looks tight to me, but the conclusion feels wrong, so I'm trying to locate the weak step. Here is the chain, laid out precisely.

(1) On Nozick's account, S knows p only if S's belief tracks p relative to S's actual method: in nearby worlds where p is false, S doesn't believe p (the sensitivity condition), and in nearby worlds where p is true, S does believe p (adherence). (2) Nozick famously uses this to reject closure: I can track "I have hands" because the nearest false worlds are ones where my hands are missing, but I fail to track "I am not a brain in a vat," since the nearest worlds where that is false are BIV worlds in which I still believe I am not one. (3) Now suppose I know p and competently deduce q from p, where p strictly entails q. The belief-forming method for q is logical inference, not direct observation. (4) To check whether this inferred belief tracks q, we must evaluate worlds where q is false relative to the inference method. But because q is entailed by p, the nearest worlds where q is false are exactly the nearest worlds where p is false—there are no closer worlds in which q fails while p holds. (5) So if my belief in p tracks via observation, then in those nearest false worlds I either lack p to infer from, or I apply the inference to a false premise and do not form the belief in q. Hence my belief in q also satisfies the sensitivity condition. (6) This suggests that tracking actually validates closure: whenever I know p and correctly deduce q, I know q. (7) Apply this to the skeptical case. If I know I have hands and deduce I am not a BIV, then by (6) I should know that denial; yet in nearby BIV worlds I still believe I am not a BIV, so tracking fails. (8) Therefore tracking either forces me to know I am not a BIV—contradicting Nozick's intended anti-skeptical result—or it denies that deduction ever preserves knowledge, which would leave me unable to know any logical consequence of my observations. (9) Both outcomes seem unacceptable. If I have to choose, I want to preserve the idea that deduction can extend knowledge, but I need to know what saving it costs. Which step should I reject—(3) through (5), about how method-relativity applies across inference, or (1), about the uniformity of tracking conditions? And what does that rejection do to the theory's ability to block skepticism without severing knowledge from deduction?
