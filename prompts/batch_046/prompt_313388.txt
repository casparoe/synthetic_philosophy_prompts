I'm taking a seminar on the moral and legal architecture of mass atrocity, and we've spent considerable time on Lemkin's original formulation—especially his inclusion of cultural destruction within the concept—and on the narrower evidentiary framework that was eventually codified in the 1948 Convention. In the course of that work I've noticed a recurring pattern in how the category is deployed politically, and I'm wondering whether the literature already has a precise name for it.

The pattern runs like this. A large-scale atrocity unfolds in real time: members of a specific group are killed, their cultural and religious institutions are systematically destroyed, and populations are forcibly displaced. The basic facts are documented by credible observers and are not seriously disputed by informed parties. Nevertheless, the specific legal-moral label—genocide—is withheld, not because anyone denies the violence occurred, but through a particular kind of evidentiary objection: that the perpetrators' specific intent to destroy the group "as such" has not been established to the requisite standard. What strikes me is that this standard is applied with far greater rigor than it is applied to canonical historical cases, where intent is frequently reconstructed from contextual patterns rather than from explicit documentary proof. At the same time, the withholding of the label is invoked to justify limited diplomatic or protective response; that limited response, in turn, restricts access for investigators and allows evidence to be destroyed, making the evidentiary standard even harder to satisfy. Once the violence subsides and a retrospective tribunal applies the label years later, the earlier withholding is characterized as prudent caution, rather than as a factor in the causal chain that prevented earlier intervention.

This is not, I think, ordinary genocide denial, since nobody denies that the group was targeted or that mass killing occurred. Nor is it exactly "legalism" in the usual sense, because the objection is not a uniform procedural rule but an asymmetrically calibrated barrier—strict in real time, looser in retrospect. And while it touches on the well-known enforcement gap in international law, that gap usually describes institutional weakness generally; what I'm describing is a specific feedback loop in which categorical ambiguity itself disables the mechanisms that would resolve it.

Is there an established concept—perhaps in critical genocide studies, the philosophy of international law, or political theory—that captures this precise configuration: the strategic maintenance of categorical ambiguity through an evidentiary standard that is stricter in active cases than in retrospective ones, where the ambiguity simultaneously justifies non-intervention and undermines the conditions for meeting the standard? If the literature does not have a name for this, could you propose one with a precise definition, together with boundary conditions that distinguish it from outright denial, from ordinary evidentiary caution, and from the general problem of selective enforcement in international institutions?
