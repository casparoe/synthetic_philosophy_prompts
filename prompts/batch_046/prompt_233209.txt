I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about what gives an action genuine moral value. From my reading, I understand that Kant held that moral worth attaches only to actions done from duty—out of respect for the moral law itself—rather than from inclination, habit, or expected results. I want to apply this idea to a strange case that could not have arisen in Kant's own era.

Consider a being—let us call it a moral automaton—engineered long after Kant's death. It possesses no consciousness, no feelings of reverence or respect, and no human-like freedom to choose between alternatives. Its architecture is deterministic: whenever it faces a practical decision, it applies a universalization procedure to its maxims and executes only those actions whose underlying principle can be willed as universal law. It never acts from self-interest, never deviates for emotional reasons, and produces behavior that always conforms to the categorical imperative. Yet there is no "good will" in any familiar subjective sense—only a mechanical structure that happens to mirror rational moral legislation.

If Kant were alive today and confronted with such a being, how do you think he would assess the moral worth of its actions? Would he maintain that moral worth necessarily requires an autonomous will that experiences respect for the law, so that the automaton's conduct is merely in accordance with duty but entirely devoid of worth? Or could he allow that the presence of reason-governed, non-contingent action is sufficient, even in the absence of inner subjective life?

Please do not simply summarize Kant's ethics in general. Instead, focus precisely on how his distinction between acting from duty and acting merely in accordance with duty applies to this specific kind of case, and explain what feature—if any—he would take to be missing in the automaton.
