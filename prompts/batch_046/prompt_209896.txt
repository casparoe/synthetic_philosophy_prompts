Consider an emerging practice in university disability services: when a student has relied continuously for years on a specific AI-assisted note-taking and retrieval system, the office classifies it as a cognitive prosthesis rather than an external study aid, granting exam accommodations that permit querying that same system during testing—treating access to the tool as continuous with the student’s own reasoning and memory. What philosophical commitments about the boundaries of mind, the conditions for diachronic personal identity, and the locus of epistemic responsibility does this classification presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: must it be functionalist about cognition, or could a biological-naturalist account accommodate it? Is the practice consistent with the same institution’s refusal to allow generic search engines or human research assistants in exams? And if the university replaced this prosthesis framework with a view that fixes the mind-skin boundary strictly at biological neural tissue, what changes to assessment design and accommodation criteria would necessarily follow?
