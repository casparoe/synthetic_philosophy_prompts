I have been thinking on my own about the argument Dostoevsky gives through the Grand Inquisitor—that human beings prefer happiness and certainty to the burdensome freedom of moral choice, and that authority does them a kindness by removing that burden. Lately I have been considering a modern mechanism that seems related but structurally different: predictive content feeds that do not merely hide dissenting views, but continuously adjust what they show using real-time biometric signals—eye-tracking, heart-rate variability, and engagement latency—so that the user never experiences the discomfort or curiosity that would motivate a search for alternatives. The choice is not openly taken away; it is preemptively dissolved because the conditions for wanting it never arise. If we imagine the Grand Inquisitor examining such a system, would he regard it as the perfected form of his benevolent tyranny—delivering happiness by eliminating freedom—or would he say it fails his own logic, since the surrender of freedom is unconscious and therefore not a genuine exchange of liberty for comfort? Based on the internal logic of his argument about human weakness, the need for authority, and what it means to be truly happy, which interpretation is stronger? And what feature of this biometric calibration—its invisibility, its automation, or its removal of friction rather than options—would be doing the real work in that judgment?
