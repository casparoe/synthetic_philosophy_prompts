In archaeological interpretation, I keep encountering a specific inferential trajectory, and I wonder whether it has a recognized name. The pattern runs as follows. An artifact class or spatial configuration—say, deliberately broken ceramics deposited in foundation trenches, or weapons placed in non-military burial contexts—is initially interpreted as direct material expression of a shared belief or institutional rule. Critics then point out that practical or post-depositional processes could produce the same pattern: construction fill, later disturbance, or functional reuse. Rather than treating this as a defeater, the interpreter typically refines the inferred belief: the institution must have been so deeply internalized that it structured even mundane practical activities, or the practical process itself must be reconceived as the ritual's material vehicle. Subsequently, when the same artifact appears in contexts where neither practical nor ritual explanations seem available, its absence is accounted for by domain restrictions inferred from the very cases that originally supported the belief. The framework thus becomes more specific about the belief's scope and intensity precisely as simpler formulations fail, without ever reaching a clear falsification point.

Does this trajectory correspond to an established concept—distinct from an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, from general confirmation bias, and from hermeneutic circularity—specifically within archaeological or historical inference? If there is no established term, I am not looking for a bare label, but for a precise characterization of the phenomenon: what exactly distinguishes it from legitimate theoretical refinement, where its logical boundaries lie, and whether there is a principled way to block it without abandoning interpretive inference altogether.
