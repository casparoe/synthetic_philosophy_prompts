In popular and philosophical expositions of quantum foundations, the Everett interpretation is frequently rendered as a tree that branches with each measurement event: every limb realizes one possible outcome, so no collapse occurs—reality simply continues along all branches—while the Copenhagen view is said to prune the tree to a single limb via an irreducible collapse, and Bohmian mechanics is described as a single trunk guided deterministically by a pilot wave. Audit this branching-tree analogy as an argument in the comparative evaluation of these three interpretations. Provide an explicit correspondence table: which features of botanical branching map onto unitary evolution, decoherence, the emergence of quasi-classical worlds, and the appearance of definite outcomes in Everett; which map onto the projection postulate in Copenhagen; and which map onto the configuration-space trajectory and hidden-variables ontology in Bohm. Then identify the disanalogies that matter: where biological growth, temporal directionality, spatial extension, or teleological organization are imported into the quantum domain without justification, and whether these imports bias the comparison—particularly the intuition that multiplying branches is ontologically extravagant. Assess whether the analogy genuinely licenses the conclusion that Everett avoids an ad hoc postulate, or whether, once the disanalogies are on the table, Copenhagen's "pruning" and Bohm's "single trunk" can be defended on independent grounds that the tree image obscures. Finally, propose a stricter analogy drawn from dynamics or information theory—rather than biology—that captures the structural difference between these interpretations without smuggling in assumptions about growth, spatial separation, or organic unity, and say what conclusion, if any, that replacement analogy actually supports.
