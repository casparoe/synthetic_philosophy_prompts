I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of perception and consciousness, and I've become fixated on a variant of Dennett's distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions that my study group developed. We think it proves far too much about the temporal location of consciousness, but none of us can see the weak joint. You are an expert philosopher of mind with particular knowledge of temporal phenomenology and predictive processing; please think carefully, begin by mapping the logical structure of the reasoning below, and then diagnose precisely where the argument fails.

Here is our reasoning, which we call the 'phenomenal latency debt' argument. (1) In the colour phi phenomenon, a subject sees a green dot at position A followed by a red dot at position B, yet reports a single dot that changes colour midway through motion — an intermediate state that never occurred physically. (2) On the Stalinesque reading, consciousness never contains the false intermediate; instead, the brain edits the narrative before it reaches awareness, delivering only the corrected sequence (green-at-A, then red-at-B-with-motion). (3) If this editing is genuinely Stalinesque — that is, it suppresses erroneous drafts entirely rather than allowing them into awareness and then overwriting them — then consciousness receives only post-processed narratives, never raw perceptual drafts. (4) Every temporal experience, from hearing a melody to feeling a sustained pressure, requires the sequential integration of discrete neural events; hence, if Stalinesque editing applies generally, all consciousness consists of reconstructed past sequences rather than direct registrations of present ones. (5) Crucially, each editorial suppression introduces a processing delay; for rapid successive stimuli these delays accumulate into what we term a 'phenomenal latency debt', meaning consciousness systematically trails physical reality by a measurable interval. (6) Therefore, on the generalised Stalinesque model, there is never any experience of the present moment — only a retrospectively assembled record of what has just happened — and the feeling that 'this is happening now' is itself a reconstructed artefact that can never be experienced as present. (7) Yet if consciousness is always displaced into the past, then subjects should be unable to react to stimuli within the latency-debt window, which contradicts standard reaction-time data; and if the model is restricted to avoid this empirical contradiction, it must allow some unedited access to the present, which collapses the Stalinesque account back into an Orwellian one. (8) So either consciousness lacks any temporal location whatsoever, or the Stalinesque revision of phi cannot be generalised without incoherence.

We find premises (2) through (5) the most attractive, and we are reluctant to abandon (6) because it captures something genuinely puzzling about the 'specious present'. Which premise or inference should we reject, and why did it seem plausible? In your answer, explain what the standard Stalinesque and Orwellian positions each have to pay to reject that step, and whether our invented notion of 'phenomenal latency debt' exposes a real tension or simply smuggles in an equivocation between neural processing time and phenomenal time.
