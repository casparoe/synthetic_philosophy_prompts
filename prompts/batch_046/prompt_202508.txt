I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I have been trying to understand why the debate over the analytic-synthetic distinction has not settled in the decades since Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" undermined it. The literature seems divided between philosophers who defend the distinction by appealing to conventional meaning or semantic rules, and critics who treat every proposed analytic statement as merely another synthetic belief open to revision. My working theory is that the stalemate is not a single disagreement but a cluster: one camp asks about epistemic privilege, another about linguistic convention, and perhaps a hidden third asks whether formal systems can contain statements whose truth depends solely on structural properties. In particular, I keep noticing that programming-language semantics draws a sharp line between syntactic well-formedness (what parses according to formal rules) and semantic evaluation (what computes under an interpretation), and this behaves differently from the examples philosophers usually discuss—logical truths, mathematical definitions, lexical stipulations—which makes me suspect that the two sides are answering different questions under one name. So I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: is the disagreement verbal, with "analytic" carrying different meanings in different camps? Is it substantive, with the sides disagreeing about which cases count as data? Is there an unacknowledged empirical question about how human cognition actually partitions knowledge? And specifically, could a precise formal distinction—such as the one made in type theory or programming-language semantics between syntax and interpretation—actually resolve anything, or does it merely reproduce the same ambiguity in technical notation? I want an honest answer about whether anything—a conceptual clarification, a formal result, or an empirical finding—could end the debate, or whether the underlying question is ill-posed and should be abandoned.
