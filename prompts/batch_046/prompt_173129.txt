After retiring from a long career in condensed-matter physics, I have recently begun working through problems at the intersection of decision theory and philosophy of mind. One framework that has particularly captured my attention is program equilibrium, in which rational agents do not merely select actions but commit to fully specified decision procedures that may take other agents' procedures as direct inputs. Unlike standard strategic interaction, where intentions remain concealed behind observed behavior, this architecture seems to build transparency into the structure of choice itself—there are no hidden mental states, only executable logic available for direct inspection.

This observation has led me to conflate, I suspect, two issues that ought to be kept distinct. If the classical problem of other minds is fundamentally a difficulty arising from informational opacity—the inability to observe another consciousness directly—then would not a world of perfect procedural transparency, whether achieved through logical architecture or imagined through a hypothetical neural interface, simply dissolve the philosophical puzzle by removing the conditions that make it seem mysterious? Or is there still a residual gap, not of missing information but of subjective presence, that persists even when every procedural detail is shared and no mental state remains hidden?
