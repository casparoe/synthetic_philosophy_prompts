I've been returning to Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the abstraction he called the public — the idea that modern media dissolves individual ethical responsibility by substituting an anonymous crowd for concrete relationships. The usual extension to social media feels almost too easy: algorithmic feeds seem like pure leveling. But I want to push the analogy into a region where it might break, because I think there's a structural feature of current platforms that Kierkegaard never faced.

Consider algorithmic feeds in which a substantial portion of visible engagement — reactive sentiment, commentary, and apparent consensus — is generated or amplified by synthetic agents: recommendation bots, LLM-generated replies, and engagement-farming networks. The result is not merely a crowd of hidden individuals, but a phantom public: a statistical audience that behaves like a collective agent in shaping opinion, yet has no corresponding set of real human subjects behind it. The leveling here operates on data traces rather than on persons.

If Kierkegaard were alive today and confronted with this architecture, how would he extend — or revise — his diagnosis? Would he treat the phantom public as an intensification of leveling, since now even the residual individuality that might resist the crowd is replaced by algorithmic noise? Or would he argue that his original critique depends on there being actual individuals to be dissolved into the abstraction, and that once part of the crowd is literally non-human, the moral pathology changes into something else entirely — perhaps a form of indirectness that no longer even pretends to connect person to person?

I'm particularly interested in whether he would maintain that genuine ethical responsibility requires an unmediated relation to another concrete individual, and if so, whether algorithmic intermediation introduces a new category of indirectness beyond what he described in his analysis of the press. Where does the analogy hold, and where does it collapse?
