I have recently begun working with refugee resettlement programs, and I keep encountering situations where well-meaning Western counselors interpret local moral practices as either deficient or merely different, without being able to articulate why. This has made the WEIRD-psychology literature on folk morality feel urgent rather than academic: I need to understand whether cross-cultural divergence in moral intuition undermines moral objectivity or merely reflects different weightings of shared values.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Design a single, realistic scenario—set in an actual workplace or clinical setting, not a science-fictional or trolley-style abstraction—in which two cultures (one WEIRD and one non-WEIRD, specified only by their documented differences in moral attention, not by invented customs) disagree about whether a particular action is wrong. The disagreement must not be about whether a harm occurred; both cultures must recognize the same harm, but they must assign it radically different moral weight because of how their folk morality structures binding versus individualizing concerns. Engineer the case so that a straightforward moral realist (who holds that moral truths are culture-independent and at least one party can be in error) is forced to conclude that the non-WEIRD culture is not merely weighting differently but is systematically blind to a moral reason—yet this conclusion must depend on a feature that, if removed, makes the realist's judgment collapse into mere assertion. Meanwhile, engineer it so that a specific form of moral pluralism—the view that distinct moral frameworks can be fully valid even when they yield contradictory judgments about particular cases, provided they share a common normative core—is forced into the counterintuitive position that the WEIRD counselor's outrage is not just different but actively illegitimate within the pluralist's own framework, which is the opposite of the usual direction in which pluralism is the more permissive view. After presenting the scenario in one paragraph, walk through what each theory says and why, identify the exact structural feature that makes the verdicts reverse (for instance, whether the disagreement concerns second-order norms about who bears the burden of moral justification), and explain how a defender of pluralism would attack the case by arguing that the feature has been smuggled in rather than generated. Finally, state clearly what evidence or argument would change your assessment of which theory handles the case correctly—whether it is empirical data about the universality of certain moral perceptions, or a philosophical objection about the coherence of non-commensurable frameworks.
