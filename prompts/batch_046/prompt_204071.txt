As a novelist who writes in close first person and worries how much self-knowledge a narrator can genuinely claim, I have been turning over an argument about Descartes’s cogito that seems to prove far more than it should, and I would like you to diagnose exactly where it collapses.

(1) The cogito establishes that whenever a first-person judgment that one is thinking is genuinely occurrent, the existence of the thinker follows with absolute certainty, since an act of thinking requires an agent.

(2) The logical form of this inference—that a mental act entails the existence of the subject performing it—is grammatically invariant: if it is true that S thinks, then it must be true that S exists, regardless of whether S is indexed by I, she, or the protagonist of a narrative.

(3) Therefore, whenever I observe or narrate that some specific subject is thinking—whether a stranger on the street, a figure in a dream, or a character in one of my novels—I may apply the same inference and derive with Cartesian certainty that that particular subject exists.

(4) But this is plainly false. I do not possess Cartesian certainty about other minds, and in fiction the narrated thinker may have no real existence at all.

(5) To avoid this overgeneralization, one might restrict the cogito to first-person indexicals, arguing that only in the case of I is the subject immediately present to itself, so only there does the inference carry its special metaphysical force.

(6) Yet if the inference’s validity depends essentially on grammatical person or on an immediate self-presence unavailable in other cases, then it is not a universal logical or ontological principle about thought and existence. It becomes a linguistic peculiarity or a phenomenological feature of self-reference, not a proof of substantial being.

(7) Consequently, the cogito either proves too much—granting me indubitable knowledge of every narrated or observed thinker—or it proves too little, reducing to a claim about the use of the first-person pronoun rather than establishing the self as a substance.

If I must choose, I should prefer to preserve the metaphysical weight of the inference rather than let it collapse into a point about language or narrative voice, but I want to see precisely what that costs. Which step should I reject, and why did that step initially seem unavoidable? In your answer, please explain why the step looked plausible, then indicate what a Cartesian internalist, a Wittgensteinian externalist about other minds, and a phenomenological reader of first-person narrative each must concede or defend in order to block the argument, and tell me which rejection you judge least costly.
