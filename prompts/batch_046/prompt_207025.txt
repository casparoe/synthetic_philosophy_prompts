I am drafting an essay for a course on comparative historiography, and I want to pressure-test an argument I developed in discussion with a friend who works on archival theory. The essay contends that microhistory and history from below—often celebrated as correctives to elite-centered and state-centered narratives—remain structurally dependent on what I call bureaucratic residue: the dense, accidental traces of ordinary life that survive only because state or ecclesiastical institutions overreached into private affairs through inquisitorial courts, conscription lists, or poor-relief ledgers. I invoke Carlo Ginzburg’s well-known study of the Friulian miller Menocchio not to rehearse his conclusions, but to observe that Menocchio’s voice reaches us precisely through the machinery of an inquisitorial court he never sought out, suggesting that microhistorians may practice a kind of parasitic hermeneutics—extracting subaltern meaning from archives designed to suppress it. I contrast this with conventional state history, which reads identical documents as intentional instruments of governance, and I ask whether this framing unfairly collapses the distinction between source conditions and interpretive agency, or whether it ignores genuinely autonomous subaltern archives such as guild records or local oral repositories. Could you comment on the coherence of the bureaucratic-residue thesis, identify any obvious historiographical objections, and assess whether the contrast with macro-level state narratives holds up?
