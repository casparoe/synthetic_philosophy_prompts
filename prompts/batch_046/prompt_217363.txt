hi — i'm a phd student in philosophy of action (been reading too much Williams and probably not enough sleep). i've noticed a weird pattern in internal vs external reasons debates that i can't pin down to an existing term. here's what happens: you start with the standard internalist line — if an agent doesn't have the relevent desire, they don't have that reason. then someone throws a hard case at them, where the agent clearly lacks the desire but we still want to say there's a reason (say, a moral one). so they reply that the reason is still internal, because it hooks onto some other desire the agent already has — but when you press them on what that connecting desire actually is, they either postulate something really thin like 'a desire to be rational' or they quietly slide into talking about reasons from an objective point of view, which is basically externalism. once the pressure's off, they slided back to the strong internalist claim for the next case. it's not quite motte-and-bailey, because the two positions aren't a defensible core and an indefensible extension — they're more like two equally contentious claims that share the word 'internal'. is there an established concept for this kind of motivational anchoring drift? if not, can you name and define it, and draw the edges clearly so it doesn't just collapse into equivocation or ordinary ambiguity? i want something that captures how the internal relation keeps being renegotiated to avoid counterexamples without ever admitting it's become external.
