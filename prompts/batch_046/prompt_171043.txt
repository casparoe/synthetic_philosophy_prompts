In seminars on value theory, colleagues keep reaching for a comparison between wrong-kind reasons and adversarial perturbations in deep image classifiers: a bribe or threat that gets someone to admire a painting is said to work like a noise pattern that flips a network’s output without altering the ground-truth properties that should fix the label. The analogy is used to license a specific conclusion—namely, that wrong-kind reasons are not internal failures of normative competence but external exploits of an intact evaluative mechanism, comparable to corrupting an input channel rather than damaging the weights. I want a rigorous audit. First, lay out the correspondence table in detail: what plays the role of the classifier architecture, the training distribution, the ground-truth labels, the adversarial noise vector, the robustness-training regime, and the post-hoc saliency map? Then isolate the disanalogies that actually bear on the normative conclusion—especially the classifier’s absence of autonomy, transparency, intentional uptake, and self-correction, and whether these differences undermine the “hacking” picture or merely ornament it. Next, identify what the analogy quietly imports about practical reason: does it presuppose a passivist, pattern-matching model of normative cognition that makes being moved by wrong-kind reasons look like a system glitch rather than like responding to considerations one ought to reject? Finally, say whether the conclusion—that wrong-kind reasons are best understood as external system errors rather than as defects in the agent’s responsiveness to value—survives once those breaks are registered, and if it does not, propose what structural feature a more adequate analogy should preserve. Keep the analysis self-contained: no bibliographic appeals, and treat the analogy on its own terms rather than as a commentary on any particular author’s formulation.
