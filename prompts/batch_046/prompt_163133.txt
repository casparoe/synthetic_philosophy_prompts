I am preparing the final session of a graduate seminar on spacetime ontology, and rather than ending with a conventional summary of the semester, I would like to offer the students a poem that captures the conceptual tension we have been tracing through Einstein's hole argument. Throughout our discussions we have moved from the 1913 worry that generally covariant field equations fail to determine the metric uniquely inside a region empty of matter—the "hole"—to the point-coincidence resolution, yet I have noticed that students often grasp the formal resolution without feeling why the substantivalist position was ever compelling in the first place. I want a poem of about twelve to fourteen lines, spoken in the first person by a single spacetime point that finds itself inside such a hole, uncertain whether it is being assigned to itself under metric g or displaced under the actively diffeomorphically transformed metric g'. The poem should observe a strict rotational constraint: the six terms point, field, hole, coincide, identity, and measure should each govern one line in fixed repeating sequence, so that the poem runs through two full cycles before reaching a final line that gathers all six terms into one syntactic unit. This formal repetition should mirror the argument's logic—each rotation enacting another coordinate reassignment that fails to fix the point's intrinsic nature—while the closing convergence should enact the relationalist resolution, suggesting that the point's identity is secured only by the coincidence of world-lines and field values rather than by any bare substrate. The imagery should be spare and geometric rather than narrative, accurate enough that a philosopher of physics recognizes the distinction between active and passive transformations and the specific failure of uniqueness, but rendered through the point's own disorientation rather than through technical exposition. There is no need for citations, references to specific texts, or a bibliography; the philosophical work should be done entirely by the form and the voice.
