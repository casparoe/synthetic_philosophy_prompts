I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on population ethics and the metaphysics of harm, and in a recent working group on the procreation asymmetry I sketched the following argument, which now strikes me as proving far more than its premises should allow. I want to locate the error—if there is one—and understand what each candidate rejection costs my broader framework.

(1) The standard explanation of the procreation asymmetry relies on a counterfactual-comparative account of harm: an act harms a particular subject only if it makes that subject worse off than they otherwise would have been. (2) Applying this to procreation: creating a child who will suffer makes that child worse off than the counterfactual state of never existing, so it is wrong; but creating a happy child makes no one worse off, since non-existence is not a deprivation anyone suffers. (3) For this explanation to be coherent, the counterfactual baseline must be applied consistently: if non-existence can serve as the “otherwise” state for the harmed subject in the suffering case, it must be available as the comparison state in structurally analogous cases. (4) Now consider death. If a person dies young, they enter precisely the same counterfactual state—non-existence—that served as the baseline in (2). (5) Therefore, on the same counterfactual-comparative account, death does not harm the deceased person, because in non-existence there is no subject present to bear the comparison; the deprivation of future goods is not experienced or suffered by anyone. (6) But if death is not directly harmful to the one who dies, then the direct wrongness of murder collapses into purely indirect considerations—grief to survivors, violation of autonomy, social instability—which seems to evacuate the special gravity we ordinarily assign to killing. (7) One might resist by saying that in death there is an antecedent existing subject who is deprived, whereas in procreation there is no antecedent subject; but if the harm analysis in (1) requires an antecedent subject to bear the counterfactual comparison, then in the suffering-procreation case there is also no antecedent subject prior to creation—only a post-creation subject comparing themselves to a counterfactual non-existence. (8) So either the counterfactual account fails to ground the asymmetry without also undermining the harm of death, or we must accept that killing is not directly harmful to the victim—both of which seem intolerable.

Which step should I reject? I am particularly reluctant to abandon (1) or (6), since the person-affecting intuition behind the asymmetry and the direct wrongness of murder both seem central to my view. Is there a principled way to distinguish the baseline comparison in procreation from that in death—perhaps by appealing to the direction of causal dependence, a modal constraint on personal identity, or to whether the comparison requires an actual prior stage of the subject—without introducing an ad hoc asymmetry? And if I do draw that distinction, what does it commit me to regarding the metaphysics of the subject and the conditions under which a harm can be assigned?
