I spent three decades running controlled field studies in behavioral ecology, and since retirement I have tried to treat philosophy as something more rigorous than a hobby. I now serve on the clinical-ethics review board of a long-term neuropsychiatric facility, where we convene monthly to classify adverse events—medication refusal, aggressive outbursts, deliberate protocol violations—into punitive, rehabilitative, or neutral institutional responses. The board is divided between clinicians who want conceptual cover and administrators who want a defensible record. I want to translate Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument against moral responsibility into a reusable decision procedure that a non-philosopher can execute in about twenty minutes before a vote. As I understand the argument, true moral responsibility requires that an agent be causa sui—the genuine origin of her own mental nature and choices—because only then could she be responsible for the conditions that produce her actions; yet nothing can be causa sui, since causing oneself requires already existing prior to that causal act; therefore true moral responsibility is impossible. I need this rendered as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the precise questions to ask about an agent’s causal genealogy, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure stops with a negative verdict on true responsibility, and where it explicitly hands the remaining institutional choice—whether to sanction despite the absence of true responsibility—to human judgment because the issue is values rather than fact. The procedure must survive three worked applications: a patient with frontotemporal dementia who strikes a nurse; a fully capacitated patient who covertly discontinues a prescribed trial drug and suffers harm; and a third case in which the procedure yields a conclusion that the board’s clinical members will find unwelcome because it undermines their preferred rationale for punishment. It should also state, without evasion, what it cannot do: which causal distinctions it collapses, which background conditions it is not equipped to trace, and the specific circumstances in which applying it mechanically will misfire. The deliverable is not a single verdict but a portable protocol, with hard cases embedded, that respects both the logic of the argument and the practical limits of a committee that must sign off.
