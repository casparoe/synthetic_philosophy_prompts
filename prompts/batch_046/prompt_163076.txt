I'm writing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on whether mass starvation should be understood as a natural disaster or as a political crime, drawing on Amartya Sen's entitlement framework and its well-known applications to the 1943 Bengal famine, the Irish Great Famine, and the mortality crisis during China's Great Leap Forward. Rather than rehearsing Sen's familiar democratic-prevention thesis, I want to identify which remaining conceptual questions in this space are actually upstream of policy design—questions that philosophers and political theorists could settle without running new econometric models. Specifically, I'm weighing three unsettled lines of work that are rarely separated clearly in the literature. The first is what I will call the counterfactual attribution problem: how to conceptually isolate the causal weight of discrete political choices—grain export mandates, information suppression, or forced procurement—from concurrent environmental stress without falling into unfalsifiable historical narrative. The second is the normative boundary problem: the precise conditions under which a state's failure to prevent entitlement collapse becomes a culpable omission rather than a tragic incapacity, especially in regimes that lack democratic accountability but are not purely predatory. The third is the internal pluralism problem: whether Sen's correlation between democracy and famine prevention survives conceptually once we require protection not merely for electoral majorities but for internally marginalized groups, which raises questions about representation, group rights, and the limits of procedural safeguards.

If you were advising a lean interdisciplinary research group—say, three philosophers and two political theorists with three years of funding and no mandate to collect primary data—how should they rank these three lines of inquiry by importance and tractability? I want a defended agenda, not a survey: for each line, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, what kind of conceptual breakthrough would cause you to reorder the priorities, and why the work is suitable for conceptual analysis rather than empirical measurement. Please state how confident you are in the overall ranking, and explicitly identify the one point—whether a dependency claim, a tractability judgment, or a normative boundary condition—about which you are least sure.
