I've been working through the literature on epiphenomenalism and the so-called paradox of phenomenal judgment — the worry that if phenomenal properties are causally inert, they cannot cause our beliefs about them, which seems to undermine any claim that we actually know what consciousness is like. In my reading group, the discussion has stalled in a peculiar way: defenders of epiphenomenalism insist that we have a direct, non-causal form of acquaintance with our own phenomenal states, so causal exclusion doesn't touch our epistemic access; critics reply that this "acquaintance" is just a renamed mystery, and that without some causal or functional link our judgments are no better than guesses. Both sides seem to agree that if there were a hidden causal pathway the paradox would dissolve, and both agree that pure coincidence isn't acceptable — yet nobody shifts position.

I'm wondering whether this is actually a disagreement about consciousness at all, or rather about what counts as legitimate epistemic access. More specifically, I've been playing with a distinction that doesn't come up in the standard treatments: between judgments that are phenomenally anchored (structured or informed by the very quality they report) and judgments that are functionally proxy-tracked (produced by a subpersonal system that monitors neural correlates without being informed by the qualia themselves). If defenders are implicitly assuming the first and critics are implicitly assuming the second is the only viable option, then they might be answering different questions under the heading "Can we know consciousness if it's epiphenomenal?" Is the impasse verbal — about the word "knowledge" or "access" — or substantive, with a hidden empirical question about whether introspection operates through direct acquaintance or proxy-tracking? Or is the question itself ill-posed because it assumes we can cleanly separate a judgment's causal role from its epistemic justification? Please don't just take a side on epiphenomenalism. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement: what each side actually commits to about the source of phenomenal judgments, whether there's a formal or conceptual distinction that could break the deadlock, and what — if anything — would count as progress here. Would it take a model of introspective architecture, a refined concept of non-causal acquaintance, or is the stalemate a sign that we're demanding the wrong kind of explanation? And please push back if I'm framing this poorly: if my "phenomenally anchored versus proxy-tracked" distinction confuses rather than clarifies, or if the task as posed can't be answered well without speculating about neuroscience we don't have, say so plainly rather than accommodating me.
