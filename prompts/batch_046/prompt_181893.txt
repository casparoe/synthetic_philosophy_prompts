Construct a one-paragraph thought experiment set in a working scientific facility—no science-fiction elements, no omniscient predictors—featuring three technicians faced with repairing a specialized atmospheric sensor. Technician A has fully internalized the technical manual (complete propositional knowledge) but, due to an acquired proprioceptive deficit, cannot acquire or execute the motor sequence; Technician B was trained solely through embodied apprenticeship, is illiterate, and therefore possesses none of the manual's propositional content, yet repairs the sensor flawlessly; Technician C possesses both literacy and intact motor capacity, but was taught the identical physical procedure through a deceptive pedagogical framing—she was told the motions calibrate the sensor when they in fact execute an emergency vent, though the bodily execution remains correct. After presenting the scenario, analyze what each figure demonstrates about the relationship between knowing-how and knowing-that: specifically, state whether A lacks knowing-how despite knowing-that, whether B has knowing-how without knowing-that, and whether C's false propositional belief about the purpose of her actions undermines her knowing-how or leaves it intact. Present the reasoning for both a Rylean separation of the two categories and an intellectualist reduction of knowing-how to a subset of knowing-that. Then identify the precise structural feature—deception about purpose versus mechanism versus outcome—that generates the divergence between these two philosophical positions in C's case, explain what verdict each position delivers and why, and describe how the analysis collapses if that feature is replaced by a false belief concerning only an inert side-effect of the repair.
