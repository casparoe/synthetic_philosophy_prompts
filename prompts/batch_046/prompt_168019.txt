Consider the institutional framework used by maritime heritage registries and museum authorities to classify historic vessels. Under this framework, a ship that has undergone decades of incremental repair—such that none of its original structural timbers remain—is still registered under its original identity, granted protected heritage status, and made eligible for restoration funding, so long as the replacements occurred continuously and the vessel never ceased to be an operational, named entity. By contrast, a vessel rebuilt from the original builder's archived plans and specifications, using entirely new materials and producing a form and function closer to the original design than the incrementally repaired ship, is classified as a replica, denied heritage registration, and excluded from the same funding streams. At the same time, these registries often preserve the legal identity of a ship for liability, inheritance, and flag-state purposes even when its hull is intact but its crew, registry, and operational purpose have been completely replaced.

What implicit theory, or cluster of theories, of diachronic object identity does this framework presuppose? Reconstruct the view that makes sense of treating material discontinuity-with-operational-continuity as identity-preserving, while treating formal fidelity-with-material-discontinuity as identity-breaking, and while treating institutional-role-continuity as sufficient for legal persistence despite functional transformation. Is the framework philosophically coherent, or do its heritage-classification rules and its legal-identity rules depend on incompatible commitments—say, to spatio-temporal continuity of parts versus persistence of a design versus socially maintained status? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Then specify what a registration policy built consistently on a design-functional theory, or on a purely social-institutional theory, would have to change about eligibility criteria, funding allocation, and the status of reconstructed vessels.
