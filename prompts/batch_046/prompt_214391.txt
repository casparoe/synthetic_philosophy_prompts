You are a professional philosopher with expertise in social philosophy, the ethics of intergenerational choice, and the conceptual analysis of macro-historical transitions. Please begin by carefully brainstorming the main distinctions at play—especially between person-affecting evaluation, aggregation across non-overlapping populations, and project-based reasoning—before addressing my question.

I have been studying debates over whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake, particularly the comparison between foraging societies—often characterized by high leisure, low inequality, and limited scale—and early agricultural societies, which typically show longer working hours, greater disease burden, sharper hierarchy, but also larger populations and the conditions for cumulative technological change. In these debates I keep encountering a specific argumentative configuration that I cannot name, and I want to know whether it has an established conceptual home.

The pattern runs as follows. A critic observes that the first generations of agriculturalists lived worse lives, by standard welfare metrics, than their foraging contemporaries. A defender replies that agriculture should not be judged by its initial welfare costs, because it functioned like a long-term investment: it sacrificed the welfare of early practitioners to generate the population density, division of labor, and institutional complexity that eventually made modern welfare possible. The critic then objects that this reasoning treats actual people in past societies as mere instrumental capital for a civilization-project they never joined, and that aggregating welfare across millennia implicitly constructs a single trans-historical agent out of populations that never coexisted and could not consent.

My interest is not in the empirical history but in the underlying evaluative architecture. Both sides appear to be applying a form of intertemporal comparison, yet they diverge on whether the correct unit of evaluation is epoch-local—each generation assessed on its own terms—or trans-epochal, treating human history as a continuous project whose moral status depends on its final yield. Is there an established term for this precise framing: the treatment of a macro-historical transition as an investment problem whose moral evaluation requires aggregating welfare across non-overlapping populations, and the corresponding dispute over whether such aggregation is legitimate? I am not satisfied with a bare label; I need to know whether philosophy has already carved out this exact distinction, how it relates to Derek Parfit's person-affecting views and to the non-identity problem, and where its boundaries lie—especially how it differs from ordinary path dependency or from standard intergenerational equity. If no established concept captures it precisely, please propose a name, provide a precise definition, and identify boundary cases that separate it from adjacent ideas.
