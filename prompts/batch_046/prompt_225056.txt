I am drafting notes for a seminar on moral genealogy and want a consistency audit before presenting. Shaped by my work as a novelist, I have settled on five commitments. First, I read the First Essay as showing that the term good originally expressed aristocratic self-affirmation, and that the slave revolt in morality was a genuinely creative inversion of value rather than mere reactive bitterness. Second, I take the genealogy overall to be descriptive, not prescriptive: it diagnoses the contingent origin of moral values without advocating a return to master morality. Third, I understand ressentiment as an imaginative restructuring of value hierarchies—analogous to how a novelist rewrites a character's motives—rather than as raw affective malice. Fourth, I think the ascetic ideal attacked in the Third Essay applies only to life-denying discipline, which allows the rigorous craft-asceticism of artistic practice to escape Nietzsche's condemnation. Fifth, I hold that if genealogy reveals the contingent origins of all moral values, then the novelist's construction of moral worlds in fiction operates as a kind of genealogical revelation rather than as escapist fantasy. Please examine whether these hang together. For each pair that is genuinely in tension—distinguishing these from apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection—state the conflict, rank them by severity, identify the least costly revision, and indicate which single change would resolve the most conflict.
