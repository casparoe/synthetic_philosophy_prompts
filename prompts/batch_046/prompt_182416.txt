As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am developing a narrative feature on the tension between inside-view reasoning and outside-view statistical humility—specifically, the kind of epistemic modesty required when an individual’s intimate grasp of a particular case conflicts with base-rate knowledge about structurally similar cases. Rather than illustrating this through the usual forecasting tournaments or investment analogies, I want a short story set within a fictional institution: the Registry of Abandoned Hypotheses, an archive where researchers deposit failed predictions, and where senior curators maintain calibrated records of disciplinary failure rates. The protagonist should be one such curator—someone whose daily work consists of comparing the statistical fate of past hypotheses—who is approached by a junior researcher convinced that her current project, despite bearing every formal resemblance to previously abandoned lines of inquiry, is genuinely different in a way that statistics cannot capture. The narrative should center on a specific, high-stakes decision—whether the curator will endorse the researcher’s funding application—without resolving the tension into a neat moral. The story should make visible, through dialogue and institutional ritual rather than exposition, what it feels like to hold both the inside view and the outside view simultaneously without being able to synthesize them. Please avoid naming specific philosophers or forecasting frameworks; let the conceptual conflict emerge organically from the characters’ reasoning and the archive’s practices.
