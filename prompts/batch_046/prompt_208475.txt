I serve as the rotating chair of an institutional review board for a consortium of cognitive-science laboratories, and every month we must render a collective verdict—approve, approve with modifications, or reject—on proposed studies involving human participants. Our charter requires us to aggregate the judgments of five appointed members, each of whom separately evaluates three premises: whether the study design minimizes foreseeable harm (P1), whether informed consent is genuinely voluntary given the recruitment context (P2), and whether the scientific value justifies the participant burden (P3). From these premises each member derives an overall conclusion (C). Because we recently adopted a transparent aggregation protocol, we regularly encounter the structural conflict in judgment aggregation where premise-based majority voting yields a different collective result than conclusion-based majority voting. I need a reusable decision procedure—something I can run in real time during a two-hour meeting without consulting a philosopher—to determine which aggregation path takes priority, when the conflict is serious enough to trigger a mandatory suspension of the vote, and how to document the reasoning so that an external auditor can reconstruct it. The procedure should specify the exact conditions for premise-priority versus conclusion-priority, the threshold at which the dilemma is declared active, the steps for resolving it through structured deliberation rather than arbitrary chair discretion, and the explicit points where logic ends and value judgment begins. It should also include a small set of diagnostic tests—questions I can put to the members—to distinguish factual disagreement about a premise from normative disagreement about its weight, and it should state what documentation is required at each branch.

Please ground the translation in the actual logic of judgment aggregation, but do not ask me to look up literature: I need this to work in a room with no web access and no citation library. Apply the procedure to three cases. First, a neurofeedback study in which four members agree on P1 and P2 but split 3-2 on P3, with premise-based aggregation pointing toward approval and conclusion-based voting yielding 3-2 reject. Second, a longitudinal study of adolescent decision-making in which all five members agree unanimously on every premise, yet three reach "approve with modifications" and two reach "approve," producing a conclusion-level tie despite unanimous premises. Third, an invasive cortical-recording proposal in non-therapeutic volunteers, where premises split 3-2 in one direction but conclusions split 3-2 in the opposite direction, and where my own judgment is that the study should be rejected even though the procedure points elsewhere. For each case, show the step-by-step execution, identify the precise point where human judgment is unavoidable, and specify what the meeting minutes must record. Then flag the classes of proposals for which the procedure is known to misfire—particularly where a single member invokes an epistemic veto on one premise—and state exactly what I, as chair, should do in those instances without violating the board's charter. Finally, include one explicit limitation: a scenario in which the procedure gives an answer that conflicts with a widely shared moral intuition about vulnerable populations, and explain how the chair should report that conflict without overturning the procedure arbitrarily.
