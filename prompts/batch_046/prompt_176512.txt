Since I recently began drafting case-level exemptions for a regulatory body that applies ethical standards to professional conduct, I have found myself caught in a recurring dispute: my judgments in individual cases seem to turn on subtle contextual features that resist codification, yet my colleagues consistently reconstruct those same judgments as applications of unstated, defeasible general rules. Rather than continue this circular disagreement, I want you to produce the joint statement that a committed moral particularist and a committed moral generalist could both sign after a serious adversarial collaboration—one that takes my institutional setting seriously rather than treating it as an afterthought.

Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims about moral reasons, contextual relevance, institutional decision-making, and the limits of explicit rules. Second, a disagreements table in which each side’s position is formulated in wording its own proponents would accept—particularly regarding whether a principle’s failure in a particular case undermines the principle or merely activates an implicit exception clause, whether moral knowledge requires explicit articulation of the reasons that guide judgment, and whether bureaucratic particularism inevitably collapses into arbitrariness. Third, the cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party, with special attention to the three noted above. For each crux, specify a concrete institutional scenario or case that both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each side predicts the scenario will show. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal—say, about what counts as a “rule” versus a “heuristic”—say exactly which part and why.

Please write formally, do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not treat either position as obviously correct.
