I have been thinking through Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator without any formal training, and I keep finding explanations that either treat sympathy as simple emotional copying or turn the spectator into an abstract rule-machine. Before I go further, I want a complete specification of what any adequate theory of this mechanism must actually deliver. Write the desiderata: the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the boundary cases it must handle, and the results it must stay consistent with, together with the consideration behind each. Mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular interpretive camps.

Specifically, the theory must treat sympathy as a proportional response rather than an identical one — we feel something like the agent's passion but scaled by our imagined distance, causal involvement, and social relation — and it must explain how this scaling works without reducing sympathy to either emotional contagion or rational calculation. It must maintain a strict distinction between sympathy (feeling with the agent) and approbation (the judgment of propriety that arises when we view the case through the impartial spectator), and it must account for cases where the two come apart: for example, when we understand and even share a wrongdoer's desperation yet still condemn the act because the spectator's view of the victim carries greater weight. This requires explaining what I would call asymmetric spectator failure, where sympathy for one party is stronger than sympathy for another but moral judgment still tracks the weaker-sympathy side, which rules out any theory that defines judgment as the mere averaging of emotional inputs.

The theory must also preserve the difference between the internal spectator — the imagined other inside our own breast — and the actual external audience, without collapsing both into a single mechanism of social conformity. It must allow for self-deception about our motives, since if the internal spectator were fully transparent to introspection we could not explain why people consistently misjudge their own actions. It must handle boundary sympathy: responses to the dead, to strangers across great distance, and to non-human animals, explaining why these are attenuated or structured differently rather than absent. It must remain consistent with basic psychological findings about perspective-taking and emotional resonance, but it cannot identify sympathy with either process alone.

Finally, indicate whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, whether full proportionality and complete impartiality conflict — and briefly assess how a pure emotional-contagion account and a normative-constructivist account stand against the list. I am not asking for citations, named scholars, or a bibliography; I want the criteria themselves and the reasoning behind them.
