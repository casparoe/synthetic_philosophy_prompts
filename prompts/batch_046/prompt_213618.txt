hey so im grading papers for this epistemology seminar and came across a student response that im not totally sure about. the exam prompt was: "A mathematician spends three months verifying a proof of conjecture C and finds no errors. Then she gets anonymous peer reviews from four experts she considers her equals, all saying there's a subtle mistake in lemma 3. She re-checks lemma 3 over and over and still can't spot the flaw; her reasoning looks intact to her. Is she rationally permitted to keep believing C? Analyze this using first-order vs. higher-order evidence, and discuss whether this is permissible level-splitting or impermissible epistemic akrasia."

the student wrote:

"In this case she should definitely keep believing C because her first-order evidence hasn't changed—she still has the proof and her reasoning is intact. The peer reviews only supply higher-order evidence about her reliability, not about C itself. This is exactly level-splitting: separating your first-order belief from your second-order belief about how good you are at forming beliefs. Since she hasn't lost confidence in her logical faculties, there's no akrasia. Akrasia only happens when you believe p but also believe you shouldn't believe p. Here she believes C and believes she's rational in believing C, so the levels align. The conciliationist view is wrong here because it ignores that first-order evidence takes priority over peer opinion. Therefore she's fully rational to maintain her belief."
