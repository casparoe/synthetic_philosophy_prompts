I have no formal background in philosophy or psychology, but I have been paying close attention to how my sister—who reads widely but has never taken an academic course—classifies her own mental life. She does not use technical vocabulary, yet her judgments are remarkably steady. Here is the record of her verdicts: she counts grief after a death, anger at a personal insult, fear upon encountering immediate physical danger, nostalgic longing for a place she grew up in, and moral outrage at distant reports of cruelty all as genuine emotions. By contrast, she denies that status to the mental satisfaction of solving a hard puzzle, the raw physical sensation of being overheated, the automatic hand-withdrawal reflex from a hot surface, and the intellectual feeling of suddenly grasping an idea.

One verdict complicates the pattern: she insists that a free-floating anxiety—waking her at three in the morning with no identifiable object or specific worry—is still an emotion, not merely a bodily malfunction.

Now I want her implicit rule tested against a new case. Imagine she listens to an instrumental piece of music with no lyrics, no triggered personal memories, and no conscious evaluation of the music's quality; she reports only a sudden warmth in her chest, tears, and a sense of being moved, with no accompanying thoughts.

Would she classify that as an emotion?

My interest is not primarily in the answer, but in identifying the principle she is actually applying. One possibility is that she requires both a distinctive phenomenological feeling and some form of aboutness, but allows that aboutness to be diffuse, implicit, or non-propositional rather than a fully formed judgment. Another possibility is that she runs a simpler bodily-profile rule: any state with the right physical and experiential signature counts, regardless of cognitive content. A third possibility—one I have wondered about—is that she distinguishes states that temporarily disrupt her sense of self-continuity from those that do not, and only the former earn the label.

Please predict her verdict on the music case, lay out the competing rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, state what each rule predicts here, and give your best guess with reasons. Then specify the single further case that would best discriminate among those rules. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in this analysis and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
