I serve on the Deployment Ethics Board for the Meridian Urban Infrastructure Consortium, and we are convening next week to approve or reject a pilot system called HYDRA-N, an adaptive water-allocation algorithm for drought-prone districts. The developers claim HYDRA-N satisfies a low-impact, side-effect-avoiding constraint: it will never reduce any household's allocation below its historical three-year rolling average, and it will never alter downstream chemical treatment protocols in ways that affect agricultural users, all without requiring the consortium to specify a complete social-welfare function or a full ranking of ecological versus economic values. A minority of board members—drawing on conceptual work in AI alignment—argues that this constraint is incoherent, because selecting the baseline and defining alteration already embed normative judgments about which states count as harm and which trade-offs are permissible; if these judgments remain implicit, the system merely disguises its value commitments behind technical parameters. The majority, led by the engineering team, insists that the criterion is operational and empirically testable, and that demanding full axiological transparency before deployment sets an impossible standard that would paralyze adaptive infrastructure.

Write a formal memo to the board recommending either approval with conditions or rejection, addressing specifically whether a negative side-effect criterion can be enforced without a complete specification of value. Your reasoning should treat the minority's objection with full seriousness—explain precisely how baseline selection and harm-definition are value-laden—without simply conceding defeat, and it should propose a concrete decision framework the board can apply, such as distinguishing implicit value commitments that remain locally revisable from those that become structurally obscured once embedded in path-dependent systems. The memo must include an honest assessment of the strongest argument on the losing side: if you recommend approval, acknowledge the risk that hidden normative premises could become locked in by infrastructure inertia; if you recommend rejection, acknowledge the practical cost of delaying drought adaptation in pursuit of a philosophical transparency that may have no institutional endpoint. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; ground the analysis in the conceptual architecture of the case itself.
