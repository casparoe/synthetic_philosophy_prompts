As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of history and collective memory, I have become preoccupied with what historians call "lost-cause" narratives—stories in which a defeated group transforms military or moral failure into a sustaining national myth. I am thinking specifically of four cases often discussed together: the American Confederate "Lost Cause," the Masada siege narrative in modern Israeli identity, the Kosovo Field mythology in Serbian nationalism, and the British "Dunkirk spirit" as a narrative of deliverance from catastrophic retreat. I want to understand whether these narratives are best evaluated by historical fidelity or by some other metric, and I believe the disagreement turns partly on whether we should treat them as "terminal myths" (myths about defeat and ending) rather than "foundational myths" (myths about origin and beginning).

For this purpose, please treat me as an observer of an adversarial collaboration between two camps.

Camp A—the Integrative Functionists—holds that these four narratives serve a necessary "social homeostatic" function (by analogy to physiological homeostasis): they stabilize collective identity across the trauma of defeat, provide moral exemplars of resilience, and allow pluralistic societies to integrate descendants of both victors and vanquished without requiring constant historical litigation. They maintain that the proper evaluation of such a narrative is not its correspondence to archival fact but its current capacity to sustain democratic cohesion without suppressing minority voices, and that attempts to dismantle these myths typically produce not enlightenment but reactive polarization.

Camp B—the Mnemonic Justice advocates—holds that these narratives are structurally falsifying: they systematically suppress central atrocities (chattel slavery in the Confederate case, the exclusion of Palestinian and other non-Jewish narratives in Masada's militarization, ethnic cleansing in the Kosovo myth, imperial overreach in the Dunkirk framing) and convert defeat into morally privileged martyrdom. They maintain that collective identity should be rebuilt through critical commemoration that records defeat without mythologizing it, and that the social cohesion produced by such myths is always purchased at the expense of marginalized groups and therefore cannot be weighed on the same scale as historical accuracy.

Please draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims—about the formal structure of lost-cause narratives and what distinguishes a terminal myth from a foundational one; about the four cases listed above; about the psychological and sociological mechanisms by which such narratives are transmitted across generations; and about what kind of empirical or historical finding would be required to resolve their central dispute. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

2. A disagreements table in which each camp's position is formulated in wording its own proponents would accept, with no straw-manning. The table should cover at least: (a) whether social homeostasis is a legitimate evaluative criterion for historical narratives; (b) whether terminal myths are structurally more resistant to archival correction than other historical errors; and (c) whether there exists a non-circular empirical measure of a myth's social benefit that could be traded against its historical distortion.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. For each crux, identify a concrete historical episode, policy change, or comparative study (drawn from the four cases or closely analogous ones) that both sides agree is relevant, and state in advance what each camp predicts that episode or study demonstrates.

4. A brief concluding section in which you state whether any part of the disagreement is verbal rather than substantive—specifically, whether "myth," "cohesion," or "justice" are being used with different but reconcilable meanings—and, if so, which part of the dispute dissolves once the terms are disambiguated.

Please write in formal American English, from the perspective of someone accustomed to scientific precision but new to philosophical method. Frame the arguments conceptually rather than exegetically, and do not invoke obscure theoretical apparatus that requires specialized memorization.
