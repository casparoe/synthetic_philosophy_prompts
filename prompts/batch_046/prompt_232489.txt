I have been working through a comparative logic project with a colleague, and we keep returning to Jain anekāntavāda—particularly syādvāda and its sevenfold predicative schema, saptabhaṅgī—because it appears to accomplish something that neither standard modal logic nor familiar paraconsistent frameworks quite capture. I want you to restate its core in the vocabulary I actually use: modal operators and accessibility relations, consistency conditions, truth-value gluts and gaps, and the object-language versus meta-language distinction. Be precise about the translation: take each of the seven syāt modes—simple affirmation, simple negation, joint affirmation-and-negation, inexpressibility, and their three compound combinations—and map each onto a specific logical state in my vocabulary, indicating for each whether the mapping is clean, requires distortion, or is impossible. Then deliver the residue report. Which elements survive intact, such as the principled refusal of unqualified universal affirmation? Which ones translate only with distortion—perhaps the ethical and epistemic role of naya (standpoint), or the idea that contradictory predicates can hold of the same object in the same respect without collapse? And what, if anything, has no expression at all in formal logical vocabulary—especially the thesis that partial perspectives are not merely subjective or epistemic limitations but are ontologically constitutive of the object known? Finally, tell me whether that residue points to a real deficiency in formal logic—something like an irreducible perspectival ontology—or whether it is largely metaphysical ornament layered over a logical pluralism I already understand. I do not want a historical survey or reading list; I want the translation itself and its systematic failures treated as the central philosophical result.
