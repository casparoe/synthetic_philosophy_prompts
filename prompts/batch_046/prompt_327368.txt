As a novelist developing an epistemological subplot, I have constructed an argument that applies Nozick's tracking conditions to an author's counterfactual knowledge of her characters, and it seems to show that tracking entails authors can never know any dispositional counterfactual about a protagonist they have designed. The steps are these. (1) Nozick's tracking requires that if S knows p, then in the nearest worlds where p is false, S does not believe p. (2) Suppose an author knows that her protagonist Elena would have refused a bribe offered on Tuesday — a counterfactual she has built into Elena's character. (3) In nearby worlds where Elena does accept the bribe — perhaps because of an editorial change or a slightly different narrative branch — the author might still hold the same artistic belief about Elena's deep disposition, since her belief is anchored to her design rather than to the actual plot outcome in that world. (4) Therefore in nearby false worlds she still believes the counterfactual, so she fails to track it, and by (1) she does not know it. (5) Generalizing: for any intended counterfactual disposition, there are nearby plot-divergent worlds in which the author retains the belief, so tracking denies authors all such counterfactual knowledge. (6) Yet authors plainly do intend and seem warranted in attributing these dispositions. Which step is the weakest, and what are the costs of rejecting it? In particular, does the standard remedy for closure failure — allowing that tracking is not preserved under entailment, or substituting a safety condition — rescue counterfactual author-knowledge, or does the problem lie in applying tracking across fictional worlds at all?
