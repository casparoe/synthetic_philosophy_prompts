Write a sestina — six stanzas of six lines followed by a three-line envoi, using the standard rotation of six end-words — about the paradox at the heart of Locke’s Some Thoughts Concerning Education. Locke insists that a child’s will must be broken early through custom, physical hardship (cold baths, hard shoes, thin clothing), and absolute obedience, because only through this broken will can reason later govern and produce a free, rational gentleman; at the same time, he holds that the smallest impressions made in tender infancy have lasting consequences, so education is not instruction but habituation. Use the end-words will, custom, reason, body, freedom, and habit, and arrange their rotation so that the first stanzas are dominated by body, custom, and broken will, the middle stanzas by habit and emerging reason, and the envoi by the meeting of will and reason. The poem should show, through the form’s repetitive returns, how Locke’s pedagogy circles back on itself: each return of an end-word should feel slightly altered, as custom becomes habit becomes reason. Do not resolve the paradox; leave open whether the freedom achieved is genuine autonomy or merely the internalised discipline of early submission. Write in roughly iambic pentameter, use British spelling, and make the poem accurate enough that a reader familiar with Locke’s educational letters recognises his specific regimen rather than a generic blank-slate theory.
