I've been going back and forth lately on whether to keep my lease or move closer to work, and it's made me weirdly obsessed with this decision theory thing my friend mentioned — the money pump. I brought it up with my roommates Alex and Jordan and they got into it.

Alex said if you let yourself be pumped you're just diachronically irrational, full stop. He said rationality means being consistent across time: if you prefer A over B on Monday and then B over A on Tuesday with no new information, you've broken a basic rule and someone can take all your cash in a loop.

Jordan said that's to rigid. She brought up this idea she calls a learning pump — the cycle isn't a mistake but part of updating your values after actually living with each option. She said if you pay a small cost each switch but gain real knowledge about what you actually care about, its not exploitation, it's investment.

Alex said that's just relabeling. He said the pump works regardless of your internal story; if there's a sequence of trades that leaves you back where you started but poorer, you've failed a consistency check, and calling it learning doesn't change the math.

Jordan countered that diachronic rationality shouldn't mean locking in past preferances when Friday-you has genuinely different reasons. She mentioned sophisticated planning — like, if you know you'll shift, you should account for that rather than pretend you're the same static agent. Then she said Alex treats the self like an object that can't change.

Alex said okay, but then there's no line between rational change and being a sucker. He said if every flip-flop is learning, then the idea of a money pump loses all bite, and you've basically said rationality is just whatever feels right in the moment.

Jordan admitted that's a risk, but said the opposite risk is worse: forcing yourself to stick with a Tuesday preference on Friday just to avoid being pumped, even when Friday-you has good reasons to differ.

They asked me to break the tie. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? Also please answer in American English, and I don't need any citations or reading lists, just your reasoning.
