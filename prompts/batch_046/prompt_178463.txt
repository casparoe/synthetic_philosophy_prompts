In debates over methodological individualism versus holism in social science, classical functionalists often lean on the analogy that a society is like a biological organism: just as a heart or liver has no independent purpose outside the living body, individual rituals, roles, or beliefs have no independent meaning outside the social totality. The analogy is meant to license the conclusion that social facts are sui generis—explainable only by other social facts—and that reducing sociology to psychology is as misguided as reducing physiology to chemistry. But recent biology itself has complicated the source domain: the holobiont view treats a single organism as a consortium of symbiotic species rather than a unified whole with clear boundaries, and evolutionary biologists now stress that many "organs" are exapted or redundant.

Audit this analogy rigorously. Keep the holobiont complication in view as you assess whether the biological source is coherent enough to support the sociological claim.

1. Explicit mapping of correspondences (about 150 words): Lay out the table of correspondences—society to organism, individual to organ or cell, social institution to physiological system, social dysfunction to disease, social cohesion to homeostasis, etc. Note which features of the biological source are actively doing argumentative work for the holist.

2. Where the mapping fractures (about 150 words): Identify disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion. Does a society possess a genome, a central nervous system, or a reproductive boundary? Can "social death" occur in the way biological death does? How does the absence of a unified genome or immune system in society affect the force of the comparison? Address whether the holobiont critique undermines the coherence of the source domain itself.

3. Smuggled normative and ontological commitments (about 120 words): What values does the analogy quietly import? Does treating society as an organism necessarily imply that the health or survival of the whole outweighs the autonomy or suffering of the parts? Does it presuppose functional integration where there may only be conflict, exploitation, or contingency?

4. Whether the holist licensing survives (about 150 words): Once the fractures are on the table, can the analogy still support the claim that social facts are irreducible to individual psychology? Or does the breakdown push us toward a different metaphysics—perhaps downward causation or emergent properties—without requiring full organic unity?

5. Verdict and a sharper comparison (about 100 words): State whether the organism analogy carries any real weight in the individualism/holism debate. Then propose one more precise comparison—perhaps drawn from distributed cognition, ecology, or systems theory—that captures what the holist wants without importing the broken biological commitments.
