I've been thinking about the standard embedding challenge for moral expressivism—the difficulty that arises when a sentence expressing a non-cognitive attitude is placed inside a conditional or disjunction, where its expressive character seems to vanish, yet inference remains valid. I understand that problem well enough, but I've noticed a related configuration that doesn't involve logical embedding at all, and I'm trying to determine whether it is just a variant of that challenge or something that needs separate treatment.

Consider a case where an agent genuinely holds an anti-deception attitude, expressed in full in ordinary discourse. The same agent then constructs an explicit decision model—say, for negotiating a contract—in which the 'disutility of deception' is entered as a negative coefficient. Within the computation, that coefficient functions purely as a quantitative parameter; the agent adjusts it, compares it against other weights, and derives strategies, all without expressing any attitude inside the model's operations. The attitude is causally present—it set the coefficient—but the reasoning that manipulates the coefficient carries no expressive force. A second case: in collaborative planning, a group member insists on treating a particular moral boundary as a hard constraint on the feasible region of a shared optimization problem. Outside the model, she asserts the boundary with full normative conviction; inside the model, it operates as an inert boundary condition, not as an endorsed evaluation being applied.

The pattern is therefore an attitude that remains structurally and causally active in practical reasoning but is semantically localized: it expresses in some contexts and operates as a mute parameter in others, without being negated, conditionalized, or attributed to another party. This differs from ordinary embedding because the attitude is not inside a propositional compound; it sits outside, feeding into an instrumental framework. It also differs from simple psychological compartmentalization, since the agent does not regard the parameterized version as inauthentic.

Does this specific localization pattern have an established name in the metaethical literature—perhaps something like expressive parameterization or attitudinal bracketing—and how is it usually distinguished from the embedding problem? If it lacks a specific term, I would like to understand how the boundaries should be drawn: what separates genuine parameterization from ordinary hypothetical reasoning, and are there borderline cases that look structurally similar but belong to a different category? I am not asking for sources or a reading list; I want to know whether the conceptual carving already exists and where its edges lie.
