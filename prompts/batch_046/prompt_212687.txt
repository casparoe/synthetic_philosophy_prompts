I do not have formal training in philosophy or political theory, but I have been thinking on my own about whether a country can be genuinely guilty—morally guilty, not just legally or practically responsible—for wrongs that were committed generations before anyone alive today was born. To clarify my own thinking, I wrote what I take to be a challenging exam question and then composed a student answer to it. I would like you to grade that answer as if you were the instructor or teaching assistant. Please do not evaluate the exam question itself; assess only the student response, and do so point by point.

The question was this: If a democratic state continues to enforce legal and economic structures that were originally established by an unjust treaty signed two centuries ago, and if present citizens—none of whom were alive at the time—derive ongoing material benefits from those structures, can the present nation be said to bear moral guilt, rather than merely historical responsibility, for the original displacement? In particular, is collective guilt impossible without a collective consciousness capable of remorse? Or might there be a distinct category—call it structural perpetuation guilt—where a collective is guilty not for initiating a wrong but for continuously maintaining the institutional conditions that allow its effects to persist?

The student answer is as follows.

Guilt, in its strictest sense, requires both causation and an appropriate mental state. Criminal guilt is always individual: a particular agent must have intended or recklessly caused harm. Because a nation has no single mind, it cannot form intentions, so it cannot be criminally guilty. Moral guilt operates similarly: it involves an inner recognition of wrongdoing accompanied by remorse. Since a collective lacks a unified consciousness, it cannot experience remorse, and therefore moral guilt is impossible for a nation. The philosopher Jaspers draws essentially this line: he reserves the term guilt for individual agents, assigning only political responsibility to citizens as members of a state.

Even if one grants the idea of structural perpetuation guilt—guilt for maintaining rather than initiating unjust institutions—this still collapses into responsibility rather than true guilt. Maintaining a structure may be negligent or unjust, but negligence belongs to the domain of responsibility, not guilt proper. The present citizens did not sign the treaty, and deriving benefit from its economic effects is not equivalent to causing the original harm. Consequently, the nation bears no guilt in any meaningful moral sense. At most, current citizens have a forward-looking political obligation to reform the institutions they inherit. Any stronger attribution confuses the nature of collective entities with that of individual moral agents.
