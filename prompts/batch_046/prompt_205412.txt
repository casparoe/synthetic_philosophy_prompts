I'm a doctoral student working on the normative structure of institutional moral psychology, and I'm trying to think through a concrete institutional design that keeps pulling me back to the old guilt-versus-shame debate—specifically, whether one emotion is morally preferable and whether an organization or culture can legitimately select for one over the other.

Consider a bifurcated disciplinary framework of the sort recently proposed in organizational ethics. When an employee violates an internal conduct standard—say, misrepresenting research data or engaging in discriminatory hiring—the institution initiates a private Accountability Protocol. This requires the employee to identify specific victims, propose concrete restitution, and commit to monitored behavioral change; successful completion restores full professional standing and leaves no public record. However, if the same violation becomes visible outside the institution—through press coverage, regulatory filing, or whistleblowing—the private protocol is immediately suspended. In its place, the institution activates a Transparency and Reputation Review that mandates public disclosure, a community-facing apology, and temporary exclusion from leadership eligibility, regardless of whether the private protocol was already completed. The framework's internal rationale is that private guilt-work repairs direct moral relationships, whereas public shame-work repairs social trust, and that the institution deliberately calibrates which emotional register applies based on visibility rather than on the severity of the harm.

What philosophical commitments about guilt and shame does this design presuppose? Does it implicitly treat guilt as the morally superior or more authentic emotional response, while treating shame as merely instrumentally valuable for audience management? Or does it presuppose that the two emotions are functionally interchangeable, with the culture—or at least the corporation—free to assign them according to context? Furthermore, is the practice internally coherent? It seems to punish the same act more harshly when it is public, not because the harm is greater, but because the emotional register must shift; does that commit the institution to the view that shame is appropriate only as a spectacle, and if so, can that be reconciled with any serious moral theory? Finally, reconstruct the implicit theory of moral culture embedded here: does the framework assume that emotional norms are institutionally selectable in something like the way a currency standard is selectable, and what would the design have to abandon or revise if we replaced that assumption with the view that shame is always morally corrosive and that visibility should never determine which emotion is demanded?
