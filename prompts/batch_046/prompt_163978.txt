I'm planning a ninety-minute discussion for a technically-minded reading group—mostly software engineers and researchers with rationalist-leaning priors—who tend to model complex systems as converging on optimal equilibria and who therefore treat "history matters" as a temporary friction rather than a structural feature. I want to teach path dependence specifically as the claim that certain historical trajectories become self-reinforcing and structurally locked in, so that reversing them is not merely expensive but requires dismantling complementary structures that have co-evolved with the initial choice. Do not explain the concept to me; plan the explanatory arc.

Lay out the dependency order: what distinction must be in place before path dependence can be formulated at all—specifically, how it differs from deterministic causation on one side and from random drift on the other? Then specify the sequence in which the three reinforcing mechanisms should be introduced—network externalities, increasing returns to adoption, and institutional complementarity—so that each depends on the prior one being understood, and state what misconception each transition is designed to prevent.

Specify the carrying example. It must be a concrete case with an obvious ex-post efficiency explanation—so the audience's convergence bias is activated immediately—but also with a hidden structural feature that makes reversal genuinely costly in a non-obvious way. State explicitly why QWERTY is disqualified (it invites the "actually it was designed for typewriter mechanics" rebuttal and collapses into an efficiency story), and identify the properties the substitute needs: a visible tipping point, a cascade of complementary investments, and the absence of a single centralized decision point that could have coordinated a switch.

Map the misconceptions this audience will import or form: the conflation of path dependence with market failure; the belief that sufficient optimization pressure eventually washes out historical contingency; and the reduction of the phenomenon to "sunk-cost fallacy at scale." For each, specify the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off and the conceptual move that blocks it—particularly how to distinguish a locked-in trajectory from a merely locally optimal one.

Design one check for understanding—a brief novel scenario or targeted question—that reveals whether they have grasped path dependence as a structural property of trajectories rather than as a narrative about bad initial conditions. It should test whether they can identify the specific lock-in mechanism operating in a case they have not seen before, and distinguish that mechanism from generic inertia.

State what should be explicitly left out at this level: acknowledge the formal literature on stochastic processes and the mathematical conditions for irreversible lock-in, but explain why deriving those conditions here would break the arc, and how to signal that the formalism exists without attempting to teach it.

Finally, inject a comparative framing not found in standard textbook treatments: apply the same three mechanisms to an epistemic domain—specifically, the adoption trajectory of a scientific paradigm or a computing technical standard—so the audience sees path dependence operating in belief-formation and institutional knowledge, not only in markets. Specify how this comparison changes the misconception map, what it reveals about reversal costs in epistemic networks that economic examples miss, and how to prevent the audience from dismissing the epistemic case as "just sociology of science."
