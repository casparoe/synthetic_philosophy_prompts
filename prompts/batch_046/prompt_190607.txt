A decision theorist has given explicit verdicts on five cases that mix predictive dependence with symmetric reasoning. First, in the canonical Newcomb problem with a perfect predictor, she advises taking one box. Second, in the Platonia dilemma — two identically reasoning agents who must each choose whether to attend a meeting without communicating — she denies that correlation alone warrants cooperation and advises absence. Third, in the Luring Lottery, where a ticket is paid out precisely when it is not bought, she advises purchasing, treating the causal link as broken. Fourth, in a Newcomb variant with a predictor that is correct seventy-five per cent of the time and whose accuracy is common knowledge, she still advises one-boxing. Fifth, in a three-agent Platonia scenario where attendance requires unanimous choice, she predicts that all three will stay away. Now consider the "Stochastic Mirror": two agents share an identical reasoning architecture, but a predictive model observes Agent A's computation in real time and Agent B only through a noisy, post-hoc trace; cooperation yields a shared payoff if both choose it, yet each agent knows that the forecast about the other is generated with different evidential quality. Predict her verdict with your reasons for preferring it, set out the competing rules — pure causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and a reasoning-correlated hybrid — that accommodate her earlier rulings, indicate which of the five precedents genuinely constrain the answer and which leave it open, and name the further hypothetical case that would settle the dispute between the hybrid and the pure evidential account. Please answer in British English.
