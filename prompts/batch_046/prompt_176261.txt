I am a doctoral student in philosophy of cognitive science, currently writing a dissertation chapter on the implicit boundary conditions that govern applications of predictive processing and the free energy principle. In our lab, my supervisor has given explicit verdicts about whether particular phenomena genuinely fall within that explanatory framework or are merely redescription. She treats binocular rivalry and placebo-modulated analgesia as canonical cases, though she insists the latter only works if precision-weighting of interoceptive priors is included in the model; she rejects rapid spinal withdrawal reflexes because they are brainstem-mediated, occur below the temporal threshold for hierarchical inference, and involve no prediction-error computation; she accepts inter-agent coordination in joint action tasks—such as two people carrying an irregular object together—as a legitimate, if noticeably stretched, extension of active inference across embodied agents; she rejects false-memory formation in the Deese-Roediger-McDermott paradigm on the grounds that consolidation and retrieval are offline storage processes rather than real-time perceptual-motor loops; and she regards formal analogies between decentralized market-price adjustments and free-energy minimization as mechanistically empty, since they lack both neural implementation and a unified agent-level boundary.

The new case on which I need a prediction is a theoretical proposal, still largely confined to theoretical biology, that bacterial quorum sensing operates as a minimal form of predictive coding. In this model, individual cells maintain hierarchical priors about local population density, update them via chemical-gradient prediction errors, and adjust gene-expression profiles in ways that minimize expected free energy; the dynamics unfold over minutes, involve no neural tissue whatsoever, and are currently described only at the population level rather than within a single organism. Given her existing track record, I want to know whether she will extend predictive processing to this phenomenon or exclude it, but I am less interested in the answer alone than in the logic behind it. Her verdicts appear jointly consistent with at least two competing implicit rules: one that requires the phenomenon to be embedded in an embodied agent’s real-time sensory-motor loop regardless of substrate, and another that requires hierarchical Bayesian inference implemented in neural architecture regardless of whether it serves an integrated agent-level loop. Please predict her judgment on the bacterial case, identify which of these rules (or a more precise alternative) best captures the criterion she has actually been running, show how each rule handles her five prior verdicts, and then specify the single further case that would most cleanly distinguish the two leading candidates.
