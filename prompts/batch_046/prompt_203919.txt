I spent last night in a long argument with a friend about Gödel's rotating universe, and I want to subject the position I defended to serious pressure. The view is this. Gödel's cosmological solution demonstrates that Einstein's field equations permit closed timelike curves, and I take this not as a mere mathematical curiosity but as a philosophical result of the first order: it shows that general relativity contains no internal physical structure capable of supporting an objective, globally extended present moment. Any recovery of temporal becoming requires imposing a non-covariant global time slice that the dynamics do not privilege; such a slice is therefore metaphysically imported rather than physically discovered. On this reading, the familiar observational objections—absence of cosmic rotation, the observed expansion and isotropy of the universe—are entirely beside the philosophical point, since logical compatibility with the field equations is sufficient to defeat any ontological claim that physics requires a flowing time. I also argued that this cosmological result should be read in continuity with Gödel's interest in phenomenology: our intuitive experience of a moving present may be a psychological projection onto a four-dimensional block that the equations describe without animating.

What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the evidence, arguments, and formal results that should actually move me, ordered by how decisive they are. Is there a principled—rather than merely empirical—restriction on the solution space of general relativity that excludes metrics like Gödel's not just contingently but necessarily? Could an interpretation of general covariance accommodate a physically privileged global time without violating the field equations? Has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability—if every observational mismatch is answered by 'the philosophical point survives'—and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a vice? What do defenders of this Gödel-inspired argument themselves concede as genuinely counting against it, as distinct from what critics allege? And does the position risk collapsing into an ordinary B-theory of time that needs no rotating universe whatsoever—if so, what distinctive work is the cosmological model doing, and can the view survive its redundancy?
