One student wrote the following.

"The bootstrapping problem, also called easy knowledge, is a challenge for reliabilism raised by Jonathan Vogel. In his car-color example, you see that your car is red and form the belief my car is red. Because visual perception is generally reliable, reliabilism grants you knowledge of that fact. But then you can reason: if my car is red, then my color vision is not currently malfunctioning; therefore my color vision is working properly right now. Once you know your vision is working, you can use that to justify the original belief that the car is red. This is circular because you're using the belief that the car is red to establish the reliability of the very process that produced it.

Stewart Cohen responds by denying closure under known entailment: even if you know p and p entails q, you don't automatically know q. So even if you know the car is red and that entails your vision is okay, it doesn't follow that you know your vision is okay. This blocks the bootstrap because you never acquire the knowledge of reliability that you need to circle back. However, this reply doesn't really work, because reliabilism is an externalist theory—reliability doesn't depend on what you know, only on whether the process is actually reliable. So denying closure doesn't address the core issue, which is that reliabilism seems to allow you to generate knowledge of reliability out of thin air. That's why the bootstrapping problem is still serious."
