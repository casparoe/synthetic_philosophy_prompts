I have noticed a recurring structural feature in comparative historical explanations, particularly in debates around the Great Divergence. A scholar poses a contrastive question—say, ‘why Britain and not China?’—and proceeds by narrating Britain’s path in detail: contingent conjunctions of coal geography, particular institutional protections, and imperial access are presented as active causes that had to come together. When the same scholar accounts for China’s corresponding absence of industrialisation, however, those same factors are usually listed as stable background conditions—no coal near the Yangtze delta, no equivalent patent regime—described as if they were default states rather than outcomes of prior political and economic trajectories that themselves require causal explanation. The asymmetry reverses neatly when revisionist accounts ask why China maintained large-scale agrarian stability: then Britain’s ‘failure’ to develop comparable bureaucratic mechanisms is treated as a missing baseline, not as a positive outcome of different institutional choices. So the framing ‘why X and not Y?’ seems to licence treating one case as a puzzle demanding narrative resolution and the other as a neutral canvas against which the puzzle stands out. Is there an established name for this asymmetrical distribution of explanatory burden in contrastive history? If there is, how precisely does it differ from ordinary selection bias or from the generic problem of counterfactual dependence? If not, can you coin and delimit a concept that captures exactly this move—specifying its boundary conditions, the ways it differs from plain equivocation or from treating one case as a control group, and whether it undermines the well-posedness of the Needham question specifically or of comparative explanation more generally?
