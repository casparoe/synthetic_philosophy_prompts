As a retired physical chemist who has only recently begun reading seriously in philosophy of probability, I keep encountering the reference class problem framed through a comparison that sounds eerily familiar from the laboratory: asking "what is the probability that X occurs?" without specifying a reference class, it is said, is like asking for the melting point of a substance without stating the pressure, the polymorph, or the purity. The analogy is deployed to license two competing conclusions—either a conventionalist stance, that we must simply stipulate conditions just as we stipulate standard temperature and pressure, or an eliminativist stance, that there is no single fact of the matter, just as there is no unique melting point for "carbon" absent a specification of graphite versus diamond. I want a disciplined audit of this comparison. Lay out the correspondence explicitly—what in the probabilistic case plays the role of pressure, polymorph, purity, and the standard-state convention; then identify where the mapping fractures, particularly regarding whether reference classes can overlap and nest in ways that thermodynamic states do not, and whether probabilities are best understood as measured properties analogous to melting points or as objective frequencies that resist such parameterization. Examine whether the analogy quietly imports the idea that one reference class is "natural" or "undisturbed" in the way that one atmosphere and pure crystalline form might be, and whether that import is legitimate. Finally, tell me whether either the conventionalist or the eliminativist conclusion actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the melting-point comparison was carrying the argument all along. I am not asking for a bibliography or a literature review; I want the conceptual mapping, the breaks that matter for the reasoning, and an honest verdict on what, if anything, the analogy proves.
