I'm in a semianr on deep time and we're arguing about whether the Anthropocene should be an official epoch and when it actually started. My professor keeps saying it's a "stratigraphic problem" but my friend in history says that's the wrong framing entirely. So I want to compare two ways of thinking about it, not just pick a date.

First there's the geological boundary model, the one the Anthropocene Working Group pushed with Crawford Lake in Ontario. The idea is you find a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point — basically a rock layer where you can see the "bomb spike" from thermonuclear testing, with plutonium and other artificial isotopes jumping around 1952. The International Commission on Stratigraphy decides yes or no based on whether that marker is globally synchronous and physically clear. They actually rejected the proposal recently, but the logic stays: geological time is discovered in sediment, and the people who decide are the ones who read the rock record.

Then there's what I'd call the historical-institutional model — not sure if that's a real term, I just made it up. On this view, calling something an epoch isn't about finding a line in mud or ice; it's about recognizing when human social systems became a dominant earth-system force. That could be 1800 with fossil capitalism and coal, or 1945 with nuclear weapons and the Great Acceleration, or even 1492 with the Columbian exchange. The deciders aren't geologists with core samples but historians, political ecologists, and maybe even international courts, because what matters is the shift in power and metabolism, not the isotope curve.

What I'm stuck on is whether these are actually competing answers to the same question, or whether they're doing totally seperate things — one treating time as layered physical substance that you "find," the other treating it as a social construct you "declare." If I'm forced to take a side, which framework actually captures why we care about naming a new epoch? Does the stratigraphic model miss the point by reducing a political-ecological crisis to a sediment layer? Or does the historical model lose rigor by letting anyone with a narrative claim authority over deep time? I want a committed take: are they reconcilable, and if not, which one should we actually use?
