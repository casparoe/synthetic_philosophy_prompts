As a journalist covering science and philosophy of technology, I am commissioning a short satirical piece—approximately 600 words, in the deadpan bureaucratic register of an internal municipal memorandum dated 2026, issued by a fictional Office of Infrastructural Deliberation. The target is technological determinism, specifically the thesis that artifacts have politics and that built form can displace democratic deliberation by presenting contingent design choices as technical necessities. Rather than rehearsing the familiar textbook illustrations—bridges with clearance heights, nuclear power requiring centralized control, or highway systems—please invent niche, mundane artifacts and show them exercising literal political authority within this bureaucracy: for example, a calibrated residential water meter that vetoes a zoning variance on the grounds that its flow-rate geometry favors single-family density; a standardized curb-cut profile that mandates particular sidewalk widths and thereby determines accessibility policy; or a noise-abatement berm whose graded slope is interpreted as a binding settlement-pattern directive. The memo should report on a routine development dispute in which a redesigned storm-drain system has formally rejected the proposed plan because its concrete channel geometry encodes low-density settlement values. Keep the irony sustained and deadpan—no winking, no direct announcement that the situation is absurd—so that the exaggeration remains legible to a specialist and feels fair rather than strawmanning. The real features being exaggerated should be visible: the intransigence of material form once constructed, the smuggling of normative commitments into apparently neutral engineering specifications, and the displacement of accountable decision-making by appeals to technical inevitability. Do not name living individuals, do not invent specific scholars or texts, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; refer only to “the determinist literature” and “standard artifactual-governance protocols” in generic terms. By the end, the reader should feel both the comic extremum and the genuine philosophical tension: if politics is genuinely embedded in artifacts, then democratic accountability might require negotiating not only with elected representatives, but with the pavement itself.
