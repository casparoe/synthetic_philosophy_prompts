I'm tracking a recurring move in debates about explanation—say, in neuroscience of consciousness, evolutionary accounts of cooperation, or philosophy of action—where a lower-level mechanism is mapped with high fidelity onto a phenomenon that originally carried some normative, phenomenological, or functional autonomy. Rather than denying that anything is left over, the proponent redefines the higher-level term so that it refers exactly to the mechanism just described: consciousness becomes global information broadcasting, altruism becomes kin-selected cooperation, free will becomes deterministic deliberation itself. The word survives, so it is not eliminativism; there is no derivation of a higher-level law from lower-level ones, so it is not standard intertheoretic reduction; and it feels different from greedy reductionism, which explains away a phenomenon by decomposing it into parts that individually lack the target property. Instead, the distinction between explaining the mechanism and identifying the phenomenon with the mechanism seems to collapse, often accompanied by the claim that "there is nothing more to explain" once the mapping is complete. I have seen this in discussions of global workspace theories, evolutionary explanations of moral behavior, and compatibilist analyses of agency. Does this specific maneuver—the post-explanatory definitional collapse that absorbs a higher-level concept into its mechanistic account—have an established name in philosophy of science or philosophy of mind? If not, how should its boundaries be drawn: what separates it from a legitimate functional reduction, from a crane-like explanation that preserves the autonomy of the higher level, and from ordinary conceptual analysis done in good faith? And is it best understood as a failure mode of reductionist programs, or as a defensible form of theoretical unification—what criteria would tell the two apart?
