As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science and social choice theory, I listened to two members of my study group—Elena and Marcus—debate judgment aggregation in an interdisciplinary advisory panel. Elena defended premise-based aggregation: if a majority accepts both that predictive climate models are empirically adequate (P) and that the risk of irreversible feedback loops lies below a critical threshold (Q), then the group is rationally committed to endorsing large-scale solar geoengineering (R) whenever P and Q entail R, regardless of whether a direct majority vote on R succeeds. Marcus objected that this imposes a monological conception of reason onto a pluralistic body; he noted that members may reject the entailment itself, or assign premises different epistemic weights—empirical versus normative—so treating P and Q symmetrically is a category error. Elena conceded that strategic misrepresentation of premises is a danger, but she argued that secret ballots or distance-based aggregation rules can mitigate it, and she pressed Marcus on whether letting a direct vote on R override shared premises does not sacrifice logical coherence for political convenience. Marcus then proposed a hybrid protocol in which premises are weighted by disciplinary kind and the group takes a separate meta-vote on whether the logical bridge holds collectively, since collective belief, he claimed, possesses a social ontology irreducible to individual doxastic states. They turned to me for arbitration. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: indicate who is right about each specific claim, state who is more defensible overall, and note what both overlook.
