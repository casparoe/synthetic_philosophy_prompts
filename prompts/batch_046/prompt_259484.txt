I was arguing late into the evening with a friend about whether the so-called “dirty hands” problem in politics is genuinely unavoidable or merely a failure of institutional imagination. She kept insisting that liberal democracies have already solved it: we do not ask bureaucrats to decide whether to cooperate with a torturing regime or to authorize an extralegal surveillance operation; instead, we require that a specific, democratically accountable minister personally sign the authorization, while the civil servants who execute it receive legal immunity and the details remain hidden from routine parliamentary oversight except in aggregated form. The minister bears political blame—resignation, electoral punishment, public disgrace—but not criminal liability, and the authorization records are typically destroyed after a fixed term unless renewed. To her, this is just pragmatic realism: moral risk is concentrated at the point of democratic legitimacy so that the state can act without corrupting the entire chain of command. I am less convinced that the design is philosophically innocent. What theory of moral responsibility does this institutional architecture actually presuppose? Does it require that blameworthiness can be cleanly partitioned between the political and the legal, or that moral taint attaches only to agents with a specific kind of democratic mandate? Can a view that holds responsibility tracks causal control and knowledge explain why the minister is the designated “dirty” node while the executors are shielded, or does the practice quietly depend on a hierarchical, almost sacramental conception of authority? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments—about agency, accountability, and the separability of moral from political responsibility—that make this design coherent rather than arbitrary. Then tell me whether the practice strictly requires those commitments or is compatible with rival frameworks, such as an egalitarian model that distributes moral risk across all participants, and describe what concrete changes—legal, procedural, or institutional—would be necessary if we replaced the underlying theory with one that denies that democratic office can function as a moral lightning rod.
