As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of history, I find myself stuck on a problem that feels structurally similar to overdetermination in mechanics but behaves very differently in narrative explanation. When historians cite economic collapse, military overstretch, and cultural decay together to explain the fall of Rome, are they describing redundant causes in the physical sense—each sufficient, none necessary—or are they offering a single, multi-stranded explanation in which removing any thread unravels the coherence of the account? I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of named schools; I want a diagnostic taxonomy, built as a branching decision tree, that sorts the actual logical positions one can hold on multi-causal explanation in history.

Please take the role of an expert philosopher of science with a specialization in historiography. Before constructing the tree, briefly audit which distinctions genuinely separate commitments from mere relabelings. Then build the instrument from the ground up: start with a root binary commitment—whether historical explanations permit genuine causal overdetermination (individually sufficient factors) or only explanatory pluralism (jointly necessary, individually insufficient strands). Ensure the tree can separate positions that agree on vocabulary but disagree on modal robustness or on the substitutability of narrative threads.
