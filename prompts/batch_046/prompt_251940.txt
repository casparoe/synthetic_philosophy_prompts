I am a PhD student in comparative religion and philosophy consulting for a digital-heritage NGO that catalogs religious artifacts for an open-access repository. Our taxonomy currently forces every object into a single tradition-label—e.g., “Jain,” “Buddhist,” “Hindu”—and I need to replace that with something defensible. Please turn Jain anekāntavāda, specifically syādvāda and the seven-fold predication (saptabhaṅgī), into a reusable audit procedure that my non-specialist labeling team can run before assigning any category.

Design it as an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the stop-points where the procedure returns “multi-label required” or “label inadequate,” and the explicit handoffs to human judgment where the issue is one of values or institutional priorities rather than classification logic. The procedure must handle hard cases—blended iconography, ritual objects with conflicting use-contexts, and artifacts for which no observer-independent aspect can be privileged—without collapsing into either relativism (all labels are equally valid) or paralysis (no label is ever adequate). Then run it on three cases: (i) a twelfth-century manuscript illumination combining Jain and Buddhist cosmological motifs; (ii) a ceremonial vessel employed in both Jain monastic rites and local folk practice; (iii) a standard Digambara icon for which the procedure still demands a complex multi-aspect label, yielding an administratively burdensome result I will not like. For each case, show exactly where the procedure lands, where judgment takes over, and what the procedure cannot resolve.

Also flag the misfire modes—where simplifying the naya doctrine for practitioners risks distorting it—and state clearly what the procedure cannot do, such as settle ontological disputes about an artifact’s essence or substitute for curatorial expertise.

Finally, please separate what is settled about the logical structure of anekāntavāda and syādvāda from what is genuinely contested among Jain philosophers and scholars of the tradition—e.g., the scope of avaktavya, whether the framework applies to moral or aesthetic predicates, and the metaphysical status of the naya doctrine—and do not present any contested interpretive claim as the consensus view.
