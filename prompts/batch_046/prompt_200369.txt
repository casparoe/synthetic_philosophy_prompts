I've been reflecting on whether a life of maximal moral goodness is actually desirable, and I keep returning to Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. Stevens, the butler, seems to embody a kind of total dedication to duty: he suppresses personal attachments, denies his own feelings, and defines himself entirely through service. I want to read the novel through the philosophical idea of the moral saint — the person who maximizes moral virtue — but with a specific distinction in mind. Rather than treating moral sainthood as a single ideal, I want to ask whether Stevens illustrates two different things that get collapsed under that label: genuine altruistic self-sacrifice on one hand, and a kind of existential self-replacement in which duty becomes a mechanism for erasing one's own identity on the other. Where does Ishiguro show Stevens's life as genuinely admirable or morally exemplary? Where does the novel resist that reading and reveal his choices as emotionally impoverished rather than morally elevated? In particular, does the structure of the narrative — his retrospective justification of missed love and unacknowledged loyalty — illuminate a difference between suppressing desire for the sake of others and suppressing desire because one has no self left to have desires for? I am not asking for a survey of philosophical literature, but for an honest critical reading: what does the novel add to the debate about whether maximal moral goodness is desirable that a pure argument about moral saints cannot capture on its own?
