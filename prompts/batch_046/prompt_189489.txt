I'm a novelist and, for reasons I still don't understand, I sit on the acquisitions commitee of the Millbrook Historical Society. We've got a vote this Thursday on wether to accept the Lena Voss collection — basically the only surviving records of a Black domestic worker from the 1950s, donated by the family of the woman she worked for. What's left are a few letters the employer kept because she found them amusing, some pay stubs, and a photo where Lena's face is half cut off. Her own family says everything else was burned. Our archivist wants to digitize it but pair it with an absence map — call it negative provenance — that tracks not just what survives but the specific mechanisms that destroyed the rest: employer curation, family shame, the usual silences. Half the board thinks displaying this just reproduces the employer's gaze and turns Lena into a spectacle of curated suffering. The other half thinks rejecting it buries her even deeper. I need a short written opinion for Thursday's meeting: recommend we accept, reject, or accept with conditions, with a concrete timeline. Treat the strongest argument on the losing side seriously — don't just wave it away. Work through wether displaying mediated voices can ever avoid becoming ventriloquism, and wether an absence map repairs the power imbalance or just aestheticizes the gap. Keep it actionable: what do we decide by Thursday, and what happens in six months if we go ahead?
