I'm running a metaphysics workshop for about 12 grad students, 75 minutes, and we're stuck in the usual loop where everyone agrees that endurantism and perdurantism "sound different" but nobody actually feels the difference in there bones. I want an in-class game or structured role-play — no lecture up front, no naming the views until after — that makes them run into the tension themselves. Specifically, I'm imagining a "presence ledger" exercise built around one fictional case that gets weird halfway through: a person named Jordan who over five life-stages loses an arm, gets a prosthetic, moves countries, and eventually has about 30% of their neural tissue gradually replaced by synthetic anologs that preserve personality and memory. The group splits into two teams with hidden rule-cards about how to represent Jordan's presence across a timeline drawn on the floor with tape, using colored tokens and string; then both teams have to jointly vote on whether Jordan-at-stage-5 inherits an estate or counts as a new legal person, but they can only defend the vote using what their representation allows them to point to. I want the rules arranged so that the "whole presence" team feels the strain of saying "Jordan is fully right here" when pieces are missing or synthetic, while the "slice" team feels the strain of linking stages into one life without being able to point to a single present whole thing. Then a debrief that maps each stage onto the metaphysical commitments, tells me predicatbly what will go wrong the first time (probably someone tears up their hidden-rule card out of frustration), and is honest about which feature of the debate — maybe the formal ontology of temporal parts versus the phenomenology of presence — the activity just can't reproduce and I have to supply by hand. Please design this with materials, the timeline, the hidden rules, the voting mechanism with small stakes, and the debrief script. Take a definite position on whether this risks collapsing into a glorified Ship-of-Theseus rehash, and flag any spot where your design leans on a contested reading of either view or takes liberties with the ideas.
