Write a short story set in a research archive that has just received conclusive evidence that human civilisation will end in exactly four centuries, with no survivors and no successor species. Tell it from the perspective of a curator who has spent her career preserving records not for present use but for what she calls "the long audience" — future generations who will build upon, reinterpret, and eventually outgrow the present. Rather than focusing on panic or grief, the narrative should explore the specific kind of value-loss associated with Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: the way in which many present activities derive their point from an assumed continuation of human life, so that knowledge of extinction does not merely cause sadness but retroactively alters what those activities meant. Include a scene in which the curator attempts one final act of preservation and finds that she can no longer determine whether the act is noble, futile, or something between, precisely because the temporal horizon against which its meaning was calibrated has vanished. Avoid generic post-apocalyptic imagery; do not explain the scientific proof; keep the conceptual focus on the backward-reaching damage to present value.
