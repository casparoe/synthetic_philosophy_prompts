Within contemporary epistemology, Timothy Williamson's argument against the luminosity of mental states—if being in a mental state necessarily puts one in a position to know it, then together with a margin-for-error principle for knowledge and the KK principle, a gradual change should yield knowledge that one feels cold at the very boundary where an indistinguishable nearby case involves not feeling cold—has produced a literature that strikes me as stalled in a peculiar way. The familiar responses are thoroughly mapped: some reject the KK principle; others deny that feeling cold is genuinely luminous; others claim the argument equivocates on what it is to 'feel cold.' I am less interested in another adjudication than in a structural diagnosis. Is the impasse substantive—do the sides disagree about the logic of knowledge-attribution—or is it verbal, with 'luminosity' serving as a label for distinct phenomena? Might it be that one camp is addressing static conditions for knowledge-at-a-time, while the other addresses the dynamics of self-monitoring over change, so that they answer different questions under one heading?

To push this further, I want you to examine a framing that has received little explicit attention. Suppose we reject the tacit assumption that the epistemic margin required for knowing one is in mental state M is uniform across all such states. Instead, consider a variable-margin model: the idea that the margin of error tolerated for knowing-one-is-in-M can contract or expand depending on the cognitive accessibility of M itself. If accessibility varies with the intensity or salience of the state, then in the slow-change scenario the required margin might widen precisely as the state becomes less accessible, blocking the derivation of contradictory knowledge-claims without abandoning either luminosity or the KK principle. Does this framing dissolve the contradiction, reveal it as an artifact of an unrealistic uniform-threshold assumption, or is it incoherent given standard epistemic logic?

Give me a diagnosis of the disagreement's real structure—what formal or conceptual commitments are hidden, whether an empirical claim about self-monitoring is doing work, and which of the standard moves are actually responsive to which problem. Then defend an agenda: if the debate should move forward, what distinction, formal result, or conceptual revision deserves priority, and why? Which lines of work should be deprioritized because they depend on resolving this upstream issue, and which are tractable now? If, however, the premise that there is a genuine stall is confused—if, say, the literature is actually converging on a nuanced middle position I have missed, or if the variable-margin framing collapses into an ambiguity about degrees of belief—say so plainly, and do not accommodate the framing for politeness's sake. I want a defended conceptual agenda, not a survey and not a reading list.
