I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science, and I keep encountering an argumentative deadlock that I do not know how to classify. The pattern is this: a theoretical framework T predicts P, but observation O contradicts P. To preserve T, defenders revise an auxiliary hypothesis A about background conditions, insisting that A is already supported by data from an unrelated domain D and therefore not ad hoc. Critics reply that the data in D only warrant A if one already accepts core elements of T, so the alleged independence is circular. Both sides invoke falsificationist criteria, yet they do not disagree about whether T is in principle falsifiable, nor about whether the revision is empirically progressive in Lakatos’s sense; rather, they deadlock over whether any neutral description of the evidential base can exist that does not presuppose T itself when judging whether A is genuinely independent. I have seen this in discussions of research programmes, but there the focus is usually on progressive versus degenerating problem-shifts. Here the impasse seems prior: the demarcation problem stalls because applying the criterion of non-ad hocness requires prior agreement on evidential neutrality that the framework itself undermines. Does this specific configuration—where falsificationism collapses into a circular dispute about the independence of auxiliary hypotheses—have an established name? If not, how exactly should it be distinguished from the Duhem-Quine thesis on one side and from Lakatos’s methodology on the other, and where do its conceptual boundaries lie?
