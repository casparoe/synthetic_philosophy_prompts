Following the recent death of my mother after a prolonged illness, I have found myself re-examining whether anything in life possesses intrinsic value beyond the mere absence of distress. I have arrived at a position that I can only describe as tranquilist, but before I adopt it more fully I should like a rigorous audit of my commitments to determine whether they form a coherent package or whether I am relying on incompatible premises. Please respond in British English.

My current commitments are as follows:

(1) I hold a tranquilist theory of well-being: the only intrinsic goods are the absence of craving (taṇhā) and the absence of suffering (dukkha). Positive mental states—pleasure, joy, aesthetic rapture—possess no intrinsic value; they are merely temporary abatements of tension, analogous to the relief experienced when an unpleasant noise ceases.

(2) On this basis, I reject hedonistic calculus altogether. To treat pleasure as a quantifiable positive magnitude is, in my view, a category error; there is only the varying intensity and duration of absence.

(3) Nevertheless, I reject negative utilitarianism. I maintain that a world populated by billions of lives of mild, persistent discontent is ethically preferable to an empty universe, despite the fact that both approximate the null point of suffering.

(4) I further maintain that phenomenal consciousness is a necessary condition for occupying any position on the well-being scale. A being that lacks inner experience—whether a thermostat, a sophisticated language model, or a plant—is not situated at well-being zero, but lies entirely outside the domain of well-being.

(5) I accept Schopenhauer's description of the will-less aesthetic state—the complete suspension of desire in contemplation—as a genuine metaphysical possibility. Yet I deny that this state yields any positive value; it is purely the neutral point, the mere absence of the will's pressure.

(6) I have begun to apply this framework personally. In the months since my caregiving ended, I have deliberately dissolved attachments to career advancement and to intimate relationships, seeking not happiness but the extinction of preference itself.

(7) I hold that species membership is morally arbitrary, but that the capacity for phenomenal craving is the sole criterion of moral status. Consequently, a human being in deep meditative absorption and a conscious non-human animal both possess moral status, whereas a non-conscious system lacks it, even though none of the three currently suffers a well-being deficit.

(8) Finally, I claim that art which genuinely induces the will-less state possesses intrinsic value, rather than merely instrumental value for the reduction of craving.

Please assess whether this set of commitments hangs together. For each pair that is genuinely in tension, specify which commitments are involved, rank the tensions by severity, and for each state the cheapest revision available and what it costs. Identify also any pair that appears tense but dissolves upon closer inspection, explaining precisely why the conflict is only apparent. Do not indicate which commitment I am least willing to surrender; treat the audit as entirely neutral. Conclude by identifying which single revision—were I forced to make only one—would resolve the greatest number of inconsistencies, and describe exactly what that revision entails. Write in British English.
