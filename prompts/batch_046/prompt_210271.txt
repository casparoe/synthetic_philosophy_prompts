Here is an argument I have been working through about revealed preference and whether it can ground the concept of utility without collapsing into either mentalism or universal attribution. I think it proves too much, but I am unsure where the leak is.

(1) The behaviorist formulation of revealed preference holds that an agent's preference ordering is fully constituted by consistent choice patterns across all feasible sets: if A is chosen whenever B is available, then A is revealed preferred to B, with no independent appeal to mental states.

(2) If preference is fully constituted by choice in this way, then there is no theoretical gap between "the agent prefers X" and "the agent is disposed to choose X"; talk of an underlying mental state of preference is either redundant or eliminable.

(3) Consider an agent who, in every context where both A and B are physically present and unencumbered, selects A. By (1), this reveals that the agent's utility for A exceeds that for B.

(4) Now suppose we discover that B is never a live option for this agent: due to a specific representational limitation, B cannot be tokened in the agent's working memory, so B is never genuinely entertained as a possibility. The agent does not reject B; B is causally inaccessible to the choice mechanism.

(5) The formal theory defines the feasible set by physical availability, not by representational accessibility. So B is in the feasible set, and the consistent selection of A still counts as revealing preference for A over B.

(6) But if revealed preference applies here, it applies to any deterministic system observed under conditions where one state is physically present but causally blocked. A river consistently occupies the lowest available elevation; a thermostat consistently activates heating below 20°C. By the same logic, they reveal preferences and possess utility functions.

(7) This universalizes utility: rivers, chemical equilibria, and neural reflex arcs all have well-defined preference orderings. But economic utility is conceptually tied to welfare, satisfaction, and normative evaluation; attributing it to non-agential systems is incoherent.

(8) The standard defense is to restrict revealed preference to "deliberate" or "intentional" choice. But this restriction requires an independent criterion of agency or mental representation—the very mental-state access that the behaviorist formulation was designed to eliminate.

(9) Therefore, revealed preference either abandons its behaviorist core by smuggling in mentalism, or it retains that core and proves too much by attributing utility everywhere. Either way, it fails to ground the specific concept of utility.

Where does this argument go wrong? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) intact, because I think the behaviorist reduction is the most defensible part of the theory. If I must give up something, I want to know whether the damage is to (4)-(5) (the live-option distinction), to (6) (the analogy with physical systems), or to (8) (the charge of mentalism). For each option: why did that step look plausible, what standard responses—behaviorist, mentalist, or instrumentalist—say about it, and what does rejecting it cost the overall project of defining utility through revealed preference?
