I hold the view that the three canonical failures of Athenian democracy—the assembly's reversal over Mytilene, the decision for the Sicilian Expedition, and the trial and execution of Socrates—are not best explained by the moral corruption of crowds, the demagogy of individual leaders, or the inherent irrationality of popular rule, but by a single institutional absence: the assembly lacked any structured procedural separation between an initial emotional deliberation and a binding decision, which meant that the same body could vote for collective punishment in a state of anger, reverse itself the next day in regret, launch an imperial campaign in collective enthusiasm, and condemn a philosopher in post-war anxiety, with no institutional brake or cooling-off mechanism to interrupt these affective swings. This makes the failures failures of decision architecture rather than failures of democracy as such. What would count against this position? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be: historical evidence that other mechanisms (not procedural ones) actually caused the outcomes; conceptual arguments that emotional deliberation and rational deliberation are not separable in the way the view requires; comparative cases from other Greek poleis with different procedures that still suffered analogous failures; and theoretical results showing that adding such procedural brakes tends to shift rather than prevent bad outcomes. I also want to know which of these a defender of the institutional-absence view should themselves concede as genuinely counting against them, and which they can legitimately dismiss—whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability (for instance, by allowing that the absence is always present but sometimes overcome by exceptional leadership), and if so whether that insulation is a problem. Finally, tell me what the view predicts that its rivals do not—say, about whether democratic failures cluster in specific procedural conditions rather than in specific social conditions—and whether that predictive claim is actually risky or has been protected by ad hoc adjustments.
