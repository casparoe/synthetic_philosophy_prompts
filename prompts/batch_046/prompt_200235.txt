I'm grading problem sets for a philosophy-of-mind seminar I run with a few people from the old rationalist blog circles. The assignment asks students to work through the classic paradox of self-deception — that it seems to require both knowing and not knowing the same proposition — and then apply their framework to a concrete case. I want to be consistent and fair, but I also want to catch subtle conflations that a quick read would miss. Here is the prompt I gave and one response.

Question: Is self-deception conceptually impossible? Evaluate the paradox that the deceiver must know the truth in order to hide it, yet must not know it in order to be deceived. In your answer, distinguish doxastic self-deception (a false belief about some state of affairs) from practical self-deception (a false belief about one's own motives or actions). Explain one single-agent response and one response that allows some form of mental partitioning. Then apply your analysis to the following case. Dr. Elena Vasquez is a senior researcher whose career depends on Model X. She receives a private dataset that, if properly analyzed, strongly undermines Model X. She does not open the file; instead she archives it in a folder she labels "noise — do not open." In public she sincerely denies having any reason to doubt Model X. Her citation patterns, however, systematically omit every study that cites the contradictory dataset, and her lab notes contain a handwritten margin note saying "check robustness — avoid the 2023 batch?" which she never follows up. Is Vasquez self-deceived? If so, is it doxastic, practical, or both? Be precise about whether she holds a false belief, avoids forming a belief, or misattributes her own motives.

Student answer:

Self-deception only looks impossible if we treat the mind as a single transparent agent. Davidson showed that the paradox dissolves once we see that the mind can be divided: one part can suppress evidence while another part sincerely reports the dominant belief. For Davidson, what we call self-deception is really a failure of rational integration rather than literal deception, but the division is real. Mele offers a different taxonomy. He distinguishes straight self-deception, where motivated biases cause a false belief, from twisted self-deception, where one acquires a false belief about one's own belief. In Vasquez's case, we see twisted self-deception. She has a false second-order belief: she believes that she believes Model X is correct, but in fact her first-order belief is false because she knows about the contradictory dataset. The folder label and the margin note prove that a sub-personal module in her cognition has registered the threat and hidden it from her conscious self-report. This is not practical self-deception, because practical self-deception is about motives and actions — for example, hiding from oneself that one is acting out of jealousy. Vasquez's problem is purely doxastic: she has formed the false belief that her model is sound, and she formed it intentionally by archiving the evidence. The citation pattern is just behavioral fallout from that false belief.

Please assign a letter grade and give specific point-by-point comments that justify it. I am especially interested in whether the student correctly assigns Davidson and Mele to their respective positions, whether the distinction between doxastic and practical self-deception is maintained consistently, and whether the analysis of Vasquez confuses avoiding belief-formation with actually holding a false belief. If there is a hidden ambiguity in the case that makes more than one interpretation reasonable, say so, but do not hedge into vagueness: tell me which interpretation the student is implicitly running and where it breaks.
