I'm writing a feature on whether culture can genuinely cause economic and social outcomes, and I'm using Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* as a test case. Read the novel through the lens of Max Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis—specifically the problem of explaining with values, and the related distinction between direct causal determination and what Weber called elective affinity (*Wahlverwandtschaft*), the non-random, non-necessary convergence of a cultural form with an economic structure. Focus on Stevens's devotion to "dignity," his professional self-denial, and the suppression of personal feeling in service of an aristocratic household. Does Ishiguro present Stevens's values as causally shaping his life trajectory—a value-rational commitment that genuinely explains why he misses the chance for love and political awakening—or does the novel suggest these values are post-hoc rationalizations of class immobility and emotional repression? Where does the idea of elective affinity illuminate particular scenes, such as Stevens's refusal to acknowledge Lord Darlington's politics or his repeated misreading of Miss Kenton's intentions? Where does the novel resist a Weberian reading—for instance, by implying that Stevens's "dignity" is itself produced by economic dependency rather than functioning as an autonomous cultural force? Finally, does the novel add something to the philosophical debate about value-explanation that abstract argument about Weber's thesis cannot—perhaps by dramatizing what it feels like to inhabit a value from the inside, even when that value may lack independent causal power?
