My roommate summed up Adam Smith's moral philosophy after skimming The Theory of Moral Sentiments. He said sympthy for Smith is basically emotional contagion — you catch feelings off other people like a cold, and that's the whole foundation. The impartial spectator is then just an internalized audience, literally society's voice in your head, judging whether your passions are 'proportional' to the situation. From this he gets that morality is conventional: right action is whatever passes the spectator's approval, and since different cultures have different spectators, there's no real cross-cultural moral truth. He also mentioned that Smith talks about 'direct' vs 'indirect' sympthy, but said that's just splitting hairs — both are just catching feelings, one step removed. Finally he claimed Smith thinks sympathy dies with physical distance, so Smith would deny we have strong moral duties to people overseas. This sounds mostly right but I wonder if he's smushed together sympathy with empathy and made the spectator sound too much like a social mirror. Is this fair? What's actually accurate, what's a common simplification, and where is he flat wrong? Especially does Smith really think morality is conventional, and what's going on with that direct/indirect distinction?
