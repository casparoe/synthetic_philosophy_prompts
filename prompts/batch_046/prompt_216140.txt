Consider the institutional practice governing surgical error in England: when a surgeon proceeds with an operation despite omitting a mandated safety check—say, failing to secure a required second opinion—the institutional response depends almost entirely on whether the patient survives or dies. If the patient lives, the omission is typically logged as a "near miss", addressed through internal retraining and perhaps temporary supervision, but rarely triggers suspension or criminal scrutiny. If the patient dies, the identical omission, with the same mental state and the same deviation from protocol, frequently leads to prosecution for gross negligence manslaughter and permanent erasure from the medical register. Meanwhile, the civil law of clinical negligence insists that a finding of breach of duty should depend on the standard of care exercised, not on the severity of the harm that happened to result; damages are calculated by outcome, but culpability for breach is meant to be outcome-independent. What theory of moral responsibility, or of institutional accountability, does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: does it treat outcomes as partly constitutive of blameworthiness, as purely evidential of a hidden culpable state, or as triggers for a distinct kind of public accountability? Is the practice compatible with a control-based account of moral responsibility, or does it quietly presuppose that moral luck is legitimate in institutional judgment? Could the divergence be defended on purely tractability or epistemic grounds—that harm reveals errors that near-misses conceal—without collapsing into outcome-dependence?
