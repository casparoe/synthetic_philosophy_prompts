The exam question was: "In the Doctrine of Virtue, Kant denies that we have direct duties to animals. Explain the metaphysical basis of this denial, the structure of the indirect duty he recognizes regarding cruelty, and whether considerations drawn from his Anthropology—particularly remarks on animal analogues of reason and the teleological framing of organic life in the Critique of Judgment—undermine the standard anthropocentric reading of these passages."

Here is the student's answer:

Kant denies direct duties to animals because they lack rational autonomy, which is the necessary condition for being an end in itself. Since moral obligations apply only between rational beings capable of reciprocal legislation, animals fall outside the scope of direct duty entirely. Nevertheless, Kant recognizes an indirect duty: we ought to avoid cruelty to animals because it deadens our moral feeling of compassion and thereby weakens our capacity for sympathy toward human beings. This is essentially an instrumental argument—animal suffering matters only as it affects human moral development, and animals themselves possess no intrinsic value whatsoever. In fact, Kant classifies animals as mere things, placing them in the same moral category as inanimate objects, so the indirect duty is not really about the animals at all but solely about preserving human virtue.

Some interpreters have noted that in the Anthropology Kant discusses analogues of reason in animals, and that the Critique of Judgment treats organisms teleologically as ends in nature. These texts clearly demonstrate that Kant attributed a form of proto-rationality to animals and regarded them as possessing a kind of indirect moral standing that the Doctrine of Virtue overlooks. Therefore, the standard anthropocentric reading is incompatible with Kant's broader philosophy of nature, and we should read the Doctrine of Virtue as allowing for direct, though limited, consideration of animal welfare.
