I've been trying to formalize what it means to "hold Germany responsible" for the First World War in light of the Fischer controversy—specifically, Fischer's thesis that the German leadership deliberately engineered the July crisis and adopted the September Program as a conscious bid for hegemony, not as defensive reaction. Here is an argument that seems to show the concept of national responsibility either collapses into useless metaphor or distributes blame to people who clearly do not deserve it, and I cannot locate the escape hatch.

(1) To hold a nation responsible for a war is to attribute culpability to the collective agent "Germany," not merely to a list of officials.
(2) Culpability requires causal contribution and the absence of a valid excuse; it does not require that every member personally intended the outcome, only that the collective's decision procedure was autonomous and not coerced from outside.
(3) Per Fischer, the German state in 1914 was not coerced; it autonomously executed the Schlieffen Plan and pursued continental hegemony through deliberate policy.
(4) Therefore "Germany" as a corporate agent satisfied the conditions for culpability.
(5) But corporate culpability must be either transmissive (it flows downward to members) or non-transmissive (it sticks to the institutional structure alone).
(6) If non-transmissive, then "holding Germany responsible" cannot justify reparations, territorial adjustments, or collective historical reckoning directed at citizens—those require individual targets—so the concept does no practical work.
(7) If transmissive, then every citizen who did not actively resist—including pacifists, children, and unborn future generations—shares in the blame.
(8) Non-transmissive responsibility is therefore conceptually empty for our purposes, and transmissive responsibility is morally intolerable.
(9) So either we abandon national responsibility entirely, or we accept that infants bear war guilt.

Which step should I reject? I want to keep (1) and (3)—that collective blame is genuinely about the nation, and that Fischer establishes autonomous, deliberate causation by the leadership. I would most like to save (6) by denying the binary in (5), perhaps by treating institutional culpability as structurally mediated rather than either flowing downward or remaining trapped at the top. But I am unsure how to cash that out without sneaking in some form of "structural complicity" that just reproduces the distribution problem at a different level. Where does this argument actually break?
