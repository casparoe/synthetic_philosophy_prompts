As a novelist, I am drawn to protagonists whose dignity derives not from triumph but from the manner of defeat—figures who maintain loyalty to a cause precisely because it was never tested by success and therefore never corrupted by power. This narrative preoccupation raises a broader philosophical question about the political analogues: societies that anchor collective identity in lost causes rather than in realized achievements. One thinks of the Confederate "Lost Cause" mythology, the Masada narrative of dignified annihilation, the Kosovo Field tradition of sacrificial defeat, and the Dunkirk "spirit" that recast evacuation as moral victory. In each case, defeat is transformed into a founding moment, yet this transformation often depends on what might be called commemorative anachronism—attributing contemporary moral values to a cause that, had it prevailed, might have produced very different ethical outcomes.

Write an essay addressing the following controversy: Can a national myth grounded in a genuinely lost cause—one that remains unrealized and therefore empirically untested—ever be ethically defensible, or are such myths inherently dangerous? In particular, evaluate the argument that an unrealized cause possesses a unique "purity of non-actualization" that makes it especially suited to moral instruction and collective resilience, against the counter-argument that this very purity renders the cause immune to refutation and thus prone to revanchism, exclusionary nationalism, or political manipulation. Defend a specific conclusion—either that lost-cause myths can be ethically contained and serve indispensable functions, or that they ought to be dismantled in favor of narratives grounded in accountable, realized achievements—while explaining what conditions, if any, would distinguish a noble lost cause from a toxic one. Treat the examples above only as illustrations; the essay should be a philosophical evaluation, not a historical survey, and should reach a verdict rather than ending in suspension.
