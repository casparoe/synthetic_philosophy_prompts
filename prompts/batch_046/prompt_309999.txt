I have been trying to work out what I actually think about the problem of dirty hands in politics — situations in which a public official does something morally wrong in order to achieve a greater public good. Rather than begin with theory, I have listed my intuitive verdicts on six concrete cases. I would like to know whether there is a single principle, or a small family of principles, that captures all of them, and if not, which verdict I ought to revise.

Case 1: A prime minister authorises the torture of a captured terrorist who knows the location of a bomb that will kill hundreds of civilians. The torture is direct, intentional, and causes severe suffering. Verdict: the act is morally wrong, but the official is justified in ordering it; her hands are genuinely dirty.

Case 2: A chancellor breaks an election manifesto promise to lower income tax after independent economists demonstrate that failing to raise revenue will trigger a sovereign debt crisis that harms the poorest citizens most severely. Verdict: this is not a case of dirty hands at all; changing course in light of new evidence is simply the right thing to do.

Case 3: A mayor approves a construction contract for a vital flood-defence wall, knowing that the only firm capable of completing it before the rainy season has bribed local officials. The mayor takes no personal payment, ensures the wall is built, and reports the corruption to auditors immediately afterwards. Verdict: not justified; using corruption as an instrument is impermissible regardless of urgency, though her refusal of personal gain reduces her blame.

Case 4: A health secretary suppresses preliminary data suggesting a minor, remediable risk of contamination in a regional water supply. She orders silent remediation and withholds publication, judging that disclosure would cause mass panic, hospital overload from psychosomatic symptoms, and economic damage far exceeding the actual health risk. Verdict: justified; the deception is wrong, but necessary to prevent greater harm, so her hands are dirty.

Case 5: A climate minister secretly approves an unauthorised solar geoengineering trial in national waters, violating international treaties and risking harm to neighbouring countries, because she genuinely believes it is the only way to prevent an imminent climate tipping point that would displace millions. Verdict: not justified; the potential harm crosses borders and affects people who have no democratic representation in her decision, and violating international law is not redeemed by urgency alone.

Case 6: A prime minister deliberately lies to parliament about an extramarital affair, knowing that disclosure would trigger a coalition collapse and snap elections during an acute international security crisis. Verdict: not justified; protecting one’s personal reputation and political position does not warrant deceiving democratic institutions, even when the consequences of disclosure are severe.

I keep returning to the tension between Case 4 and Case 5: both involve secretive, deceptive, or treaty-violating actions taken by an official who sincerely believes they prevent catastrophe, yet I judge one acceptable and the other not. Is the decisive factor the presence of democratic consent, the geographical scope of risk, or something about the nature of the wrong done? Similarly, Case 1 involves direct harm to a guilty party, while Case 6 involves deception about a private matter; both are wrong, but only one feels permissible.

Please propose the principle or principles that best capture my verdicts, state precisely which case motivates each clause, indicate what it predicts for a case I have not considered — for instance, a local councillor who falsifies paperwork to redirect emergency supplies from a technically lower-priority district (according to an algorithmic allocation model) to an area with hidden severe need, saving lives — and be honest about which of my six verdicts, if any, must be overturned for the sake of coherence.
