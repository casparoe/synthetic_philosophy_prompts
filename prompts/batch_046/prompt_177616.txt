As a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science—fluent in predictive-processing and active-inference accounts of cognition, but with only a working familiarity with the modern Stoic revival—I want you to restate the Stoic dichotomy of control in the vocabulary of generative models, precision weighting, active inference, and expected free-energy minimization. Focus specifically on the revivalist formulation that distinguishes direct authority over assent and prohairesis (judgment, intention, and the selection of policies) from the absence of authority over outcomes, bodily states, and reputation, and that treats indifference to externals not as emotional suppression but as the normative claim that externals carry no intrinsic value for virtue. Translate each core component—the dichotomy itself, the identification of virtue with correct assent, the value-neutrality of externals, and the idea that control here is evaluative authority rather than causal efficacy—into predictive-processing terms: for instance, model whether authority over assent corresponds to high-precision weighting of priors governing one's own action hierarchy, and whether indifference corresponds to assigning minimal epistemic value to prediction errors arising from states outside that hierarchy. Then provide a residue report: which Stoic claims map cleanly onto this framework, which require distortion or loss of normative force (e.g., whether the metaphysical assertion that externals are genuinely neither good nor bad collapses into a merely pragmatic recommendation about precision allocation), and which, if any, have no counterpart in an ontology that treats all states as predictions with associated precision and value. Finally, assess whether the residue points to a genuine limitation in predictive processing—namely, its inability to accommodate a normative metaphysics of value-neutrality—or whether it reveals that the modern revival's formulation of the dichotomy was already implicitly a heuristic about belief-state management rather than a substantive thesis about the scope of agency.
