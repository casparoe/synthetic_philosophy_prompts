I've been trying to figure out what "alive" actually means by testing my intuition on concrete cases. A swimming bacterium is clearly alive. A virus injecting its genes into a host cell to make copies is not alive—more like a hijacked instruction manual. A candle flame that consumes wax, grows toward oxygen, and maintains its shape is not alive, even though it takes in fuel and responds to its surroundings. A salt crystal growing in a jar and replicating its own lattice structure is not alive. A synthetic cell built with artificial DNA that divides, metabolizes sugar, and repairs its membrane is alive, despite being made in a lab. A seed buried for centuries that suddenly sprouts was alive all along, just dormant. A computer program that copies itself across networks, accumulates mutations, and competes for memory feels like it should be in the same category as the virus—so I say not alive—but I'm uneasy about why. Meanwhile, a sealed chemical loop in a tube with no DNA or cells that maintains steady temperature, rebuilds its container if cracked, and sustains its own reaction cycles for years seems autonomous in a way that pulls me toward calling it alive, yet my gut says it is not.

The tension is that I treat the synthetic cell and the chemical loop differently, even though both maintain themselves and adapt, and I treat the biological virus and the computer program the same, even though one is made of molecules and the other is pure code. Is there one principle that captures all of these verdicts, or must I overturn one? And whatever principle survives, what should it say about a purely digital organism that evolves by natural selection inside a server farm with no physical body at all?
