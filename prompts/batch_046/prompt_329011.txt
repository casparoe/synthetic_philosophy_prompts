Here is an argument I constructed for a metaphysics seminar that now strikes me as suspicious because, if sound, it would eliminate dispositions altogether, yet I want to preserve at least some dispositional properties. (1) A property counts as a genuine disposition of an object only if there is some counterfactual scenario in which the object manifests that property, and in which the manifestation is not prevented by any factor that is itself part of the object’s intrinsic state. (2) For any candidate disposition D of an object O, we can conceive of a fink—an intrinsic mechanism or environmental shield—that is present in O and that would block D’s manifestation the very moment the triggering stimulus occurs, without altering O’s observable categorical base. (3) Similarly, for any categorical base B that is supposed to ground D, we can conceive of a mask that allows B to be present while suppressing D, or allows D to be absent while B mimics it. (4) Therefore, for any O, the presence of D is compatible with both manifestation and non-manifestation, and the presence of B is compatible with both the presence and absence of D. (5) Now apply an identification constraint on ontology: if two hypotheses about O—one attributing D, one denying it—are compatible with all possible accessible information about O, then the difference between them is ontologically idle; it makes no difference to the structure of reality that we should recognize. (6) Because finks and masks are always conceptually available, the D-hypothesis and the non-D-hypothesis are never distinguishable by any accessible evidence. (7) So attributions of D are ontologically idle; there are no dispositions in our ontology, only categorical bases plus the occasional illusion of tendency. (8) But by parallel reasoning, any categorical base B can itself be finked or masked at a deeper level—e.g., the structural properties that ground fragility could be shielded by quantum-level interference patterns—so the same argument applies recursively until we are left with no intrinsic properties at all, or with a purely relational structure lacking any categorical ground. Where is the misstep? I am inclined to reject (5) rather than (1) or (2), because I think dispositions can be real even if epistemically inaccessible in particular cases. But I want to know: if I reject (5), do I have to abandon a general principle linking ontology to epistemic accessibility, or is there a narrower reading of (5) that fails? And if I instead reject (2) or (4), what does that commit me to about the modal scope of finks? Please diagnose the argument step by step, say which premise is least costly to surrender, and explain what each rejection costs in terms of metaphysical or epistemological commitments.
