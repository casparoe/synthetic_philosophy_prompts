I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on democratic theory and its ancient origins. I argue that Cleon's urgency at Mytilene, Alcibiades' compressed strategic vision for Sicily, and the compressed moral panic after the mutilation of the herms all exhibit the same pattern: the assembly transforms complex policy evaluation into a single-axis decision under time pressure, structurally encouraged by immediate voting, the absence of procedural cooling-off mechanisms, and the rhetorical economy of the ekklēsia.

My audience is a faculty-led reading group of six: three classical historians who specialize in fifth- and fourth-century Athenian institutions and are deeply skeptical of anachronistic theoretical framing; one political theorist who works on contemporary democratic deliberation and will test whether my framework maps cleanly onto ancient source material; one graduate student in ancient philosophy who treats the Socrates trial as an exceptional moral event rather than a structural symptom; and one comparative politics scholar who will ask whether "deliberative compression" applies only to direct democracy or extends to representative systems.
