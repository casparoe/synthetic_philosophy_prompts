As a novelist who occasionally leads interdisciplinary seminars, I am preparing a ninety-minute session for twelve graduate students in literature and philosophy. The subject is hinge epistemology in Wittgenstein's On Certainty: the claim that certain propositions do not function as premises supported by evidence but as hinges—conditions that must stand fast if doubt, justification, and inquiry are to have any intelligible form at all. I want the participants to feel this structurally, not to hear it explained.

Please design a structured, rule-based activity—ideally a collaborative deliberation or role-play with assigned roles, material constraints, and an explicit timeline—that stages the experience of hinge-collapse. The scenario should be a fictional tribunal evaluating testimony about an invented historical event, but with one hinge proposition embedded invisibly in the rules of evidence-evaluation itself (for instance, a procedural rule that presupposes the continuity of personal identity across testimony, or the reliability of a specific kind of record). The rules must be arranged so that when one participant attempts to doubt that hinge, the group discovers not that the claim is false but that the very moves for assessing truth, contradiction, and relevance have become unavailable—like a game whose board has disappeared.

Include the setting and specific roles; the rules and any non-monetary payoff or scoring mechanism; a minute-by-minute timeline for the full ninety minutes; a debrief that maps precise moments in the activity onto Wittgenstein's distinction between hinges and riverbed propositions, and onto the idea that hinges are not premises within the framework but conditions of it; an honest note on which feature of hinge epistemology this simulation necessarily fails to reproduce and that the facilitator must supply verbally; and a prediction of what will predictably go wrong in the first run, with instructions on recovery. Do not open with a lecture; do not ask participants to read secondary literature in advance; and do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to someone accustomed to constructing narrative architecture.
