I'm drafting notes for a short essay on the debate between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over just deserts and the justification of punishment. In a recent philosophy of law seminar we were discussing whether compatibilism actually rescues retributivism, and I found both positions unsatisfying in a specific way: they seem to agree that the central question is whether the offender metaphysically deserves suffering for a past wrong, but they diverge only on whether determinism undermines that metaphysical relation. I want to propose a different framing. Instead of asking whether an agent is the ultimate source of her action, I think we should ask whether the institution of punishment addresses the offender in the right temporal register. Dennett tends to treat moral responsibility as a kind of local, evolutionary achievement: we are responsible if we are responsive to reasons in the right way, and punishment can be justified as a kind of reactive attitude that reinforces that responsiveness. Caruso, by contrast, rejects this because he thinks determinism makes the reactive attitude irrational, and he wants to replace punishment with a public-health model that treats crime as a social pathology to be managed.

My idea is that both miss a second-order problem: punishment is not merely a reaction to a past event or a causal intervention on a future one, but a communicative act that presupposes the offender's present capacity for normative uptake. I call this prospective desert—not that the offender earned suffering, but that the institution must be able to justify its coercive measures to the offender as someone who can now recognize and engage with the normative structure being enforced. This doesn't require libertarian free will, but it also doesn't collapse into Caruso's purely instrumental framework, because the justification fails if the offender is genuinely incapable of such uptake, for instance due to severe cognitive impairment or coercive duress that has destroyed agency. At the same time, it doesn't depend on Dennett's evolutionary story about reason-responsiveness, because it focuses on the institutional conditions of moral address rather than on the metaphysics of action. The key distinction I am drawing is between diachronic authorship, which concerns whether the agent originated the wrongful act, and synchronic normative capacity, which concerns whether she can presently be addressed as a participant in the normative order being enforced.

Could you comment on these notes? Specifically, I am interested in whether this framing genuinely avoids collapsing back into either Dennett's compatibilist defense of desert or Caruso's rejection of it—does prospective desert just rename Dennett's reactive attitudes, or does it secretly reintroduce a retributive element? I am also unsure whether the distinction between diachronic authorship and synchronic normative capacity is philosophically robust, or whether it is merely a verbal distinction without institutional bite. Finally, I wonder if there are obvious objections from either side that I have missed: for example, might Caruso argue that any form of desert, even prospective, still treats the offender as a means to institutional stability? And might Dennett reply that my framing ignores the evolutionary function of punishment as a mechanism for stabilizing social cooperation? Please do not provide citations or a bibliography; I want to see whether the conceptual structure holds up on its own.
