I have recently been diagnosed with a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and am evaluating an experimental therapy in which damaged cortical tissue is replaced incrementally—roughly two percent per month—with functionally identical synthetic substrates. Between each surgical batch, the patient is placed in a medically induced coma lasting several days, after which psychological continuity, memory, and behavioral dispositions are verified to be intact.

Please argue for the position that, despite perfect preservation of psychological continuity and functional equivalence, this protocol fails to preserve my personal identity. The core claim should be that genuine self-persistence in gradual replacement requires an unbroken diachronic chain of first-person indexical reference—the continuous capacity to think of present experience as belonging to the same being who had yesterday's experience—and that the intermittent induced comas sever this chain in a way that mere sleep or routine anesthesia does not. Distinguish this failure from standard psychological-continuity accounts (along Parfit's lines) that tolerate gaps, and explain why the synthetic substrate's functional identity does not restore the broken indexical link once consciousness resumes. Address the objection that if every mental state is causally connected across the gap, identity is already secured. Do not rely solely on Ship-of-Theseus analogies; develop the specific point that gradual replacement preserves identity only when there is never a stage at which the subject lacks even the potential for immediate self-attribution. Conclude by stating what this implies for my decision to enroll, without asking for clinical recommendations.
