John Stuart Mill defended freedom of expression on the ground that even false opinions invigorate the search for truth, and he permitted interference with speech only where it caused direct, assignable harm to identifiable others—famously illustrated by the corn-dealer case. Today, synthetic media and algorithmic amplification allow false or misleading claims to be generated and distributed at scale without any particular speaker, audience, or direct causal chain to physical injury. Some contemporary theorists argue that this produces diffuse epistemic externalities: a cumulative degradation of shared informational conditions that undermines rational deliberation itself, yet cannot be captured by Mill's direct-harm criterion because the damage is systemic, probabilistic, and distributed across an anonymous public.

Predict, with careful reasoning, how Mill might have responded to this challenge. Would he have expanded the harm principle to encompass systemic damage to the conditions of public reason, or would he have maintained that such diffuse risks must be tolerated to preserve individual autonomy of judgment? In your answer, distinguish between state censorship and private platform governance—since Mill's concern was primarily with social coercion and state power, though he also recognized the force of custom and public opinion. Further, consider whether his distinction between harm and mere offense can survive when synthetic speech operates at a scale and anonymity that severs the link between utterance and accountable agency. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; construct the argument solely from the internal logic of Mill's position as applied to these novel conditions.
