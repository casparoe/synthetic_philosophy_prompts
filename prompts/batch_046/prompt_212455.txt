I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy, currently auditing a graduate seminar on defensive harm and institutional roles in war, and I've become fixated on a recurring argumentative pattern I keep encountering both in the course discussions and in informal conversations with peers about the moral equality of combatants. The pattern is not exactly Walzer's separation of jus ad bellum from jus in bello, nor is it the revisionist denial of that separation, but something more unstable that I don't have a name for. An interlocutor will defend a soldier fighting for an unjust cause by emphasizing his epistemic limitations, conscription, or bureaucratic role—insisting that he is not personally blameworthy and should be treated, with respect to culpability and desert of punishment, as morally on a par with a soldier on the just side. Yet when the conversation turns to whether the opposing soldier is permitted to kill him, the same interlocutor describes him as a legitimate target precisely because he participates in an unjust aggressive enterprise, treating him as a wrongful threat who forfeits rights against harm, despite having just insisted that he lacks the culpability that usually grounds such forfeiture.

What I want to understand is whether there is an established concept for this specific bifurcation—this preservation of moral equality at the level of blame and post-war accountability combined with its suspension at the level of defensive permissions—so that the unjust soldier functions as a non-culpable yet rights-forfeiting aggressor. I have noticed it arising in concrete cases, from debates about drone operators with limited situational awareness to discussions of conscripted infantry and historical examples like soldiers in unjust invasions, where the speaker wants to block moral condemnation without blocking the permissibility of defensive killing. Does this configuration have a name of its own, and if not, can it be defined precisely in a way that distinguishes it both from Walzer's symmetrical rights view and from revisionist accounts that ground forfeiture in culpability? I would especially value an account of where its conceptual edges lie—under what conditions it collapses into pure equality, under what conditions it collapses into revisionism, and whether it represents a genuinely distinct theoretical position or merely an unstable hybrid.
