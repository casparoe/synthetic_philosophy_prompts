I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for a mixed audience of philosophy graduate students and clinical psychology trainees on the problem of ascription universality and its entanglement with epistemic dominance. The central claim I want them to grasp is that the universal applicability of psychological and normative concepts—treating them as default ascriptions that apply uniformly across subjects—is not a neutral descriptive baseline but a structural posture that can dominate or displace local, contextual, or alternative conceptual schemes. I want the session to make this tension philosophically visible without allowing it to collapse into either naive relativism or blanket skepticism about other minds.

Plan the explanatory arc precisely. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be secured before the problem of universality can arise at all, which must follow to prevent the audience from conflating universal ascription with empirical generalization, and where the notion of epistemic dominance must be introduced so that it functions as a critical lens rather than a mere accusation of bias. Second, specify the one carrying example or case study that should carry the entire session—something concrete enough for the psychology trainees, philosophically sharp enough for the philosophers, and possessing both widespread recognizability and a specific point of conceptual fracture; explain what properties it must have and why more familiar illustrations (standard other-minds scenarios, color-variation cases, or simple translation failures) fail to carry this particular idea. Third, map the misconceptions: I expect they will initially take universal ascription to be an empirical claim about human similarity, and they will understand epistemic dominance as explicit suppression rather than as a structural default built into conceptual practice; state exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted and defused. Fourth, propose one diagnostic question or brief conceptual exercise I can administer at the close to determine whether they have genuinely understood the tension between universality and dominance, rather than having merely adopted a skeptical posture. Finally, state explicitly what should be refused at this level—whether a particular strand of descriptive metaphysics, an empirical literature on cross-cultural psychology, or a formal apparatus—and justify why leaving it out is necessary for the idea to land clearly.
