I'm giving the toast at my older sister's retirement dinner next month. She spent thirty years as a counselor at a community mental-health center, mostly working with people in addiction recovery, and the room will be split: about half her colleagues—social workers, therapists, people who think about moral agency professionally—and the other half extended family who have never read philosophy. Over the past year I've been turning over, entirely on my own, the line of thought associated with Galen Strawson's basic argument against moral responsibility: the idea that if anyone is truly responsible for an action, they must be responsible for the nature or character that caused it, which in turn requires being responsible for the causes of that nature, and so on, in a regress that can only end if someone is causa sui—cause of themselves—which is impossible. I want this idea to do real structural work in the toast, not just decorate it. Specifically, I need to honor her career without using the language these dinners usually rely on: praising her for having "chosen" compassion as if it were a pure act of will she fully deserves credit for, or describing her clients as people who "made bad choices" and then "took responsibility" for fixing them. The argument makes me think that framing recovery as the correction of an ultimate moral failure misses something essential, and framing her own dedication as an uncaused choice she can claim full desert for is equally shaky. At the same time, I must not lecture the family; they should feel warmth and pride, not sit through a seminar on free will. She always said her job was listening to people explain themselves to themselves, and I want that to be in there. Can you write a five-minute toast that holds this line—warm, specific, shaped by the thought that none of us is the ultimate author of our nature—so that her colleagues recognize the philosophical move and everyone else simply hears a loving tribute that never assigns blame or ultimate credit where it doesn't belong? Please don't include citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts; I only need the speech itself.
