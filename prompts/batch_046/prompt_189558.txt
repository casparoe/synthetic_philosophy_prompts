I am drafting a philosophical novel in which a character must choose whether to disperse a singular suffering across multitudes or to concentrate it, and to do that I need to understand my own intuitive judgments about aggregation. Here are my verdicts on five constructed cases; they feel mostly consistent, yet I cannot reconcile the second and the third without strain.

Case 1. One innocent person is tortured intensely for a full year. The alternative is that every living human—roughly eight billion—receives a single dust speck in one eye for exactly one second, with no lasting effect. Verdict: the year of torture is incomparably worse; the dust specks do not accumulate into a harm of comparable moral weight.

Case 2. One person suffers the same intense torture for exactly one hour. The alternative is that 3.6 million people each suffer the identical intensity for exactly one millisecond—no more, no less—with each experiencing it as a real, if unrecalled, event. Verdict: I find the distributed version worse, not equivalent; the multiplication of violated subjects seems to outweigh the brevity of each violation.

Case 3. One person endures a moderate, fully conscious headache for ten years. The alternative is that every human endures a headache of precisely the same qualitative character, but so faint that it never reaches the threshold of awareness, sustained for the same decade. Verdict: the single conscious headache is a genuine harm; the distributed, sub-threshold discomforts do not aggregate and are not morally comparable.

Case 4. A policy reduces every human being's lifespan by exactly one minute, spread uniformly across eight billion people. The alternative is that one randomly chosen person dies instantly and without pain. Verdict: the single death is far worse; the diffuse minute-loss is not a harm of the same order.

Case 5. A hypothetical technology divides one hour of intense suffering into ten million discrete fragments, each experienced by a different temporal slice of the same original consciousness, none retaining memory or identity with the others; together the fragments sum to the full hour. The alternative is that one continuous, intact consciousness experiences the hour undivided. Verdict: the fragmented version is barely harmful at all—perhaps not torture—while the intact version is horrific.

The scenario I keep revising, and on which I genuinely oscillate, is this: 100 million people are brought into existence solely to experience exactly ten seconds of severe pain each, then are painlessly ended, having had no other conscious states; versus one existing person who suffers severely for one hour. Sometimes the 100 million feel like an overwhelming aggregate wrong; sometimes they feel like isolated flickers that do not sum.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps involving continuity of identity, the threshold of conscious registration, or the moral status of discrete subjects—that captures all five verdicts? If no single principle can save them all, which verdict should I abandon as the outlier? Please state your principle exactly, show how it applies to each listed case, predict its verdict on my 100-million-person scenario, and then apply it to one further case of your own design: a world in which every human lives with a constant, imperceptible background anxiety that never crosses the threshold of awareness but cumulatively reduces global well-being by an amount equivalent to one lifetime of severe clinical depression.
