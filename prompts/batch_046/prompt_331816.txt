You are delivering the opening remarks at a workshop on institutional resilience convened to honor the retirement of a senior civil engineer whose career was spent designing flood-management infrastructure. The audience is evenly divided: academic political theorists, several of whom specialize in Machiavelli’s distinction between fortuna and virtù, and practicing engineers and municipal planners who have never read The Prince. You wish to honor the engineer by arguing that her finest achievements exemplify a specifically Machiavellian form of virtue—not the domination of fortune, but what you term “calibrated yielding”: the disciplined design of systems that preserve human agency not by denying contingency but by accommodating it structurally. The remarks must do genuine philosophical work: they should make it conceptually impossible to praise her for “mastering nature” or “eliminating risk,” and should instead redirect every claim about her legacy through the lens of virtù as responsive adaptation rather than conquest. The theorists should hear the distinction operating precisely; the practitioners should hear it as an account of good craft. Write a speech of approximately ten minutes. Do not lecture the non-specialists on Machiavelli, do not cite editions or secondary literature, and do not include a bibliography or reading list. The distinction between fortune and virtù should shape what you claim she accomplished, what you explicitly concede she could not accomplish, and what kind of legacy this leaves for her field.
