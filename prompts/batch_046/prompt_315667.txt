For my senior philosophy seminar, I am preparing a creative submission that uses Adam Smith's moral framework—specifically the distinction in The Theory of Moral Sentiments between sympathy (the imaginative exchange of situations) and mere emotional contagion, and his notion of the impartial spectator as an internalized standard of propriety—to examine a contemporary problem not usually paired with Smith. Please write a short story about a forensic accountant who discovers that her firm has deployed predictive algorithms designed to identify and exploit the emotional vulnerabilities of retirement-age investors, thereby automating what Smith might call a failure of sympathy. In the narrative, she should attempt to apply genuine Smithian sympathy: she must imaginatively enter the situation of the investors without allowing her own fear of professional ruin to distort the exchange. Render her impartial spectator not as a single serene internal voice, but as a fractured chorus composed of the specific individuals she has wronged, whose judgments she has internalized in incompatible ways. The plot should take a definite position: it should argue, through its imagery and resolution, that Smithian sympathy requires an irreducibly personal imaginative labor that collapses when mediated by data-driven risk models, and therefore cannot be outsourced to algorithmic assessment. After the story, include a concise author's note that identifies precisely where the narrative takes liberties with Smith's account—especially whether a fractured, plural spectator is consistent with Smith's conception of the internal judge—and that states clearly, with reasons, whether you believe Smith's framework actually supports the anti-automation thesis the story advances.
