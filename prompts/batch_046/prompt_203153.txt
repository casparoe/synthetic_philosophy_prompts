I'm taking a graduate seminar on jurisprudence, and we've been tracking my professor's verdicts on proposed legislation that enforces a particular moral view without an obvious harm-based justification—precisely the terrain of legal moralism. She endorsed an ordinance banning the open-air display of graphic violence in storefront advertising within five hundred meters of schools, arguing that the state may preserve the "moral atmosphere" of shared public spaces even when no direct psychological harm to viewers is documented. She opposed criminalizing private, consensual adult cohabitation arrangements involving more than two partners, insisting that intimate domestic structures lie beyond the state's legitimate jurisdiction. She also supported a law requiring all non-essential retail businesses to close on a nationally designated day of collective mourning, explicitly grounding the mandate not in public-order risks but in the duty to maintain shared moral rhythms. Finally, she rejected a bill that would have made it a misdemeanor to possess, in one's private residence, artistic works that a state board deems "morally corrupting," maintaining that private belief and consumption are not proper objects of moral legislation.

Now the city council is considering a new regulation: it would require every restaurant open to the public to display, in each private dining room, a government-issued statement affirming the moral value of traditional family structures, with civil fines for refusal. Proponents argue that because restaurants are spaces of public accommodation, displaying the statement reinforces the communal moral fabric without preventing patrons from holding dissenting views behind closed doors.

Given these precedents, what should my professor conclude if she wants to remain consistent with herself? Identify the two or three plausible underlying rules that could equally well explain her four earlier verdicts, and for each rule say whether it predicts endorsement or rejection of the dining-room display mandate. Which of her prior rulings is the closest analog to this case, and what is your best guess at her actual verdict, with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others? Name the one further hypothetical case that would most sharply distinguish the competing principles. If she endorses the mandate, what principle is she implicitly adopting about the boundary between public moral expression and coerced private speech in commercially shared spaces? If she rejects it, what principle is she adopting about the limits of moral legislation in physically enclosed but publicly accessible domains?
