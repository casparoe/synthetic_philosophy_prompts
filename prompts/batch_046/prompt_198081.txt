I sit on the patient-family oversight board for a regional neurology clinic that has proposed a five-year pilot trial for individuals with progressive cortical degeneration. The protocol would replace approximately two percent of the patient’s neurons each month with synthetic analogues that replicate action potentials and synaptic weights, with the researchers claiming that because no single interruption exceeds ordinary cellular turnover, personal identity and conscious experience remain intact across the full transition. I have been discussing this with relatives and trying to read about gradual replacement on my own, and I have become stuck on a concern that feels philosophical rather than medical: each synthetic unit might reproduce the biological original’s signaling almost exactly, but with a minute, systematic bias—say, a slightly altered weighting of emotional salience—so that over sixty months the accumulated “salience drift” produces not the original patient but a psychologically continuous successor who remembers her life with subtly different emotional coloring. The board must issue a formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressing whether this possibility undermines the claim that gradual replacement preserves the very person being treated, not merely a functional copy that inherits her memories.

In drafting this memo, please treat my salience-drift scenario as the central philosophical crux rather than a technical malfunction: explain whether identity can survive a slow, imperceptible transformation in how experience is valued, address the strongest counter-argument I have heard—that ordinary aging and sleep already alter emotional weighting without destroying identity—and include an honest assessment of why that reply might still miss the point in this engineered case. If you recommend approval, specify concrete conditions—such as divergence-monitoring thresholds, periodic consent reassessment protocols, or limits on cumulative synthetic integration—that respond directly to the drift concern. The recommendation should be actionable for the clinic and the reasoning should be accessible enough that board members without philosophy backgrounds can follow it, yet precise enough that the researchers could reply to each point in writing.
