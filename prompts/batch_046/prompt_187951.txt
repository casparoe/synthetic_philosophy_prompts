My friend and I argued for hours last night about mental causation, and I have tried to formalise our exchange into the argument below. It now strikes me as suspicious, because if its logic is sound it does not merely exclude mental properties from the causal order but seems to evacuate causation from every intermediate scientific level as well. I want to know exactly where the slip is.

(1) Non-reductive physicalism holds that mental properties M metaphysically supervene on physical properties P without being identical to them.
(2) The causal exclusion principle states that if P is causally sufficient for an effect E, and M supervenes on P, then M is causally redundant with respect to E, unless we are willing to tolerate systematic overdetermination.
(3) In every actual case, some neural-physical property P is causally sufficient for the corresponding behavioural effect E.
(4) Hence, by (2), mental properties are causally redundant for all behaviour.
(5) But physical properties P themselves supervene on more fundamental microphysical arrangements Q, and Q is causally sufficient for P and therefore for E.
(6) Applying (2) again, P is itself causally redundant with respect to E.
(7) Iterating downward, only the most fundamental microphysical properties retain causal efficacy; every intermediate level—chemical, biological, neural, mental—is rendered epiphenomenal.
(8) Yet if only fundamental properties are causally efficacious, then scientific generalisations about tables, genes, or neurons have no stable causal grounding, since fundamental properties never recur across contexts in projectible ways.
(9) Therefore either (2) is false, or we must accept that science is systematically about causally inert kinds.
(10) The latter is absurd, so (2) must fail—but my friend insists that we can retain (2) for the mental-physical relation while blocking its downward application elsewhere.

Which step should I reject, and why did it appear sound? In particular, is there an equivocation between token-level and type-level causal sufficiency, or between metaphysical supervenience and constitutive realisation, that allows the argument to slide illegitimately from mental properties to macroscopic physical ones? If I must choose, I would prefer to preserve the intuition behind (2) for mental properties but deny its iteration—so tell me what that choice costs for the standard positions: the non-reductive physicalist who appeals to non-strict mental laws, the reductionist who identifies mental and physical properties, and the emergentist who accepts downward causation. What must each pay to keep (2) local?
