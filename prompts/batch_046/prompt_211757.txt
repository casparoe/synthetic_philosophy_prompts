I'm trying to determine where rational satisficing ends and negligent stopping begins, going by my gut on concrete cases. A hiring manager who ends interviews the moment she finds a candidate who meets every ex-ante criterion feels right, even if a stronger applicant might appear later; the search cost is genuine and the threshold was preset. A climate modeler who accepts a coarse parameterization of cloud feedback because finer resolution is computationally intractable, knowing this introduces a predictable bias, also feels correct—this is bounded rationality accommodating a hard constraint. But a philosopher who stops hunting for counterexamples to her view the instant she devises one reply feels wrong; she hasn't hit a computational wall, she's just avoiding discomfort. An emergency physician who halts diagnostic testing once she finds a condition that explains the chief symptoms feels right, unless the remaining unexplained symptom is precisely the marker that distinguishes a benign illness from a fatal one—there the same stopping behavior strikes me as reckless rather than bounded.

Some cases sit in between. A shopper who buys the first laptop satisfying her budget and spec sheet seems rationally bounded. Yet applying the identical "first adequate option" rule to selecting a cancer treatment protocol—choosing the first oncologist-suggested plan that sounds acceptable—feels negligent, as if the stakes dissolve the license to stop searching. Similarly, an optimization algorithm that terminates once per-iteration utility gain drops below a fixed epsilon behaves like a proper satisficer, but if that epsilon is hard-coded identically for office-supply allocation and for organ-transplant matching, the uniformity feels like a category error in the bound itself.

Is there one principle that separates the legitimate cases from the illegitimate ones—perhaps that the aspiration level must be set before encountering options, that search costs must be structurally unavoidable rather than motivational, and that stopping rules must scale with the asymmetry of potential error? Or do institutional, cognitive, and algorithmic satisficing require separate principles? And what should I conclude about the case I keep revisiting: a researcher who stops data collection upon reaching p < .05, but whose judgment about whether that was appropriate shifts sharply depending on whether the observed effect size is trivial or substantial? Does this mean her stopping rule should be indexed to effect magnitude, or that she is smuggling an optimizing criterion back into a framework that was supposed to replace it?
