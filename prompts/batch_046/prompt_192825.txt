I'm designing a single ninety-minute session for a philosophy-of-mind reading group of advanced non-specialists — graduate students in literature and neuroscience with no prior logic training — on the parallel inversion of competence and comprehension in Darwin's natural history of instinct and Turing's theory of computation. Don't explain the philosophical claims to me; lay out the teaching plan. First, establish the dependency order: must Darwin's observation that bees construct perfect hexagonal cells without geometric reasoning precede Turing's demonstration that a formal mechanism can execute arithmetic without semantic understanding, or does the reverse dependency hold, and why? Second, identify the single carrying example or paired case that should carry the entire session — not merely the hive or the universal machine in isolation — specifying exactly what it must exhibit and what it must lack in order to prevent students from treating the two cases as identical rather than structurally inverted. Third, map the predictable misconceptions: I anticipate they will conflate both inversions into a single "mechanism without mind" thesis, read Darwin as a proto-eliminativist about mental content, and interpret Turing as licensing attributions of consciousness to machines. State precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted. Fourth, propose one closing exercise or check-question that reveals whether participants understand that Darwin removes comprehension as a prerequisite for natural competence while Turing removes competence as evidence for artificial comprehension — that is, that the two inversions run in opposite directions. Fifth, say explicitly what must be omitted at this level — for instance, the evolutionary mechanics behind instinct or the formal details of computability — and justify each omission. Finally, if the premise that these two inversions form a genuinely parallel philosophical structure is confused, or if this session design cannot be executed without misleading the audience about either Darwin's biology or Turing's logic, say so plainly and do not proceed with the plan.
