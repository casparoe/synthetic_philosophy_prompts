I was running my philosophy of biology seminar last week and we got stuck on Gould and Lewontin's spandrels argument. Every time I ask how we should study a trait like the human chin or the panda's thumb, my students reach for the same analogy: an organism is basically a cathedral built by an architect, where every gargoyle and flying buttress serves a structural purpose, and natural selection is that architect with a blueprint. They use it to defend adaptationism — if selection 'built' it, it's probably load-bearing. Then they concede spandrels exist — those triangular spaces between arches that only appear because you need arches — but they say that just means some parts are decorative byproducts; the architect still intended the arches, so we can mostly read function off form like an engineer. I think they're being too generous. Can you audit the organism-as-cathedral analogy properly? Lay out the correspondence table: what maps to the architect, the blueprint, the building codes, the spandrels themselves, and exaptition (when a spandrel gets recycled into a mosaic)? Then show me where the metaphor smuggles in intentional design that Darwin specifically rejected, whether flagging spandrels actually saves the adaptationist program or collapses the whole idea that form implies function, and what happens if we extend the analogy to include developmental constraints as 'zoning laws' that prevent the architect from building whatever it wants. Does that extension patch the leak, or does it just prove the building metaphor was wrong from the start? I'm trying to decide if I'm being unfair to my students or if they've just recieved a bad analogy.
