In my philosophy of biology reading group we have been stuck for months on a question about the prisoner's dilemma and the evolution of cooperation, and the impasse feels stranger the longer it lasts. One camp holds that direct reciprocity—Tit-for-Tat, generous strategies, and the shadow of the future—genuinely solves the one-shot dilemma without altering its strategic structure: the payoffs remain those of a prisoner's dilemma at each encounter, yet cooperation becomes rational or evolutionarily stable because the interaction is iterated with a sufficient discount factor. The other camp insists that every successful evolutionary explanation—partner choice, punishment, spatial clustering, or reputation—works precisely by transforming the payoff matrix or the strategy space, so that cooperation evolves only because the interaction is no longer, in any meaningful sense, a prisoner's dilemma. Both sides accept the same formal results, cite the same tournament data and Nowak’s five rules, and agree that cooperation is widespread in nature; yet they talk past each other about whether this counts as “solving” the dilemma or “dissolving” it by changing the game.

I wonder whether the stall is a hidden conceptual disagreement about what an explanation must preserve—whether it needs to keep the defection incentive intact at the level of the individual interaction—or whether it is a verbal dispute about the word “solution” that masks two genuinely different explanatory targets, one drawn from rational-choice theory and one from evolutionary dynamics. Could there also be an unacknowledged empirical divergence about whether real-world biological interactions are best modeled as repeated games with imperfect monitoring or as one-shot games embedded in a larger partner-choice market? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here: a formal equivalence result between iterated games with error correction and one-shot games with endogenous partner selection; a conceptual distinction between solving and dissolving a strategic dilemma; or an argument that no single framework can cover both rational agents and evolving populations, so that the question itself should be split? And if the disagreement turns out to rest on bedrock intuitions about what makes an explanation mechanistically honest rather than structurally reductive, should we expect it to move at all?
