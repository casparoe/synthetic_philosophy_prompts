I have recently begun working as a clinical ethics mediator for a regional hospice and long-term care network, and in nearly every family conference about withdrawing life-sustaining treatment from a permanently unconscious patient, I encounter the same conviction: that to stop the machines is to harm the patient, as if death or unconsciousness were an injury being inflicted rather than a state being allowed. I want to turn the Epicurean argument—that death is not harmful to the one who dies, because harm presupposes a subject who can experience it, and where the subject is absent there is no one for whom the state can be bad—into a reusable, fifteen-minute decision procedure my mediation team can apply consistently, without requiring philosophical literacy from the clinicians or family members present. To prevent the framework from dissolving into generic cost-benefit reasoning, I am integrating a niche distinction I have been working with: between terminal absence, in which no experiencing subject can possibly resume, and interrupted presence, in which the neurological conditions for subjectivity are suspended but not destroyed, with the harm-block applying only to the former.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist that separates empirical questions—does the patient currently instantiate the conditions for experiential harm? could those conditions resume?—from value questions—do surrogates prefer continuation regardless? does the institution have obligations that override the harm analysis? For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure must stop with a clear determination that no harm-to-patient is occurring, and where it must hand the decision to a human mediator because the issue is one of values or irreducible ambiguity. The checklist must include explicit tests for four subject-persistence conditions: Type A (no present and no recoverable subject), Type B (no present but recoverable subject), Type C (present subject without experiential suffering), and Type D (present subject with suffering). It should also flag the cases the procedure cannot settle—particularly where diagnostic uncertainty blurs the line between Type A and Type B.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: first, a patient in a confirmed permanent vegetative state whose adult children demand indefinite continuation on religious grounds—this is the case where I expect the procedure to yield an answer I personally find uncomfortable, since it will indicate that withdrawal does not harm the patient, leaving the conflict purely in the domain of values rather than patient welfare; second, a locked-in patient who reports severe, unrelieved suffering despite intact consciousness; and third, a patient in a minimally conscious state with disputed, intermittent awareness where the procedure should fail to deliver a definitive answer. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure is not designed to do: it does not resolve conflicts among family members, it does not determine whether life has intrinsic value independent of experience, and it does not substitute for legal or institutional protocols on surrogate consent. The deliverable should be something my team could literally print, laminate, and run in a conference room with no outside resources.
