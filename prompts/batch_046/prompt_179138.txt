I'm working through Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling in a seminar on philosophy of religion, and I have built an argument about the teleological suspension of the ethical that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to license almost any sincerely felt moral violation. Here is the chain I have in mind. (1) Abraham is justified in suspending the universal ethical prohibition against killing Isaac only because he stands in an absolute relation to the Absolute—God—rather than to any finite or shared value. (2) This relation is essentially singular and incommunicable; Abraham cannot justify himself to Sarah, Isaac, or the ethical community, and that incommunicability is part of what distinguishes the knight of faith from the tragic hero who remains within the universal. (3) But if singularity and incommunicability are the formal conditions that make suspension possible, then anyone who experiences an unconditional, non-negotiable call—whether from God, conscience, or an ultimate value like justice or beauty—occupies the very same formal structure: an individual relating absolutely to an absolute that cannot be mediated to others. (4) Therefore, the knight of faith is not a unique biblical exception but a recurring existential type available to any agent with an intense subjective conviction that violates shared norms. (5) One might reply, as Kierkegaard seems to, that Abraham differs because he retains inward love for Isaac within the finite and expects to receive him back; the suspension is teleological, not destructive, and this inward retention distinguishes true faith from mere fanaticism. (6) Yet this reply depends on access to Abraham's inward intention, which is precisely what is incommunicable; from any reflective or third-person standpoint, the inward state is epistemically opaque, so the distinction collapses into an unverifiable claim that anyone could invoke. (7) We are then left with a dilemma: either we restrict suspension to biblically attested prophetic revelation, which saves the distinction but renders the knight of faith historically obsolete for modern ethics, or we accept that formal similarity licenses suspension for anyone claiming an incommunicable absolute, which proves far too much by justifying moral violations that share only subjective intensity with Abraham's act. I feel something slips between steps (3) and (6)—perhaps treating formal structural similarity as sufficient for identity of kind—but I cannot see whether rejecting that inference saves the concept or destroys its uniqueness. Which premise or step should I give up, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced to preserve something, I would keep (5) over (7), but I want to understand the price of each option.
