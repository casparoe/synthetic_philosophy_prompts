I host a small interview series for an audience of self-taught thinkers, and next week's guest is a researcher who works on logical induction — the framework for assigning probabilities to logical statements one has not yet had the time or capacity to prove. I have no formal training in mathematics or logic, but I have spent many months with the accessible accounts of the idea, and in thinking through it on my own I have arrived at two concerns that I do not see addressed in the standard presentations.

The first is a reflexive loop in the market analogy itself. The framework imagines a prediction market for logical truths, where prices update over time and are protected against exploitation by efficient computational strategies. But if the market can form beliefs about its own future prices — for instance, a sentence that asserts something about where its own price will stand at a later time — then the dynamics that determine that price seem to depend on the belief that is being priced. I want to know whether the convergence guarantees implicitly forbid such self-referential conditions, or whether they genuinely accommodate them and still preserve coherence.

The second is an analogy I have been developing to financial market behavior. In ordinary markets, liquidity can vanish when every trader expects every other trader to withdraw, producing a freeze that has nothing to do with the underlying value of the assets. I wonder whether logical induction contains an analog: sentences on which the rationality conditions become so tightly coupled through mutual expectation among the traders that the market stops updating — not because the sentence is formally undecidable, but because beliefs about one another's beliefs lock into a fixed point. I think of this as a possible "logical liquidity freeze."

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that stay deep in the mechanics of the framework. I need at least one question pressing whether the efficiency-based protection against exploitation silently rules out reflexive price conditions; at least one asking whether a logical liquidity freeze is compatible with the convergence theorems or whether it reveals a hidden non-circularity assumption; and for each, a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that the framework already handles this through its bounded-computation design, pushing that reply further rather than letting it close the matter. The questions should assume the listener understands the basic market image but should not ask for biographical background, definitions of terms, or recommendations for further reading. Keep the tone respectful but technically precise, and avoid anything that would require the guest to cite specific papers or texts from memory.
