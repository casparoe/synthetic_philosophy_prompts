I was discussing this with my freind over coffee yesterday and I still can't shake it. I'm on the grant committee for our neighborhood community foundation — nothing fancy, just local stuff — and we're down to our last 80k for the fiscal year.

There's two proposals left on the table. One is this massive outdoor sensory garden that'd be free for anyone in the district; survey data says it would bump up quality-of-life scores for maybe three thousand people, mostly in small ways like less stress during walks. The other is a year-long intensive crisis program for about twelve residents who are stuck in this kind of extreme, unrelenting psychological pain — not just sadness, but the sort that makes holding a job or even feeding yourself basically impossible. Our charter asks us to maximize aggregate welfare, so if you literally add up all the wellbeing points the garden obviously crushes the other option by miles. But Mara, who's on the board with me, keeps arguing that severe suffering isn't just negative points on the same scale as pleasure. She says once pain hits a certain intensity it becomes categorically different, and you can't cancel it out by giving thousands of people a slightly nicer afternoon, no matter what the totals say. The rest of the committee thinks she's ignoring the math and being sentimental. I feel pulled both ways: part of me thinks the numbers don't lie, but another part feels like there's something morally wrong about letting those twelve people stay in that state just so a larger group can have a pleasant garden. Is Mara's intuition actually defensible, or am I just being irrational?
