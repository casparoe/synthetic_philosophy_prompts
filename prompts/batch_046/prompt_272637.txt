Here is an argument about holding Germany responsible for the First World War that seems to prove too much. (1) To hold a nation morally responsible for a war requires treating that nation as a collective agent that intended or authorized the conflict. (2) The historian Fritz Fischer's archival work shows that key German civilian and military leaders in 1914 actively pursued continental hegemony and treated the July crisis as an opportunity for expansionist war aims. (3) Therefore, treating Germany as a collective agent with aggressive intentions is historically justified. (4) But if shared intentions among a subset of state leaders are sufficient to constitute collective national agency, then any state whose officials encourage an ally's aggression also becomes an agent for that ally's acts. (5) By that standard, Austria-Hungary's encouragement of Serbian irredentism, or Russia's backing of Serbia, makes each empire a collective agent for its partner's provocations, and every alliance system dissolves into a single undifferentiated responsible party. (6) So either collective national agency is impossible—which undermines Fischer-style blame—or blame is distributed so universally that it assigns no distinct accountability to any particular state. (7) I reject (6): universal shared blame is not meaningful blame, yet I want to preserve (2) and (3). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does saving Fischer's conclusion require abandoning collective agency in favor of some other model of state responsibility?
