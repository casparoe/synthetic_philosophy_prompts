I run a small philosophy-of-probability seminar, about 10 grad students, one 60-minute session. Design an in-class betting game that makes them feel Bertrand's paradox in their bones before anyone names it — specifically that applying the principle of indiference to "pick a random chord longer than an inscribed triangle side" gives 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 depending on whether you paramaterize by endpoints, radial distance, or midpoint area. Use a big circle taped on the floor and a thrown stick; run three betting rounds with fake chips where the physical process never changes but the description does. Each round they allocate chips to "yes, longer" vs "no" at odds they think are fair, then you reveal they all watched the same throw and demanded different payouts. Include rules, chip payouts, a debrief mapping each round to its hidden measure, and an honest note on what the game can't reproduce — probably the actual transformation-group argument that tries to break the symmetry. Also tell me what's gonna predictably go wrong (like students catching on in round two and refusing to bet differently) and how you'd salvage the debrief if that happens. Don't just rehash the textbook version; inject a weird framing — maybe call it a "crack in a circular window" with colored sectors that visually bias where they think the stick "should" land, so their intuition gets hijacked twice. Keep it somewhat in the weeds and runnable without anyone needing to look up a citation.
