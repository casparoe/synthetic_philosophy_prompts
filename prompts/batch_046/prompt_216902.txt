I'm preparing questions for a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher who defends a strict singularist reading of Kierkegaard's Abraham and the teleological suspension of the ethical—she holds that the suspension is not an exception carved out of universal moral law but an exposure of ethics' hidden dependency on a prior, non-universalizable relation to the absolute, so that Abraham does not violate the ethical so much as reveal that the ethical is always already conditioned by the singular call that makes it possible. I want a sequence of eight to ten questions that avoid biography and never ask for citations, bibliographies, or further reading. Please begin by pressing her on how she distinguishes Abraham's silence from the silence of a delusional or demonic subject, since both withhold public justification and both act against the universal: what feature of inwardness, if any, is available to Abraham himself rather than reconstructed only by the observer? Include a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to inwardness—ask whether that inwardness is epistemically accessible in the first person or whether it collapses into a retrospective narrative imposed by the believer. Next, ask whether her reading can treat the knight of infinite resignation as a genuine ethical achievement rather than a failed religious stage, and if she invokes Hegel's universal here, press her on whether she is attributing to Kierkegaard a direct critique of Hegel's ethical life or merely a complementary supplement to it. I also want a question about imitation: if Abraham's movement is strictly singular and non-transferable, what normative work can the story perform for anyone other than Abraham, and does that render the text useless for ethical formation rather than edifying? Then introduce a comparative framing she may not have addressed—ask how her account of the singular call relates to contemporary moral particularism, specifically whether the call functions like a particularist reason that defeats all general principles or whether it operates at an entirely different logical level; follow up by asking what would have to change in her view if particularism turned out to be incoherent. End with a conceptual challenge rather than a literature request: what would have to be true about Abraham's psychology, or about the structure of ethical obligation itself, for her singularist reading to collapse—what experience or argument could genuinely count against it? Throughout, anticipate her standard replies and build the follow-ups around them, keeping the focus on the actual content of her position rather than on generic philosophy-of-religion talking points.
