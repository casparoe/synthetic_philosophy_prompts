I'm auditing a philosophy course on ethics and deception, and I've noticed a recurring argumentative structure that I can't place in the literature. The pattern runs like this: someone defends an absolute prohibition on false speech, often invoking Augustine's view that lying is always wrong regardless of consequences. Then, when presented with a harm case—say, hiding a pursued person in your home and being asked directly if anyone is present—they do not concede that you should utter a falsehood. Instead, they relocate the deception into technically accurate formulations arranged to guarantee misunderstanding: "There is no one in this visible area of the house," or "I have not seen anyone enter through the front door," knowing full well that the listener will interpret these as denials of presence. The false statement remains categorically forbidden, but the deliberately misleading true statement is treated as a separate, permissible category. I have seen the same maneuver in engineering analogies—like a server returning an HTTP 200 with an empty payload rather than a 404, technically not misrepresenting the resource state but ensuring the client's parser draws the wrong conclusion—yet I don't know whether philosophers have a precise term for this boundary strategy.

What I'm asking is whether there is an established concept—distinct from generic "misleading" or "equivocation"—for the practice of preserving an unconditional ban on falsehood by pushing all deceptive intent into technically-true formulations. Within Augustine's own framework, is there a recognized distinction between lying and this kind of technically-true deception, or is that boundary a later addition? I'm not satisfied with a bare label; I want to know whether the phenomenon has defined edges, whether it is treated as a legitimate exception or as an ad hoc loophole, and which nearby concepts it is closest to without being identical. If there is no established term, I would also be interested in how one might coin and delimit one, with boundary cases that separate it from ordinary omission of information on one side and from straightforward lying on the other.
