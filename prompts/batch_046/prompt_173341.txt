I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on epistemic logic, and I want a taxonomy—not a literature review, but a decision-tree-style classification—of where one can stand on the problem of logical omniscience. Rather than sorting people by whether they have heard of particular formalisms, I want the branches to be diagnostic commitments that someone could answer without knowing the label that awaits them at the leaf.

The tree should begin with a single root commitment: logical omniscience—the idea that knowing p entails knowing all logical consequences of p—is descriptively false for real agents. From there, the first split should ask whether the fault lies in the formal apparatus (the logic of knowledge is too strong) or in the agent model (real reasoners are not ideal). If the apparatus is at fault, split again between weakening closure rules, for instance through non-normal modal logics or neighbourhood semantics, and expanding the space of worlds to include impossible ones so that not every tautology is accessible. If the agent model is at fault, split between syntactic restriction—awareness of only a subset of formulas—and procedural or resource-bounded restriction, where closure holds in principle but only over feasible inference steps. I want a further cross-cutting branch on whether one allows "epistemic fragmentation": the view that an agent's cognition is modular, so closure holds within each module but fails globally, producing a hybrid that combines awareness-style restriction with impossible-world accessibility across modules. Each leaf should carry the name of an actual position, or be marked "unnamed/unoccupied", together with a one-sentence diagnosis of its most awkward commitment—for example, whether it must deny that mathematicians genuinely know theorems they have not explicitly derived. Where two named interpretations collapse into the same leaf, flag them explicitly. Please also note any combination of answers that seems logically available but is apparently undefended—whether because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please write in British English, take a definite and committed stance on which branches are genuinely distinct and which are merely notational variants, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. I am not asking for a history of the debate; I want an instrument that sorts a respondent by what they actually believe about knowledge, inference, and idealisation.
