I've been thinking on my own about Machiavelli's claim that fortune governs about half of human affairs while virtù can master the rest if it is exercised with sufficient violence—like building banks against a river or beating fortune into submission. In politics and self-help I keep encountering the line "fortune favors the bold," used to justify risky investments, aggressive leadership, or simply taking chances. But I can't tell what precise claim it is making, and whether it actually captures Machiavelli's view or just turns him into a generic motivational speaker.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that slogan could express? For each reading—whether it treats boldness as a statistical filter that captures more upside, a causal force that reshapes the field of possibilities, an expression of virtù that literally reduces fortune's share, or an ex-post rationalization that re-labels lucky outcomes as deserved—say whether it is a tautology, whether it is defensible, and whether it fits Machiavelli's specific idea that fortune must be beaten rather than courted. I am especially interested in whether any reading allows boldness to change which half of affairs you fall into without actually reducing fortune's overall domain, and whether that distinction matters for his comparison of fortune to a woman who must be subdued.
