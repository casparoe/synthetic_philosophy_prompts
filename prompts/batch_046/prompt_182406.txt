Design a classroom game for an upper-level philosophy-of-science seminar of 14 postgraduate students, lasting 80 minutes, that makes participants feel the force of the so-called F-twist in Friedman's methodology—the claim that wildly unrealistic assumptions can yield more accurate predictions than realistic ones, and that adding realism often degrades predictive power rather than rescuing it. Structure the session as a tournament: divide students into groups of three or four and ask each to build a predictive model for a concrete but unfamiliar scenario—forecasting queue lengths and wait times at a new campus café with two baristas—by selecting three assumption cards. One deck offers highly idealised assumptions (customers are identical, omniscient optimisers with no social preferences or fatigue); the other offers richly realistic ones (customers vary in patience, switch lines according to social cues, experience fatigue). Each group states a quantitative prediction for a hidden dataset that you have pre-generated. Score purely on predictive accuracy. After round one, allow groups to spend accuracy points to replace an idealised card with a realistic one, but reveal that the realistic cards introduce uncalibrated parameters that widen prediction error. In round two, groups defend their models against peer critique focused solely on the implausibility of their assumptions, not on predictive failure. The debrief should map each stage onto Friedman's argument—prediction versus explanation, tractability, and the asymmetry between testing assumptions and testing outcomes—while being frank that the activity cannot reproduce the long-run empirical comparison across diverse domains that Friedman appeals to, nor the distinction between predictive success and explanatory understanding that realist critics emphasise. Include the rules, materials, the payoff scheme, a timeline, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time (for instance, students conflating predictive accuracy with explanatory adequacy, or resisting the scoring system on normative grounds). Write in British English, keep it focused, and do not ask for bibliographies or citations.
