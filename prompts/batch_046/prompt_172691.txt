I've been taking this philosophy course on practical rationality and my study group got into a huge argument about the absent-minded driver problem. It actually hit close to home because I drove to campus yesterday and missed my exitt twice while zoning out, so the idea of being literally absent-minded behind the wheel feels real now. Anyway, Maya was explaining the classic case: there's a driver who needs to take the second highway exit to get home, but the first one is wrong and gets him lost in some industrial zone. The catch is he's totally absent-minded, so when he reaches an exit he can't tell whether it's the first or the second. Maya said the rational thing is to plan ahead — ex-ante, before he starts driving — and commit to a probabalistic rule, like deciding in advance he'll exit at the first point he comes to with a certain frequency, because that maximizes his expected payoff over the whole trip given he can't distinguish the nodes.

Leo jumped in and said that's pure fantasy. Once you're actually at the exit, you don't have access to your "plan" — you just have the thought "this might be the right exit, and if I miss it I'm screwed." So the reasoning at each point pushes you to exit immediately, and applying that consistently means you take the first wrong one. He said Maya is smuggling in a false unity: there isn't one driver stretching through time, just two mental snapshots with no memory link between them. He even compared it to branching worlds — like each exit is its own copy of the agent, and there's no "you" that spans both, so diachronnic coherence doesn't apply and it's wrong to talk about what "the driver" should do as if there's one subject.

Maya came back with: even if memory is broken, your preferences should still be consistent across time. She said treating future selves as strangers is a mistake; it's like saying you don't care about tomorrow just because you'll be asleep. She also pointed out that if Leo is right, the driver should always exit at the first one, which means he never makes it to the second exit even when he should — that's clearly not optimal. Leo replied that "optimal for whom?" is exactly the question. If there's no continuous self, there's no one for whom the strategy is optimal. He said Maya is just relabeling the mistake by calling it "diachronnic coherence" — it's a nice word for pretending that disconnected mental states belong to the same agent. They asked me to refferree and I honestly don't know who's right. Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who's right about what? Specifically: is the ex-ante plan actually available to the driver once he's on the road? Does the absence of memory break personal identity enough to kill diachronnic rationality? And is Leo's branching-world analogy doing real work or is it just a metaphor? Also say what both are missing — maybe there's a middle ground about commitment devices or about how we should model absence of mind in the first place.
