I have just finished a long and rather heated discussion with a friend about Jeremy Bentham’s private writings on sexual morality — in particular, the manuscript in which he argues that certain consensual acts, which he describes as irregularities of the venereal appetite, ought not to fall under criminal law. We could not agree on how to interpret the relationship between that position and his broader utilitarianism. She insists that Bentham applies the principle of utility consistently: punishment creates more suffering, through blackmail, hypocrisy, and fear, than the acts themselves ever could. I counter that his argument smuggles in a liberal conception of privacy that sits uneasily with the aggregate calculus, and that if one takes the greatest happiness principle seriously — including the disgust and social disquiet experienced by the majority — the case for decriminalisation becomes far less obvious.

To settle our dispute, or at least to clarify where the real disagreement lies, I should like you to rank the following three interpretive positions on a scale from 1 (least convincing) to 5 (most convincing) as accounts of Bentham’s reasoning in that manuscript.

Position A: The pure utility thesis. Bentham evaluates the act solely by its direct effects on pleasure and pain for the participants, and treats social revulsion as an irrational prejudice that carries no moral weight in the calculus.

Position B: The institutional-cost thesis. Bentham’s argument is not about the intrinsic value of the act but about the secondary misery produced by legal prohibition — blackmail, arbitrary enforcement, and psychological anxiety — and he would accept criminalisation if those institutional costs could be eliminated.

Position C: The proto-liberal compromise. Bentham recognises that the majority’s disgust is a real source of unhappiness, but he weights it against the intense suffering of the persecuted minority and concludes that decriminalisation maximises aggregate welfare only because the minority’s suffering is severe and the majority’s merely aesthetic.

For each position, assign your score and provide a brief justification — no more than a few sentences — explaining whether that reading can account for Bentham’s refusal to publish the manuscript during his lifetime without invoking anachronistic concepts such as personal autonomy or sexual identity that he would not have recognised. Then, please indicate which single position you regard as the crux of our disagreement, and rate your confidence in that identification from 0 to 10. Finally, assign an overall coherence rating to Bentham’s sexual ethics — from 1 (internally contradictory) to 10 (fully unified with his utilitarian system) — with a short explanation of whether you think the tension my friend and I identified is genuine or largely an artefact of our modern framing.
