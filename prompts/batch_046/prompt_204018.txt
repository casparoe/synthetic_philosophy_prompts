In philosophical debates about the Church-Turing thesis, one frequently encounters the analogy to a fundamental physical law — for instance, the Carnot limit on heat-engine efficiency or the relativistic speed-of-light barrier. The analogy is used to support an empirical reading: just as those laws state absolute boundaries derived from physical theory and supported by every observation, yet remain contingent on how the world is actually structured, so too the Church-Turing thesis might state an absolute boundary on computation derived from our best physical and mathematical models, open in principle to falsification by a naturally occurring mechanism that computes a function no Turing machine can compute. Those who reject the analogy argue that it conflates two different kinds of claim: physical limits are derived within explicit theoretical frameworks (thermodynamics, relativity) that specify conditions and domains, whereas the Church-Turing thesis aims to capture a pre-theoretic, intuitive notion of effective calculability and therefore functions more like a definition or conceptual analysis than like an empirical constraint. Lay out the correspondence in detail: what plays the role of the physical law, of the theoretical framework that derives it, of the observational evidence, of a potential counterexample mechanism, and of the contingency that makes falsification coherent? Then identify the specific disanalogies — for example, whether there exists a physical theory of computation comparable to statistical mechanics, whether a mechanism computing beyond Turing-computability is conceptually coherent in the way faster-than-light travel arguably is not, and whether the thesis is better understood as fixing the extension of the term algorithm rather than describing a property of nature. Conclude by assessing whether the empirical interpretation can stand once the analogy is withdrawn, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argumentative weight.
