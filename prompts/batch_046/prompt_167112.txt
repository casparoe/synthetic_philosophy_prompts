I've been arguing with my friend about whether societies have a duty to remember past atrocities or if there's actually a strong case for collective forgetting, kind of in the Margalit vs Rieff space but with a weird twist I'm trying to inject. The Margalit-style view says remembering wrongs is part of honoring victims and keeping justice alive; the Rieff-style view says clinging to grievance poisons the present and healthy societies need to let things fade. But in our philosophy club we've been going in circles for months. Everyone agrees trauma is real, everyone agrees archives matter, yet neither camp has moved an inch. I started wondering if we're treating collective memory like a finkish dispostion—societies build these remembrance mechanisms, but then bureaucratic protocols or political incentives act like masks that intercept the very manifestation they're supposed to produce. So is the stall because we're actually disagreeing about moral obligations, or is it verbal—one side using 'remembering' for ritual commemoration, the other for cognitive retention? Or are we just answering different questions, justice versus social healling, under one heading? Maybe there's even a hidden empirical question about whether societies that forget actually recover faster, but nobody's admitting that's driving the intuition. Diagnose it for me: substantive, verbal, bedrock, hidden empirical, or ill-posed? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress—a new distinction, a reframing, or should we expect this to stay stuck forever?
