Having spent thirty years in condensed-matter physics and only recently turned to philosophy of mind, I have been trying to clarify one recurring question in debates over computationalism: whether symbolic representations genuinely push around the physical substrate that implements them. To fix my intuition, I have constructed a minimal toy system I call a careenium: a continuous dynamical substrate in which discrete symbolic tokens—simmballs—are embedded as localized excitations. The substrate evolves according to its own field equations, but the presence and configuration of simmballs alter the boundary conditions at each time step. My tentative claim is that, within a careenium, the simmballs do not exert downward causation on the substrate in the manner of an external force; rather, they function as selective constraints that prune the set of dynamically permissible trajectories. In other words, the substrate does not move because the symbols tell it to; the symbols are stable patterns that survive only along those substrate paths that happen to satisfy the constraints they encode. I have sketched this as a distinction between pushing (efficient causation by symbols) and steering (selection among pre-existing substrate dynamics).

I would welcome comments and suggestions on these preliminary notes, particularly on the following points.

First, is the pushing/steering distinction conceptually coherent, or does it collapse once we recognize that boundary conditions are themselves physical states? Second, does my framing illegitimately import analogies from Hamiltonian mechanics—where constraints are genuinely physical—into a domain where the constraints in question are semantic? Third, if simmballs are merely patterns that ride on substrate dynamics, does this render them causally inert in a way that undermines any meaningful form of computationalism, or can a weaker, non-causal form of implementation survive? Fourth, are there hidden circularities in defining the careenium's dynamics independently of the simmballs, given that I have defined the substrate precisely as that which can host them?

Please treat these as rough conceptual notes rather than a finished argument. I am especially interested in whether I have missed a distinction that philosophers of computation routinely draw but that I, coming from a physical-sciences background, tend to overlook, and in whether the careenium framing clarifies anything or merely obscures the original problem behind a novel vocabulary.
