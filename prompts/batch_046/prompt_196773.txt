In debates about artificial consciousness and substrate independence, it is common to say that the mind stands to the brain as software stands to hardware: just as a program can run on a laptop or a server without altering its essential operations, so a mental state might be realised in silicon, carbon, or any other suitable medium. This analogy is frequently used to license the claim that functional identity alone is sufficient for mental identity, regardless of material composition. Audit how far the comparison actually holds. First, set out an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the source code, the compiler, the runtime environment, the user interface, and the hardware malfunction? Next, identify the precise points where the mapping fractures—particularly where biological realisation involves tight coupling between structure and function, degeneracy, or developmental plasticity that lacks any clear counterpart in digital computation. Does the analogy quietly import the assumption that function is fully separable from its material vehicle, and if so, is that import justified? Separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the substrate-independence conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic. Finally, assess whether the relaxed conclusion—that consciousness can be straightforwardly transferred across substrates—survives once these fractures are acknowledged, or whether it collapses into a more restricted, perhaps ecologically embedded, form of functionalism. If the software-hardware picture ultimately misleads, sketch what a better analogy for multiple realisability might be.
