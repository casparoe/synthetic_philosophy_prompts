Hungary under Fidesz — rewriting the constitution, packing courts, gerrymandering districts: definitely backsliding. Poland with Law and Justice taking over judicial discipline but leaving elections competitive: still backsliding, but a different flavour, more legal capture than vote rigging. Turkey after Erdogan's referendum expanding presidential powers plus media purges: obviously erosion, yet part of me hesitates — if a majority genuinely voted for the constitutional change, is that democratic backsliding or just democratic choice? The US case where state legislatures restrict voting access after losing an election: I call it backsliding, but my friends say it's just federalism in action, and I can't quite pin down why they're wrong.

Then there's this hypothetical I've been turning over: a country where the ruling party nationalises two major newspapers (not all of them), changes the electoral commission rules so appointments need a supermajority rather than simple majority, and at the same time rolls out genuinely popular welfare reforms that boost polling. Is that backsliding? Does the welfare bit cancel the intitutional damage, or are they on separate tracks?

I'm trying to see if there's a principle that separates "erosion of competitive uncertainty" from "erosion of liberal rights" — maybe backsliding needs both, or maybe one is enough? I want something that explains why Hungary and Poland both count despite feeling different, why my Turkey hesitation might be a mistake, and why the US voting restrictions aren't just federalism. And I need it to tell me what to say about that hypothetical newspaper-and-welfare case — does popular delivery of goods offset the institutional meddling, or should we treat them as non-comparable dimensions?
