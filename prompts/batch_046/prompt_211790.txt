I cover philosophy and the history of ideas for a science-and-ideas publication, and I am preparing a feature on the ethics of collective memory after strategic defeat. Please write an essay on the following controversy: are “lost cause” national myths—specifically the Confederate Lost Cause, the Masada suicide narrative as reconstructed in early Israeli state culture, the Kosovo Field myth in Serbian collective identity, and the British framing of Dunkirk as a “miracle” rather than a retreat—necessarily pathological exercises in collective bad faith, or can they operate as legitimate “counterfactual scaffolds” that preserve moral continuity when a group faces irreversible strategic failure? Weigh the argument that these narratives are always toxic—falsifying history, denying responsibility for defeat, and licensing future aggression—against the argument that they serve an irreplaceable ethical and epistemic function: maintaining an active, structurally supported counterfactual in which defeat is interpreted as a morally significant choice rather than a mere accident of force, thereby preventing total nihilism or reactive extremism. Reach and justify a clear verdict: is there a defensible form of commemorative defeat that democratic societies should tolerate or even cultivate, or should such myths be systematically dismantled?

The response should begin with the essay itself, opening with the bottom-line thesis in the first sentence, then developing the reasoning with no preface, no commentary outside the essay, and no restatement of the question. Please use the concept of “counterfactual scaffolding” as the central analytical lens throughout: treat it as the idea that a collective maintains not only a memory of defeat but an actively supported alternative trajectory of agency, and ask whether this scaffolding is structurally necessary for post-defeat moral survival or inherently deceptive. Do not supply bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the argument should stand on its own conceptual and historical reasoning.
