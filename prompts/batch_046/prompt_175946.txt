Hey — retired biophysicist here, only been reading philophy for about a year, so bear with me. I got obsessed with Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle (the one where Hamlet's brain is in a vat and Yorick gets the new brain) and tried to build a little argument around it. Now I'm spooked because it seems to kill off introspecion entirely, which can't be right.

1. After the surgery, Hamlet in the vat gets direct sensory feed — temperature, hum, camera vision — and can sincerely report "I'm in this container."

2. Yorick in the bed still has intact proprioceptive anchoring — his nerves map cleanly to the body — and reports "I'm lying right here."

3. Both reports are introspectively grounded; neither speaker is lying or confused about what they feel.

4. Both also use "I" to point back to the pre-surgery person, Dennett.

5. So if introspection gives authority over both location and identity, then Hamlet is Dennett-in-the-vat and Yorick is Dennett-in-the-bed.

6. But one person can't be in two bodies with different brains, so at least one identity claim is false.

7. The only reason to pick Hamlet is third-person neuroscience — tracking the original brain — not anything felt from inside.

8. So for personal identity, first-person access is always overriden by external biology.

9. And if introspection fails for the one thing it should be best at — knowing who you are — then it's unreliable for pain, belief, desire, and everything else.

10. Hence introspection is globally unreliable.

This proves way too much, I think. I really want to hang onto step 3, because people do seem to know where they are from the inside. But I also don't want to land at step 10. Which link should I cut? And what's the cost of cutting it — do I lose the idea that "I" is just an indexical, or do I have to give up proprioceptive anchoring altogether? Is there a hidden equivocation between feeling a location and being that location?

Please walk me through where I'm going wrong.
