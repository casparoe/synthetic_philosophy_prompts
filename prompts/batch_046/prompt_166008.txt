I am preparing a single fifty-minute session for first-year philosophy of science graduate students who have done basic metaphysics but have not read the literature on dispositions. I want to teach the structural distinction between dispositions, finks, and masks, using a paired comparison: a chemical solubility case (a crystalline salt in an aqueous solution) and an engineered artifact (a brittle ceramic protected by a shock-absorbing polymer coating). The pedagogical goal is not merely to label the three notions, but to show why a naive counterfactual analysis collapses differently for natural kinds than for designed functions.

Plan the teaching sequence: the exact order in which the three concepts and the counterfactual failure must be introduced so that the problem emerges organically; the one concrete scenario—drawn from either the chemical or the engineered case—that should carry the session, specifying what features it must possess and what it must lack; the predictable misconceptions students will import from prior metaphysics training (for instance, treating a mask as a mere absence of stimulus, confusing a fink with an epistemic gap, or assuming all dispositions are intrinsic) and the point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; a brief check for understanding that distinguishes grasping the structural relation from merely naming the categories; and what standard complications—such as multi-track dispositions, conditional analyses, or the ontology of powers—should be explicitly bracketed as beyond this level.

Keep the deliverable focused on the dependency structure of the explanation and the misconception map, not on delivering the explanation itself.
