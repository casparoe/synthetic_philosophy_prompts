I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of law working with a clinical ethics review board, and I've been asked to help them formalize assessments of moral responsibility in cases involving coercive psychological conditions—specifically, where a patient or defendant appears to have acted under duress, yet it remains unclear whether any alternative course of action was genuinely available at the moment of decision. The board needs a reusable decision procedure they can run in approximately thirty minutes per case, without requiring philosophical training, to determine whether the principle of alternative possibilities is satisfied or violated.

Please construct an ordered, step-by-step procedure: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure stops with a determination that responsibility is undermined, where it stops with a determination that responsibility stands, and where it must hand the decision to a human reviewer because the issue is one of values rather than facts. The procedure must handle standard Frankfurt-style counterexample structures—where an intervention is present but does not actually cause the action—but it must also accommodate what I am calling nested-blocker cases: situations in which a potential blocker to action is itself disabled by a second-order condition, leaving a first-order desire intact but removing any practical pathway to an alternative. The procedure should also distinguish robust alternatives, which would lead to substantially different outcomes, from flimsy alternatives, which are merely logical possibilities with no accessible practical pathway, and it must specify what to do when these two classifications conflict.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (1) a classic Frankfurt-style scenario in which a hidden neurostimulator would have forced a decision but never activates; (2) a nested-blocker case involving a patient with addiction who has taken a blocker drug that suppresses withdrawal-driven compulsion, leaving them apparently free to refuse treatment but with no therapeutically viable alternative accessible; and (3) one case in which the procedure yields an answer the board will likely resist—perhaps attributing responsibility despite the presence of what appears to be a robust blocker—and explain precisely what the procedure cannot settle.

Please separate what is settled in the philosophical literature on Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities from what remains genuinely contested among specialists in free will, and do not present a contested interpretation—such as whether Frankfurt cases successfully defeat the principle or whether minimal remaining alternatives preserve it—as if it were the consensus view.
