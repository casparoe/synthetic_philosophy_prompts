Notes for an essay titled "The Unreliable Narrator of Posterity"

I am drafting a piece that treats constitutional precommitment not through the standard rational self-binding frame—the familiar image of tying oneself to the mast—but as a problem in narrative unreliability. The central claim is that a constitution's assertion of authority over future generations operates like a novelistic promise made by a narrator who knows he will not survive to see it honored.

The essay develops three analogies. First, the founding moment as proleptic binding: the text establishes rules and amendment procedures meant to constrain future citizens, yet those citizens never consented to the narrative contract. This raises whether constitutional legitimacy requires something like readerly assent, or whether retrospective narration can genuinely bind those who come after. Second, amendments as narrative retcons. In serialized fiction, later installments revise earlier commitments. Do amendments honor the original contract—tightening the ropes—or do they cut them? I want to examine whether the structural difficulty of amendment (supermajorities, conventions) functions like an author refusing to retcon despite pressure, and whether that resistance preserves democratic autonomy or merely freezes an obsolete plot. Third, the problem of the sequel constitution. When a polity replaces its founding text entirely—revolutionary replacements rather than amendments—is the new document a continuation of the same narrative, inheriting the binding force, or is it a work of fan fiction with no claim on the original contract's authority?

I would value your comments specifically on whether this analogy is conceptually fruitful or merely decorative; whether I mischaracterize the standard account of precommitment; and whether the sequel case reveals a tension that undermines the framework rather than illuminating it. I am particularly interested in whether the essay can avoid rehearsing the familiar objections—presentist democracy, the dead hand of the past—and instead push the narrative framing to do genuine philosophical work. Does the unreliable-narrator lens clarify anything about binding posterity that the Ulysses-contract framing obscures, or does it collapse into metaphor once the legal mechanics are examined closely?
