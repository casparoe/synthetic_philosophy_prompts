Design a 75-minute classroom simulation for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy or sociology seminar of about 18 students. The objective is to make participants feel Weber's problem of explaining with values—specifically, whether a culture's value-orientation can operate as an independent causal factor in economic behavior, or whether it is always reducible to material conditions and instrumental adaptation. The deliverable should be a runnable activity with explicit rules, roles, and a small-stakes payoff structure, followed by a debrief in which the idea emerges from what happened rather than from a lecture.

Divide the group into two subgroups of 9, seated separately with no cross-communication. Give both an identical economic environment: four rounds of 8 minutes each. In every round, each student receives 10 tokens. A token kept yields 1 private point; a token contributed to a collective pool yields 1.6 points divided equally among all nine members, but only if the subgroup's total contribution reaches 60 tokens that round. If the threshold is missed, contributed tokens are lost. This creates a free-rider tension with a collective reward.

Before round 1, assign each subgroup a value script that members must use to justify every decision aloud. Subgroup A receives an ascetic-rational script: accumulation is moral duty, consumption beyond subsistence signals spiritual failure, and capital growth serves a calling. Subgroup B receives a reciprocal-status script: hoarding is shameful, generosity creates binding obligation, and wealth exists to be redistributed. The scripts are randomly assigned; the facilitator should not reveal that they have no historical basis.

Between round 2 and round 3, introduce a calibrated material shock: inform Subgroup A that consumption is now taxed at 20 percent, making saving instrumentally rational, and inform Subgroup B that distribution is now subsidized by 15 percent, making giving instrumentally rational. The shocks are designed to align material incentives with the assigned value scripts.

After round 4, reveal both the hidden shocks and the arbitrary assignment of scripts.

The debrief should map moments in the game onto Weber's conceptual framework: the divergence in behavior under initially identical conditions; the participants' own explanatory asymmetry, in which they invoke values to account for the other subgroup but cite economics for their own post-shock shifts; any value-lag in which behavior briefly resists reversal after the shock is revealed; and the structural difficulty of distinguishing value-rational action from instrumental rationality when material conditions and values move together. Include a precise timeline (setup, four rounds, revelation, debrief), instructions for token tracking, and a facilitator script for the revelation moment.

Note what will predictably go wrong: some students will reject the value script and free-ride regardless; groups may try to reverse-engineer the hidden shock during round 3; and the debrief will tend to collapse into economic reductionism unless the facilitator explicitly asks whether placing a token in the pool meant capital formation in one group and prestige investment in the other.

Be frank about which feature of Weber's thesis the activity cannot reproduce: the long-term, institutionally embedded, historically irreversible development of a value-system that reshapes legal, educational, and organizational structures over generations. The facilitator must supply this limitation by contrasting the 32-minute simulation with the centuries-long institutional embedding Weber describes.

Inject one somewhat niche conceptual framing to prevent pure textbook regurgitation: require that the debrief address whether the value scripts functioned as causal mechanisms that changed preferences, or as interpretive frames that changed the meaning of the same physical act without altering underlying preferences—an ambiguity Weber himself never fully resolved.

Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not depend on specific publication dates or obscure secondary literature, and do not include any verbatim quotations from real texts. Specify that participants should come away seeing that values can function as independent variables in behavior, but that demonstrating their causal independence from material conditions requires counterfactual reasoning that is structurally hard to secure in a single session.
