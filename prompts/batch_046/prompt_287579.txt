I convene a small interdisciplinary reading group—colleagues from science studies and moral philosophy, none specialists in American pragmatism—who have just finished a unit on liberal democratic theory and now need to encounter Dewey’s claim that democracy is not primarily a governmental form but the social form of cooperative inquiry. They are comfortable with voting, representation, and public reason, yet they have never worked through the idea that inquiry, understood technically as the transformation of an indeterminate situation into a resolved one through distributed experimentation, is meant to serve as the normative model for democratic life itself. I want to design a single ninety-minute session that teaches this connection without reducing it to the banal slogan that democracy is like science.

Plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering the lecture. What idea must be firmly in place before democracy can be reconceived as inquiry—specifically, the structure of a problematic situation and why its resolution requires cooperative, trial-and-error adjustment rather than expert decree or preference aggregation? Choose one concrete carrying example drawn from collective institutional practice—perhaps an adaptive watershed-management board operating under iterative monitoring protocols—explain exactly what features it must possess and what it must lack (for instance, why a fixed regulatory mandate or a pure market-allocation mechanism fails to capture the experimental logic), and justify why this example carries the conceptual load better than the usual town-hall meeting or jury-deliberation scenario.

Then map the misconceptions they will form in predictable sequence: first, that Dewey offers only a loose metaphorical analogy between scientific method and politics; second, that experimental democracy requires every citizen to possess technical expertise; third, that treating situations as indeterminate implies institutional instability or perpetual revisionism; and fourth, that the view collapses into epistemic elitism disguised as participatory inquiry. For each, identify the precise point in the sequence—before introducing the example, during its exposition, or in the transition back to democratic theory—where you should head it off, specifying the conceptual distinction (not a mere reassurance) that does the work. Tell me also what I should deliberately leave out at this level—whether Dewey’s broader metaphysics of experience, his pedagogical writings, or the formal architecture of his logic of inquiry—and say so explicitly. End with one diagnostic question I can pose that reveals whether the group understands democracy-as-inquiry as a normative structure of social intelligence rather than as a procedural mechanism, together with an indication of what a response that misses the point sounds like versus one that lands it.
