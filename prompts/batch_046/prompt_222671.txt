I'm trying to push Frege's puzzle on identity statements a bit further than usual for a seminar draft, and I think I've overreached. Here's my argument:

(1) If 'a=a' and 'a=b' differ in cognitive value, they must express different Fregean senses.
(2) So a sincere assertion of one and denial of the other report two distinct belief states, not a contradiction in one mind.
(3) Apply this to Lois: she asserts 'Superman can fly' but denies 'Clark Kent can fly', so she has two beliefs with different senses.
(4) Millians reject this: both sentences express the singular proposition <Clark Kent, being able to fly>.
(5) So Millianism forces us to say she both believes and disbelieves the exact same proposition, which looks psychologically impossible.
(6) Hence Millianism is false and sense must live inside the head.
(7) But now extend the reasoning: if I judge 'this cup is empty' at noon and again at 12:01, the demonstrative picks out the same cup but my cognitive perspecitve has shifted.
(8) By parity with (1)-(2), any shift in cognitive value means a new sense, hence a new belief.
(9) So I never litterally hold the same belief across time; belief retention collapses.
(10) Since belief retention is real, something in (1)-(8) has to go.

Which step is the weak link? I'd prefer to save (9) being false and maybe keep (5) if I can, but I'm open. What's the standard fix — hidden indexicals, mental-file theory, or just denying that same-content conflict is impossible? And what does each option pay?
