As I construct a multigenerational novel in which each protagonist’s existence depends upon a prior generation’s moral failure, I find my intuitions about reproductive and structural wrongs do not align neatly. Here are five verdicts I hold, and I wish to know whether one principle governs them or whether coherence demands I abandon one.

First, when parents conceive a child—Mara—during an ecological crisis they recklessly helped cause, and Mara lives a genuinely flourishing life, I feel she has no legitimate grievance against them for that decision in particular; her existence is inseparable from their recklessness, and she values her life.

Second, when parents choose an experimental fertility protocol that succeeds only by inflicting a mild but permanent disability upon the resulting child—Leo—whose life remains worth living but diminished, I feel Leo can object to the protocol itself, not merely to the disability, even though refusing the protocol would have meant Leo never existed.

Third, in an alternative narrative branch where the same parents wait for safer conditions and conceive a different, healthy child—Nora—I feel they acted correctly, despite knowing that Mara therefore never comes to be.

Fourth, when a character—Elias—learns he was conceived through violence, lives well, and states that he wishes the violence had never occurred even at the cost of his own non-existence, I find this attitude coherent and not self-contradictory.

Fifth, when a society deliberately engineers a future caste of workers with limited cognitive capacities who are nevertheless genuinely content and lack any conception of deprivation, I feel this is profoundly wrong, even though no particular engineered person can claim to have been harmed relative to never having existed.

The tension lies especially between the first and fifth cases: Mara’s parents are excused because she flourishes, yet the engineered caste is condemned despite comparable flourishing. Is there a single account—perhaps one that distinguishes particular individuals’ standing to complain from structural or authorial wrongs—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which should I overturn? And what should the resulting principle predict for a case I have not listed: a novelist who revises an earlier manuscript in a way that erases one version of a character and replaces her with an ontologically distinct successor who lives more happily—does the erased figure have a claim, or is any wrong purely aesthetic?
