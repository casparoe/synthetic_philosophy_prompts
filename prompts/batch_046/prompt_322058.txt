I serve on a hospital ethics review board that conducts monthly audits of autonomous clinical decision-support systems, and we have repeatedly encountered failures in how these systems update patient-state representations: they preserve clinical properties that should have lapsed—such as a temporary contraindication—after new data arrives, or they discard stable background conditions—such as chronic renal impairment—when recalculating treatment recommendations. Rather than treating these solely as engineering errors, I want to treat them as practical manifestations of the philosophical frame problem—the challenge of specifying which features of a situation persist through an event and which do not—translated into a reusable audit tool that non-engineer board members can actually run.

Please construct a step-by-step audit procedure, ordered so that it can be applied consistently to any system update log. The procedure should move through four fixed stages: first, identifying the explicit frame axioms the system uses to mark persistence; second, surfacing implicit persistence assumptions embedded in its clinical ontology; third, testing for missing frame conditions that ought to have been specified but were not; and fourth, flagging updates where the persistence of a clinical status is ethically contested rather than technically determinable, requiring direct human judgment rather than algorithmic resolution. For each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the audit must stop with a mandatory redesign flag, and where it must hand the decision back to the board because values, not facts, are at stake. Include applied test cases for two update scenarios—specifically, a patient record updated after a negative allergy challenge test, and a record updated after a family-initiated change in code status—and work through both in detail, including one case where the procedure yields a technically correct but ethically uncomfortable verdict (for instance, the system correctly retains a stigmatizing diagnostic label that contextual clinical judgment suggests ought to be suspended). Be explicit about what the procedure fundamentally cannot do, particularly where philosophical ambiguity about the persistence of clinical identities exceeds any engineering specification. I ask for a committed response: take a definite stance on why this four-stage ordering is the correct one and defend it, render unambiguous verdicts on the test cases with reasons rather than surveying alternative interpretations, and clearly mark the boundaries where judgment is unavoidable rather than disguising them as procedural outputs.
