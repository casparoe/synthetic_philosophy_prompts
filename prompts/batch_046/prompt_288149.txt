At study group last week two people got stuck arguing about whether we can actually know anything about the distant past, not just tell stories. (1) Maya said source criticism from Herodotus through Thucydides to modern methods gives us real tools: weigh witness proximity, check internal consistency, cross-reference with archeology, and build warranted belief. The fact that some records are lost doesn't mean ontology collapses. (2) Leo said that misses the deeper issue -- for the distant past information isn't just missing, it's structurally inaccessable. The kind of data you'd need to identify specific ontological entities (events, people, institutions) is permanently outside reach, so source criticism only organizes ignorance rather than overcoming it. (3) Maya came back that this proves too much: we know about dinosaurs through material traces that survive independently of texts, so past ontology is anchored in physical remains, not just narratives. (4) Leo said that's the circular part -- we identify those remains using taxonomies and dating methods that already embed historical assumptions. There's no neutral observation outside the source-chain, so we never verify ontology independently; we just widen the circle. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please go move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify. Also say who's closer right overall, and whether both miss something important about how Herodotus vs Thucydides actually treat the problem of missing evidence.
