Here is an argument that seems to show that global-workspace broadcasting necessarily produces phenomenal consciousness even in architectures that plainly lack it, and I cannot locate the weak step. (1) Global workspace theory holds that a mental representation is access-conscious if and only if its content is globally broadcast to multiple cognitive subsystems for report, reasoning, and action guidance. (2) Phenomenal consciousness is defined by there being an integrated, unified "something it is like" for the subject—a single experiential field rather than discrete, unbound updates. (3) If global broadcasting is sufficient for phenomenal consciousness, then any representation that meets the broadcasting condition must be accompanied by such an integrated field. (4) Now consider a "fragmented workspace" architecture: a central workspace broadcasts identical signals to every downstream module, yet each module processes its copy in complete isolation, with no cross-modal binding, no shared point of view, and no integrated subject. (5) In this system, representations fully satisfy the broadcasting condition in (1), so they are access-conscious. (6) By (3), they must therefore carry an integrated phenomenal field. (7) But by construction there is no unified "what it is like"—only parallel, disconnected functional updates—so (6) is false. (8) Therefore either broadcasting is not sufficient for phenomenal consciousness, which leaves global workspace theory with no explanatory bridge from access to experience, or phenomenal consciousness can exist without integration, which collapses the very distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness that the theory was meant to preserve. Tell me which premise or step should be rejected, why it initially looked plausible, and what the standard positions—those who treat phenomenal consciousness as primitive, first-order representationalists, higher-order theorists, and defenders of a strict access/phenomenal divide—each have to pay to reject that particular step. Please answer in American English.
