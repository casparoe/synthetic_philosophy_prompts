I'm drafting a novel in which a philosopher-character becomes convinced that phenomenal consciousness is not an unsolved problem but a broken concept—what philosophers sometimes call "quining" qualia, treating them as an incoherent theoretical posit in the same category as phlogiston or the luminiferous ether. The version she defends, shaped by the line of argument associated with Chase and Sanborn, holds that the notion of "intrinsic phenomenal quality" collapses under three simultaneous demands: it must be non-functional and therefore causally inert, yet epistemically accessible enough to be reported by the subject, and finely individuated without being reducible to structural or relational properties. Hold these together, she insists, and the result is not a hard problem awaiting neuroscience but a contradiction in terms—like maintaining a concept of "absolute simultaneity" after the structure of special relativity has made it both redundant and descriptively empty.

I want to understand, for the integrity of her character, what would actually count against this incoherence thesis. Not the familiar appeals to introspective certainty or inverted-spectrum thought experiments, but the specific conceptual distinctions, formal results, or empirical observations—perhaps about indexical reference, about the architecture of perceptual reporting, or about what first-person authority requires—that would genuinely damage her position. Which of these defeaters would be fatal, which merely awkward, and which could she dismiss by qualifying the thesis into unfalsifiability? I also want to know whether defenders of this view—those who think qualia should be eliminated rather than explained—would themselves concede that certain findings, say about blindsight or about the grain of working-memory representations for hue, actually count against them. Or has the position been insulated so thoroughly that nothing could move it? If it is well insulated, does that make it philosophically robust, or does it reduce the claim to a methodological stipulation? I'd like the answer ranked from decisive to cosmetic, with an honest assessment of whether the view risks anything at all.
