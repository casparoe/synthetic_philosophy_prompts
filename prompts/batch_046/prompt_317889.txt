In the debate over consequentialist cluelessness — the claim that long-run effects of actions are typically too unpredictable for expected-value reasoning to guide choice — the literature has settled into a pattern I find suspicious. One side treats cluelessness as an epistemic failure that undermines the applicability of the theory altogether; the other treats it as a practical obstacle that can be circumvented by decision rules, robustness considerations, or restricting evaluation to near-term effects. Both sides agree on the underlying empirical point, that forecasting distant outcomes is unreliable; neither has produced a formal result the other accepts; and the arguments have not shifted in years. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about how much we can predict, but about a hidden ambiguity in what the theory demands by "compare outcomes" — specifically, whether expected-value reasoning requires a cardinal ranking of complete world-histories, in which case ignorance of distant branches is fatal, or only an ordinal selection among available options, in which case cluelessness about remote effects may be irrelevant. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is there a concealed empirical disagreement — perhaps about the predictability of specific domains like climate or technological diffusion — that neither side has made explicit? Or are the two camps simply answering different questions, one about rational decision-procedure and one about moral justification, under a single heading? I want a structural diagnosis supported by what each position actually commits to, not a survey of authors: is this a verbal dispute about "cluelessness," a substantive disagreement about the demands of rationality, or an impasse of bedrock intuitions about whether a moral theory must be action-guiding? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress — a formal model of partial comparison, an empirical study of predictive accuracy in a defined domain, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the problem, or an argument that no resolution should be expected because the question is ill-posed? Please answer honestly about whether anything short of abandoning the expected-value framework could actually move the debate.
