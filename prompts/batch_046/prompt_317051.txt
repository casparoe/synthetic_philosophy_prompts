I have been discussing Peter Singer's drowning child thought experiment with colleagues who remain sceptical of its extension to obligations of global poverty relief. One comparison that keeps arising in these conversations is the analogy between Singer's moral claim and statutory duties of rescue—particularly the civil-law obligations found in jurisdictions such as France and Germany, where bystanders may face criminal liability for failing to assist a person in immediate peril when intervention would carry minimal risk or cost to themselves. The analogy is typically deployed to suggest that if liberal democracies already codify a positive duty to intervene in direct, low-cost rescue scenarios, then refusing to donate to effective poverty-alleviation charities is not merely miserly but structurally analogous to walking past the pond: the legal recognition of proximity-based rescue, on this view, reveals an internal inconsistency in our moral framework that ought to be corrected by extending the duty across geographical distance. I want to audit this comparison rigorously rather than let it do rhetorical work unexamined.

Please construct an explicit correspondence table mapping the elements of Singer's pond scenario onto the legal duty of rescue—identifying what plays the role of the drowning child, the bystander, the shallow water, the temporal spread of charitable giving over months or years, and the state's coercive enforcement mechanism—then set out the disanalogies that actually matter for the normative conclusion. In particular, address whether the legal duty's dependence on physical presence and immediate, visible peril quietly imports features that undermine the analogy; whether the state's coercive power in the legal case has any parallel in Singer's voluntary morality; and whether the conclusion—that we ought to extend rescue obligations globally—survives once these breaks are visible. Finally, say whether a better analogy exists—perhaps to a contractual obligation, a tax liability, or a professional duty of care—and whether that substitute carries the intended conclusion more honestly or merely shifts the burden of justification elsewhere.
