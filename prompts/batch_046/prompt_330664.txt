I work as the senior ethics reviewer for a national transportation and product-safety board, and lately we've been drowning in proposals where one design change hurts millions of people in ways they barely notice, while leaving things alone lets a rare disaster kill or maim a small group. I need a reusable decision procedue my team can actually run in about twenty minutes before we sign off — a checklist, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each gate, where we stop with a hard no, where we hand it up to the board chair because it's values not facts, and where the thing just breaks. The idea is to operationalize the dust-speck-versus-torture problem, but I want it to include this specific gate I've been arguing about with my coworker: call it the singel decision node check. The question is whether all the micro-harms are structurally coupled to one choice we actually control — like one policy switch, one product update — or whether they're scattered across independent causes we don't govern together. I think that distinction should be formal, not decorative, and I want the checklist to treat it as an actual gate that can kill a proposal or force it upward.

Apply the procedue to three cases and be honest about where it gives an answer I'll hate. Case one: a mandatory software patch for hospital infusion pumps that adds a 0.4-second lag to every button press, affecting maybe twelve million clinical interactions a year, but fixes a fatal glitch that kills around thirty-five patients annually. Case two: a highway noise-barrier design that creates a barely imperceptable low-frequency hum for eight million nearby residents but eliminates a blind curve causing about twenty-five fatal crashes each year. Case three: a proposal to slightly dilute a municipal water additive across a region of four million people — causing an imperceptable taste shift — to prevent a localized toxic contamination event that severely harms about six hundred people in one town. In that last one, my gut says approve it, so if your procedue is worth anything it should either tell us not to, or clearly flag why it misfires here and forces a value call.

Don't just lay out options; take a definite position on which gates should be hard stops, why the singel-decision-node condition actually changes whether aggregation is morally legitimate, and exactly where judgment cannot be eliminated. Also say what the checklist absolutely cannot do, especially around subjective experience thresholds and political pressure from manufacturers. Keep it practical: my team has engineers and lawyers, not philosophers, and the board chair needs to know precisely when she's being asked for a value judgment rather than a factual call.
