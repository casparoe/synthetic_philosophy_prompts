I've been examining the procedural rules of a university institutional review board that oversees human-subjects research, and I've encountered a peculiar revision protocol. When investigators apply to update the standard informed-consent template—a document that has accreted clauses through four decades of incremental amendments—the board requires more than a forward-looking justification for any deletion. The applicant must supply a reconstructed "legacy rationale" for each clause they wish to remove, explaining the specific harm, misconception, or vulnerability that the original provision was designed to address, even when contemporary reviewers regard that risk as obsolete or negligible. The board treats the form not as an instrument open to rational redesign from first principles, but as an accumulated institutional artifact whose individual parts may encode practical knowledge that exceeds the present understanding of any single committee member.

This practice seems to instantiate something in the neighborhood of Chesterton's fence—do not dismantle a barrier until you understand why it was erected—mediated through a Burkean skepticism of rationalist reconstruction. Yet I am uncertain what philosophical commitments are genuinely doing the normative work. Does the rule presuppose a particular epistemology of institutions, according to which distributed historical experience generates authority that individual deliberation cannot replicate? Or does it entail a normative theory of intergenerational obligation, such that altering inherited protections places a burden of proof on the reformer analogous to overturning legal precedent? Might the same procedural design be sustained on purely pragmatic or risk-averse grounds—say, as a hedge against unknown unknowns—without any deep commitment to tradition or continuity as values in themselves? And if one replaced the underlying framework with a straightforward presentist consequentialism, evaluating each clause solely by its current expected utility, what precisely would collapse? Would the legacy-reconstruction requirement become incoherent, or could it survive as a useful heuristic? Please reconstruct the implicit theory that best makes sense of this institutional design, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, identify where rival philosophical views could license the same rule, and specify what elements of the practice would have to change if the presupposed view were abandoned.
