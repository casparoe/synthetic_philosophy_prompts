I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a friend about whether generational labels—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and so on—track real ontological kinds or are merely convenient demographic fictions. She maintains that shared formative conditions, such as economic regimes, technological infrastructures, and geopolitical events, create genuinely unified cohorts with common dispositional essences, not unlike biological species. I countered that the boundaries are arbitrarily fixed by census intervals, that intra-cohort variance on nearly every attitudinal and behavioral measure dwarfs the inter-cohort differences we treat as defining, and that calling “Gen Z” a natural kind commits us to implausible diachronic identity conditions: if membership requires having been formed by a specific cluster of experiences, then anyone within the birth-year bracket who missed those experiences—through geographic isolation, subcultural insulation, or simple temporal misalignment—ought to be excluded, yet in practice we apply the label universally to the age bracket regardless.

Rather than rehearse the whole dispute, I want to press a more focused conceptual point. Give an example of a conceptual issue—described in concrete, everyday terms rather than statistical abstraction—that shows why treating a generation as a natural kind collapses under its own internal logic. Your example should involve two individuals who fall within the exact birth-year range that conventionally defines a single cohort (for instance, 1997–2012), but who, because of radically divergent formative environments, share none of the dispositions or cognitive habits that the kind is supposed to explain. Then show how attributing those cohort-typical traits to both of them solely on the basis of their shared birth window produces a clear contradiction: either the necessary conditions for kind-membership are violated (since the formative experiences are absent), or the sufficient conditions for applying the label are shown to be purely chronological and therefore ontologically empty. In constructing this case, avoid well-worn references to smartphone adoption curves or workplace loyalty surveys; instead, keep the focus on the ontological tension between the formative-experience criterion that is supposed to make the cohort a real kind and the birth-date criterion that is actually used to assign membership in practice.
