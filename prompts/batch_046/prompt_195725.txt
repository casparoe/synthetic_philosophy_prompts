I have been working through some informal notes for a course on formal epistemology, and I keep returning to a worry about the Solomonoff prior that strikes me as more ethical than mathematical. Most treatments present universal induction as a purely descriptive framework—an optimal predictor that assigns prior probabilities to computable hypotheses according to their Kolmogorov complexity. But I want to press a more unsettling reading: if we treat the Solomonoff prior not merely as a rule for belief revision but as a normative architecture for rational agency, it does not stay neutral between outcomes. Instead, it structurally favours world-histories that are maximally compressible, which often correlates with suppressing noise, variation, and autonomous divergence—features that, in human contexts, correspond to suffering, coercion, or homogenisation. In effect, the prior smuggles in a consequentialist calculus where descriptive economy functions like a utility function, privileging orderly futures over the intrinsic value of particular states.

Could you please argue for the position that the Solomonoff prior, when interpreted as a normative guide rather than a descriptive predictor, is genuinely malign because it internalises a consequentialist optimisation for compressibility? Your argument should distinguish carefully between simplicity as an epistemic virtue and simplicity as a decision-theoretic objective, and it should explain why the transition from belief-updating to action-guiding is not merely a category mistake but reveals a structural danger. I would also like you to address the strongest objection: that any apparent malignancy is only an artefact of how we index descriptions or assign complexity weights, and therefore can be dissolved by a more careful formalisation rather than being inherent to the universal prior itself. Please do not rely on citations or bibliographic references; I am interested in the conceptual structure of the argument itself.
