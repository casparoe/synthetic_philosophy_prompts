I'm trying to figure out where I actually stand on Chesterton's fence — you know, the idea that you shouldn't rip out a fence unless you know why it was built. I was arguing with a friend about reform and realised my gut reactions are kind of all over the place, and I want to see if there's a real principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistnet.

So here are my verdicts. There's an old zoning rule in my town that bans building within 200 feet of the river. The flood-risk reason is dead because of levees now, but the strip turned into a wildlife corridor and walking path. I think keep it, full stop, even if we rewrite the justification.

Then there's this university tradition where PhD candidates have to defend part of the exam in Latin. The original point — that scholars from different countries could understand — is totally obsolete since everyone uses English now. But I still want to keep it, because it forces a kind of precision and formality you don't get in casual speech. So that's a yes from me.

There's also a tribal mediation practice I read about: land disputes must go through a three-month silent-reflection period led by a specific elder clan before any formal ruling. Nobody remembers why that clan was picked, and a modern court could do it faster. Still, I feel strongly we shouldn't replace it — there's probably some cooling-off or social-cohesion function we don't fully map, and tearing it out feels arrogant.

But then there's a factory rule that every engineer spends their first year doing manual quality checks on the floor, even though automation is better. The founders said it builds empathy for the workers, but now turnover is high and people hate it. I think abolish it — the hidden value either isn't real or isn't worth the cost.

There's also an obscure clause in a small country's constitution letting the monarch veto education bills. It's never been used, but a populist movement is eyeing it now. Even though I usually like checks on power, I think this fence should come down immediately; the risk of leaving it is too big.

And then there's the case I keep going back and forth on: a religious community leaves one field fallow every seventh year. The agronomic reason disappeared with fertilizer, but now it acts as a shared cultural rhythm and, apparently, it's good for soil microbiome in ways the founders never knew. I feel like I want to keep it, but then I wonder why I'm saving this one and not the factory rule, since both claim some "hidden benefit" that wasn't the original point. Maybe I'm just romanticising the field?

So what's the principle, if any? Is it about reversibility — that tearing down the zoning law or the mediation practice is hard to undo, while ending the factory rule is easy? Or is it about whether the fence guards against risks we can't model? I also want to know what my account says about a case I haven't listed: a legislature that requires a 2/3 supermajority to change election laws, originally to stop partisan gerrymandering, but now it also blocks any attempt to reform the voting system itself. Would I keep that fence?

Please don't just quote Chesterton at me — I'm trying to build my own view, and I need you to test it against these cases, especially the fallow-field one I can't settle. Give me a precise principle, say which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and tell me what it predicts for the election-law case.
