I hold a strengthened anti-aggregationist position in the spirit of Taurek: when lives are at stake and one can rescue either one group or another, the mere fact that one group is larger provides no moral reason to prefer saving them, because each claimant’s moral claim is strictly pairwise and non-aggregative. I extend this beyond simultaneous rescue dilemmas to sequential resource allocation over time, and I maintain that where claims are genuinely tied, symmetric respect requires a genuinely random selection procedure—not merely permits one—so that any deterministic tie-breaker violates the equality of claimants. What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I want the defeaters mapped in full: formal results in social choice or decision theory that expose pairwise cycles or undermine lottery-based resolution; empirical findings about institutional triage and public-health rationing that show aggregation is inescapable in practice; conceptual arguments that extending the view to sequential cases forces an unacceptable paralysis, arbitrariness, or collapse into indifference; and intuitions about particular cases—probabilistic rescue, distributed harms across generations, or partial overlap of claimants—that the position cannot accommodate without ad hoc qualification. Please rank these from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed defender would themselves concede as damaging and which they would dismiss as illicitly smuggling in aggregation, and assess whether the position—especially with its extension to mandatory randomisation in sequential allocation—has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If it is well insulated, is that insulation a virtue or a vice for a moral view of this kind? I would find it most decisive if you could show that the view either collapses into a rival aggregative framework once its institutional implications are drawn out, or that it makes predictions about permissible practice that no functioning institution could honour.
