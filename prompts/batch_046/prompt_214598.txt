I'm drafting a chapter on the ontology of normative reasons and have been trying to formalize my advisor's implicit framework by tracking her spontaneous judgments in our metaethics seminars over the past year. Her verdicts on whether a "free-floating" rationale exists—one that binds regardless of any actual or counterfactual mental states—run as follows. She holds that there is a reason not to torture an innocent person and that this reason survives even in a possible world emptied of all sentient beings and all records of the act. She denies that there is any reason not to drink poison that will kill you if you genuinely and stably lack any desire to live or avoid suffering. She accepts that any thinker, even one with no practical goals whatsoever, has an epistemic reason to avoid contradiction. She denies that there is any aesthetic reason to preserve a masterpiece if it will never be observed and all aesthetic sensibilities have vanished. And she accepts that there remains a reason to keep a promise made to someone now dead, when breaking it harms no living person, benefits the promiser, and will never be discovered.

The case I need her verdict on concerns a methodological norm in science. Imagine a researcher running a single study whose results will never be published, will never influence policy or practice, will never be read by anyone, and have no bearing on her career, reputation, or welfare. Knowing this, she selectively reports her data to make the statistical pattern appear cleaner and more decisive than it actually is, thereby generating a false claim about the world that no one will ever believe or act upon. Would my advisor say that a free-floating rationale against this manipulation exists—binding the researcher even in the absence of any audience, affected party, or practical goal? Which of her earlier judgments is the closest analog: the epistemic case about contradiction, the moral case about torture, or the prudential case about poison? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, what principle would she be endorsing by saying yes versus saying no—perhaps a distinction between categorical and hypothetical norms, or between domains with irreducible objective standards and those reducible to psychological or social states? And what further case—say, involving an artificial system with reasoning capacities but no welfare, an institutional role occupied in complete isolation, or obligations to generations that will never exist—would settle which principle she actually runs?
