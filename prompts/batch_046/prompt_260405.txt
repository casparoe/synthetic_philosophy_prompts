I'm working on a novel where one of the characters is this annoying philosopher who keeps trapping people in arguments at dinner parties. There's this one about the expanding circle that I think is broken but I can't find the exit, and I need it for a scene where another character finally cracks it. Here's how he lays it out, step by step:

(1) Moral progress is nothing but the expanding circle — extending direct moral consideration to beings that used to be left out. (2) If this is real progress, not just fashion, then at any time there's a correct circle: the set of all beings who actually deserve direct consideration right now. (3) But our cognitive and emotional limits mean we can always imagine a wider circle — synthetic minds, distributed ecosystems, whatever comes next — that includes beings we currently exclude. (4) So the correct circle is always wider than our actual circle. (5) That means at every point in history we're failing to include beings who genuinely belong inside. (6) If we're always falling short of the correct circle, then we never actually achieve progress — we only achieve different flavors of failure. (7) Worse, since there's no endpoint where imagination stops, there's never a time when progress is complete; it's just permanent inadequacy dressed up as improvement.

He uses this to claim that talking about "moral progress" is definatly just self-congratulation. I feel like the jump from (3) to (4) is suspicious — just because we can imagine more doesn't mean they're actually inside the correct circle. But maybe the problem is deeper, in (2) or (5). Which step do I reject, and what does it cost? I'd really like to keep (1) and (6) if I can, because the novel's theme is about humility and never being done, but I don't want the argument to be valid if those are kept. Help me find the crack.
