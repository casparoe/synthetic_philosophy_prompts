Two colleagues in a philosophy of science seminar—call them Jordan and Riley—have been debating whether Milton Friedman’s F-twist applies to recent macro-labor models that study wage elasticity during demand shocks. To recall the twist: Friedman argued that unrealistic or false assumptions in a model can be a virtue rather than a vice, because they strip away confounding noise and isolate the essential causal mechanisms, with predictive accuracy serving as the ultimate test. The model in question predicts wage declines during recessions by assuming that workers have perfect, costless information about future labor-market conditions—an assumption both agree is false for actual gig and contract workers, who face search costs, information asymmetries, and behavioral biases. Their exchange proceeded as follows.

(1) Jordan argued that the perfect-information assumption is precisely the strategic simplification Friedman endorsed. By removing search frictions and cognitive limits, the model isolates the pure price mechanism linking demand shocks to wages. Because it accurately predicts observed wage drops, the unrealistic micro-foundation is vindicated: the F-twist shows that information frictions are not necessary to generate the macro-pattern, only to obscure it.

(2) Riley objected that Friedman developed the twist for competitive-market domains where unrealistic rationality assumptions can still yield stable aggregate outcomes. In labor-market dynamics, however, perfect information is not background noise but a causally opposite condition: workers’ actual misinformation and search behavior partly constitute the elasticity mechanism. Substituting perfect information therefore does not isolate the mechanism; it replaces it with a different causal structure.

(3) Jordan replied that Riley illegitimately demands mechanism fidelity, which Friedman explicitly rejected. The twist separates predictive adequacy from ontological realism: if false micro-assumptions reproduce correct macro-outcomes, they have fulfilled their methodological role. Jordan pointed out that climate models often treat complex geometries as spherical approximations—obviously false—without being rejected, provided temperature predictions hold. The labor model is analogous: false micro-claims, correct macro-predictions.

(4) Riley countered that the climate analogy misfires. In physics, geometric simplification is a boundary-condition approximation that does not interact with the thermal dynamics being measured; in labor economics, the information structure is the dynamics itself. She further noted that if policymakers use this model to design unemployment benefits, they will treat information failure as causally irrelevant and set inadequate search subsidies—demonstrating that predictive success in forecasting can mask explanatory failure that carries practical harm.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate Jordan’s or Riley’s position from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall, identify what both parties overlook—especially whether there exists a principled distinction between isolating simplifications and mechanism-substituting falsehoods, and whether predictive success in recession forecasting should constrain model selection in policy design.
