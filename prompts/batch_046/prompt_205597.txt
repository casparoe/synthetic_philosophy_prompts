I'm working through a philosophy of biology unit that treats DNA as carrying information, and I keep running into the slogan "DNA is a code" — or sometimes "the genetic code" — being used to do very different argumentative work. In one discussion, a synthetic biologist described CRISPR editing as "rewriting the source code of life," apparently relying on the idea that genes are modular instructions that can be swapped like subroutines. In another, a critic dismissed that framing as a category error, arguing that DNA lacks syntax, semantics, and a compiler, so calling it code is at best a loose metaphor and at worst anthropomorphizes molecular chemistry. I want to understand exactly what claims are hiding inside that one phrase, because I need to evaluate whether the metaphor illuminates or obscures.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "DNA is a code" might express? I have in mind at least the following dimensions, but I want the full menu: (1) a purely syntactic or mapping claim — that triplet nucleotide sequences stand in a regular, arbitrary correspondence to amino acids, analogous to symbol-to-symbol encoding; (2) a semantic or representational claim — that DNA carries intentional content about phenotypic traits, so that a gene "means" or "specifies" an eye color in something like the way a word means its referent; (3) a functional or informational claim — that DNA transmits quantifiable information or plays a causal-informational role in development, regardless of whether it has genuine meaning; (4) a teleological or design-analogy claim — that the mapping exists for a purpose, as engineered software exists to execute instructions, perhaps implying that evolutionary processes are analogous to programming; and (5) a synthetic-engineering claim — that genetic parts are genuinely modular and composable like software libraries, so that biological engineering should proceed by importing logic-gate designs directly into chromosomes.

For each reading, please say whether it is a near-trivial restatement of biochemical facts, a substantive and defensible thesis, or actually false — and, crucially, whether it supports the synthetic biologist's "rewriting source code" rhetoric or the critic's dismissal. I am especially interested in how degeneracy (multiple codons specifying the same amino acid) and near-universal but not perfectly universal mapping (for instance, mitochondrial variants) bear on the comparison: computer codes usually avoid redundant symbol-to-output mappings unless designed for error correction, while the genetic mapping is redundant but not evidently optimized for error correction in the same way. Does that feature push us toward one reading and away from another?

Finally, there is a case I keep going back and forth on, and I would like your principle (or competing principles) to deliver a verdict: if researchers synthesize an organism with an expanded genetic alphabet — say, adding artificial base pairs that are read by engineered polymerases but that do not occur in nature — does that expansion make DNA more like a human-designed code (because the alphabet has been deliberately engineered) or less like one (because it breaks the near-universal "natural" mapping and shows the original system was not a designed language but an evolved chemical mechanism)? Please state the principle behind each candidate reading precisely, note which of my implicit judgments it forces me to drop, and indicate what the resulting account predicts for a case I have not mentioned — for instance, whether a viral genome that hijacks a host's ribosomes to translate its own genes should be said to contain "code" in the same sense as the host genome, or in a different one.
