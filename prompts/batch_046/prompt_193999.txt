I sit on the Allocation and Ethics Committee of the Meridian Foundation, and following a lengthy and rather pointed argument with a close friend last evening about whether consequentialist reasoning can legitimately require an institution to abandon its local commitments, I should like you to assist me in drafting the formal opinion I am obliged to circulate before Thursday's meeting. Our Foundation currently operates under a charter that directs us to maximise expected welfare across all beneficiaries, yet we now face a concrete renewal decision: our domestic hospice programme costs approximately £180,000 per quality-adjusted life year saved, whereas redirecting the equivalent sum to antimalarial bed-net distribution in a partner region would save roughly forty times as many statistical lives. Several trustees maintain that retaining the hospice represents a permissible deviation, arguing that the demandingness of complete redirection violates what my friend termed 'structural integrity' — the claim that an institution's embedded historical obligations to its founding community generate a non-optional floor beneath which consequentialist optimisation cannot legitimately fall. Others reply that this is precisely the demandingness objection allowed to metastasise into proximity bias, privileging near beneficiaries over distant ones without principled justification. A proposed charter amendment, the 'proportionality safeguard', would permit retaining up to fifteen per cent of annual disbursement for high-cost local programmes serving the Foundation's original founding community, but only if the committee articulates a principled boundary rather than mere sentiment. Please draft the committee's decision memo: recommend whether to renew the hospice grant, redirect the funds in full, or adopt the safeguard subject to specific conditions, together with a clear time frame for implementation. The memo should treat the strongest form of the demandingness objection with genuine seriousness — including the distinction my friend insisted upon between 'agential demandingness', which concerns what a single moral agent may be required to bear, and 'structural demandingness', which concerns what an institution with inherited obligations may legitimately preserve — and should respond to that distinction with arguments that proponents of full redirection could actually engage with rather than dismissing out of hand. The document should be written in polished British English, appropriate for circulation to trustees, and should conclude with an actionable bottom line rather than personal guidance.
