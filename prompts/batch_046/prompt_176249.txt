In our philosophy of religion course, we discussed Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief, and a fellow student summarized his position like this: Wittgenstein treats religious belief as a self-contained language-game governed by its own grammar, so claims such as "God exists" are not empirical hypotheses but expressions of a form of life. Because the rules are internal to the practice, religious statements can never genuinely conflict with science, and believers do not actually disagree about matters of fact—they merely operate with different pictures. Furthermore, religious experience resembles a private language: since the criteria for correct belief are internal to the believer, outsiders can never correct them, which means faith is essentially subjective and immune to rational criticism.

This sounds suspiciously neat, particularly the leap from language-games to private-language subjectivity and the denial of real disagreement. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what accurately captures Wittgenstein's separation of religious and empirical belief, what subtly overstates the "grammar" analogy into claims about immunity from contradiction or absence of disagreement, and whether comparing religious belief to a private language is a legitimate extension of his view or a distortion that ignores his remarks on religious pictures, the Last Judgment, and the possibility of being wrong about one's own faith.
