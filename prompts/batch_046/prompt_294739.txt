Construct a realistic, one-paragraph thought experiment about collective moral responsibility across generations, designed for philosophical analysis in the tradition—associated with Karl Jaspers and subsequent thinkers—that asks whether a nation can be guilty in any sense that outlives the individuals who committed the original wrong. Your scenario should concern a fictional polity, not a well-documented historical case, and should center on a grave injustice committed generations ago (for example, a founding expropriation systematically embedded in the state’s constitutional order) that continues to shape present institutions.

Engineer the case so that three theoretical positions yield clearly distinct verdicts. First, a strict individualist view should deny that present citizens bear any guilt, collective or otherwise, since moral responsibility attaches only to the voluntary actions of discrete agents, and none of the present citizens participated in the original act. Second, a structural-inheritance view should assign responsibility to present citizens not because they have individually done wrong, but because they are embedded in and actively maintain an institutional framework—say, a citizenship entitlement or constitutional privilege—that was established by the injustice and would collapse if they ceased to uphold it; here responsibility is inherited through non-voluntary structural membership rather than personal choice. Third, a collective-agent view should hold that the polity itself, conceived as a continuing moral subject, remains guilty for the original act, yet this guilt is not distributable to present individuals; instead, it attaches to the collective as an enduring bearer of obligations.

The scenario must isolate the feature that activates the structural-inheritance verdict while leaving the individualist verdict intact: present citizens must be genuine constitutional maintainers of the unjust institution, not merely passive beneficiaries of its legacy. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that each theory delivers its specified verdict and explain precisely which structural feature does the work. Then examine the counterfactual: if the institutional link were severed—if, for instance, all benefits were fully reparative and detached from any present constitutional duty—would the case collapse into a standard debate about causal benefit? Finally, provide an honest assessment of how a defender of individualism would attack the structural-inheritance verdict, and whether that attack succeeds without begging the question against collective moral agency.

Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid references to exotic technology or science-fictional devices. Ensure that the institutional mechanism you describe is intelligible to an ordinary citizen without specialized knowledge, and that the scenario remains within the bounds of realistic social and legal structures.
