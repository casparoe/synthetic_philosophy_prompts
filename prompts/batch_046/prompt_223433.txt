Here's an argument I cooked up for a semanar on cross-cultural ethics and now it feels like a trap. (1) A speech act is disrespectful if it treats the listner as unable to bear the truth. (2) White lies do exactly this: they substitute a comfortable falsehood for reality, assuming the listener can't handle it. (3) Japanese indirectness (the honne/tatemae distinction, avoiding direct refusal) also substitutes a socially comfortable signal for the speaker's true belief, with the same paternalistic assumption. (4) Dutch directness, by contrast, refuses that assumption: it offers the truth plainly and lets the listener deal with it. (5) So if we condemn white lies on grounds of respect, we should also condemn Japanese indirectness and praise Dutch bluntness as the only respectful mode. (6) But if Dutch bluntness were applied in Japanese contexts, it wouldn't show respect; it would destroy social trust by ignoring the shared expectation that direct refusal is itself a kind of agressive act. (7) So either (1) is wrong—disrespect isn't about substituting comfort for truth—or our evaluation of indirectness/bluntness has to flip depending on culture, which means (1) can't be a universal principle. (8) I want to keep (1) because it sounds right, but then I'm stuck with praising Dutch directness everywhere, which feels wrong. Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep (1) than accept that Dutch bluntness is always best, but I want to know what I'm paying if I drop one of the middle steps.
