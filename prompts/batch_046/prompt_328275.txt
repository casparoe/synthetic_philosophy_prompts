I'm serving as a teaching assistant for an upper-division existentialism seminar, and I am grading the midterm responses independently before comparing notes with the professor. Our section has adopted a somewhat specific framing of Sartrean bad faith: we treat it not merely as psychological self-deception but as an ontological misalignment in which consciousness attempts to stabilize its own being by treating either transcendence or facticity as if it possessed the self-identical, object-like stability of an in-itself. The exam presented the case of Marcus, a philosophy graduate student who has spent three years avoiding his dissertation. Whenever colleagues ask about his progress, Marcus insists, "I've realized I'm just not the kind of person who can finish long projects—my temperament is too scattered. It's not a choice; it's who I am." At other moments, however, he remarks that he "could have been a different person" had he taken another path years earlier.

The exam question asked students to explain how Marcus's attitude exemplifies bad faith in Sartre's sense, to distinguish facticity from transcendence, to clarify why Sartre describes bad faith as a "lie to oneself," and to address whether Marcus is displaying what Sartre calls the "spirit of seriousness." Please grade the following student answer on a scale of 0 to 10, and provide specific point-by-point comments that justify the score. I want a precise assessment rather than a general impression: indicate exactly where the reasoning is sound, where a subtle conflation or misattributed view occurs, and where a step does not actually follow from Sartre's position as developed in his major ontological work. I am especially interested in whether the student grasps the structural, ontological point about misalignment rather than reducing the analysis to ordinary psychology.

Marcus is in bad faith because he lies to himself. For Sartre, bad faith differs from lying to others because the deceiver and the deceived are the same consciousness, so Marcus both knows and refuses to know his freedom. He treats his scattered temperament as pure facticity—like an in-itself—claiming he is fixed that way and therefore cannot finish long projects. Yet when he says he could have been different, he appeals to transcendence, showing he secretly recognizes that he is not a thing. This is the double structure of facticity and transcendence that defines the for-itself. The remedy is authenticity, which requires recognizing that we have no predetermined essence. Marcus should therefore acknowledge that his past choices have shaped his present character in a determinative way, yet remain free to reinterpret them and begin writing. Furthermore, this is a clear case of the spirit of seriousness, specifically the form Sartre analyzes with the café waiter who over-identifies with his social role: Marcus treats his psychological temperament as if it carries an intrinsic, pre-given meaning that determines his actions, rather than seeing it as a project he has freely chosen to maintain.
