I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or historiography, but after a recent discussion with colleagues about why our decade-old production platform continues to fail in highly predictable patterns despite constant team restructurings, tooling migrations, and sprint-level interventions, I found myself drawn to Fernand Braudel's conception of the longue durée — the claim that deep geographical, climatic, and economic structures move on timescales far slower than political events or individual biographies, and that these slow-moving layers ultimately constrain what rapid events can occur within them. Translating this analogically into my own domain, I now hold that in large-scale software systems, the "geography" of the architecture — legacy database schemas, network latency topologies, accumulated technical debt distributed across modules, and the thermodynamic and economic costs of data storage — functions as a kind of structural longue durée. More precisely, I contend that for any production system maintained continuously for more than ten years, these architectural layers explain a greater proportion of long-term failure modes, security vulnerabilities, and adaptation trajectories than the rapid sequence of events recorded in version-control histories, management timelines, or personnel turnover.

Please present this position straight, and then ask what would genuinely count against it. I want to know which empirical observations from software archaeology or infrastructure studies, which conceptual distinctions — for instance, between genuine structural constraint and mere path-dependent lock-in — and which methodological findings might show that this view is either false or only a loose metaphor. Please distinguish carefully between defeaters that would be fatal and those that would merely be awkward; indicate which concessions defenders of such a structuralist position ought themselves to accept; and assess honestly whether the view risks being insulated from refutation by vague appeals to "deep structure". Please write your answer in American English.
