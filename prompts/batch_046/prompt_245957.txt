As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of mind, I have been working through Douglas Hofstadter’s suggestion that the self is essentially a pattern that can persist across substrates and even be distributed among other minds, and I want to test whether my intuitions about particular configurations form a coherent view. You are a professional philosopher of mind with expertise in personal identity and distributed cognition. Before answering, please brainstorm at least three distinct principles that could organize the judgments below, then evaluate each one step by step. Lead your response with its bottom line in the very first sentence—state the principle (or tied leading candidates) that best captures my pattern, which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and what it predicts for the two unconsidered cases—then provide the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of the question, and no bibliographies or citation requests.

Here are my verdicts.

Case A: A standard human brain with continuous neural activity. Verdict: exactly one “I” is present.

Case B: A split-brain patient after complete callosotomy, both hemispheres functioning with no direct interhemispheric link, each controlling one side of the body. Verdict: there are now two distinct I’s, though they share historical origin.

Case C: Two adults linked by a high-bandwidth brain-computer interface allowing direct sharing of working-memory contents and emotional states, but each retains fully private autobiographical memories and can disconnect at will. Verdict: there remain only two separate I’s; no distributed self arises.

Case D: A collective of ten adults linked in a mesh network with shared working memory, joint attention, and collective decision-making, but each retains separate autobiographical storage and can leave. Verdict: I think there is probably a genuine group-I in addition to the ten individual ones, though I am unsure.

Case E: A digital emulation of my own brain running on servers across three continents with 300–500 ms latency between nodes, preserving all my memories and functional architecture but lacking biological substrate. Verdict: my I still exists, implemented more slowly and across space.

Case F: A gradual ten-year replacement in which each of my biological neurons is replaced by a synthetic device implanted in one of five other people’s brains, maintaining all functional connections so that by the end my cognitive architecture is distributed across five heads with no central location. Verdict: I still exist, but now distributed across those five brains; there are not five separate I’s.

These judgments feel like they should cohere, yet I see tension: in Case C I deny a distributed I despite intense coupling, whereas in Case D and Case F I seem to accept distribution despite separation and separate autobiographical storage (in D) or absence of a single head (in F). Meanwhile Case E accepts non-biological distributed implementation but not distribution across other minds.

Please state the precise principle—framed in terms of functional integration, informational boundary conditions, narrative continuity, or another concept you find appropriate—that best accommodates Cases A through F. Apply it to two further cases I have not listed: (1) a temporary collective formed only during sleep via synchronized EEG that dissolves completely upon waking; and (2) a single brain with two parallel consciousness streams running in separate hemispheres that are reconnected every hour for full synchronization. Then say honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot save without contradiction, and whether preserving that verdict is worth the added complexity.
