If a museum-quality forgery reproduces every visible feature of a Vermeer painting—brushwork, pigment chemistry, and compositional relation—so precisely that even expert conservators cannot distinguish it from the original without consulting provenance records, then in what sense can the original possess genuinely greater aesthetic value? Any defense of its superiority seems forced either to invoke hidden physical differences that carry no perceptual weight, or to treat the work's causal connection to Vermeer's hand as an indexical property that matters despite being entirely imperceptible. But if artistic value genuinely depends on such causal chains, why does the same standard not apply to literature, where translations and modern editions are accepted as the work itself without requiring direct physical contact with the author's manuscript? Is the premium placed on original paintings therefore just a category mistake about which media preserve identity through physical continuity rather than through structural form, or is there a defensible asymmetry between painting and poetry that justifies treating causal history as aesthetically relevant in one case but not the other?
