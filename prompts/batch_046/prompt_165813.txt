Here are my considered verdicts on cases of harmful side-effects, and I want to know whether they cohere under one principle—or which I should abandon.

Case 1. A runaway trolley threatens five people. You can flip a switch that diverts it onto a side track, killing one. Verdict: permissible.

Case 2. You can stop the trolley by pushing a heavy stranger off a footbridge onto the tracks; his body halts it, killing him and saving the five. Verdict: impermissible.

Case 3. The side track loops back onto the main line. Pushing a person onto the loop will cause the trolley to strike him, stop, and never reach the return point; the five survive, but he dies. Verdict: permissible—structurally closer to Case 1, though it involves direct bodily interference.

Case 4. A surgeon kills one healthy patient and harvests organs to save five others who need transplants. Verdict: impermissible.

Case 5. A commander orders an airstrike on a munitions factory, foreseeing but not intending the deaths of ten adjacent civilians; the strike will disable production and shorten a war that will otherwise kill thousands. Verdict: permissible.

Case 6. The commander orders the strike directly on the ten civilians, intending their deaths as the means to demoralize the enemy and end the war. Verdict: impermissible.

Case 7. You operate a flood-control system. A storm threatens a levee protecting hundreds. The only way to open the emergency gate is to activate a hydraulic mechanism; you know a maintenance worker is trapped inside the chamber, and the pressure will crush him. His death is not the mechanism by which the gate opens—the gate opens by hydraulic force—but his presence makes the crushing unavoidable. The town is saved; he dies. Verdict: impermissible, though I recognize its structural resemblance to Case 5.

Is there a single account—perhaps a refined doctrine of double effect, or an alternative altogether—that captures all seven judgments? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and on what grounds? Please state the principle (or the best competing pair) with precision, linking each clause to the case that motivates it, giving its verdict on an unconsidered scenario in which saving five requires permanently paralyzing an innocent bystander rather than killing her, and stating honestly which of my judgments—especially in Case 3 or Case 7—it cannot accommodate. I want a committed response: take a definite position, defend it with reasons, and do not merely present options for me to choose between.
