I've been thinking through whether my own choices—about meat, travel, or voting—matter morally when millions of others are making the same ones. In casual arguments about climate obligations and diet I keep running into the one-liner, "If your act doesn't change the outcome, it isn't wrong," and in voting debates its cousin, "One vote never decides an election." People use these to end discussion, but I suspect they hide very different arguments, including distinctions I keep encountering between statistical harm and threshold effects. Could you lay out, as a menu, the precise theses each slogan might express—from the trivial point that individual effects are small, through claims about causation, counterfactual dependence, collective agency, statistical harm, and moral thresholds, up to radical views about individual irrelevance? For each reading, say whether it's an obvious truth, a defensible philosophical claim, or false, and tell me which versions actually justify dismissing personal obligation in climate, meat, or voting contexts. I met these lines in everyday debates where they're used to shut down guilt or action; I want the disambiguation, not citations or a bibliography, so I can see exactly what work the slogan is doing.
