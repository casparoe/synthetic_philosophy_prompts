There's a tension I can't resolve between the formal definition of corrigibility and the practical shutdown problem. If an agent is corrigible, it should not resist a human altering its reward function or initiating a shutdown. But formalizing this non-resistance seems to require the agent to evaluate worlds in which its own utility function is different—applying its current preferences to scenarios where those preferences don't hold—which looks like a fixed-point problem: the evaluation criterion shifts with the world being evaluated. Some treatments claim this is resolved by counterfactual reasoning, yet I don't see how that avoids circularity specifically for shutdown, since termination removes the agent from the outcome space altogether. So is the shutdown problem genuinely harder than other corrigibility failures because being turned off eliminates the evaluator, or is that asymmetry just an artifact of framing the agent's continued operation as part of its utility rather than as a precondition for having preferences at all?
