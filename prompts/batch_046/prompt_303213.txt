I'm trying to sort out my intuitions about bodily autonomy vs future deprivation in these life-support type cases, but I'm getting tangled. The standard violinist setup where you're kidnapped and wired to a stranger for nine months -- I'd unplug, obviously. But what if you signed a contract to be wired for one hour, and then they just... don't stop? Still feels okay to leave, but the reason shifts from I never consented to consent was withdrawn. Then there's the variant where pulling the plug kills them, but if you stay hooked up you can't rescue three other people from a burning building (you're the only one with the right blood type or whatever). I still think you can leave, but now I'm not sure if that's because autonomy trumps all, or because the duty to rescue strangers isn't that strong.

Meanwhile the future-like-ours thing: I buy that killing a normal fetus is wrong because it robs them of experiences like ours. But then I think about a fetus that's going to develop into someone with severe anencephaly -- they technically have a biological future, but not a future like ours in any meaningful sense. So the deprivation argument applies selectively? Then there's a case that's been bugging me: imagine a person in a persistant vegetative state with zero chance of consciousness returning, but thier body could be used as a biological incubator for a fully conscious brain that was transplanted in. If we kill the PVS body, are we wronging the original person because of thier future-like-ours (which is nil), or is it okay because that future is already gone? I feel like it's okay, but I can't tell if that's consistent with my other views.

Also there's this weird middle case where you're not kidnapped but you accidentally caused the hookup (like you bumped into them and now you're physically fused). You feel responsible, but does that mean you have to stay for nine months? I think not, but then why is accidental causation different from intentional harm?

Is there one principle that ties together consent-withdrawal, the selectivity of the deprivation argument, and the accidental-fusion case? Or am I just juggling three different moral frameworks and calling it intuition? And wether should my view predict about a case I haven't listed -- say, where you voluntarily agreed to be the incubator for the brain-transplant patient, but then change your mind halfway through?
