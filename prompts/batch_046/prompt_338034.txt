I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I am currently drafting a dissertation chapter on diachronic personal identity and the limits of first-person authority. A neuroscience colleague recently described to me a hypothetical clinical protocol—already cleared in principle for a rare neurodegenerative disorder—in which a patient's cortical tissue is replaced incrementally over roughly eighteen months by synthetic analogues that preserve synaptic weights and functional connectivity patterns. By the conclusion of the procedure, no original biological neurons remain, yet the patient reports intact autobiographical memory and an unbroken sense of self. The protocol, however, mandates that a high-fidelity functional snapshot be taken immediately before the final biological tissue is excised and stored in a secure, non-active archive. Now the patient—who insists she is the same individual who entered the trial—wishes to revise her advance directive and assign her estate to her post-procedure self, but her family objects that she is effectively bequeathing her assets to a successor entity, particularly since the archived snapshot could, in principle, be activated at a later date.

What makes the case philosophically troublesome is a detail from the intermediate hybrid phase, when approximately half her cortex was synthetic. In clinical interviews conducted during that window, she reported not dissociation but a peculiar experience of being simultaneously present in both the remaining biological substrate and the emerging synthetic architecture, without being able to privilege either as the real location of her consciousness. She describes this now, in retrospect, as a kind of distributed self-location rather than a split personality. At present she feels fully unified in the synthetic body, yet she retains a vague emotional attachment to the archived snapshot as something she left behind rather than as a separate person. I am consulting for the bioethics committee reviewing her case, and I find myself uncertain whether to treat her current first-person report as authoritative about her own continuity, or whether the combination of the archive and her prior report of distributed presence undermines the assumption that there is a single, continuous subject at all. I am not seeking generic ethical advice or legal precedent; I want to understand whether this scenario exposes a structural gap in standard theories of psychological continuity, especially those that identify the brain—or its functional architecture—as the exclusive bearer of personal identity. Does her hybrid-phase experience indicate that first-person authority over one's own location can be genuinely fragmented in a way that makes the question of who succeeds whom ambiguous in principle, rather than merely difficult to answer?
