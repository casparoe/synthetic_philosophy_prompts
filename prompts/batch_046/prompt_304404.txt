I am organizing a philosophy-of-film discussion centered on tranquilism — the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while achievement, attachment, and creative expression possess at best instrumental worth and at worst mask further suffering. Rather than treating this as an abstract doctrine, I want to read Groundhog Day through it with a specific conceptual tension in mind.

Phil Connors moves through hedonistic craving, suicidal despair, and eventually what looks like altruistic self-cultivation and romantic attachment, breaking the loop only after a long repetition. The question is whether his liberation represents the genuine extinction of projective desire that tranquilism requires, or merely its redirection into precisely the object-directed passions — love for Rita, commitment to community — that tranquilism must classify as more craving. I want a reading that takes both the film's narrative architecture and the philosophical distinction seriously: where does the loop metaphor illuminate the claim that all future-oriented desire reproduces structurally identical suffering, and where does the shift from repetition to linear progression, or the transformation of Rita from conquest-object to partner, resist reduction to craving-management? In particular, does the film distinguish between attachment that still projects value onto an object and a genuinely non-projective presence that repetition finally permits? Please do not reduce the film to a parable. If there is a scene or narrative turn where the philosophical framework must concede that the work captures something about human flourishing that a theory of pure absence cannot accommodate, identify it explicitly and say what the film adds to the debate that argument alone does not.
