In personal-injury tort law, courts routinely award damages for “loss of enjoyment of life”—a head of compensation intended to cover not economic losses but the diminution of pleasures the plaintiff can no longer experience. To reach a figure, juries and expert witnesses often treat qualitatively disparate capacities—tasting food, playing a musical instrument, engaging in sexual intimacy, appreciating a landscape—as if they were exchangeable units on a single scale of hedonic value, and then assign a monetary equivalent to the lost share. This institutional practice sits in tension with the philosophical heterogeneity problem for hedonism: if the pleasure of solving a theorem and the pleasure of warmth share no common qualitative feature beyond being called “pleasant,” what justifies treating them as comparable losses, and what makes it meaningful to speak of a net “amount” of enjoyment lost?

What philosophical commitments about pleasure, well-being, and commensurability does the practice of hedonic-damages assessment presuppose? Must it assume that all pleasures share a uniform quantitative dimension—say, intensity multiplied by duration—despite their qualitative differences, or can it be reconstructed on a pluralist or preference-satisfaction view of welfare? Is the practice strictly committed to hedonism about well-being, or is it philosophically underdetermined, licensing several rival theories?

Moreover, the same legal system often insists, in negligence doctrine, that liability turns on foreseeable harm rather than on the particular subjective experience of the victim. Does the subjectivity built into hedonic-damages assessment therefore sit in tension with that objective framing? And if one compares this legal practice with the epidemiological use of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), where diverse health states are weighted by a single utility metric—does the convergence suggest a shared implicit philosophy of value, or do the two practices presuppose different answers to the heterogeneity problem?
