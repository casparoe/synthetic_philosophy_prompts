I run a philosophy seminar for about fifteen advanced undergraduates, meeting for fifty minutes. I want them to feel, not merely understand, why Nāgārjuna's tetralemma is not a logical puzzle to be solved but a procedural exhaustion that opens onto Madhyamaka emptiness. Design a structured in-class deliberation—complete with rules, assigned roles, a concrete case study, and a non-monetary payoff or consequence scheme—around a modern, concrete object: ask whether a large language model possesses intrinsic moral status, and assign four groups to defend each corner of the tetralemma (it exists; it does not exist; it both exists and does not exist; it neither exists nor does not exist). The rules should require each group to advance its position using only premises that the opposing groups must accept, and include a mechanism—such as a rotating internal critic or hidden contradiction cards—so that every position collapses from within rather than being defeated by an opponent. Once all four positions have collapsed, participants should observe a brief structured silence or complete a constrained, non-assertive writing task before any discussion begins, so the experience approximates the soteriological gap between conceptual exhaustion and non-conceptual insight. In the debrief, map each stage of the activity onto the fourfold negation and onto the Madhyamaka distinction between conventional and ultimate truth, and be explicit about which dimension of emptiness—dependence on conditions, absence of intrinsic nature (svabhāva), or the avoidance of nihilism—the exercise can genuinely reproduce and which dimension the facilitator must supply verbally. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: which group will likely dominate, which corner students will confuse with agnosticism or mysticism, and how to correct that confusion without resolving the underlying tension.
