The Frege-Geach embedding problem for moral expressivism has been running since the 1960s—Blackburn’s quasi-realism, Gibbard’s plan-expressivism, and the standard critical reply that any compositional account collapses into descriptivism—and in the philosophy seminars I follow it now feels like ritual repetition rather than progress. The expressivists refine the semantics of embedded clauses; the critics insist that logical validity still demands shared representational content; and the meter has not moved in years. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not another round. Is this a verbal dispute about whether “meaning” covers cognitive role or truth-conditional contribution? Are the sides answering different questions—one about formal compositionality, one about the psychology of normative commitment—under the single heading “the embedding problem”? Or is there a hidden bedrock intuition, shared by both camps, that normative language must behave representationally in embedded contexts, which makes the debate circular? Here is the twist I cannot shake: both sides assume that an embedded moral clause either expresses the speaker’s current attitude or describes a moral fact, but neither considers whether embedding might systematically shift the speech-act potential of the clause without either describing or expressing in the base sense—something like a dynamic pragmatics of normative discourse. Is that third option genuinely available, or does it merely rename the problem? Given your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is—substantive, verbal, cross-purpose, or intuition-locked—what would count as progress? Would it take a formal result about compositionality for non-representational languages, an empirical study of how people process embedded moral judgments, or a reconceptualization of what “valid argument” requires in normative domains? And if the hidden shared assumption I mentioned is real, is there any way to dislodge it without abandoning expressivism altogether?
