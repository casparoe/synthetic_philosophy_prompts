I am designing a graduate seminar module on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, but I want to move past the familiar Dennett-versus-Frankish dichotomy that students tend to treat as exhaustive. Please build a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a branching decision tree—that classifies positions according to commitments that standard surveys usually collapse together.

The root question should be: Is there a genuine fact of the matter about what it is like to be you, beyond all functional, representational, and dispositional facts about your mental economy?

From there, the tree should split by three successive distinctions, each phrased as a concrete claim that someone could endorse or reject without already knowing the philosophical label it corresponds to.

First branch (applies when the root is answered negatively): Does the appearance of phenomenality arise from (a) a representational misattribution of real but non-phenomenal cognitive states, (b) a functional framing error in which no specific mental content is misrepresented but the self-model assigns the wrong ontological category to its own operations, or (c) a linguistic reification of first-person report practices?

Second branch (applies within every illusionist limb): Is the illusion in principle eliminable—could a being with perfect introspective and neuroscientific information dispel it—or is it structurally necessary for any system capable of self-reference?

Third branch (applies when the root is answered affirmatively): If phenomenal consciousness is real, is its nature intrinsic and non-representational, or is it constituted entirely by representational content?

Each leaf should carry either the name of an actual position or theorist—e.g., Frankish-style strong illusionism, Dennett-style user-illusion functionalism, a Chalmers-style naturalistic dualist realism, first-order representationalism—or be marked "unnamed / unoccupied," accompanied by one sentence diagnosing the vacancy as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please also flag any pair of named positions that this particular tree fails to separate, explaining precisely why the branch points do not discriminate between them.

Constraints: Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not invoke ultra-obscure theories that depend on specialized literature a general model could not be expected to have encountered. Keep the analysis at the level of conceptual commitments rather than empirical neuroscience. Where you reference a named view, paraphrase its commitment in your own terms; do not place any passage in quotation marks.

Please organize the response in the following sections and approximate lengths:

1. The tree structure (approximately 350 words): present the branching nodes in sequence, stating the claim that defines each split and listing the leaves attached to each path.
2. Unoccupied logical spaces (approximately 150 words): identify the combinations of answers that yield no named position and briefly diagnose each gap.
3. Separation failures (approximately 150 words): state which named pairs the tree collapses and explain why the branches are insufficient to distinguish them.
