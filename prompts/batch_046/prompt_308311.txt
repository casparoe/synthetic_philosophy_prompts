I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, currently drafting a chapter on scientific realism that treats verisimilitude not merely as Popper's abandoned program but as a persistent structural problem for any account of theory-choice that wants to honor both predictive accuracy and truth. In my seminar, we have been debating whether the failure of logical-content measures to deliver a consistent ordering of theories by closeness-to-truth is a technical glitch or a deep feature of how approximation works. To close the session, I want a poem that makes that structural feature felt rather than argued. Specifically: write a villanelle—nineteen lines, ABA rhyme, two refrains—in which the first refrain expresses the intuition that distance from truth is measured by what a theory gets wrong, and the second refrain expresses the counter-intuition that a false theory rich in true consequences can be closer to the truth than a barren true statement. The refrains should alternate through the six tercets, never resolving their contradiction, until the closing couplet forces them into adjacency—not synthesis, but co-presence. The rhyme scheme and repetition should do the philosophical work: the reader should experience why the two metrics keep passing each other, and why any attempt to collapse them into one measure fails. Please render the idea precisely enough that a philosopher of science recognizes the Popperian framing—truth-content versus falsity-content, the possibility that falsehood and proximity are not inversely related—without turning the poem into a summary of the literature. I am not asking for citations, author-date references, or a reading list; the point is conceptual accuracy embodied in form. The audience is graduate students who have read the basic texts but need to feel the problem rather than just recall the objections.
