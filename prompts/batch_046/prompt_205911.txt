I have been thinking about the classic parable of Death in Damascus, in which a servant flees to Aleppo to escape a prophecy that he will die in Damascus, only to encounter Death there and learn that the meeting was always destined for Aleppo. Most treatments I have found reduce the puzzle to a simple choice between fatalism and free will, but that seems too crude; I want to understand the actual logical landscape of responses, not just the labels people attach to them.

Could you build me a taxonomy — specifically a branching decision tree — that sorts the live philosophical positions by what one believes about concrete variants of the case? I want six to eight branch nodes, each phrased as a specific scenario or claim that someone can answer without knowing philosophical jargon, so that the instrument sorts people by their judgments rather than by which name they prefer. For instance:

- The seer issues a 99% probabilistic forecast rather than a certainty, and the agent knows this. Is fleeing still self-defeating, prudent, or rationally neutral?
- The prophecy is explicitly conditional: "If you remain in Damascus, you will meet Death there." The agent flees anyway. Does this dissolve the paradox, or does it merely relocate the constraint?
- In a world without causal determinism, a predictor with perfect but non-causal foresight issues the warning. Does the same fatalist bind arise?

Each leaf should be a genuinely defended position (logical fatalism, libertarian open-future views, compatibilist evasion, epistemic-resolution accounts, and any others you think belong), with one sentence stating its core commitment about what the agent ought to do. Where a combination of answers produces an empty leaf — for example, denying that prediction ever constrains choice while also holding that fleeing is necessarily irrational — please flag it and diagnose briefly whether that gap is incoherent, merely unexplored, or unpopular for some clear reason.

Keep the tree focused on distinctions that change the agent's rational choice in the story, avoid citations or reading lists, and do not depend on obscure technical literature; the point is to map the logical space from the inside using reasoning alone.
