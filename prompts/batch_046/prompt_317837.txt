As a novelist who has spent the last decade writing fiction that attempts to render the interior lives of people caught in historical machinery, I find myself returning repeatedly to a claim I first encountered in a graduate seminar on African American aesthetics: the slogan, often associated with W. E. B. Du Bois, that all art is propaganda. In our workshop discussions, my colleagues wield this phrase as if it were a single blade—sometimes to justify the didactic turn in a narrative, sometimes to dismiss any work that refuses an explicit political program, and occasionally to argue that aesthetic judgment itself is always already an ideological maneuver.

I am trying to understand what precise theses are being smuggled in under that compression. Could it be the trivial observation that every artwork emerges from a social position and therefore carries some orientation toward the world? Or the stronger claim that the primary function of art is instrumental, that beauty is legitimate only insofar as it advances a collective cause? Or something more radical—that the distinction between aesthetic experience and political persuasion is not merely porous but illusory, and that anyone who insists on art for art's sake is either naive or deliberately obscuring power?

I want a menu of these readings laid out with verdicts: which are platitudes no one could deny, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which are actually being invoked when a writer uses the line to defend a polemical scene or to condemn a lyric poem for its silence on injustice. In particular, I need to know whether the slogan can bear the weight of the arguments my friends are making with it, or whether they are trading on ambiguity to move from an uncontroversial premise to an unwarranted conclusion about what a novelist like me ought to be writing.
