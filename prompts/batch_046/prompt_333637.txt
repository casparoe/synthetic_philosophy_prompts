I was trying to make sense of this part in Hume's Enquiry (around 1748) where he asks why we expect one billiard ball to move when another strikes it. He argues that we don't see any necessary connection in the objects themselves—there's no logical link visible between the collision and the motion—so our expectation must come entirely from custom or habit formed by repeated past observations. He basically challenges anyone to point to the actual connecting principle in nature, and when they can't, he concludes that induction isn't grounded in reason but in a kind of mental reflex.

Now I'm wondering if this connects to the no-free-lunch results in learning theory, which show that without some prior assumption about the world, no learning strategy can beat any other across all possible enviroments. The way I see it, Hume's custom might be functioning exactly like that prior: we assume the future will look like the past, not because we've proven it, but because our cognition is habbituated with that bias. But I'm not sure if Hume allows that this bias actualy justifies our predictions in a practical sense, or if he thinks it's still completely irrational—just unavoidable. Does the passage support reading custom as an inductive bias that makes learning possible, or does Hume insist that even with that bias, we're still not reasoning at all, just reacting? I'm not after a bibliography, just whether this analogy holds up when you sit with the passage itself.
