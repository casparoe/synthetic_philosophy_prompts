At a philosophy-of-history seminar, two participants—Dr. Elena Voss and Marcus Reed—debated whether the 'man of his time' defense holds for Winston Churchill, Thomas Jefferson, and Christopher Columbus, centering on what Voss called 'peer divergence': the presence of near-contemporaries in comparable social positions who articulated and acted on the very moral views that now condemn the figure. Voss argued that Churchill actively resisted decolonization even when other British statesmen of his class and era accepted Indian independence as inevitable; that Jefferson designed slave codes and resisted manumission despite abolitionist voices within Virginia's planter elite; and that Columbus, by contrast, faced almost no internal peer dissent on indigenous rights, making the defense genuinely available to him but not to the others. She concluded that peer divergence should be a graded criterion: the defense weakens proportionally to how close and credible the dissent was within the figure's own epistemic circle. Reed replied that this standard proves too much—every dominant moral view of an era coexists with dissent, so if peer divergence alone removes the defense, then no historical actor could ever be excused for holding mainstream beliefs, which makes moral progress conceptually impossible. He added that Voss risks conflating explanation with excuse: showing that Churchill or Jefferson could have known better explains why they are blameworthy, but it does not show that they should be judged by our standards rather than by the ones they faced. Reed also noted that grading by 'closeness' of dissent introduces arbitrary line-drawing—why should a British cabinet colleague count as a peer for Churchill but not a Quaker pamphleteer for Jefferson?—and that Columbus's case is actually harder, not easier, because the absence of dissent might reflect structural ignorance rather than personal virtue, which should not make him more excusable.
