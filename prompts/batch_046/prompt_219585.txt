I am a high school senior delivering the opening remarks at our school's interdisciplinary symposium on change and identity. The audience includes philosophy teachers, fellow students, and parents with no philosophical background. I want the speech to turn structurally on the Buddhist doctrine of anatta—specifically the analysis of the five skandhas, the chariot-style argument that identity is a conventional designation rather than an ultimate substance, and the Madhyamaka distinction between conventional truth and ultimate emptiness—rather than using these ideas as decoration. Because there is no permanent self, I want to reject the standard message about "finding yourself" or "staying true to who you are," and instead argue that recognizing ourselves as processes of dependent origination is what makes moral growth and collective celebration coherent: the class of 2025 is not a set of enduring souls but a genuinely transformed pattern of conditions, and that is precisely why the ceremony matters. Please write a five-minute speech in a voice appropriate to a thoughtful student, accessible to non-philosophers without flattening the conceptual difficulty, and constructed so that any phrase assuming a fixed personal essence is explicitly excluded. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to texts so obscure that they could not be discussed from general knowledge.
