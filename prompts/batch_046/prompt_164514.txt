I am convening a postgraduate seminar on practical reason—eight participants, one two-hour session—where I want the group to encounter incommensurable values not as an abstract puzzle but as a lived structural constraint. To move beyond the familiar trolley-problem repertoire, I propose we work with a framing device I have sketched but not fully developed: each participant receives a "value architecture" card assigning them three irreducible dimensions—say, loyalty, autonomy, and aesthetic integrity—with an explicit prohibition on translating any one into the others. The group must then collectively distribute a scarce, indivisible resource between two projects that each maximise a different pair of these dimensions, using only deliberation, not calculation. Once the allocation is fixed, every participant is required to articulate a justification for the outcome; the critical twist is that they must then examine whether that justification invented a comparison metric that did not exist prior to the choice, and whether such invention counts as rational resolution or as rationalisation.

Please design the complete session: the precise rules of deliberation and allocation, including time limits and speaking order; the assigned roles—particularly an "archivist" who records justifications without evaluating them and a "sceptic" tasked with challenging any emergent common currency; a debrief that maps moments of deadlock onto the distinction between epistemic uncertainty and genuine value incommensurability; and an honest assessment of which aspect of hard choice—specifically, the experience that preference can be rational even when no covering value determines it—the exercise necessarily fails to reproduce without direct philosophical instruction. The activity should require no bibliographies, citations, or named authorities; the philosophical pressure must arise entirely from the rules and the stakes built into the task.
