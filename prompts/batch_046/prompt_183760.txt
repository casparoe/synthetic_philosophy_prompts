I've been stuck on why the internal compatibilist split about free will worth wanting hasn't moved in decades. One camp, the hierarchical folks in the Frankfurt tradition, says you only need your second-order desires to align with your first-order ones — internal coherence is enough. The other camp, the reasons-responsive people, says that's too weak: your mechanism also has to be counterfactually responsive to reasons, so you need modal robustness, not just actual alignment. Both claim they're describing the variety that's actually worth wanting, and neither has budged since the eighties.

My working theory is that it's mostly verbal: they're fighting over wether "control" means internal coherence or modal flexibility. But I've also been playing with a niche split I don't see used much — call it "deliberative free will" (the long-term capacity to revise your values) versus "executive free will" (the moment-to-moment capacity to act on them). I suspect the hierarchical camp is really defending deliberative free will, while the reasons-responsive camp is defending executive free will, and they're talking past eachother because the phrase bundles both together.

So diagnose it: is this a hidden empirical question about whether people blame differently for failures of deliberation versus execution? A bedrock intuition clash? Or is "free will" just ill-posed here? Use what each side actually says, not a survey. And be honest — could a distinction like deliberative/executive actually resolve the stall, or just rename it? What would count as progress: a formal model, an experiment on blame attribution, or admitting there's no unified concept?
