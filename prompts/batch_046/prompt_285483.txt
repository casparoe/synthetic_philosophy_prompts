I'm a PhD student in comparative ethics and political philosophy, and I'm trying to draft a workshop proposal—maybe for a small three-month university grant—on this weird tension I keep running into in the literature on folk morality. Everyone frames the German vs. American contrast as Ordnung versus individual conscience, rule-following versus gut feeling, Weberian bureaucracy versus Protestant-style moral independence. But I think that framing actually hides the interesting part. I want to ask whether the real divergence isn't in how they rank rules against conscience, but in how they distribute what I'd call moral residue—the leftover obligation, guilt, or repair-work that remains after you've either followed the rule or listened to your conscience and still feel the harm hasn't been fully addressed.

The precise question: in cases of institutional failure or bureaucratic harm, does German folk morality tend to relocate that residue back onto the system itself, treating it as a failure of Ordnung that requires structural correction, while American folk morality dumps the residue onto the individual conscience, framing it as a personal failure to speak up or resist? And if that's right, which allocation does the better normative work for actual moral repair, especially in contexts like post-war administrative accountability versus whistleblower culture?

I need the proposal to state why the standard comparative ethics answers leave this open—they mostly just describe the cultural contrast and then pick sides based on liberal preferences, without asking what happens to the emotional and practical leftover after the decision is made. The approach should be conceptual rather than empirical: build a normative framework for residue-allocation that doesn't presuppose either culture's answer, apply it to two structured comparative cases, and produce a typology with clear criteria for success. Success would be arguing that one culture's distribution is more coherent or humane, or (more likely) showing both fail in predictable ways and sketching a hybrid. Failure is if it collapses back into "Germans like rules, Americans like freedom." The deliverable should be a 40-page working paper plus a discussion guide for people in compliance and organizational ethics—not a bibliography, since I'll fill in the Williams and Weber references myself, but a sharp argument that this is philosophy, not sociology with branding.
