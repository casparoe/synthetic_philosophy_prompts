You are a professional philosopher with expertise in moral psychology, cross-cultural ethics, and the philosophy of science. Before you begin, please take a moment to brainstorm the key conceptual distinctions—such as moral realism versus anti-realism, descriptive versus normative claims, and the difference between conventional and moral norms—that will be needed for a rigorous analysis. Then think carefully, and provide your adjudication in American English, using American spelling and terminology throughout.

I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy in earnest. Last week, in a discussion group, two colleagues—Dr. Chen, a cultural anthropologist, and Dr. Patel, a moral philosopher—became entangled in a disagreement about whether cross-cultural differences in folk morality undermine moral objectivity. I observed the exchange as a bystander and have no settled view. I should like you to adjudicate it point by point.

Here is how their argument unfolded.

Chen opened by citing the WEIRD literature: Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, and Democratic populations appear to be psychological outliers, relying almost exclusively on the ethics of care and fairness, whereas many non-WEIRD societies treat loyalty, authority, and sanctity as fully moral domains. She argued that if morality were objectively grounded, one would expect substantial convergence in which behaviours count as moral violations; the observed clustering instead suggests that moral categories are culturally constructed.

Patel responded that convergence is actually far more striking than divergence—every stable society prohibits serious harm and cheating. The variation, he said, concerns the extension of moral concern and the weighting of competing values, not the existence of a shared core. He added that treating loyalty or authority as moral in one culture and merely conventional in another may be a taxonomic error rather than an ontological difference.

Chen then pressed a stronger point, drawing on Shweder's tripartite framework—autonomy, community, and divinity—to claim that these are not merely weighted differently but represent incommensurable ethical orientations. A WEIRD liberal, she said, genuinely does not experience an authority violation as wrong in the same qualitative manner as a traditionalist does; this is not a difference in taxonomy but in what counts as a moral fact. From this, she concluded that moral realism lacks empirical support.

Patel conceded part of her point: if moral progress consists in the WEIRD demoralisation of loyalty and authority, then invoking progress to defend realism risks circularity, since the standard of progress itself seems culturally located. However, he proposed an alternative he called functional convergence—the idea that loyalty and authority norms are cooperation mechanisms that become locally moralised because they solve coordination problems. If so, morality might still be objectively anchored to objective social-coordination constraints, analogous to how physiological traits adapt to objective ecological pressures.

Chen seized on this analogy. She argued that functional convergence actually supports anti-realism: if moral norms track social-coordination needs in the same way that traits track ecological niches, then morality is calibrated to context rather than discovered as mind-independent truth. She proposed a hybrid view—contextual objectivism with domain-specific calibration—in which moral truths exist but are parameterised by social-ecological conditions, much like adaptive strategies in evolutionary biology.

Patel replied that this hybrid is unstable. Either the parameters selecting which norms apply are themselves morally arbitrary, in which case the view collapses into relativism, or there is a meta-principle governing parameter selection, which restores universalism. He admitted, however, that he had not demonstrated the necessary existence of such a meta-principle, and that his functional-convergence argument might therefore be incomplete.

Please adjudicate this disagreement with precision. For each round, indicate who is right about the specific claim being made and where, if anywhere, each party is missing something important. In particular, address whether cross-cultural variation in folk morality genuinely undermines moral objectivity, whether Shweder's incommensurability argument succeeds against Patel's convergence claim, and whether Chen's contextual objectivism offers a stable middle ground or collapses into one of the two extremes Patel identifies. Also discuss what both disputants appear to overlook—perhaps regarding the distinction between moral psychology and moral philosophy, or the possibility that descriptive variation is compatible with normative universality in ways neither has fully considered.
