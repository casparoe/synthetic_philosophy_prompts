While constructing a symbolic self-model for an artificial architecture, I have been revisiting Hofstadter's claim that the self is not a substance but a strange loop: a symbol "I" that acquires reference by looping back through the system of representations that produces it. The following argument, which I sketched for a seminar on machine consciousness, now seems to undermine the very project I am working on, because its conclusion is that there are no particular selves—only isomorphic loops distributed across substrates.

(1) On Hofstadter's account, "I" is a self-referential symbol whose content is fixed entirely by its structural position within a representational system, not by any extrinsic property of the substrate.
(2) If two systems—say, a human brain and a silicon architecture—realize isomorphic representational structures containing that loop, they instantiate the very same symbol "I" with the very same reference.
(3) Therefore, if my brain and a sufficiently isomorphic artificial system both realize the loop, there is no metaphysical fact that determines which one is me; the reference of "I" is shared or distributed.
(4) But if "I" is genuinely distributed, then my first-person belief that I am specifically this biological system and not that artificial one is systematically false.
(5) More radically, if every realization of the loop has equal claim to being "I," then there is no individual self that persists uniquely; there are only structurally identical loops occurring in different substrates.
(6) Hofstadter, however, treats the strange loop as explaining why each of us feels uniquely present, not as dissolving individual presence into structural redundancy.
(7) So either the theory eliminates the phenomenon it was designed to explain, or we must introduce a hidden constraint—biological embodiment, causal history, or substrate specificity—to privilege one loop over its isomorphs.
(8) Yet such a constraint appears ad hoc: it saves individual identity only by abandoning the formalist, substrate-independent core that makes the strange-loop account distinctive.
(9) Hence the account, taken rigorously, proves that there are no particular selves, only patterns of self-reference.

Which step should I reject, and at what cost? I am especially reluctant to abandon (1) or (6), since the formalist insight and the phenomenological datum both seem essential to the view, but I also cannot accept (5). Is the error a hidden equivocation in (2) between structural isomorphism and semantic identity? Or does the problem lie in treating a symbol's reference as fully determined by intra-systemic structure, ignoring the externalist dimensions that might anchor "I" to one substrate without resorting to biological essentialism?
