I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates who have already read Schlick’s essay on the meaning of life but have uniformly arrived at the same misunderstanding: they interpret his denial of cosmic purpose as a form of nihilism, and they assume his positive view must therefore be some kind of hedonistic escapism. I need them to grasp his actual thesis—that meaning is not the discovery of an external purpose but the achievement of “play,” that is, activity undertaken for its own intrinsic value rather than as “work” performed for a separable end. Please act as an expert philosophy educator with deep familiarity with early twentieth-century logical empiricism and with teaching conceptual distinctions that students systematically invert. Before you lay out the session plan, briefly brainstorm the dependency structure: which prior confusion about purpose versus value must be dissolved before Schlick’s distinction can even register, and what is the misconception map—what false readings will appear, in what order, and why?

Plan the explanatory arc in full pedagogical detail. First, specify the one idea that must be firmly in place before introducing Schlick’s play/work distinction—what conceptual ground has to be cleared so that denying cosmic purpose does not immediately sound like denying all meaning? Second, identify the single carrying example that should thread through the entire session: I want to use the case of a neighborhood community garden that residents continue to cultivate with care and ritual even after the land has been sold and they know with certainty it will be bulldozed for development, leaving no harvest, no legacy, and no audience. Explain precisely what this scenario must possess (intrinsic engagement, awareness of futility regarding external ends, collective rather than solitary practice) and what it must lack (hidden utility, future reward, religious transcendence) in order to function as a living model of Schlickian play rather than as mere stoic endurance or denial. Third, lay out the dependency order of the session’s components—what must come before what, and which step depends on the successful resolution of the prior one.

Fourth, map the misconceptions you expect the students to form or bring, in the chronological order they will arise during the session: the “nihilism” misreading that Schlick is saying life is worthless; the “mental-image” error that play means childish fantasy or leisure-class recreation rather than serious, absorbed activity; the “elitism” objection that only people free from labor can afford to live this way; and the “category mistake” that if an activity produces no outcome it is therefore irrational. For each, indicate the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and how the garden example should be redeployed at that moment to defuse it. Fifth, design one concrete check-for-understanding question for the end that would reveal whether a student has genuinely internalized Schlick’s distinction—something that cannot be answered correctly by simply repeating “life should be play” but that requires applying the purpose/meaning separation to a novel situation. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—such as Schlick’s broader verificationist commitments, his relation to Carnap or the Vienna Circle, or biographical details about his death—and explain how to mark that boundary openly without making the session feel truncated or evasive. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not ask for quotations from Schlick’s text; and keep the plan concrete enough that I could teach from it directly.
