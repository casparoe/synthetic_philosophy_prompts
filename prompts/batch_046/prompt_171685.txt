Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Mara and Elias, concerning whether David Hume's argument in his suppressed essay on suicide can be reconciled with the moral psychology developed in the Treatise of Human Nature. Mara should contend that the essay's reasoning is essentially rationalist and utility-based—assessing whether one's continued existence benefits society and oneself—thereby clashing with Hume's well-known view that moral distinctions arise from sentiment and sympathy rather than from reason alone. Elias should defend compatibility by advancing a specific interpretive claim: that Hume's reference to obligations toward oneself is best understood through the lens of character-formation and the durable self, such that the decision to end one's life must be evaluated not by a momentary balance of pleasures and pains but by whether it preserves or fractures the narrative integrity of the agent's social and moral identity. The exchange should proceed with genuine back-and-forth: Mara should press Elias on whether this appeal to narrative coherence smuggles in a non-Humean, rationalist criterion for self-governance, and Elias should respond by explaining how sympathy with one's future self and concern for reputation can ground that coherence without abandoning sentimentalism. Neither interlocutor should be treated as a strawman. Avoid bibliographies, citation requests, or references to obscure secondary literature. Paraphrase any textual claims rather than using verbatim quotation marks drawn from real sources.
