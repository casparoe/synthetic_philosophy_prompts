I am composing a novel in which a philosopher-character becomes obsessed with the idea that moral blame requires an impossible form of self-creation. In my research I encountered a summary—perhaps from a popular podcast or an opponent's essay—of what philosophers call Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. The summary proceeds in five steps.

First, it asserts that being truly morally responsible for an action requires being truly responsible for the mental states—desires, beliefs, temperament—that caused it. Second, it claims that to be responsible for those mental states, one must have intentionally fashioned them, which requires being responsible for an earlier set of mental states, generating an infinite regress that can never reach a foundation. Third, it concludes that because no person can be the self-creating origin of their own character, true moral responsibility is impossible for everyone. Fourth, it draws the practical inference that all punitive justice is therefore irrational and should be replaced by a purely therapeutic or managerial approach to wrongdoing. Fifth, it states that the argument depends entirely on determinism, so if quantum mechanics introduces genuine indeterminacy into human choice, the regress is broken and moral responsibility is restored.

As a writer, I wish to incorporate this idea accurately into my character's voice without distorting the underlying philosophy. I am therefore asking for an audit of this summary. Would a careful defender of Strawson's position accept each of these five claims? Where does the summary conflate Strawson's specific notion of true moral responsibility with familiar libertarian demands that an agent be an uncaused cause? Does the leap from the impossibility of self-creation to the abolition of punitive justice actually follow from Strawson's view, or does it overlook his well-known distinction between reactive emotional attitudes and desert-based blame? And is it correct that quantum indeterminacy defeats the argument, or does Strawson treat random causation as equally incompatible with true responsibility? Please examine the summary claim by claim: identify what is faithful, what is a subtle misrepresentation, and what is flatly wrong, and tell me whether the practical conclusion—that we ought to abandon punishment—survives once the philosophical errors are corrected.
