I'm preparing the take-home final for my upper-level seminar “Epistemology and the Archive,” and I want to avoid a prompt that students can answer by reciting standard distinctions between collective memory and documentary history. To force genuine conceptual engagement, I have introduced in our recent unit the idea of “mnemonic dissent”—situations in which a community’s living oral tradition sustains a narrative (for example, of a violated treaty or an erased displacement) that state archival records explicitly deny, and in which members treat this divergence not as empirical error but as a morally necessary resistance to what they call “archival hegemony.” For the exam, please write a single essay question, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a detailed marking rubric. The assessment is open-note, 72 hours, 2,500-word maximum, worth 40 percent of the final grade. The essay prompt should present a concrete hypothetical scenario—an indigenous community maintaining an oral tradition of a broken treaty that official archives reject—and ask students to adjudicate whether collective memory or the historical record should give way in this conflict. Crucially, top answers must not simply choose one side; they must disentangle epistemic authority from moral authority in remembrance, examine whether collective memory can be self-correcting in the absence of documentary verification, and identify the precise point at which a “counter-memory” risks collapsing into an epistemic bubble. The marking scheme should specify that A-range answers must locate the conflict between epistemic and moral authority and rigorously assess the self-correction problem, while B-range answers treat the issue as purely empirical or merely summarize canonical collective-memory literature. Please do not require bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; evaluation should turn entirely on argument structure and conceptual precision.
