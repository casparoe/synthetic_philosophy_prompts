I've been arguing with friends about whether skipping an election is actually a moral failure, and we kept circling back to the 2000 florida recount — bush v gore, hanging chads, eventually certified by 537 votes. I want you to treat that episode as a live case study for the inefficacy objection that people throw around for climate change, meat consumption, and voting: the idea that one person's contribution gets causally drowned out, so you can't blame them for the collective harm.

But here's the twist i can't shake. Voting produces a discrete harm — either the wrong candidate wins or they don't — which means there's an actual threshold, and in florida that threshold was literally crossed by a few hundred votes. Climate damage and factory farming are continous; every extra ton of co2 or burger adds some incremental suffering, but there's no single 'tipping vote' analog. So map the florida recount onto the collective-harm framework: who are the agents, what is the harmful collective outcome, and exactly where does the causal chain hold up versus dissolve? Does the 537-vote margin prove the inefficacy objection is empirically wrong for voting, or does it show that the objection was always about the rarity of being pivotal rather than true causal impotence? And running it the other way: does the discrete structure of voting mean it's actually a different kind of collective-action problem — maybe a coordination game with a sharp cutoff — compared to climate or meat, where harm is linear? I'm not looking for the standard expected-utility reply about probabilities of being decisive; i want the case to actually test whether the inefficacy argument depends on the shape of the harm function, and whether florida reveals that we've been treating continous and discrete collective harms as the same thing when they shouldn't be.
