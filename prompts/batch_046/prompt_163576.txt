The line "If morality were objective, disagreement about it would be impossible—or at least far less widespread than it actually is" circulates constantly in debates about WEIRD psychology and cross-cultural folk morality, especially when people discuss whether the divergence between Western harm- and fairness-centered intuitions and other cultures' stronger emphasis on purity, loyalty, or authority undermines moral realism. I encountered it being used in a philosophy-of-morality discussion group to do two incompatible jobs at once: first, to argue that deep cross-cultural disagreement proves moral objectivity is a myth; second, to defend a parochial form of objectivism that treats Western individualizing foundations as the correct baseline and treats other moral frameworks as mere cultural noise. Separate the precise theses this slogan could be expressing—epistemic, metaphysical, statistical, normative, or methodological—indicate which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which are actually being invoked in each of those two argumentative moves. In particular, address whether the claim requires universal agreement for objectivity, whether it confuses descriptive divergence with normative error, and whether it implicitly treats disagreement about binding foundations (purity, authority, loyalty) differently from disagreement about harm and fairness without justification. Present the readings as a menu with verdicts rather than selecting one, and specify which reading, if any, actually supports using the slogan to dismiss non-WEIRD moral frameworks as objectively mistaken rather than merely different.
