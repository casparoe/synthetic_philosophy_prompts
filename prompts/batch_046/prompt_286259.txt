So I retired from the lab a few years back and lately I've been hanging out with a philosophy group at the community center. Last week two regulars, Jules and Rita, got into it over Kelly betting and whether this "geometric rationailty" idea actually applies to how scientists should update beliefs. I tried to follow along but they went pretty deep and I couldn't call it.

Jules started by saying Kelly is for gamblers with known odds, not scientists. In science you're dealing with one-off hypotheses where you don't even know the true probabilities, and there's no "reinvestment" of a correct belief like there is with money. So treating credence like a bankroll is a category mistake.

Rita pushed back: but being wrong about a big theory is like going bust - you discount future evidence that contradicts it, your reputation takes a hit, and your epistemic capital is ruined. So you should scale your commitment to the strength of your evidence, exactly like Kelly scales your bet size. She said this is especially true in non-ergodic settings where you only live one trajectory, so avoiding total ruin matters more than average gain.

Jules came back with what seemed like a sharper point: Kelly assumes ergodicity, that the ensemble average equals your time average. But a scientist isn't an ensemble of paralel selves; they're one person on one timeline. Optimizing geometric mean over imaginary parallel worlds is metaphysically weird. He said if you drop that, all you've done is adopt a log-utility risk aversion, which is just standard expected utility in disguise - the "geometric" framing doesn't add anything.

Rita replied that even on a single trajectory, log utility captures something real: scientific communities actually behave this way. They don't go all-in on one preliminary result. So Kelly captures a collective norm, not just individual psychology. She also said Jules was ignoring the optional-stopping problem - if you keep running experiments until you reach a Kelly-optimal commitment level, you're biasing your results, so the framework actually warns against p-hacking.

Jules said that was backwards: Kelly tells you how big a bet to place given your edge, not how long to keep sampling. He argued that if you apply Kelly to belief, you're essentially saying "update less aggressively than Bayes," but he couldn't see why Bayes needs a correction factor from gambling theory.

Specifically: is Jules right that Kelly requires ergodicity? Is Rita right that epistemic ruin is analogous to financial ruin? And is the optional-stopping point she raised actually relevant, or is Jules right that it's a confusion? Also, what are they both missing about whether geometric rationality collapses into risk-averse Bayesianism?
