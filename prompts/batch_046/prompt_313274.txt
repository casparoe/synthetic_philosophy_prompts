I am trying to determine what I actually think about the appropriate level of causal explanation in historical change, working within the Braudelian distinction between the longue durée of geography and climate, the medium-term rhythms of economies and societies, and the short time of events and individuals. Rather than arguing from theory, I want to go by my intuitive verdicts on concrete episodes and see whether they converge on a principle, or whether some must be sacrificed.

Here are my gut judgments, case by case.

1. The gradual contraction of trans-Saharan trade and the decline of Sahelian urban centers between approximately 300 and 1100 CE. I judge this primarily a long-duration structural story: slow desertification, shifting monsoon belts, and soil degradation over centuries. The particular dynastic choices of Ghana, Mali, or Songhai rulers seem secondary.

2. The Meiji government's 1872 decision to construct the Tokyo-Yokohama railway with British capital and engineers rather than through domestic labor mobilization. I judge this primarily event- and individual-level: Okubo Toshimichi's specific fiscal preferences, the immediate post-Restoration budget crisis, and the particular terms negotiated with British lenders matter more than Japan's long geographic isolation or its lack of coal near the capital.

3. The persistence of smallholder rice cultivation in the Red River Delta versus large plantation structures in the Mekong Delta through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. I take this as structural: soil chemistry, flood regimes, and population-density patterns established over centuries dominate, with French colonial policy and individual landlord decisions playing only a conditioning role.

4. The collapse of Classic Maya political centers in the southern lowlands around 800–900 CE. I see this as long-duration: multi-decadal drought cycles, soil-nutrient depletion from continuous maize cultivation, and progressive deforestation. The specific wars or dynastic failures of individual city-states feel like surface manifestations.

5. The abandonment of Angkor and the Khmer court's relocation to Phnom Penh in the fifteenth century, despite drought stress and hydraulic complexity comparable to those in case 4. Here I switch: I judge this primarily event- and individual-driven—the specific military defeat by Ayutthaya in 1431, the particular king's decision to relocate, and the failure of specific reservoir projects under his administration.

6. The Iranian fertility collapse after 1986, falling from roughly 6.5 to below 2.5 within fifteen years. I judge this mostly event- and policy-driven: the 1989 family-planning law, the direct state campaigns, and the specific fatwas issued by identifiable clerics and ministers. That neighboring countries with comparable geography and economic profiles did not replicate the speed reinforces, for me, that long-duration modernization structures are not doing the work.

The pair in cases 4 and 5 is the one I keep returning to. Both are pre-industrial, agrarian, hydraulic empires facing comparable climate stress, yet I assign one to structure and one to events.

There is also one case on which I genuinely oscillate.

7. The differential impact of the 1783 Laki fissure eruption in Iceland across northwestern Europe in the 1780s. The eruption's climatic effects—crop failures, extreme winters, elevated mortality—were geographically widespread. In France, harvest failures contributed to the political crisis leading to 1789; in Britain, comparable harvest stress did not produce analogous institutional rupture. I cannot settle whether the divergence is structural—France's centralized grain-market architecture and absence of flexible poor relief versus Britain's more distributed system—or whether it turns on specific contingent decisions made by French ministers in 1788 and the particular accident of Necker's dismissal.

As a tentative repair, I have considered a principle of counterfactual inertia: an outcome belongs to the Braudelian level at which the relevant causal variables show the lowest counterfactual elasticity—specifically, if altering the decisions of identifiable agents within historically plausible bounds does not change the macro-outcome, the explanation is structural; if it does, it is event-level or individual. But I suspect this is either circular or too permissive, and I am unsure how it handles case 7.

What I want is not a lecture on Braudel but a reflective equilibrium exercise.

Please propose one principle, or a small competing set, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible. For each principle, state it precisely, identify the clause or condition that is motivated by each specific case above, and apply it to cases I have not considered: (a) the spread of the Black Death across Eurasia between 1346 and 1353—structural (trade-route geography, climate-driven rodent ecology) or event-driven (the siege of Caffa, the particular ship in Messina); and (b) the recurrence of resource-curse patterns in post-colonial economies—structural (geography of extraction, long-term exchange-rate dynamics) or event-driven (the specific policy choices of identifiable finance ministers).

Then, honestly state which of my verdicts—especially among cases 4, 5, 6, and 7—the principle cannot accommodate without overturning them, and whether saving every verdict requires a complexity that makes the principle unilluminating.
