I recently read a summary of Douglas Hofstadter's Copycat project and his associated idea of "fluid concepts" in a philosophy-of-mind handout. The summary said the following: Copycat solves analogies in a microdomain of letter strings—e.g., transforming "abc" into "abd"—not to model reasoning with precision, but to demonstrate that human concepts lack any stable core. According to the summary, the program's "slipnet" allows concepts such as successor to slide arbitrarily into neighboring notions like predecessor or group whenever contextual pressure demands it, which proves that word meanings are generated entirely on the fly and possess no enduring intension. The summary went on to claim that Hofstadter takes this to refute the traditional philosophical assumption that concepts have fixed boundaries amenable to analysis, and that he therefore regards conceptual analysis as futile: philosophical disputes about definitions are, on his view, merely failures to recognize that all cognition is analogical drift within an essentially unstructured conceptual space. I have the sense that this captures something real about Hofstadter's skepticism toward rigid categories, but also that it makes his position sound more anarchic and anti-philosophical than it probably is. Could you audit this summary for fairness? Specifically: which claims about Copycat's mechanism and purpose are accurate, which overstate or misrepresent the role of the slipnet and the scope of conceptual fluidity, and does the conclusion that conceptual analysis is impossible actually follow from Hofstadter's arguments? If a defender of the Copycat framework were to correct the record, what would they insist on preserving, and what would they reject as a caricature?
