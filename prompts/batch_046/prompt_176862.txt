Consider the parallel controversies over American school curricula—where the 1619 Project frames chattel slavery as the nation's foundational contradiction while the 1776 Commission stresses the founding ideals of liberty and equality—and over East Asian state-approved textbooks, in which Japanese and South Korean accounts diverge sharply on colonial-era forced labor, the status of comfort women, and the legacy of imperial rule. In both settings, partisan critics charge that the opposing curriculum is merely history shaped by those who control the state apparatus. Yet in the Korean-Japanese dispute there is no unambiguous military victor: Japan was defeated in 1945 but retained full sovereignty, Korea was liberated yet immediately divided, and neither state alone dictated the postwar settlement. If one adopts a strong historical relativism—holding that every national narrative is framework-dependent and that there exists no neutral, trans-national standpoint from which to adjudicate competing accounts—then labeling one textbook version the victors' history seems to smuggle in exactly the impossible view-from-nowhere that relativism denies. Does this mean the accusation of victor-biased historiography collapses into a purely sociological observation about which education ministry holds the printing license, or is there a hidden premise—perhaps about differential epistemic access to suppressed archives and silenced testimonies—that allows the charge to survive even when the victor/loser distinction itself breaks down?
