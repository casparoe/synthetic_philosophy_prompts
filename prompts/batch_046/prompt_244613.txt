Since I have recently begun assisting my doctoral supervisor with the comparative case classifications for her study on regime change, the boundary conditions of democratic backsliding have become personally urgent for me, because my own work in administrative law turns precisely on whether institutional decay requires formal constitutional amendment or can occur through regulatory capture and delegated suppression without ever altering the text of the constitution. Over the past several years she has rendered firm judgments on a range of regimes: she classifies Hungary since 2010 and Poland between 2015 and 2023 as clear instances of democratic backsliding, stressing in each instance that the governing party used its parliamentary majority to restructure constitutional courts, capture public broadcasting boards, and rewrite electoral administration rules; she treats Turkey after the 2016 purges not as backsliding but as completed competitive authoritarianism, on the ground that the erosion exceeded institutional restructuring and entered direct personalist repression with the near-elimination of meaningful electoral contestation; and she maintains that the United States at the federal level, despite the violence of January 6, 2021, and despite an expanding patchwork of state-level voting restrictions, does not qualify, because national electoral administration remains in the hands of career officials, a peaceful transfer of power occurred in 2021, and no central constitutional or statutory framework has been systematically captured. Now she asks me to apply her framework to a case that deliberately lacks these legislative markers: a long-established European constitutional democracy in which the current governing coalition has never amended the constitution, never enacted formal press restrictions, and never altered national electoral statutes, but has instead replaced the leadership of every public broadcasting authority and anti-corruption agency through existing statutory appointment powers, negotiated confidential algorithmic moderation agreements with dominant digital platforms that suppress opposition discourse without any legislative mandate, and permitted sub-national administrative bodies to tighten voter registration through discretionary interpretation of existing rules rather than through new national law. I need you to predict whether she will classify this regime as undergoing democratic backsliding, to identify the principle or principles that consistently explain her four earlier judgments, to set out at least one alternative rule that fits those same judgments equally well, and to specify the further hypothetical case that would best distinguish between them. I am especially interested in whether her framework turns on the presence of formal constitutional change, on the substantive erosion of horizontal accountability regardless of the mechanism employed, or on a threshold of demonstrable partisan intent to entrench power that can be satisfied even without legislative action.
