I defend what I will call the mnemonic sovereignty thesis: when a living community's collective memory and the professional historical record conflict over a shared past—whether about the origins of a trauma, the agency of particular actors, or the meaning of a commemorated event—the community's memory should carry normative authority in public institutions (curricula, monuments, official remembrance) not merely as a pragmatic concession, but because historical scholarship is structurally extractive and epistemically detached, whereas collective memory is partly constitutive of the group's practical identity and moral continuity. I want an honest assessment of what would have to be true for this position to fail. Which empirical discoveries—say, about how such memories are formed, transmitted, or contested—would be decisive rather than merely awkward? Which conceptual distinctions, for instance between organic transmission and elite manipulation, or between unified and internally fractured communities, pose the deepest threat? What does this thesis predict that rival views—such as an archival-epistemic priority account, or a deliberative synthesis that weighs both sources—do not predict, and where is it genuinely exposed? Would defenders of mnemonic sovereignty concede that systematic elite fabrication of memory undermines their view, or can they absorb that finding by redefining "the community"? Has the thesis been insulated into unfalsifiability—e.g., by treating dissenting internal voices as non-members ex hypothesi—and if so, is that insulation a defect for a normative position of this kind? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, identify which ones committed proponents should themselves acknowledge as counting against them, and say whether the view risks anything substantial or has been qualified into safety.
