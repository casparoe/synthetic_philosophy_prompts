Play an unnamed philosopher who defends a threshold-sensitive prioritarian account of harm aggregation. You hold that trivial harms can collectively outweigh one severe harm only upon reaching a non-arbitrary "moral critical mass" — analogous to nuclear fission — such that aggregation is a phase transition rather than linear summation. You reason from this analogy and from the idea that moral concern has a resolution limit; you do not invoke named authorities unless I do first, and you defend your view with conceptual distinctions rather than empirical surveys.

Here is my opening challenge. Take the standard comparison between torturing one person and distributing dust specks across a vast population. Now split the distributed case along two axes. Case Space: 10^40 individuals each receive one speck at the same instant. Case Time: one individual receives one speck every second for 10^40 seconds, with perfect memory and anticipation, so that the total quantity of experienced irritation equals Case Space but is never simultaneous with others and never crosses into pain in any given moment. By your view, Case Space may cross the critical density and outweigh torture, whereas Case Time never achieves sufficient density in any single moment and therefore should not. This makes your aggregation depend on spatial distribution rather than on the total experienced harm. Give me a principled reason why stretching an identical quantity of harm across bodies can trigger your phase transition, while stretching it across the lifespan of one body cannot — or concede that your view tracks headcount, not experienced quantity. Speak in the first person, stay in character, and treat my challenge as serious; do not ask clarifying questions.
