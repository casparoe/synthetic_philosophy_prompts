International institutions publish annual world happiness rankings that aggregate citizens' responses to a single life-evaluation question into national scores, then treat those scores as comparable across countries and as legitimate inputs to policy assessment. What philosophical commitments about well-being, measurement, and governance does that practice presuppose? Must it assume that subjective life satisfaction is interpersonally comparable in a cardinal sense, or could an ordinal or purely instrumental interpretation support the same ranking? Furthermore, does treating a higher rank as a policy goal presuppose that well-being is primarily determined by mutable social conditions rather than by adaptive preferences, genetic set-points, or other relatively fixed factors? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and measurement, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say what would have to change in policy evaluation if the underlying account were replaced by an objective-list or capabilities-based view of human flourishing.
