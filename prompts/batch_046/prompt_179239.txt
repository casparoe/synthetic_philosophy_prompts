I have recently taken on a coordinating role for a community health board’s ethics review committee, and a case we discussed last week has made the metaphysics behind our judgments impossible for me to ignore. A weekend patient deterioration was traced to an on-call nurse who did not escalate a borderline vital sign; the committee split sharply over whether her failure to call the physician was genuinely a cause of the harm, or merely a missed opportunity to prevent it. I am now preparing to facilitate a small seminar on the conceptual architecture of omission-causation, and I want a diagnostic decision-tree taxonomy that sorts actual positions in the debate—not by the labels philosophers attach to them, but by what a reasonable person would say about concrete cases.

The instrument should begin with a single root question about whether omissions can stand in causal relations at all, then branch through no more than six further binary or ternary diagnostic questions, each anchored in a specific scenario rather than in an abstract principle: for instance, a security guard who never activates an alarm that was never required by protocol; a physician who forgets to check a lab result that later proves abnormal; and a bystander who watches someone drown without throwing a nearby life preserver. Each terminal path should map onto one of eight named positions, including at minimum: a strict eliminativist view that denies omissions any causal status whatsoever; a normative-dependence view that grants causal power only when a duty, role, or explicit expectation is in force; a counterfactual-dependence view that treats omissions as causes solely in virtue of the outcome’s counterfactual dependence on the unperformed act; and a negative-event view that treats the omission as an actual absence with direct causal efficacy. Combinations of answers that do not correspond to any defended position should be flagged explicitly—whether they are incoherent, collapse into one of the named leaves, or remain merely unexplored—and the tree should note which pairs of leaves it cannot cleanly separate given the cases selected. Because the seminar participants will answer from reasoning and intuition alone, please do not include bibliographic prompts, citation requests, or references to specific texts they should consult; the classification should work entirely within the logical space the questions open up.
