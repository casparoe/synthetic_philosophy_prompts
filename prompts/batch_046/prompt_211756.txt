Here is an argument I have been turning over about republican liberty, and it ends somewhere I do not want to go. (1) Republican freedom, in the tradition of Pettit, is non-domination: the absence of arbitrary power to interfere, not merely the absence of actual interference. (2) A predictive risk-scoring system used by a state agency—say, for welfare eligibility—does not actually interfere with anyone's choices unless it triggers an audit or denial. (3) Nevertheless, the system gives officials an arbitrary capacity to target anyone whose score crosses an adjustable threshold; subjects live with that standing power. (4) So by (1) and (3), anyone subject to such scoring is dominated and therefore unfree in the republican sense, even if the score is never acted upon. (5) But the same reasoning applies to any statistical model that classifies citizens: tax-fraud detection, epidemiological tracking, even public-transit scheduling that allocates resources based on predicted demand. (6) If republican liberty condemns all of these, then a modern administrative state can never achieve republican freedom for its citizens, which makes the concept useless for institutional design. (7) I want to keep (1) and reject (6), so I think one of (2)–(5) must fail. Where exactly does the slide happen? Is there a principled way to distinguish arbitrary domination from benign administrative capacity, or does the republican have to bite a very large bullet? Please take a committed position: identify the specific step you reject, defend that choice with reasons rather than surveying all options, and state clearly what cost that rejection imposes on the republican view of freedom.
