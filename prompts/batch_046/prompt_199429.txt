Before responding, please think step-by-step and briefly outline the main philosophical tensions between predictive accuracy and explanatory realism, especially as these arise in debates around Milton Friedman’s claim that unrealistic assumptions are harmless for prediction yet damaging for explanation — the position philosophers of economics often call the F-twist.

Rank the following five philosophical defenses of deliberately false modeling assumptions on a scale of 1 (weakest) to 10 (strongest), judging each by how well it survives the F-twist inversion — that is, by how well it preserves both predictive utility and some form of explanatory transparency when the micro-level premises are known to be false: (1) strict instrumentalism, which denies that successful models must represent their targets at all; (2) the as-if defense, which holds that false assumptions are harmless heuristics so long as agents behave as if they were true; (3) robustness analysis, which justifies unrealistic premises by showing that macro-results persist across structurally different models; (4) representational minimalism, which demands correspondence only at the level of aggregate outputs, not micro-mechanisms; and (5) constrained counterfactualism — a framing introduced here — which treats a model as a fiction whose false premises are bounded by empirical constraints on boundary conditions rather than by direct correspondence to internal processes.

For each stance, state its score and, in a single sentence, name the precise philosophical liability that keeps it from a perfect 10. If you judge that the task rests on a confused premise — for instance, if the F-twist makes it impossible in principle for any stance to serve both predictive and explanatory aims simultaneously, or if constrained counterfactualism is incoherent as defined — say so plainly and refuse to force a ranking.
