I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has begun reading systematically in philosophy—mostly ethics and the philosophy of life—though I still think with the habits of a scientist and tend to be suspicious of claims that sound more like metaphysical assertion than like distinctions that could be tested or clarified. At a recent meeting of a local philosophy circle, a fellow member summarized Moritz Schlick’s essay on the meaning of life in the following terms. He said that Schlick argues life has no purpose whatsoever—neither a divine plan nor an evolutionary function—and that asking for the “meaning” of life is therefore a category mistake unless we distinguish meaning sharply from purpose. Meaning, on this account, is not an objective property of living beings but a by-product of play: activity pursued for its own sake rather than for any external goal. The speaker went further and claimed that Schlick derives this distinction from evolutionary biology, specifically from the idea that play is surplus energy remaining after survival needs are met, and that Schlick uses this biological observation to argue that all genuinely meaningful human activities—art, friendship, even scientific curiosity—are literally forms of play in the zoological sense. He concluded that Schlick’s position therefore implies that any activity pursued for instrumental reasons, including moral obligation or research aimed at practical benefit, is necessarily devoid of meaning, and that this is why Schlick rejects both traditional morality and religious purpose.

Something about this summary strikes me as both illuminating and suspiciously tidy, and I would like an accuracy audit before I accept it. Which parts align with what Schlick actually argued? Where does the speaker conflate Schlick’s own philosophical claims with ideas he borrowed or discussed—such as the evolutionary surplus-energy theory of play common in Schlick’s era? Is it fair to say that Schlick denies all purpose to life, or does he keep purpose and meaning conceptually separate in a more nuanced way? And does his view genuinely entail that instrumental or duty-driven activity is meaningless, or is that an overstatement that makes his position easier to dismiss? I am particularly interested in whether the biological framing is faithful to Schlick’s text, or whether it turns a philosophical argument into something closer to a scientific reduction. Please go through the summary point by point, separating what a careful reader of Schlick would accept, what they would correct, and whether the speaker’s final conclusion—that Schlick’s view undermines moral commitment and practical science—actually survives those corrections.
