Next Thursday I am presenting to our department's Renaissance political thought seminar a thesis about Machiavelli's treatment of fortune and virtù in The Prince: specifically, that virtù should be understood not as a stable dispositional trait or a set of civic virtues tempered by necessity, but as a performative, situational mode of responsiveness that collapses the distinction between ethical character and strategic simulation. On this reading, the familiar beast-and-man analogy in the central chapters does not merely license occasional deception by an otherwise intact moral agent; rather, it implies that virtù just is the capacity to adapt one's public persona with such fluidity to fortuna's shifts that there is no underlying "true" self being concealed. Consequently, the standard civic-humanist framing—according to which Machiavelli advises republican leaders to preserve genuine moral integrity while accommodating political necessity—fundamentally misreads the text, because the accommodation is total and what looks like retained integrity is in fact sustained theatrical control.

The audience will be precisely the kind that can damage this argument without ever being unfair. There will be three senior intellectual historians who read Machiavelli firmly within the civic-humanist tradition and who treat any vocabulary of "performance" or "theatricality" as an anachronistic importation of Goffman into early modern Italy; one analytic philosopher of action, skeptical of all intentionalist history of philosophy, who will ask whether I am attributing to Machiavelli a theory of agency he could not have possessed; a graduate student in early modern rhetoric who knows the theatrical metaphors scattered through the text better than I do and will test whether my claim is more than a redescriptive gloss on passages already well catalogued; and several normative political theorists who will care less about textual fidelity than about whether this reading renders Machiavelli useless for contemporary ethical reflection. Their commitments are not uniform, but they share a resistance to interpretations that dissolve moral psychology into strategy.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face in that room—not the ones I would get from a rationalist forum or from a general philosophy audience, but the ones these specific interlocutors will raise. Order them by the combined probability they will be voiced and the damage they could do to my position.
