I've been trying to extract the underlying principle behind my intuitions about personal location across brain-transplant and distributed-cognition scenarios, in the spirit of Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle but pushing into architectures that don't appear in the standard literature. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases, mostly sensible but with at least one tension I can't resolve:

1. Classic single-copy transplant: my brain is scanned, the original destroyed, and one copy is placed in a new body. I locate myself with the copy. Psychological continuity dominates biological continuity.

2. Gradual neuron replacement: silicon analogues replace my neurons one at a time over years without loss of consciousness. I remain in the same body throughout; there is no jump-point.

3. Corpus callosotomy with dual embodiment: each hemisphere is placed in a separate body. I think neither resulting person is strictly me; survival as a single subject fails, though both inherit my memories.

4. Simultaneous dual-copy awakening: two perfect copies wake up from the same scan. I think neither is me; we have two successors and no survivor.

5. Biological brain in vat, prosthetic body: my brain stays alive in a vat, and my body is controlled remotely by a radio-linked prosthetic brain in the skull. I feel I am in the vat—the biological substrate generating experience is the locus—even though my actions are executed elsewhere.

6. Passive shadow architecture: my brain continuously uploads a real-time functional mirror to a server, but the server is purely passive. If the biological brain dies and the server is activated, I think I am dead; the shadow was never a locus of consciousness.

7. Narrative-center extraction: only the small network responsible for my first-person narrative—something like a self-referential symbolic loop—is moved to a server and kept synchronized with the rest of my brain, which remains in my body. If the server fails, my body continues with intact cognition but reports feeling like no one is home. If the body dies but the server persists, it reports being me, but without sensory input. I want to say I am distributed across both, yet if forced to choose I lean bodily—unless the server begins initiating actions independently, in which case I think there are now two subjects.

Cases 1 and 4 seem to hinge on uniqueness: one copy survives, two do not. But case 3 also involves uniqueness (one hemisphere per body) yet I deny survival, which suggests uniqueness isn't enough; there must also be some integration or continuity of the narrative loop. Meanwhile case 5 privileges the biological substrate, while case 7 allows part of the loop to be elsewhere, yet I still lean bodily—unless independence occurs. That feels ad hoc.

What precise principle—perhaps framed in terms of continuity of a self-referential loop, uniqueness of its functional instantiation, and integration with a sensory-motor stream—captures cases 1 through 4 and either accommodates or forces revision of 5 through 7? Or does saving all seven require an irreducibly patchwork account?

And for a case I haven't considered: suppose replacement is gradual, but the replacement substrate is not local silicon; instead, it is a globally distributed cloud computation that maintains real-time coherence with the remaining biological tissue, with no single physical locus. Where am I in that architecture, and which of my verdicts above does that prediction contradict?
