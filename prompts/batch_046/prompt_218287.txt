I'm trying to identify whether there's an established term for the following pattern. A person acquires good evidence that their judgment in some domain is systematically flawed—say, that experts like them routinely overestimate the stability of early findings, or that framing effects distort their risk assessments. Rather than lowering confidence across the board, they deploy this meta-awareness selectively. For conclusions they want to preserve, they treat knowledge of the bias as proof they've "already corrected" for it, making those beliefs seem especially well-founded. For conclusions they want to reject—especially those held by rivals—they cite the exact same bias as a reason for dismissal. The result is that the higher-order warning doesn't function as a general correction mechanism; it becomes a partitioning tool that shields favored beliefs and disqualifies others, leaving the person's overall confidence structure largely intact but now wrapped in a layer of self-reference. I know this touches on cases where one believes P while believing one's belief in P is unreliable, and on how higher-order evidence is supposed to work, but it doesn't match the standard picture of being torn or akratic, since here the person feels more justified, not less. Does this specific configuration—using awareness of one's own reasoning flaws as a defensive filter rather than a universal corrective—have a name in the literature? If so, how is it distinguished from ordinary motivated reasoning and from the standard form of epistemic akrasia? And if not, what would be the precise boundaries that separate it from both?
