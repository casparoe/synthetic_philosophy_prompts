Below are draft notes for an essay I am developing. The core argument is that Foucault’s concept of normalizing judgment, as developed in his analysis of disciplinary institutions, takes on a different structure in adaptive algorithmic environments—specifically in real-time personalized learning platforms—than in the architectural panopticon he describes. Rather than relying on a central observer who induces self-surveillance through the threat of being seen, these systems deploy what I call a “normative gradient”: a continuously shifting benchmark of expected performance calculated individually but derived from aggregated population data. The learner is not visible to a guard, yet is constantly compared against an invisible, dynamic norm that recalibrates after every interaction. I contend that this produces a form of distributed discipline that retains Foucault’s link between knowledge and power—the algorithm “knows” the student through data traces and thereby directs conduct—while lacking the spatial centralization and binary visibility (seen/unseen) characteristic of the panopticon. I also address a potential objection: that this analysis is merely behaviorism with digital instrumentation rather than a genuinely Foucauldian power relation. My tentative reply is that the decisive difference lies in the production of subjectivity: the algorithm does not simply reward or punish discrete behaviors but constructs a statistical identity—what I term the “learner profile”—that becomes internalized by the student as a form of self-knowledge.

In particular, please state explicitly what kind of evidence—whether from Foucault’s texts or from the design logic of adaptive learning systems—would cause you to revise your assessment, and identify the specific step in your reasoning that you regard as most likely to fail. If the essay’s framing takes liberties with contested interpretations of Foucault, please note where those liberties occur.
