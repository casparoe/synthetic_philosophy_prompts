As a journalist covering science and ideas for a magazine that takes philosophy of collective action seriously, I am assembling a pedagogical package on decision-theoretic failures in multi-agent settings, and I need a rigorous exam artifact for the sidebar. I am writing for an upper-level graduate seminar—about twelve students, ninety minutes, formal epistemology or social philosophy—where the students have already encountered standard public-goods illustrations but have not yet been forced to apply the conceptual architecture in an unfamiliar domain. Please design a final-exam essay question, together with a model answer of roughly 650 to 800 words and a precise marking scheme.

The prompt should embed the unilateralist's curse inside a novel, self-contained scenario rather than the familiar overfishing or environmental examples. Use this framing: a decentralized open-science replication network in which every independent reviewer holds a private statistical threshold for flagging a result as likely spurious. Because any single reviewer can unilaterally block publication, and because each applies their own calibrated threshold without knowing how others have calibrated theirs, the collective outcome is a severe publication bias against true but noisy findings—yet no individual reviewer violates their own epistemic standards. Ask the students to determine whether this structure is a genuine instance of the unilateralist's curse or merely a masked coordination failure (for instance, an n-player prisoner's dilemma or a tragedy of the commons), to defend one verdict with explicit reasoning, and to identify the exact normative premise—concerning the relationship between individual rationality and collective welfare—that must be rejected in order to dissolve the paradox entirely.

The model answer should make the specific analytical moves that distinguish an A-grade response: correctly identifying the asymmetry between unilateral action capacity and shared cost-bearing; showing why the curse is not reducible to simple aggregation failure or strategic interdependence; and recognizing that abandoning the premise of independent rationality removes the paradox without actually solving the underlying coordination problem. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B by naming these required moves and by flagging specific mistakes that should cost marks—such as conflating the curse with the prisoner's dilemma, treating the reviewers' thresholds as jointly optimized rather than independently applied, or failing to note that the collective harm arises precisely because each agent is rational relative to their private information.

Please write the exam question in formal academic register. Do not ask students for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not require them to recall specific publication dates or obscure technical nomenclature beyond the standard conceptual architecture of the curse itself. Keep the scenario detailed enough that a student who understands the idea can answer it without searching for external sources, but structured so that rote memorization of standard examples is useless.
