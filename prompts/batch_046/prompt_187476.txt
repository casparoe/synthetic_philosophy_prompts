I serve on the Joint Ethics Oversight Board of the Trans-Pacific Research Consortium, a binational review body with Dutch and Japanese partner institutions that sets evaluation standards for cross-disciplinary grant applications. We face a binding decision before the next funding cycle: should our peer-review protocol mandate “radical directness,” in which reviewers must state methodological criticisms in unvarnished language and applicants are instructed not to interpret bluntness as personal disrespect, or should it mandate “structured indirectness,” in which negative assessments must be framed through contextual qualification and applicants agree not to infer hidden praise from omission?

The dispute was triggered by a concrete case. A senior Dutch reviewer told a junior Japanese applicant that her statistical model was conceptually bankrupt and should be abandoned, prompting the applicant to withdraw her proposal and cite psychological harm. Japanese board members argue that the review violated a duty of face-preserving honesty—that strategic softening is not deception but respect—while Dutch members counter that diluting the critique would have constituted a white lie that misled both the applicant and the funding committee about the proposal’s actual merit. Half the board supports a universal directness mandate with cultural-sensitivity disclaimers; half supports an indirectness protocol with explicit translation rules for negative feedback.

Draft the board’s formal policy memo recommending one of the three options—radical directness, structured indirectness, or a hybrid framework with operational criteria—for adoption in the upcoming cycle. The memo should provide: (1) a concrete, vote-ready recommendation with enforceable language criteria reviewers must apply; (2) reasoning that engages the philosophical question of whether white lies or strategic omissions in professional evaluation are morally wrong, treating considerations of autonomy, epistemic fairness, and respect for persons without treating any named philosophical doctrine as an authoritative citation; (3) an honest and substantive treatment of the strongest objection to your recommendation, specifically the charge that it either institutionalizes deception or institutionalizes cruelty; and (4) a fair implementation timeline and an appeals mechanism for applicants who believe the protocol was misapplied. Write it as a polished institutional document that both partner institutions could circulate.
