I am drafting a historical novel set in twelfth-century Baghdad, and in shaping the philosophical interior of my protagonist—a jurist drawn to kalām—I have become persuaded by a reconstructed occasionalism that I want to put in his mouth with intellectual honesty. The position is this: not merely that fire fails to burn cotton by any intrinsic nature of its own, but that the very concept of a “natural power” is an illegitimate reification of observed correlation; that the contingency of every conjunction is metaphysically prior to any law-like structure; and that what we call causation is in reality a sustained lattice of direct divine instantiations regular enough to ground scientific prediction yet carrying no modal force independent of that will. I take this to be more parsimonious than modern causal realism, which appears to smuggle necessity into the world by treating dispositional properties as primitive and then leaving unexplained why those dispositions should be active rather than inert.

Before I commit my protagonist to this view, I need a map of its genuine exposure. What would count decisively against it—not just the familiar Humean problem of induction, but which formal results in the philosophy of science, which metaphysical commitments about mental causation, and which structural features of successful counterfactual theories it cannot replicate without abandoning its parsimony? Would a compelling account of intentional action, in which mental states appear to structure physical outcomes without an intermediary law, force occasionalism into an implausible multiplication of particular divine interventions? Does modern structural realism, or the dispositional essentialist claim that powers are metaphysically primitive, offer a rival that captures occasionalism’s economy without its theological cost—and if so, does that comparison reveal a hidden vulnerability rather than a mere alternative? Please rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward; distinguish what a committed occasionalist would concede as genuinely damaging from what they would dismiss as resting on a question-begging assumption that powers are real; and say whether the view is well-insulated by qualifications or whether it pays a real explanatory price for its parsimony. I want an honest assessment of how exposed this reconstructed position actually is.
