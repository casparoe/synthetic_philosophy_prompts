In my graduate seminars on philosophy of mind, I have observed that the debate over mental causation—specifically the causal exclusion argument and the non-reductive physicalist responses—has reached a state of refined stagnation that puzzles me. The exclusionist maintains that if every physical effect of a mental cause has a sufficient physical cause, then mental properties are either redundant or epiphenomenal; the non-reductive physicalist responds by invoking realization, proportionality, or non-causal determination to preserve mental efficacy without violating the causal closure of the physical domain. Over decades, both sides have developed elaborate taxonomies of dependence relations—supervenience, grounding, realization, and their variants—and have addressed overdetermination, downward causation, and the distinction between causal relevance and causal efficacy. Yet the core dispute remains immobile: each new technical refinement on one side is matched by an accommodating refinement on the other, and the fundamental intuition that mental properties either genuinely cause bodily movements or do not remains intact.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily a metaphysical disagreement about ontology, but rather a disagreement about what counts as an adequate causal explanation—specifically, whether explanation requires that cited properties make an independent difference-making contribution, or whether it is sufficient that mental properties systematically organize and predict physical processes in ways that license interventionist reasoning and practical agency. I wonder whether the sides are, in effect, answering different questions—one about the metaphysics of production, one about the logic of explanatory practice—under the single heading "mental causation." Alternatively, there may be hidden empirical assumptions about neural implementation, or bedrock intuitions about causal closure and agency that function as unargued premises.

Is there a hidden formal question—perhaps about the logic of exclusion relations across different accounts of causation—that is doing the work?
