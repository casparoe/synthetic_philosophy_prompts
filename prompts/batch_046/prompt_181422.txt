I'm currently in an advanced seminar on nineteenth-century liberalism and colonial administration, and we're examining the fracture in John Stuart Mill's political philosophy between his universalist defense of individual liberty and his practical defense of imperial tutelage. For our closing discussion, I'd like to commission a poem—specifically, a single poem of about 28 lines in blank verse with a decisive volta near line 14—that enacts this fracture structurally rather than merely describing it. The first half should unfold in the register of Mill's liberal universalism: self-protection, the harm principle, the claim that liberty belongs to rational beings as such, rendered with the formal confidence of an axiom meant for all humanity. The volta should not be an external accusation but an internal pivot: the introduction of the colonial exception—those societies deemed backward or not yet capable of representative self-government, requiring what Mill considered benevolent despotic guidance—delivered in the same authoritative voice. The second half should then show how that exception does not sit alongside the principle but dissolves it from within: the conditions of tutelage redefine who counts as the kind of being to whom liberty applies, so that the universal claim collapses into its opposite. I want the form to make this audible—perhaps through a shift in rhythm or syntax at the volta—so that a reader familiar with Mill's India writings recognizes the argument, while the poem remains comprehensible and formally coherent to someone who has never read them. Please avoid direct quotation of any specific text, and do not include citations, bibliographic notes, or references to secondary scholarship. The poem should feel like an internal monologue in which the logic of liberty and the logic of empire share one voice, and the blank-verse structure should make their identity and contradiction audible at once.
