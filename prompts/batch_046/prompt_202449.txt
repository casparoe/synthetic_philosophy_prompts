Write a dialogue between two philosophers—call them Mara and Eli—who are both fluent in collective action theory and neither is a strawman. They are debating whether the inefficacy objection genuinely dissolves individual obligations in large-scale harms like climate change and meat consumption. Mara argues that when harm is mediated through institutional supply chains and democratic aggregation, individual actions are causally redundant; she specifically challenges "tipping point" defenses by noting that market elasticity usually absorbs marginal changes unless coordination is already present, making any obligation conditional on an implausible prior. Eli accepts causal redundancy for direct physical effects but distinguishes "causal contribution" from "normative reinforcement": he holds that visible, substitutable abstention can shift social equilibria by altering expectations, though only if it avoids moral licensing and targets practices with coordination potential—say, professional air travel norms rather than dispersed dietary choices. Mara responds that this makes obligation depend on contingent social psychology rather than principle, and asks whether Eli's view collapses into a demanding virtue ethics of constant visibility-calibration. Eli denies this, proposing a "threshold-sensitive complicity" framework: one should not participate in collectively destructive practices when one's participation is both observable and substitutable, regardless of marginal causal impact, but this weakens as institutional mediation becomes opaque. Mara then presses him on the opacity criterion—does it excuse investment in fossil-fuel-linked markets while condemning direct consumption?—and whether he can distinguish complicity from association without smuggling causal reasoning back in. Have them actually respond to each other's points rather than talking past one another, and keep the exchange focused and rigorous.
