I serve on the advisory board of a mid-sized regional land-use authority, and lately we have faced a recurring pattern: well-meaning staff and elected members propose to abolish or radically streamline long-standing procedural mechanisms—paper-based public-notice requirements, redundant approval signatures, mandatory in-person hearings for minor zoning variances—without being able to state what problem the existing form was originally designed to solve. In a course I am taking on conservative political philosophy I have encountered Chesterton’s fence and Burke’s defense of prescription, but I am struggling to see how either can be turned into an actual board protocol rather than a rhetorical posture. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, evidentiary thresholds, burden-of-proof rules, and explicit stop-points—grounded in Burkean conservatism and Chesterton’s fence, that my board can run on any proposal to dismantle an institutional practice whose original rationale is now obscure or forgotten.

The procedure should distinguish rigorously between “this mechanism serves no purpose” and “we have lost sight of its purpose”; specify what kind of evidence is required to justify removal and who bears the burden of producing it; identify how to detect unintended but valuable functions (for instance, a redundant sign-off that accidentally prevents conflicts of interest, or a delay that allows latent opposition to surface); and clearly flag the points at which the remaining disagreement is one of values rather than institutional knowledge, so the board knows to hand the decision back to democratic judgment rather than disguising a political choice as technical administration. Then apply it to three concrete proposals: replacing all in-person hearings for small-scale permits with digital submissions (which staff strongly prefer and neighborhood groups defend); digitizing and destroying the original paper archive of zoning variances granted since 1950; and eliminating a mandatory thirty-day public-comment window that was instituted after a 1970s corruption scandal but is now criticized as pure bureaucratic delay. Include at least one case in which the procedure instructs the board to preserve the fence despite the majority’s strong preference to remove it, and state plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
