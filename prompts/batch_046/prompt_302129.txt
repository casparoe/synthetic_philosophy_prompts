I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level graduate seminar on moral psychology and the architecture of practical reason, and I need an essay question that forces students to work inside Bernard Williams's integrity objection—specifically the problem of one thought too many, in which treating a ground project as requiring explicit moral justification undermines the commitment that gives a life its shape—without letting them retreat to the familiar drowning-spouse illustration. To inject the necessary difficulty, the question should present a constructed institutional case: Dr. Elena Vasquez, a senior archivist who has sustained for thirty years a ground project of preserving documentary evidence of state violence against her indigenous community, now faces a statutory destruction order for a specific tranche of records that would protect current officials. She considers refusing, but recognizes that if she frames her refusal as the claim that historical justice outweighs legal obedience, she has already adopted a universalizing stance that treats her partial attachment as one competing value among others. The exam should ask students to analyze whether a refusal motivated solely by the ground project itself—without prior moral calculus—escapes the one-thought-too-many problem, and whether her institutional role as a sworn, neutrality-bound public archivist complicates or nullifies Williams's analysis.

Please write the exam prompt itself, then provide a model answer of approximately 700 words, and conclude with a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five must-hit analytical moves. The scheme should specify the marks allocated to each move and include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail that point—for example, conflating integrity with mere partiality, misidentifying the structural feature that makes explicit justification self-undermining, mishandling the institutional-role complication, or collapsing Williams's position into either a consequentialist or a Kantian framework. The question should be answerable in about 90 minutes and should be designed so that a student who has only memorized the standard summary of Williams's view cannot earn more than a passing grade.
