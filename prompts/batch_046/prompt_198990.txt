Write the full specification of desiderata that any adequate theory of distributed first-person persistence—whether an “I” can genuinely span multiple brains—must satisfy, with the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind each requirement, and flag which are non-negotiable versus camp-specific preferences. The theory should be evaluated against its ability to handle not only the familiar Hofstadter-style pattern-copying scenario, in which a self-like loop is reproduced on a new substrate, but also a more strained configuration I will call the three-node asymmetry: Brain A retains direct experiential access and first-person authority; Brain B holds only reconstructed, causally downstream memory traces that mirror A’s past states; and Brain C runs predictive models of A’s responses with no direct causal or experiential link to either. Your list must cover: (1) the null single-brain baseline that any distributed account must not violate; (2) conditions under which copying produces continuity, duplication, or fission rather than a shared “I”; (3) how partial overlap or merging of mental content across substrates should be adjudicated; (4) whether asymmetrical nodes (direct, reconstructed, predictive) can all fall within one first-person boundary, and if so what determines membership; (5) indexical coherence—how singular first-person pronouns can refer without ambiguity when distributed, including the problem of “indexical leakage” where one brain’s “I” grammatically targets experiences occurring in another; (6) whether phenomenological continuity is required for distributed persistence, or whether functional/narrative integration suffices; (7) the distinction between having self-status and having a particular degree of self-status, so that the theory does not collapse differences in authority or access into an all-or-nothing judgment; (8) consistency with known neuroscience of consciousness and integration (e.g., no dependence on physically impossible synchronization), without being held hostage to every unsettled empirical detail; (9) resolution of the no-privileged-node problem—if no single brain is the “real” seat of the self, how first-person authority is allocated or shared; and (10) explicit identification of any subset of these desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable, with the tension explained (for instance, between singular indexical reference and full symmetry across nodes, or between biological realism and unrestricted distribution). For each desideratum, state the consideration that makes it a genuine constraint rather than a prejudice of one camp, and mark which are preferences (e.g., determinism about mental content, preservation of bodily continuity, rejection of phenomenology). Then, briefly and without citation lists, score how a Hofstadterian pattern-persistence view, a narrative-identity account, a global-workspace model extended across brains, and an eliminativist “single-brain-only” position fare against your list—focusing on which desideratum eliminates the most candidates and where the deepest unresolved tension lies. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scoring should be subordinate.
