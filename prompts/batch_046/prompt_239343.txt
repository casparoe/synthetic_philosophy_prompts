I'm planning a single 75-minute workshop for philosophy undergraduates who have completed basic propositional logic but have not yet studied modality. I want to teach the indicative/counterfactual distinction without relying on the standard weather-and-match examples. Instead, the carrying case should be a single ambiguous sentence form—"If the defendant lied, the jury convicted" versus "If the defendant had lied, the jury would have convicted"—set in a legal-reasoning context where the speaker genuinely does not know whether the antecedent holds. Plan the explanation: what must be introduced first (the grammatical ambiguity or the epistemic commitment?), what this legal example must display and what it must suppress so students do not reduce the difference to tense alone, the predictable misconceptions—especially the collapse of both forms into generic "hypothetical reasoning"—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. Include one end-of-session diagnostic question that forces students to identify which reading is operative in a fresh case with identical surface grammar but a different speaker epistemic state. Also specify which elements of the standard similarity-semantics framework should be explicitly withheld at this stage and why.
