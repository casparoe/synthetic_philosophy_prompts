I'm planning a single fifty-minute session for first-year philosophy students on Leibniz's claim that justice is essentially charity guided by wisdom—love ordered by rational insight into perfection—rather than divine command, social contract, or sentiment. The students will arrive thinking Leibniz is only the "best of all possible worlds" optimist who therefore cannot take moral evil seriously, and they will assume that any ethical appeal to "love" must be either religious sentiment or disguised consequentialism. Design the explanatory arc: what concept must be secured first so that justice-as-charity sounds coherent rather than naive, and why the metaphysics of rational perfection should come after rather than before that ethical framing; choose one carrying example that distinguishes Leibnizian justice from modern distributive justice without collapsing it into utilitarian calculation; map the predictable misconceptions—especially "this is just benevolence with a God-figure" and "wisdom here just means being smart about outcomes"—indicating exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted; propose one closing question that reveals whether students grasp the structural constraint that wisdom places on love, as opposed to having memorized a slogan; and explicitly state what should be left out at this level, such as the Monadology's metaphysical grounding of harmony, with a brief justification for the omission.
