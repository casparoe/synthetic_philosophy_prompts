I've been working through some philosophy-of-mind material with a small discussion group—mostly people who've read the rationalist literature on artificial intelligence and representation—and we've hit a recurring snag about whether teleosemantic theories of mental content can be extended to engineered systems without collapsing into mere instrumentalism. To help me think it through, I'd like you to write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers debating this in the weeds.

One should defend a classical biosemantic position, roughly along Millikan's line: mental content is grounded in evolutionary selection history, so misrepresentation is literally the failure of a cognitive mechanism to perform the proper function for which it was selected, and this normative dimension cannot be reconstructed from design specifications alone. The other should defend a synthetic teleosemantic view—call it engineered functional normalization—according to which internally assigned tracking jobs, evaluated against architectural norms and stabilized by feedback loops, can ground genuine misrepresentation even in systems with no evolutionary ancestry, provided there is a real gap between assigned proper function and actual performance.

Set their exchange around a concrete engineered scenario: a vision-language model fine-tuned for radiological screening that consistently classifies benign shadow artifacts in lung CT scans as early-stage carcinomas, not because of random noise but because its feature-extraction layers have developed a stable but environmentally mismatched mapping. The biosemanticist should argue that calling this "misrepresentation" is a category error—the model has no proper functions, only statistical weights—while the synthetic theorist should reply that the fine-tuning protocol itself established a normative architecture: specific convolutional filters were assigned the job of tracking tissue density, and the system's own loss-gradient updates function analogously to selection pressure, generating a genuine gap between what those states are supposed to do and what they actually do. Have them respond directly to each other: the biosemanticist should challenge whether gradient descent can constitute selection in the relevant normative sense, and the synthetic theorist should answer by distinguishing selection-for-reproduction from selection-for-stability within a functional economy. Keep both voices sharp and non-strawman, avoid asking for citations or bibliographies, and make the disagreement substantive rather than verbal—by the end they should still disagree, but each should have incorporated a point from the other.
