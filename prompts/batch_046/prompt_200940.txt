I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on twentieth-century political economy and the philosophy of value. I need a single essay question that tests whether students can do more than summarize Keynes's argument in his essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren; they must apply his conceptual distinction between absolute and relative needs to a scenario he never addressed, without relying on memorized biographical detail. The scenario is a post-scarcity society governed by an AI allocator that eliminates all material scarcity—thereby satisfying every absolute need—while simultaneously operating an aspirational benchmarking system that continuously exposes citizens to micro-variations of consumption patterns just above their own, manufacturing new relative needs in real time. The question should ask students to determine whether this society has achieved Keynes's vision of the good life, or whether the mechanism of manufactured relative need reveals that the love of money has been structurally replaced by an equivalent insatiability that undermines the good life from within.

Please write:
1. The exam question itself, specifying a 90-minute response of approximately 1,200 words. Instruct students not to provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, but instead to reconstruct Keynes's argument from its conceptual structure and apply it directly to the scenario.
2. A model answer of roughly 800 words that demonstrates the required reasoning: correctly identifying that Keynes treats relative needs as insatiable in principle rather than merely difficult to satisfy; explaining why Keynes tied this insatiability to the economic problem of growth and accumulation rather than to psychological manipulation alone; analyzing whether the AI scenario collapses the boundary between satisfying needs and generating them; and evaluating whether Keynes's good life—defined by the pursuit of art, friendship, and leisure—can survive when the machinery of relative need is internalized and algorithmically personalized.
3. A marking scheme totaling 20 marks, with explicit point allocations for each of the four argumentative moves listed above. The scheme should separate an A answer (16–20) from a B answer (13–15) by noting that A answers recognize the structural parallel between monetary accumulation and algorithmic aspiration without reducing Keynes's argument to a simple anti-consumerist moralism, whereas B answers tend to conflate Keynes's good life with mere leisure or treat all relative needs as morally illegitimate regardless of context. Include one-line descriptions of the specific errors that should cost marks: for example, misreading Keynes as claiming that all desire beyond subsistence is corrupt; failing to distinguish the economic problem of production from the problem of endogenous need-creation; or asserting that the AI scenario proves Keynes wrong without addressing whether his framework anticipates such personalized, non-monetary insatiability.
