You are delivering the opening address at a joint ceremony honoring the retirement of a senior dynamical-systems researcher and inaugurating a new interdisciplinary center on predictability and practical reasoning. The audience is split between mathematicians and philosophers of science, who will catch sloppy analogies, and family members, administrators, and students from other fields, who will not. The speech should run about seven minutes and must be structured — not merely decorated — by a precise conceptual distinction within deterministic chaos: the difference between trajectory-level divergence, driven by positive Lyapunov exponents and rendering long-range point prediction impossible for any embedded observer, and system-level robustness, or structural stability of invariant sets, which allows qualitative behavior to persist despite parameter perturbations. Use this distinction to shape what you can and cannot claim about human agency, collective planning, and moral responsibility: reject both the fatalist cliché that outcomes are already fixed and the romantic cliché that unpredictability licenses arbitrary hope. Instead, argue that rational action should aim not at forecasting specific trajectories but at preserving or shifting the structural conditions — the attractors and their basins — within which desirable dynamics remain stable. This framing should determine the speech's architecture, including which comforting platitudes are available and which are forbidden. Write for the mixed audience: technically serious enough that experts recognize the distinction is being used correctly, and fully intelligible to those without mathematics. Do not include citations, references, a bibliography, or any verbatim quotations from real texts; paraphrase without quotation marks if you invoke a specific idea.
