I spent three decades in experimental condensed-matter physics before retiring into philosophy, and I am now trying to determine whether the St. Petersburg paradox exposes a genuine failure of expected-utility theory or is neutralized once physical realizability conditions are taken seriously. Most resolutions invoke bounded utility or risk aversion, but I want to focus on the open questions that remain if we accept that no physically implemented betting mechanism can realize unbounded payoffs—given, for instance, information-storage limits in any finite spatial region. Specifically, among the unresolved issues—whether non-expected-utility frameworks genuinely dissolve the paradox without ad hoc restrictions, whether the resolution demands a substantive axiological theory rather than mere risk-weighting, and whether formal analogues in quantum measurement theory (where expectation values can diverge in certain limiting procedures) illuminate the normative problem—which lines of work should I treat as upstream? If I have about a year to study and want to understand whether physical realizability should constrain normative decision theory at all, please rank these directions by their dependency relations, tractability with current logical and mathematical tools, and potential to change the broader debate. Defend the ordering, and indicate which answers would leave the others untouched.
