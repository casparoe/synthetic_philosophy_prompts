Compare the canonical form of expected-value fanaticism—where a non-zero credence in an outcome of arbitrarily large utility dominates all bounded alternatives—to its under-discussed mirror image, which one might call negative fanaticism: a decision rule in which any non-vanishing risk of unbounded disutility overwhelms all other considerations. Are the two structurally symmetric within standard von Neumann–Morgenstern frameworks, or do asymmetries in risk-attitude, credence assignment, or the epistemology of extreme outcomes break the parallel? Furthermore, assess whether both can be neutralized by the same theoretical remedy—such as bounded utility, risk-weighting, or a principled threshold on actionable probabilities—or whether negative fanaticism demands a distinct treatment because of how catastrophic risk is evaluated in practice. I am particularly interested in whether the philosophical literature's near-exclusive focus on the positive case obscures a more fundamental problem for rational choice under existential risk.
