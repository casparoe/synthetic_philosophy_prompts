I cover the history of ideas and their migration across disciplines, and I am currently developing a feature on whether historians of political thought unconsciously borrow epistemic frameworks from the quantitative sciences they read about. In conversations with colleagues, I have been struck by how the Whig interpretation—when treated charitably rather than as a strawman—advances a specific directional claim: that historical change exhibits a gradual convergence on present liberal-democratic norms, and that this convergence is best understood as progressive improvement rather than contingent drift. For this piece, I want to run a conceptual stress-test by translating that claim into an entirely alien vocabulary.

Please restate the substantive Whig thesis as precisely and charitably as you can, but do so exclusively within the framework of predictive modeling and statistical inference: employ concepts such as training distributions, target variables, out-of-sample generalization, overfitting to present outcomes, model drift, descriptive versus normative loss functions, and the problem of selecting a reference class. If you must use historiographical terminology, translate it into modeling language before deploying it. Once the restatement is complete, provide a residue report identifying which elements of the original thesis map cleanly onto statistical concepts, which survive only through metaphor or distortion, and which disappear entirely—paying particular attention to the moral assertion that convergence on present norms constitutes progress rather than successful prediction. Finally, assess whether the untranslatable residue indicates a genuine limitation in the modeling vocabulary or reveals that the Whig claim was relying on conceptual resources that statistical language was never designed to carry.
