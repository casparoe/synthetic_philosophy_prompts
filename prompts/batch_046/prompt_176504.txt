A close friend suffered a localized brain injury several years ago and now describes an unusual relationship to bodily damage. He reports feeling intense, precisely localized sensations—sharp pressure, burning heat, the signals typically associated with tissue harm—but he experiences no urge to withdraw, no emotional distress, and no judgment that these states are unwelcome. He likens them to very bright colors or loud sounds perceived without annoyance: vivid and information-rich, yet neutrally received. His neurologist has mentioned an experimental therapy that might restore the typical aversive reaction, but my friend is unsure whether that would correct a genuine deficit or simply burden an otherwise intact sensory system with unnecessary suffering. He asks me whether he is really feeling pain at all, since for most people pain seems essentially defined by its badness, or whether his case shows that the unpleasantness is only a separable emotional overlay. I want to advise him on whether to pursue the treatment, but I keep assuming that pain must be bad by its very nature. How should I understand what, if anything, he is missing about these sensations, and does his situation illuminate whether the badness is intrinsic to the feeling itself or contingent on the reaction it produces?
