I am composing a multi-volume novel centered on a historian-protagonist whose cognitive life is partly constituted by an external artifact I have invented for the narrative: a woven mnemonic lattice that physically encodes her beliefs, intentions, and episodic memories in interlaced silk threads, with the distinctive property that alterations to later sections can retroactively restructure earlier entries—a phenomenon I term retroactive coherence—without the protagonist’s conscious awareness. Because the story spans decades and requires strict continuity across installments, I need a reusable decision procedure that my continuity editor and I can apply during drafting to determine, in any given scene, whether a particular mental state should be narratively attributed to the protagonist’s internal cognition or to her extended cognitive system comprising the lattice. The procedure must be grounded in the conceptual architecture of the extended mind thesis—particularly the parity principle and the criterion of functional integration—without requiring philosophical training to execute.

Please construct an ordered, stepwise procedure presented as a sequence of questions, each with explicit criteria for what counts as a sufficient affirmative or negative answer, that a non-philosopher can complete in under fifteen minutes per case. For each step, specify where the procedure terminates with a definitive “treat as internal” or “treat as extended,” and flag precisely those junctures where the question is irreducibly one of narrative values—where fidelity to character psychology conflicts with functional parity—so that judgment cannot be eliminated and must be documented. The procedure must accommodate three hard cases specific to my narrative constraints: first, the lattice is physically damaged and the protagonist acts in direct contradiction to its remaining intact records; second, the protagonist deliberately reweaves a section to align the record with a desired future outcome, raising the question of whether the altered state counts as her genuine belief at the moment of alteration; and third, an antagonist secretly modifies an entry, and the protagonist subsequently relies on the corrupted record in forming an intention, with the result that treating the state as extended yields a plot consequence I find narratively unsatisfying—specifically, it absolves her of moral responsibility for an action that, intuitively, ought to remain hers. Apply the procedure to all three cases, tracing the path through each step, and design one additional hypothetical of your own that tests the boundary between genuine functional integration and mere causal dependence on an external tool.

Conclude by stating exactly what the procedure cannot settle: where it will necessarily misfire, which conceptual distinctions it collapses for the sake of usability, and what categories of continuity error it is powerless to prevent, so that my editor and I know when to override it with an explicit narrative judgment. Constraints: I draft under monthly deadlines; the procedure will be used by myself and one continuity editor with no formal background in philosophy of mind; every decision must be documentable in a single paragraph of justification for the publisher’s continuity file; and the procedure must never invoke bibliographic references, citation lists, or appeals to scholarly authority, since neither I nor my editor can verify those without leaving the manuscript.
