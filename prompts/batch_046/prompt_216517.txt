I am writing from a position that has recently become personally urgent: my father has been diagnosed with a progressive cortical neurodegenerative disorder, and I have begun working in a lab that develops region-specific neural prostheses intended to replace failing biological circuitry with synthetic analogues that preserve local synaptic dynamics. This convergence has made the philosophical stalemate around mind uploading and gradual replacement impossible to treat as abstract. The debate—stretching from Parfit’s gradual-replacement scenarios through contemporary discussions of functional isomorphism and psychological continuity—has, in my view, stalled completely. Proponents of organismic continuity and defenders of psychological-continuity accounts both accept the same empirical premises (the synthetic replacements are locally functionally identical, no phenomenal gap occurs during the procedure, autobiographical memory and personality traits are preserved) and both agree that whether he survives matters deeply for moral and legal status. Yet they have not moved in decades: the continuity camp insists that biological substrate is essential for numerical identity, while the psychological-continuity camp treats substrate as irrelevant so long as the right causal-functional relations hold. My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden empirical disagreement but a case of two questions being run together—one metaphysical, about the persistence conditions of human animals; one practical-normative, about the conditions under which we should extend recognition and rights to a post-replacement subject. However, I am unsure whether this diagnosis holds, or whether the real work is being done by an unexamined disagreement over what I will call integration topology—whether identity in hybrid architectures depends on global coherence patterns that are disrupted when replacement is patchwork rather than uniform—or by bedrock intuitions about naturalness that neither side can defend. I want a diagnosis that engages the actual commitments of each camp: is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or a misalignment of questions? Is there a formal or conceptual distinction—perhaps between diachronic functional coherence and metaphysical persistence—that could actually move it? Or is the impasse a sign that the original question is ill-posed, and if so, what question should replace it? And given your diagnosis, what, if anything, would count as progress: a model of coherence in hybrid neural architectures, a separation of metaphysical and practical criteria, or an argument that no further movement should be expected?
