I am drafting a graduate seminar exercise on H. P. Grice’s account of speaker meaning and conversational implicature, and I would like you to reconstruct what Grice might have argued about a phenomenon that has only become widespread since his death: large language models that generate fluent conversational text—including apparent floutings of the maxims of quantity, quality, relation and manner—despite possessing no beliefs, intentions, or awareness of an audience.

Please do not merely summarise Grice’s published doctrines. Instead, extrapolate carefully from his distinction between natural and non-natural meaning, his analysis of M-intentions, and his insistence that conversational implicature requires a speaker to intend that an audience recognise a particular belief or attitude, in order to address the following. Would Grice regard an LLM’s production of, say, an ironic understatement or a deliberately ambiguous reply as a genuine case of conversational implicature? Or would he treat it as a kind of hollow mimicry—structurally analogous to implicature but lacking the intentional backbone that makes it speaker meaning? Further, consider whether his framework can accommodate a distinction I am tentatively calling algorithmic pseudo-implicature: outputs that systematically exploit the statistical patterns of cooperative dialogue without any underlying commitment to the Cooperative Principle itself.

Structure your response in the following sections:

1. Reconstructed Gricean position (approximately 250 words): Set out the view Grice would likely defend, grounded explicitly in his concepts of speaker meaning, M-intention, and the Cooperative Principle.

2. Application to LLM-generated discourse (approximately 250 words): Analyse how this reconstructed view applies to the specific case of LLM outputs that appear to flout maxims—e.g. generating an apparently deliberate understatement or an evasive reply—without possessing intentions.

3. The algorithmic pseudo-implicature distinction (approximately 200 words): Evaluate whether the concept sketched above is coherent within Grice’s framework, and whether it helps or hinders his account of meaning.

4. Critical reservations (approximately 150 words): Identify one significant tension or limitation in projecting Grice’s intentionalist framework onto contemporary language technology.

Write in British English, avoid bibliographic references, citations, or a reading list, and do not include direct quotations from Grice’s texts.
