I was discussing with a friend late into the evening about whether Alan Turing’s conception of machine education—particularly his analogy between training a computing machine and educating a child through reward and punishment—would have led him to endorse or reject the use of modern adaptive language systems as autonomous evaluators of philosophical reasoning in university settings. My friend insists that Turing, having argued that learning machines could eventually match human cognition through proper instruction, would naturally welcome algorithmic grading of open-ended conceptual essays as a logical extension of mechanized thought. I am less certain, especially given that Turing seemed to treat the pedagogical relationship as one of genuine interaction rather than bureaucratic assessment, and that he might distinguish sharply between a system trained to generate coherent arguments and one qualified to judge the originality and depth of human philosophical judgment.

Given this disagreement, I would like you to reconstruct what Turing’s likely position would have been on the specific issue of using learning machines not merely as tutors but as credentialing authorities—awarding grades or accreditation in disciplines where the evaluation criteria are themselves contested and evolving. Would he have seen this as consistent with his vision of machines that learn through experience, or would he have regarded it as violating some implicit condition of his educational analogy—perhaps that the evaluator must share in the vulnerability of being wrong, or that legitimate assessment requires a form of mutual recognition that statistical prediction cannot supply? In answering, please attend carefully to the distinction he drew between simulating intelligent behavior and genuinely possessing the learned capacities that justify evaluative authority, and clearly indicate where your reconstruction is forced to speculate beyond what his published remarks on child-machines actually support.
