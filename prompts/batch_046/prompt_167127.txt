Two philosophy students, Priya and Jordan, debated over coffee whether East Asian relational accounts of the person genuinely correct the Western liberal picture or merely relocate its problems.

(1) Priya argued that Confucian and related East Asian views treat the self as constituted by roles—parent, child, neighbor—rather than as a pre-social rights-bearer. The liberal notion of an unencumbered self prior to social ties is a fiction; intelligible personhood arises only within a web of relationships.

(2) Jordan replied that liberalism need not deny relationships; it treats them as subject to consent or critical evaluation. The liberal subject is not pre-social but possesses the distance to ask whether a given role is worth occupying. Without that distance, moral reform—rejecting filial obedience that demands injustice—becomes impossible, since the self is fully embedded.

(3) Priya countered that Jordan’s distance is historically specific, not universal. Confucian traditions contain internal resources for correction, such as the view that human nature tends toward goodness and can be cultivated through ritual and reflection. Reform does not require an atomized outsider but a more fully realized relational self.

(4) Jordan said this pushes the problem back: if identity is constituted by relationships, then challenging those relationships is self-destructive. Liberalism explains why one can reject communal norms without annihilating identity—because identity is not identical to social position. Relational theories, he added, struggle to protect dissenters, for whom leaving a role can appear not as liberation but as loss of self.

(5) Priya answered that Jordan assumes a static identity, whereas relational selves are dynamic. She also argued that liberal consent often masks structural coercion—preferences are shaped by social conditions before anyone asks for agreement—so liberalism does not eliminate social constitution but renders it invisible.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right about the central disagreement—whether relational accounts correct liberal ones, whether liberal distance is indispensable, or whether the framing is flawed—and identify what both parties miss, particularly regarding whether a hybrid or differently structured relational view might resolve the tension they present as exhaustive.
