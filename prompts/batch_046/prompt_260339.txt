At a philosophy reading group, two members—Nadia and Omar—got into a sustained disagreement about the cosmological argument. Nadia was defending a kalam-style version: she said that whatever begins to exist has a cause, that modern cosmology gives us good reason to think the universe began with the Big Bang singularity, and therefore there must be a transcendent cause that itself does not begin to exist. Omar defended a contingency version: he said that even an eternal universe remains contingent—its existence is not self-explanatory—so the Principle of Sufficient Reason demands an explanation in a necessary being.

In the first round, Nadia claimed Omar's argument proves too much. She said that if every contingent thing needs an explanation, then even God's own modal properties—why God is omnipotent rather than some other necessary being—would require explanation, generating an infinite regress of necessary beings. Omar replied that God is not contingent but necessary, and that contingency applies only to beings that could fail to exist, which God by definition cannot do. He added that Nadia was conflating ontological contingency (the capacity to not exist) with explanatory contingency (the need for a reason for every particular feature), and that the PSR only addresses the former.

In the second round, Omar attacked Nadia's kalam argument for smuggling in temporal causation. He said the premise that whatever begins to exist has a cause is supported only by examples within the universe, where causes precede effects in time; but a transcendent cause of the universe as a whole could not be prior in time, since time itself begins with the universe. Nadia responded that causation does not require temporal priority. She invoked hierarchical causation, in which a cause and its effect exist simultaneously—like a hand moving a stick that moves a stone—to show that a timeless cause can still be genuinely causal. Omar conceded the logical possibility but said Nadia had now abandoned the intuitive empirical support for her premise, since all our observed causation is linear and temporal, not hierarchical.

In the third round, Nadia pointed out that Omar's Principle of Sufficient Reason seems to license explaining not just the universe's existence but every particular state of affairs—why this galaxy formed here rather than there—without any principled stopping point except an ad hoc exemption for God. Omar answered that the PSR applies to the existence of contingent beings as a totality, not to every internal arrangement within them. The universe as a single contingent being cries out for explanation, whereas the arrangement of its parts can be explained internally by physics, provided one accepts that physical laws themselves are either necessary or ultimately grounded in the necessary being.

They asked you, as a neutral observer, to referee. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each specific claim—Nadia's opening kalam premise, Omar's necessary-being reply, Nadia's overgeneration worry, Omar's distinction between ontological and explanatory contingency, Nadia's hierarchical-causation response, Omar's empirical-objection reply, Nadia's overgeneration of internal arrangements, and Omar's totality reply—from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether either argument actually avoids the problem of explaining why there is something rather than nothing without circularity.
