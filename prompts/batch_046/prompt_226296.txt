The exam asked: Some philosophers have recently proposed an 'interstitial personhood' model, in which the self is not housed within a single consciousness or body but is constituted in the relational gaps between agents. Evaluate whether ubuntu is best read as an instance of interstitial personhood, or whether it retains a stronger embodied and communal locus. In your answer, contrast Desmond Tutu's post-apartheid theological framing with John Mbiti's earlier ethnographic formulation in African Religions and Philosophy (1969), and assess whether a relational ontology necessarily undermines individual moral rights.

The student wrote:
Ubuntu, captured in the phrase umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, is indeed a form of interstitial personhood. Unlike liberal theories that locate identity inside the individual mind, ubuntu distributes the self across the network of relationships. This aligns perfectly with the interstitial model, which denies any core self.

Desmond Tutu developed this idea in the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, framing ubuntu as a theology of forgiveness where one's humanity is bound up in others', especially enemies. By contrast, John Mbiti's earlier work treated ubuntu as a system of ancestral reciprocity, in which personhood is earned through ritual obligations to the dead. Where Tutu saw horizontal reconciliation, Mbiti saw vertical continuity with ancestors.

Because the self in ubuntu is entirely interstitial, there is no room for individual moral agency outside the collective. Rights cannot be inherent to a person if personhood itself only exists between people; they must instead be collective permissions. Therefore, ubuntu does not merely ground rights differently — it dissolves the individual rights-bearer entirely, replacing it with a distributed relational node.
