I'm a journalist covering philosophy and institutional ethics, and I've been invited to deliver the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop—"Personhood Beyond the Individual: Ubuntu and Institutional Design"—hosted by a public university's center for applied ethics. The audience is evenly divided: academic philosophers, including scholars of African philosophy and Western moral and political theory, and practitioners—policymakers, legal advocates in transitional justice, and directors of community-based governance programs. The remarks should run about ten minutes and set the intellectual agenda without resolving it.

The philosophical framework must do structural work, not serve as ornament. I want to center Ubuntu, understood not as a generic appeal to community but as a relational ontology in which moral standing is co-constituted through reciprocal recognition rather than possessed by discrete individuals prior to relationship. To sharpen this, I want to work with the distinction between thin relationality—where relationships are instrumental to pre-existing individual interests—and thick relationality—where the relation itself is constitutive of the persons involved. This distinction should govern the form of the remarks themselves: I should not adopt the posture of an autonomous speaker welcoming separate agents into a shared space, since that framing presupposes the individualist ontology the workshop is meant to interrogate. Instead, the speech should model the dependency it describes—acknowledging that my voice here is already incomplete and indebted to the audience, the institutional history that convened the room, and the unacknowledged labor that makes such gatherings possible.

The occasion is a working session, not a ceremony. Concrete proposals for institutional reform will follow, so the remarks must avoid both untranslated philosophical jargon that excludes the practitioners and vague inspirational language that patronizes the philosophers. They should navigate a specific tension: many participants operate within legal traditions that treat recognition, harm, and apology as transactions between discrete moral agents, whereas a thick relational view implies that injury and repair are distributed across networks of persons. I want the speech to open with that tension—showing how the very act of giving opening remarks presupposes an individual speaker—without pretending to a false neutrality. The remarks should be meaningful to someone without web access: no appeals to obscure specialist debates, no requests for bibliographies or reading lists, and no citations that I cannot verify independently. Please write them so they remain conceptually precise, avoid reducing Ubuntu to cultural wisdom, and leave the workshop with a shared problem rather than a settled conclusion.
