I'm preparing a unit on quantum foundations for my advanced philosophy of science seminar, and I want to test whether students can locate an overreach in the standard narrative about Bell locality. A colleague presented the following argument in a recent workshop, and while each step sounds defensible, the conclusion strikes me as an ontological crisis that outruns the premises. (1) Bell's theorem demonstrates that any theory reproducing quantum correlations must violate at least one of parameter independence or outcome independence—jointly, local factorizability. (2) Experimental violations of the Bell inequalities therefore establish that locality, in at least one of these statistical senses, fails. (3) The conventional interpretation rejects parameter independence, allowing that the distant measurement setting can influence the local outcome, while retaining outcome independence. (4) But parameter independence is only a condition on hidden-variable distributions; it is not identical to causal locality, which prohibits superluminal influence. (5) If we interpret the quantum state not as an incomplete hidden-variable description but as a relational structure—so that correlations reflect the joint system rather than intrinsic properties of parts—then parameter independence can fail without any superluminal causal influence. (6) By parity of reasoning, macroscopic correlations—say, two sealed envelopes with matching colors separated by light-years—should also violate factorizability if macroscopic properties are relational in the same way. (7) Yet macroscopic correlations are fully accounted for by common-cause preparation, with no need to invoke relational ontology or reject parameter independence. (8) Therefore, either quantum and macroscopic domains are governed by radically different ontological principles with no explanatory bridge, or the conventional reading of Bell's failure is mistaken and no superluminal influence exists anywhere. (9) But if no superluminal influence exists and causal locality holds, then Bell's derivation applies and quantum mechanics should satisfy the inequalities—contradicting (2). (10) Hence Bell's theorem demonstrates not merely non-locality, but the non-objectivity of all physical properties, since only then can correlations persist without either causal links or hidden-variable completeness.

Please identify the precise step or hidden equivocation where this reasoning slips—particularly regarding the conditions under which Bell's derivation applies—explain why that step initially looks defensible in light of relational interpretations of the quantum state, and then state what accepting or rejecting it costs for our understanding of macroscopic causation and the quantum-classical boundary. I prefer to preserve (1) and (5) if possible. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
