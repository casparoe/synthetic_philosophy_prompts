The Parliamentary Committee on Political Integrity has issued five advisory opinions on whether officeholders may commit morally questionable acts in pursuit of public goods. Its rulings are as follows.

Case 1: A security minister authorized warrantless surveillance of opposition figures; the operation yielded no security benefits but violated privacy rights. The Committee ruled that this was an impermissible instance of dirty hands, because the anticipated public benefit was speculative and the harm was concrete and irreversible.

Case 2: A municipal executive made a secret payment to a transit union to avert a strike that would have halted emergency medical transport. The Committee approved the act, on condition that the payment be disclosed within thirty days, on the grounds that the harm averted was immediate and measurable, the deception was minimal and targeted at a bargaining obstacle rather than the electorate, and transparency was eventually restored.

Case 3: A legislator cast a decisive vote for a tax reform she regarded as unjust, in order to secure passage of an attached public-health funding measure. The Committee held that this did not constitute dirty hands at all, distinguishing a personal moral wrong from a systemic legislative compromise in which the actor does not herself lie, coerce, or violate rights.

Case 4: During an election, a candidate disseminated a technically accurate statistic that, by selective framing, created a false impression of her opponent’s record on corruption. The Committee censured the candidate, ruling that deceptive framing in democratic competition corrupts deliberation regardless of the electoral outcome.

Case 5: A diplomat forged an internal routing authorization to expedite the delivery of humanitarian supplies across a closed border; the forgery was revealed to the relevant bureaucracy within forty-eight hours and caused no public harm. The Committee approved with conditions, emphasizing that the deception was directed at an institutional barrier, was immediately rectified, and did not involve misleading citizens.

The Committee now faces a new proposal. The leader of a governing coalition has secretly promised a cabinet ministry to a moderate faction of a rival party in exchange for that faction’s support on a comprehensive climate-mitigation bill. The promise requires breaking an explicit, public pre-election pledge never to share executive power with that party. The existence of the negotiation and the promise have been concealed from both the electorate and the coalition’s own supporters; the leader intends to disclose the arrangement only after the bill has passed. The rival faction’s base will experience the deal as a betrayal of shared principles, though no legal rights are violated. The climate bill is projected to reduce national emissions by a substantial margin within a decade. The leader defends the action by invoking a distinction—drawn in recent commentary on the Committee’s jurisprudence—between instrumental deception, which serves as a temporary means that can be undone by subsequent disclosure, and constitutive deception, which embeds falsehood into the political arrangement itself and cannot be rectified without undermining the good achieved. She argues that her concealment is purely instrumental: it concerns coalition mechanics, not the substance of the bill, and full transparency after passage will restore accountability.

What should the Committee decide if it wishes to remain consistent with its own precedents? Identify the closest analog among Cases 1–5 and explain whether the new situation genuinely maps onto it. Then articulate at least two distinct principles or rules that are jointly consistent with the five published opinions but that diverge in their prediction for this case—one that treats the deception as falling within the permissible scope of systemic or institutional compromise, and one that treats it as an impermissible corruption of democratic deliberation—stating for each whether it approves or rejects the leader’s action. Give your best-guess verdict for the Committee, with the reasoning that makes one rule more compelling than the other in light of the track record. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case—specifying the act, the target of any deception, the nature of the public benefit, and the conditions of transparency—that would most clearly distinguish the two rules you have identified.
