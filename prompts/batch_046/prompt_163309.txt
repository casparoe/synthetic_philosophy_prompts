My verdicts on whether testimony is an irreducible epistemic source, judged case by case. (1) A patient accepts her oncologist's interpretation of an MRI she cannot read: this collapses into expert authority plus deferred inference; it is reducible. (2) A friend tells me she is grieving, and I thereby come to know her specific emotional state: this is irreducible, because her first-person access is structurally non-substitutable—no instrument or third party could occupy her position and deliver the same content to me. (3) I learn from a museum plaque that a battle occurred in 1066: reducible to institutional authority and documentary transmission. (4) A village collectively remembers the location of an ancestral well through distributed oral repetition, with no single speaker holding the full account; an ethnographer learns it from them together: this is irreducible testimony, but not because of one speaker's unique mental access—rather because the social distribution itself creates an epistemic dependency that cannot be reconstructed from individual beliefs or physical records alone. (5) A child learns not to touch fire because her parent warns her, then she observes the burn: the testimonial component reduces to parental authority combined with her own subsequent perception. (6) I receive a accurate translation from a language-model interface with no human speaker behind it: there is no testimony here at all, so reductionism is moot.

These verdicts are mostly stable, but (2) and (4) sit in tension. Both feel irreducible, yet (2) depends on a single speaker's irreplaceable mental position, while (4) depends on distributed social structure with no such position. Meanwhile (1) and (5) both involve authority, but (1) feels like pure delegation while (5) feels like authority that is quickly verified and therefore dissolved.
