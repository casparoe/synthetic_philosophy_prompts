I'm writing this as a student in a philosophy of medicine seminar, working on an essay I have tentatively titled "Retrospective Authorization versus Prospective Binding: A Phenomenological Challenge to Advance Directives." My central claim is that defenses of advance directives that appeal to autonomy typically conflate two relations a present self can bear to a past decision: retrospective authorization, in which I now actively endorse what I previously decided, and prospective binding, in which an earlier decision constrains me now irrespective of my current attitude. I want to argue that the authority of a directive depends on the first relation, but in conditions like advanced dementia the present self lacks the conceptual and experiential framework to perform retrospective authorization. At the same time, the directive does not operate as a genuinely experienced external constraint—like a law or a promise made to another—because the patient does not experience refusal; rather, the patient experiences an absence, a gap where certain options are silently removed by clinicians or family members acting on the document. So the directive does not bind the present self in any phenomenologically meaningful way; it merely substitutes one form of substituted judgment (by the past self) for another (by doctors or relatives). I also want to push a further asymmetry: autonomy is always exercised by a present agent with present capacities, and so "precedent autonomy" is really temporal substituted judgment disguised as self-rule.

I would like targeted comments on four points. First, is my distinction between retrospective authorization and prospective binding coherent, or does it collapse into a more familiar distinction—say, between authentic and inauthentic preferences, or between first-order and second-order desires? Second, am I missing the strongest reply, perhaps that narrative identity or value continuity does not require present endorsement, only that the present self is the appropriate temporal part of a single life? Third, can the authority of directives be salvaged on non-autonomy grounds—such as protecting the earlier self's interests, avoiding harm, or respecting a kind of diachronic contract—without falling back into the phenomenological problem I have raised? And fourth, structurally, should I frame this as a local critique of autonomy-based justifications specifically, or as a broader conceptual argument that advance directives are confused regardless of whether they are defended through autonomy, welfare, or narrative continuity?

Please focus on the coherence and development of the line itself rather than on summarizing standard literature; I am trying to work out a somewhat specific argument, and I want to know precisely where it is weak, where it needs sharper distinctions, and whether there is a counterexample or conceptual move I have not anticipated.
