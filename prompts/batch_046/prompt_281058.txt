I am preparing a feature for a serious general-interest magazine on science and ideas, examining whether the familiar philosophical distinction between knowing how and knowing that still holds up under scrutiny from cognitive science and ordinary language. For an interactive sidebar, I need a diagnostic decision tree — not a personality quiz, but a classification instrument — that sorts readers into precise philosophical positions by asking them about concrete cases, not about labels.

Please construct the tree from the logical space first, then map the positions onto it.

Begin with three concrete cases that readers must adjudicate:
- an expert cyclist who maintains balance without being able to articulate any rule;
- a medical student who can recite the biomechanics of suturing but cannot perform the action;
- a surgeon who retains the ability to operate after amnesia has erased her explicit anatomical vocabulary.

From answers to these and follow-up questions, build branches that separate the following commitments:
(a) whether knowing-how can exist in the complete absence of the corresponding knowing-that;
(b) whether any form of representation (not merely propositional belief) is necessary to the skill;
(c) whether the distinction is ontologically robust or pragmatically useful but ultimately deflationary.

The tree should have the following six leaves, each described by its core claim and its most awkward commitment:
1. Classical Rylean anti-intellectualism: knowing-how is a sui generis, non-representational disposition.
2. Direct intellectualism: knowing-how is reducible to propositional knowledge with practical content or mode of presentation.
3. Modal-competence intellectualism (a hybrid rarely named in standard surveys): knowing-how consists in propositional knowledge of one's own counterfactual capacities, coupled with a non-cognitive disposition to act, without requiring procedural representation.
4. Enactive embodied view: knowing-how is constituted by direct sensorimotor coupling, with no representational intermediary whatsoever.
5. Predictive-processing or procedural-representational view: knowing-how involves hierarchical, non-propositional representational structures — for instance, generative models of action dynamics — that guide behaviour without being articulable.
6. Deflationary eliminativism: 'knowing how' denotes a family-resemblance cluster of abilities with no unified underlying nature.

Please ensure the branch nodes are phrased as questions a reader could answer without knowing philosophical jargon — for example, 'If she forgets all the names, can she still cut?' rather than 'Do you accept the autonomy thesis?' No question should ask 'Are you an intellectualist?' or invoke named camps.

After laying out the branches, identify any logical combinations that remain unoccupied — for instance, a position that denies both representation and any skill-constituting disposition, or one that treats knowing-how as non-propositional yet essentially structured by propositional representations. For each empty cell, briefly diagnose whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Also flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate on the basis of the case answers, explaining exactly where the overlap lies — for example, the modal-competence intellectualist and the predictive-processing view may converge on certain answers about representation.

The output should be a text-based flowchart with clear node labels, path descriptions, and the scoring key mapping answer patterns to leaves. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific page numbers. Use British spelling and maintain a formal, polished tone appropriate for an intellectually curious general audience.
