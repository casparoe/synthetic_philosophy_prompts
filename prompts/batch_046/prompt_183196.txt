I am preparing a long-form philosophical essay on the practical fallout of the free-will debate for criminal justice, concentrating specifically on the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso regarding whether the rejection of libertarian free will undermines just deserts and retributive punishment. I am not seeking a survey of their positions, but rather a defended agenda: which open conceptual questions and downstream institutional implications genuinely merit sustained attention, which are largely dead ends, and how limited philosophical or policy resources ought to be allocated among them.

To frame the task, I want to highlight a tension that is often noted but rarely pursued in detail. Dennett’s compatibilist account preserves a thin, forward-looking conception of moral responsibility that he takes to be sufficient for democratic self-governance; Caruso, by contrast, argues for a public-health quarantine model of criminal behaviour that seems to require not merely the abandonment of retributivism but a systematic re-engineering of our reactive attitudes—an enterprise that may depend more on empirical social psychology than on metaphysical argument. Given this, please prioritise the following lines of inquiry, defending the ordering with explicit criteria.

First, whether the notion of basic desert can be reconstructed in purely institutional or contractualist terms without tacitly importing libertarian presuppositions. Second, whether empirical work on reactive attitudes—specifically, the stability of blame and resentment under deterministic belief—actually supports Caruso’s claim that they can be systematically suppressed, or whether Dennett is correct that they are too deeply embedded in human social cognition to be engineered away. Third, whether abolishing retributive sentencing in criminal law would generate unavoidable spillover effects for punitive damages in civil liability, and whether those effects depend on the same metaphysical commitments about moral agency. Fourth, whether the debate’s most significant practical stakes lie not in penal policy at all, but in the justification of moral anger and its functional role in democratic deliberation.

Please rank these four, defining importance relative to a researcher or policymaker with limited time whose goal is to alter institutional practice rather than merely to settle a conceptual dispute. For each item, indicate whether it is upstream or downstream of the others, assess its tractability through conceptual analysis alone versus interdisciplinary collaboration, and specify what kind of argument or evidence over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list. I am particularly interested in which of these you would deprioritise on the grounds that resolving it would change little in either philosophical theory or institutional design. Finally, I ask whether there is a fifth, less obvious line of work—perhaps concerning the compatibility of Caruso’s quarantine framework with existing legal doctrines governing mental-health detention and preventive confinement—that ought to sit near the top of any serious research agenda in this area.
