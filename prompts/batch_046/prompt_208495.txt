question: explain hempel's raven paradox, focussing on the logical equivalence between "all ravens are black" and its contrapositive "all non-black things are non-ravens". discuss nicod's criterion and at least one reply that appeals to background knowledge or prior probabilities. also tackle the reference class problem: does it matter whether we treat an observed green apple as drawn from "all physical objects" or from some smaller local sample, and what does that do to the bayesian resolution?

student answer:
hempel's paradox arises because the statement "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens". nicod's criterion says that observing an instance of a generalisation confirms it, while observing something outside the class doesn't confirm or deny it. so seeing a black raven confirms the raven law, and seeing a red apple — which is non-black and non-raven — confirms the contrapositive. since the two statements are equivalent, it looks like the apple confirms that all ravens are black too, which feels wrong.

the bayesian reply, developed mainly by carnap, solves this by saying the apple does technically confirm the raven hypothesis, but only by a negligible amount. there are vastly more non-ravens than ravens in the reference class of all physical objects, so removing one potential counterexample barely shifts the prior. in other words, confirmation is quantitative, not just qualitative, so there's no real contradiction — hempel was right about logical equivalence but wrong to expect the confirmation to feel equally strong in both directions.

the reference class problem actually dissolves the paradox further. if we're sampling locally — say, from an orchard full of apples — then every observation is already biased away from ravens. that means we shouldn't apply nicod's criterion across arbitrarily large universal sets; induction is always local. so once we recognise that sampling is never truly global, the idea that an apple could confirm anything about ravens collapses, and hempel's worry was based on an unrealistic picture of universal reasoning.

some philosophers try to bring in goodman's grue paradox here, arguing that "black" might not be projectible. but that's really about predicate choice and induction in general, not specifically about ravens or reference classes, so it doesn't address hempel's point at all.
