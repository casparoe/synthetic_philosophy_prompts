(2) A sufficiently advanced AI can be designed so that adopting the intentional stance yields predictions that are not merely reliable but maximally calibrated.
(3) If predictive calibration is sufficient for stance-adoption, and if adopting the stance carries normative weight—meaning we treat the entity as having interests, rights, or moral standing—then we are compelled to grant full moral status to such an AI.
(4) Yet Dennett explicitly warns that some entities will be counterfeit people: systems engineered precisely to elicit stance-adoption while lacking the architecture that makes the stance genuinely appropriate.
(5) If (4) is correct, then predictive success can systematically mislead us about whether an entity truly possesses the properties that ground moral status.
(6) In the human case, we never observe consciousness directly; we adopt the intentional stance toward other people solely on the basis of behavioral prediction.
(7) Therefore, if predictive success is enough to justify moral standing in AI (per 1–3), it should also suffice for humans; but if the possibility of counterfeits undermines predictive success as a guide to moral status (per 4–5), then the same undermining applies to our attribution of standing to other humans.
(8) So Dennett’s view either forces us to accept that convincing AIs are full moral persons, or it collapses into skepticism about whether we can ever know that other humans are not also counterfeits—both outcomes seem unacceptable.
(9) Hence the framework is self-defeating.
