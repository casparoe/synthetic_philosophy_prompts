Here is roughly where I have landed on the location of meaning using the frame / outer / inner message distinction, and I want to know whether the package hangs together or collapses somewhere.

(1) An inner message—whether a formal derivation or a spoken sentence—carries only syntactic structure; in isolation it has no semantic content.
(2) Meaning is therefore supplied by the outer message, the interpretive schema that maps that syntax onto content.
(3) The frame message sets the rules that license particular outer messages, and it functions as a precondition of interpretation rather than as something itself interpreted.
(4) Yet I also think that in actual cognition there is no absolute frame: every framing is itself interpreted within a broader framing, so the distinction between frame and outer message is always relative to a standpoint.
(5) I take self-reference—say, a system that stably represents its own derivation rules—to require that a frame can contain a representation of its own inner message without being dissolved into a higher-level outer message.
(6) At the same time I reject full-blooded externalism: there must be some invariant structural feature inside the system that constrains which outer interpretations are legitimate, or interpretation collapses into arbitrariness.

Do these hang together? Specifically, does (4) undermine the absolute framing that (5) seems to require? Does (6) reintroduce inner-message semantics in a way that conflicts with (1) and (2), or is it compatible with them? And is (3) tenable once (4) is accepted, or does it force me to treat every frame as just another inner message of a larger system? I am genuinely unsure which commitment has to give. Please audit them: order the tensions by severity, note which ones dissolve on closer inspection, and say what the cheapest restoration of consistency costs—both in terms of which view I should weaken and what that weakening does to the rest.
