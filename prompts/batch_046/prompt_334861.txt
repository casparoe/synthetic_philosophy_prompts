You are a professional philosopher of science with particular competence in the metaphysics of physics and the history of scientific theory change. Before you begin drafting, take a moment to brainstorm: list the two or three standard moves in the structural-realist literature (epistemic vs. ontic, the preservation of mathematics, the problem of emergence), identify the point where those moves stall, and sketch one unconventional angle—then commit to it.

Write a proposal for a one-semester graduate term paper of approximately 6,000 words. The proposal should read as a serious, self-critical plan of research, not as an essay that already knows its conclusion. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or requests for citations; refer to positions, arguments, and formal frameworks generally, and indicate that specific references will be supplied later.

Topic and precise question. Structural realism is usually defended and criticized through high-level historical transitions—most commonly, the move from Fresnel’s luminiferous ether to Maxwell’s field equations. I propose to shift the focus to a technically denser but philosophically under-explored sequence: the evolution of statistical mechanics from Boltzmann’s kinetic theory through Gibbs’s ensemble formulation to quantum statistical mechanics. In this domain, predictive structures (entropy relations, partition functions, equilibrium distributions) remain remarkably stable, yet the ontological interpretations shift radically—from colliding particles, to imaginary ensembles of copies, to quantum states in Fock space. Compounding the difficulty, each stage admits empirically equivalent but conceptually divergent mathematical formulations (for instance, canonical versus grand-canonical descriptions, or distinct representations of entropy).

The precise question is this: Can structural realism supply a philosophically principled distinction between mere notational variants and genuinely distinct structural commitments within statistical mechanics? Or does its core anti-ontological stance force it to treat all extensionally equivalent formalisms as structurally identical, even when those formalisms carry different explanatory and modal implications—such as accounting for fluctuations, chemical potential, or symmetry-breaking phenomena? In short, does preserving “the structure” require preserving explanatory structure as well, and if so, has the view smuggled ontology back in under another name?

Why the question remains open. Epistemic structural realists avoid ontological commitment, yet they still need criteria for theoretical equivalence that do not tacitly depend on ontological judgments about what counts as the “same” physical situation. Ontic structural realists, by contrast, identify structure with all that exists, but they then face an acute version of the emergence or “collapse” problem: if the same mathematical relations can be instantiated by particles, fields, or abstract ensembles, what determines which particular objects, if any, exist? Existing treatments rarely test these difficulties against cases where the ontology is not merely replaced but multiplied, and where different structures are compatible with a single ontology. The statistical-mechanics sequence provides exactly that test.

Proposed approach and method. Rather than surveying the entire structural-realist literature, the paper will focus tightly on two paired conceptual case studies. First, the Boltzmann-to-Gibbs transition, examining whether the shift from a dynamical, particle-based ontology to an ensemble-based ontology preserves a single mathematical structure or replaces one structure with an extensionally equivalent but conceptually different one. Second, the canonical versus grand-canonical formulations in quantum statistics, analyzing whether differences in explanatory reach—particularly regarding open systems and chemical potential—can be captured purely structurally or whether they require ontological supplementation (for example, commitment to particle number as a physical variable rather than a statistical parameter).

The method will be conceptual analysis paired with close formal comparison: reconstructing the relevant equations, identifying which relations are invariant across formulations, and assessing whether any invariant relations are lost or gained. This is not an empirical or historical project; the history serves only to frame the conceptual problem. The obvious alternative—defending structural realism through the familiar Fresnel-Maxwell case—will be explicitly set aside as insufficient for testing the view’s resources in domains of ontological multiplicity.

What a result would look like, and what failure would be. A successful project will yield either (a) a refined criterion of structural identity that allows the structural realist to differentiate notational variants from substantive structural alternatives without appealing to ontology, or (b) an argument that structural realism must either expand its notion of “structure” to include explanatory and modal structure—thereby blurring the boundary it seeks to maintain between structure and ontology—or accept that some empirically equivalent theories are structurally underdetermined in a way that weakens the view’s anti-realist credentials.

The project fails if the analysis collapses into a purely syntactic comparison of equations with no philosophical payoff—if, for example, the canonical and grand-canonical formulations turn out to be resolvable by standard conventionalism about coordinate or parameter choices, leaving structural realism entirely untouched. Another mode of failure is technical overreach: if the quantum-statistical case requires more physics than can be responsibly handled in a philosophy term paper.

Scope and risk assessment. One semester is adequate for two tightly focused case studies, a conceptual framework, and a critical conclusion. It is not adequate for a full history of statistical mechanics, a technical defense of quantum field-theoretic formalisms, or a comprehensive evaluation of all varieties of structural realism. To manage this risk, the proposal includes a fallback: should the quantum case prove too technically dense, the project will restrict itself to the classical Boltzmann-Gibbs transition, deepening the conceptual argument and using the quantum case only as a suggestive extension.

Keep the framing precise, the ambitions modest, and the self-criticism explicit. The deliverable is a plan that convinces a reader the work is both necessary and doable within the time available.
