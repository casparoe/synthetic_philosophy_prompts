I am drafting a novel in which a neurosurgeon explains commissurotomy research to a patient who fears losing her singular sense of self. A fellow writer who attended a recent neuroscience colloquium summarized the philosophical debate for me in these terms: after the corpus callosum is severed, the right hemisphere does not become a second person with a hidden autobiography; rather, it sustains a fragmented, non-linguistic spatial awareness that never acquires the narrative indexical "I." Meanwhile, the left hemisphere's interpreter module retroactively weaves behavior into one continuous life story, not by accessing the right hemisphere's contents but by confabulating coherence. The real disruption, she insisted, is not phenomenal unity—which remains intact through subcortical integration—but interhemispheric access, leaving the patient as one consciousness with an internal blind spot, comparable to a single camera whose dead pixel is corrected by image software the viewer never detects. I intend to put this in the surgeon's mouth, yet I worry it smooths over real disputes. Please audit this summary in American English: identify which claims about hemispheric specialization and the interpreter module are broadly accurate, which subtly conflate access consciousness with phenomenal unity, and whether the camera analogy misrepresents the phenomenology reported by split-brain patients. Would defenders of a divided-consciousness view accept that subcortical pathways preserve basal integration, or is that an overstatement? And does the conclusion—that the patient remains one self with an unnoticed lacuna—actually follow from the corrected premises, or does it depend on the distortions?
