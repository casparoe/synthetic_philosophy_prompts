Please explain the conceptual core of the material-constitution problem as it arises between a statue and the clay that makes it up, but do not merely rehearse the familiar puzzle about identity over time or the replacement of parts. Instead, use that framework to analyze what philosophers of art call instruction-based or multiple-instance works—artifacts defined not by a unique physical substrate but by a set of specifications that can be realized in different materials, such as a conceptual installation rebuilt with different components or a score performed on different instruments. Specifically, clarify whether the statue-clay tension dissolves, intensifies, or changes its logical structure when the work in question lacks a single continuous physical bearer. Address whether the standard metaphysical options—accepting coincident objects, adopting a deflationary conventionalist stance that treats the work as a useful grouping rather than a distinct entity, or denying that there is any object over and above the material—remain equally available and plausible in these cases, or whether the absence of a unique substrate forces a revision in how constitution itself is understood. Focus on the conceptual mechanics and on what the multi-instantiability of such works reveals about whether material constitution requires spatiotemporal continuity, not on art-historical examples.
