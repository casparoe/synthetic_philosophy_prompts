I serve on the long-range planning review board for a mid-sized municipality, and we are required to evaluate infrastructure and land-use proposals with effects that unfold over a century or more—river rerouting, industrial zoning expansions, climate-adaptation resettlement corridors, and large-scale ecological restoration contracts. Because these projects alter where people live, work, partner, and migrate, they change the genetic and social composition of future generations: the specific individuals who will exist in 2125 depend on which proposals we approve today. I need a reusable operational procedure—call it the Nonidentity Audit—that my board can run on any proposal without requiring philosophy training, but that remains rigorously faithful to the logic of the nonidentity problem. The core idea is that if a policy determines which people come into existence, it cannot be rejected on the ground that it harms those particular people, since they would not have existed otherwise; yet we still need to detect genuinely objectionable outcomes, such as engineering a large future population that lives in severe deprivation or altering demographic trajectories for reasons that depend on identity-contingent comparisons.

Please construct the audit as an ordered checklist of questions, specifying for each what counts as a sufficient answer, where the board must stop with a clear approval or rejection, and where the question is irreducibly normative and must be referred to the elected council with a flagged value conflict. The procedure must include one niche operational tool—the Composition-Sensitive Substitution Test—defined within the prompt itself: instead of comparing the proposal to a "do nothing" baseline, the board compares two counterfactual futures with the same number of future people but different welfare distributions, asking whether the objection survives when the identities are held constant. Apply the full procedure to three worked cases: (1) a flood-control river rerouting that forces relocation into an industrial zone with elevated pollution exposure, thereby changing settlement patterns and, consequently, who is born over the next century; (2) a subsidized pre-conception screening program that increases births of children carrying a specific heritable condition that is non-debilitating but heavily stigmatized; and (3) a carbon-capture forest plantation that renders traditional subsistence land unusable, shifting migration and partnership patterns. For each case, show the step-by-step evaluation, identify the point at which identity-dependence blocks a standard harm claim, and flag where ordinary moral intuition conflicts with the audit’s output—especially in case (1), where the procedure may recommend approval despite the creation of a large, moderately deprived future population that would not have existed otherwise. Finally, list the explicit limitations: what kinds of objections the audit cannot process, which factual inputs it requires that we may not have, and the specific judgment calls that remain even after the procedure is complete.
