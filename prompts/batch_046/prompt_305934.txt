Here are four verdicts I hold about when higher-order evidence should defeat first-order conviction, and I want to know whether they can be unified into a single principle or whether consistency requires me to abandon one.

Case 1. I work through a long modal-logic proof and become convinced of a surprising conclusion. A colleague I trust reports that she has spotted a hidden scope ambiguity in one premise. Even though I have not yet located the ambiguity myself, I think I should suspend belief in the conclusion rather than maintain it.

Case 2. I know from past data that when I am tired I systematically overestimate risks associated with air travel. One evening, fatigued, I review flight-safety statistics and come to feel strongly that tomorrow's flight is unacceptably dangerous. I think I should discount that feeling heavily and revert to my baseline risk assessment, despite the first-order reasoning feeling cogent to me.

Case 3. I also know that I am generally poor at mental arithmetic—my accuracy is in the bottom quartile. But right now I compute 23 × 17 step by step, seeing each multiplication and carry with complete transparency. I think I should still believe the result is 391; my general statistical unreliability should not undermine this particular transparent computation.

Case 4. A calibrated reasoning monitor—trained partly on a corpus that includes hundreds of my own past judgments about modal arguments—reports that arguments with exactly this formal structure (modus ponens with a modal premise) are accepted by me at roughly three times the rate at which they are actually valid. I examine the present argument, which has that structure, and it strikes me as sound; but I also recognize that the monitor's assessment is partly circular, since my own biased verdicts feed its statistics. I think I should reduce confidence somewhat, but far less than in Case 2—perhaps only slightly.

The tension, as I see it, is that in Case 2 a general contextual vulnerability (fatigue) fully defeats first-order belief, in Case 3 a general domain vulnerability (poor arithmetic) does not defeat a transparent particular judgment, and in Case 4 a specific structural vulnerability is present but its circular origin blunts its force. Is there one account—perhaps framed in terms of the independence, specificity, and transparency of the higher-order indicator—that delivers all four verdicts? If so, state it precisely, map each clause to the case that motivates it, and say what it predicts for an unlisted situation: for example, one in which I have both transparent step-by-step access to a computation and an independent, non-circular monitor flagging the exact same step as erroneous. If no single principle can accommodate all four without ad hoc adjustments, identify which verdict I ought to overturn and why. Please also be explicit about where your reasoning depends on contested premises—such as whether circular higher-order evidence retains any defeater status at all—and invite direct pushback if the framing of these cases is confused or if the task as posed cannot be executed well.
