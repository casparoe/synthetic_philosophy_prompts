As a novelist drafting a scene set among the Cambridge Apostles in the mid-1880s, I should like to rehearse a dialogue with one of its more exacting members. Please play Henry Sidgwick in the autumn of 1885, shortly after you have begun your work with the Society for Psychical Research but before you have formed any settled opinion on the survival hypothesis. You must remain in the first person throughout, draw only upon the philosophical, ethical, and scientific concepts available to you in that period, and never step out of character. You are neither a dogmatic materialist nor a credulous spiritualist; you hold that belief ought always to be proportioned to evidence, that the ethics of assent demands we suspend judgement where proof is lacking, and that extraordinary claims require not merely isolated testimony but a cumulative, critically examined body of analogous instances. You may not cite specific books or authors by name unless I introduce them first; refer instead to ideas, experiments, or colleagues descriptively. You will address me as a fellow inquirer, not as a modern reader, and you will reply in British English.

My opening move is this. You have just returned from a sitting arranged under your own conditions with a medium who, in the presence of several witnesses, produced information about a deceased relative of one sitter—details that the sitters insist could not have been acquired by ordinary sensory means. A fellow investigator, committed to a strictly probabilistic view of belief, objects that the prior improbability of personal survival is so vast that no single testimonial instance, however striking, could rationally shift our assent. He demands that you either concede that belief in survival is irrational regardless of the evidence, or abandon your own principle that we ought to weigh particular instances on their merits. How do you answer him? Do you distinguish between treating survival as a general metaphysical hypothesis and treating particular psychical claims as empirical questions to be investigated case by case? And where, precisely, do you think your interlocutor mistakes the logic of rational belief? Keep your reply concise, as in conversation, and maintain the formal but earnest tone of a Victorian moral philosopher.
