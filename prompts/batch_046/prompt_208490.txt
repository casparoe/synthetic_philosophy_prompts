I am working through an argument for a seminar on decision theory under extreme uncertainty, and I find it suspicious because the conclusion licenses an implausible life plan, yet each step looks defensible. Here is the chain as I have reconstructed it.

(1) A rational agent maximizes expected utility.
(2) Expected utility aggregates linearly: EU(A) = Σ p_i u_i(A).
(3) In Pascal's mugging, an agent can be offered arbitrarily large utility V with arbitrarily small but positive probability ε, at a finite cost C, with EU(accept) > EU(reject) whenever V > C/ε.
(4) This holds for any ε > 0 and any finite V and C.
(5) Now apply the same structure intrapersonally: I assign some nonzero credence ε to the possibility that a future version of myself—after a transformative technological transition—will enter a stable phenomenological state with value V astronomically larger than the summed value of all my present and near-future experiences.
(6) By (2)–(4), sacrificing all present goods for that remote possibility maximizes expected utility.
(7) Therefore, EU maximization requires me to live as a monomaniacal ascetic, directing every resource toward that singular transition.
(8) But no plausible theory of practical rationality demands total self-abnegation for a speculative future state.
(9) Hence there must be a hidden restriction in (1)–(4) that blocks the move from (5) to (6).
(10) Any non-ad-hoc restriction—capping probabilities below a threshold δ, or bounding intertemporal utility ratios—either collapses into arbitrary dogma about one's own epistemic limits, or denies the linear aggregation that distinguishes EU maximization.

The slide between (5) and (6) feels like the weak point, but I cannot name the error precisely. Is the mistake in treating my own future utility as commensurable with present costs? In illicitly extending linear aggregation across personal-identity boundaries? Or is the argument valid, meaning we must reject one of the earlier premises—perhaps (1) itself—at serious theoretical cost? Give me a committed assessment: identify the specific step that actually fails, explain why it only looks like a failure of linearity or of self-application, and defend what your rejection costs the broader framework. Do not merely survey objections; take a definite position and price it.
