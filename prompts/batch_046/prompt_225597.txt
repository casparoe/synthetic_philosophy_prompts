In my graduate seminar on comparative moral philosophy this term, two doctoral students—let us call them M and N—became entangled during our discussion of South Asian ethics in a disagreement that I believe isolates a genuinely unsettled question about the structure of Buddhist normativity, though I want an independent conceptual audit rather than my own adjudication. The dispute is whether classical Buddhist ethics is best reconstructed as a species of consequentialism, with the cessation of dukkha as its sovereign aim and the precepts as defeasible rules of thumb, or whether it is irreducibly centered on the moral quality of volition (cetana) in a way that blocks consequentialist assimilation.

M opened with the consequentialist reconstruction: the Four Noble Truths identify suffering and its elimination as the foundational problem, and every normative prescription—right action, right livelihood, the perfections—derives authority instrumentally from its contribution to that end. She argued that the Mahayana doctrine of upaya, or skillful means, confirms this reading; in the canonical narrative of the ship's captain who kills a robber to save hundreds of merchants, the captain's act is praised not despite its lethal outcome but because the compassionate intention produces the best aggregate result, which looks like direct outcome-weighing licensed by expected consequences. N replied that this misinterprets both upaya and the Abhidharmic account of karma: moral evaluation turns primarily on the root mental factors (kusala or akusala mula) present at the moment of volition, not on posterior results. Thus a well-intentioned act that accidentally produces widespread harm does not generate negative karma, while a malicious act that accidentally benefits others still does. M objected that this makes Buddhism acutely vulnerable to moral luck: if intention alone determines moral status regardless of actual consequences, then an agent who sincerely intends healing but through negligence causes an epidemic is blameless, which seems implausible. N conceded the counterintuitive implication but defended it as Buddhism's distinctive virtue—its explicit rejection of moral luck—though he struggled to articulate a principled boundary between acceptable outcome-indifference and culpable recklessness.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Rate each of the four positions—M's consequentialist reconstruction, N's volition-centered rebuttal invoking cetana and upaya, M's moral-luck objection, and N's defense via the rejection of moral luck—on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification for each rating. Specifically, assess whether upaya genuinely licenses outcome-based reasoning or whether N's interpretation remains defensible, and determine whether N's rejection of moral luck distinguishes Buddhism from consequentialism in a stable way, or whether it collapses into a form of virtue ethics that implicitly evaluates intentions partly by their likely consequences. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want conceptual clarity on whether the consequentialist analogy survives once the roles of cetana, karmic root-conditions, and skillful means are precisely specified.
