As a retired biologist who has only recently begun reading philosophy seriously, I find myself stuck on a dispute I cannot properly diagnose. In Lewis’s framework, a convention is a behavioral regularity sustained by common knowledge of mutual preferences and rationality: we drive on the right because we all know that we all prefer coordination and will act accordingly. Over the past several decades, evolutionary game theorists have developed models—replicator dynamics, learning rules, stochastic stability—showing how norms can emerge and persist in populations of agents who lack any such common knowledge, who may not be deliberatively rational, and who observe one another only with noise. Philosophers of language and social ontology often treat these evolutionary accounts as illuminating but ultimately about something else, “norms” rather than “conventions,” while the evolutionary modelers often present their work as having dissolved Lewis’s rationalist puzzle altogether. Yet neither camp seems to have moved the other in years.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily an empirical disagreement about how real human norms form, nor a purely verbal dispute over the word “convention,” but a deeper disagreement about explanatory adequacy in social ontology: whether we must cite agents’ higher-order beliefs and reasoning, or whether functional stability in a dynamic system is sufficient. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is a hidden formal disagreement—perhaps about uniqueness conditions for equilibria, or about whether evolutionary stability can capture the arbitrariness that Lewis thought essential to convention.

Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Please distinguish what is genuinely settled—for instance, that evolutionary dynamics can produce stable coordination patterns—from what remains genuinely contested among people who understand both literatures well, and do not present one side’s framing as the consensus view. In particular, is the stalemate due to different standards of explanation, to a hidden empirical question about human cognition, or to the two sides answering different questions—one about the logic of coordination, one about population dynamics—under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal result linking common-knowledge conditions to evolutionary stability, a conceptual distinction that separates two previously conflated phenomena, or an argument that one framework is a limiting case of the other? I want a defended diagnosis rather than a survey, and I want an honest assessment of whether anything could genuinely move this debate, or whether the impasse is structural.
