I have been trying to understand whether a predictive-processing account of emotion—according to which emotions are not innate affect programs or propositional judgments but constructed predictions that integrate interoceptive signals with conceptual knowledge—renders the traditional philosophical taxonomy of emotions as judgments, perceptions, or feelings obsolete. This framework seems to dissolve the classic recalcitrance objection to cognitivism: if fear is a predictive inference rather than a belief, then its persistence after explicit risk-assessment is not a failure of rational revision but merely the temporal inertia of a generative model, analogous to a visual afterimage. Given this, is it accurate to say that the dispute over whether emotions are judgments, perceptions, or feelings has been superseded by neuroscience? Or does the predictive framework still leave unsettled whether these constructed states possess the right kind of intentionality—directedness toward objects in the world—to count as genuine emotions rather than as sophisticated interoceptive illusions? Please separate what is well established in the relevant literature from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and cognitive scientists who work on this topic, and do not present any single framework as the consensus view.
