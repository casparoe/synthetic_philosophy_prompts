In my economic history module, our lecturer has offered verdicts on whether particular technological and institutional outcomes were path-dependent—locked in by contingent origins and high switching costs—or whether they represent functional convergence that would have arrived regardless of specific historical accidents.

So far, she judges the QWERTY keyboard layout path-dependent; she judges the British railway gauge of four feet eight and a half inches path-dependent, despite later Parliamentary standardisation; she judges the market victory of VHS over Betamax not path-dependent, arguing that consumer demand for longer recording times made a long-play format functionally inevitable; she judges the global adoption of metric measurement in scientific publishing convergent, allowing that a rationalised imperial scheme might have served in counterfactual worlds; and she judges the persistence of left-hand traffic in the United Kingdom and Japan path-dependent, treating it as a pure coordination equilibrium with no engineering basis.

We are now examining the standardisation of intermodal shipping containers at twenty-foot and forty-foot lengths, derived largely from existing North American truck chassis dimensions rather than from maritime optima, and locked in by the International Organisation for Standardisation in the late 1960s. Predict her verdict on whether this container standard is path-dependent or convergent, set out the two or three rules that best explain her pattern—distinguishing, for example, between physical infrastructure lock-in and consumer-market selection—and identify which further case would best discriminate between those rules. Please reply in British English.
