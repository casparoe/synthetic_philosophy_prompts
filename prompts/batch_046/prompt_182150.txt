I have been working through Simone Weil's essay calling for the abolition of political parties, where she contends that party membership necessarily replaces individual moral and intellectual judgment with collective passion, rendering parties structurally incompatible with justice. At the same time, in her notebooks on labor and social obligation—particularly her reflections on factory life and the conditions for what she calls attention to objective reality—she treats collective institutions not as inherent threats to judgment but as indispensable media for its formation, arguing that isolated individuals lack the discipline required to perceive truth. This seems to generate a tension in her framework. If collective structures are necessary to cultivate the very capacity for truthful judgment, and if parties are merely one organizational form among others—like unions, religious orders, or workplaces—then singling parties out for abolition appears to depend on an unstated distinction. Is her argument that parties are uniquely resistant to being disciplined by attention, perhaps because democratic competition demands unconditional loyalty to collective victory? Or is she implicitly relying on a theological premise—drawn from her comparison of parties to churches without God—that any collective claiming to embody justice is necessarily idolatrous, regardless of whether its members practice the kind of impersonal attention she elsewhere demands? In other words, does her abolitionist conclusion actually follow from her epistemology of attention, or does it require a separate moral claim about the impossibility of collective loyalty being reconciled with orientation toward the absolute good?
