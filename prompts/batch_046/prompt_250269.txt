As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy systematically, I attended a discussion group where two philosophers—Dr. Elena Soren and Dr. Marcus Voss—debated Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism and its bearing on scientific causation. I could not determine who was correct, and I am hoping you can adjudicate their exchange with precision.

Soren began by insisting that Ghazālī's denial of necessary causal connections is not an attack on science but on metaphysical overreach. She said that for Ghazālī, fire and cotton are present together with burning, and God creates the burning in their presence; the correlation is perfect and exceptionless, so science, which studies regularities and predictive laws, remains entirely intact. The only casualty is the philosopher's claim that fire possesses an intrinsic, necessary power to burn.

Voss replied that this conflates predictive success with explanatory depth. He argued that when modern science discovers that combustion requires oxidation, it treats the chemical properties of oxygen and cellulose as explaining why cotton burns—not merely as correlates, but as mechanisms that carry causal work. On occasionalism, oxidation and burning are simply two effects God creates in tandem; there is no explanatory arrow from mechanism to outcome, only a simultaneous divine decree. Therefore occasionalism collapses scientific explanation into taxonomy.

Soren countered that explanation is itself contested. She noted that on a covering-law model, Ghazālī's divine habits provide perfect, exceptionless laws, and on a causal-mechanical model, mechanisms can be understood as the occasions for divine action rather than autonomous powers. The engineer who knows that flipping a switch produces light needs only the reliable correlation; she need not commit to electricity as an intrinsic flow. Thus occasionalism preserves scientific practice without endorsing metaphysical mechanism.

Voss then pressed what he called the redundancy objection: if mechanisms carry no causal power but only occasion divine action, then the correct ontology underlying chemistry is theological, not chemical. You cannot have it both ways—either mechanisms matter causally, or science is merely cataloguing the habits of God. He added that even strong emergence in systems biology, which allows macro-level properties not reducible to physics, still commits to domain-specific modal constraints: once the organizational law is instantiated, the outcome must occur within that domain. Ghazālī denies even that constrained necessity, since God could alter the habit at any moment. So emergence is not occasionalism in disguise.

Soren responded by invoking modern physics. She pointed out that Bell's theorem demonstrates that local micro-physical properties do not determine macro-observables in quantum mechanics; correlations require non-local constraints or holistic conditions that are not locally mechanistic. If physics can abandon local necessary causation without abandoning science—replacing it with probabilistic laws governed by unitary evolution—then Ghazālī's more general abandonment is not categorically alien to scientific reasoning, but merely extends the same humility about local production to all domains.

Voss rejected the analogy. He said quantum mechanics replaces local determinism with probabilistic necessities—governed by the Born rule and Hamiltonian dynamics—that still constrain outcomes with mathematical rigor. Occasionalism has no analogous constraint; the divine habit is contingent on will alone. Comparing quantum correlation to occasionalist creation is like comparing a chess game with fixed rules to one where an unseen player changes the rules every move. The former remains science; the latter is perpetual miracle.

Please go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about each specific claim—particularly about whether occasionalism preserves explanation, whether emergence offers a genuine parallel, and whether the quantum analogy holds. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both have missed, perhaps about the distinction between causal governance and causal production, or about how a retired scientist should regard the metaphysical commitments actually embedded in laboratory practice.
