I have been working through Harry Frankfurt’s hierarchical account of freedom of the will and Daniel Dennett’s well-known essay on the varieties of free will worth wanting, and I am attempting to apply these distinctions to a design problem in institutional governance that my colleagues and I have been debating in a seminar on collective agency. Specifically, we are examining distributed decision-making protocols—similar to those found in certain decentralized autonomous organizations—where participants routinely express first-order preferences about particular resource allocations, yet hold divergent, and sometimes unstable, second-order attitudes toward the kind of collective agent they wish the institution to become. Some members identify wholeheartedly with their immediate preferences; others wish they desired different outcomes altogether; and still others maintain only weak, strategically adopted meta-preferences that lack the reflective endorsement Frankfurt associates with genuine personhood.

Given this framing, please describe the most important implications of Frankfurt’s hierarchical model and Dennett’s pragmatic taxonomy for how such institutions ought to structure accountability and responsibility. In particular, I want to understand whether the presence of authentically second-order volitions—rather than merely instrumental meta-preferences—should change how we assign blame or credit for collective decisions that violate a participant’s first-order interests yet align with their endorsed higher-order desires. How ought we to treat cases in which a member’s second-order identification with a collective outcome appears to have been manufactured through informational framing or social pressure rather than arrived at through the kind of sustained reflective equilibrium that Frankfurt takes to be essential? Furthermore, if Dennett is correct that not all varieties of free will are worth wanting, which specific capacities should such a governance system cultivate or protect, and which hierarchical inconsistencies should it treat as disqualifying for full participatory standing? Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; I am seeking conceptual clarification that can be applied directly to our model of institutional design.
