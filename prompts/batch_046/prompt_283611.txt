I'm a graduate student working at the intersection of philosophy of biology and conservation ethics, and I've kept noticing a recurring discursive pattern in how practicing scientists invoke species boundaries—one that doesn't seem to map neatly onto the standard realism/pluralism/instrumentalism taxonomy I'm familiar with. In evolutionary studies, a researcher might describe two populations as linked by continuous gene flow through a hybrid zone or along a ring, treating the boundary between them as a conventional cutoff justified only by administrative convenience; yet in the very same grant proposal or recovery plan, they invoke that identical boundary as marking a real, intrinsic division with its own evolutionary trajectory that demands separate management "because this species is distinct." The shift is not between different technical concepts—say, the biological species concept versus the phylogenetic one—because the same label and the same boundary are retained; rather, the ontological weight given to that boundary seems to change with the practical context, without the researcher flagging any change in justification. I see parallel moves elsewhere: microbiologists who defend 16S rRNA similarity thresholds as pragmatic conventions calibrated differently for different bacterial clades, yet speak of "discovering a new species" in language that sounds like uncovering a pre-existing natural kind; or paleontologists who treat fossil lineages as genuinely bounded historical entities when constructing phylogenies, but as arbitrary temporal segments when discussing anagenesis and gradual transformation.

What I'm trying to identify is not the broad question of whether species are real, nor the familiar pluralist claim that different concepts suit different groups, but this more specific oscillation within a single research program—sometimes within a single paper—where the same boundary is defended with ontological language in one context and with purely instrumental or pragmatic language in another, without acknowledgment that the grounding has shifted. Does this pattern have an established name in philosophy of biology or in science studies? If it is covered by existing frameworks—perhaps a form of context-sensitive realism, or pragmatic pluralism applied intra-conceptually—I'd like to understand precisely how it differs from those, since in the cases I observe the speaker is not saying "we apply concept A here and concept B there," but rather "this is the species," while silently altering what kind of claim that is. And if there is no established term for this precise move, how should it be carved off from both ordinary pluralism and from mere inconsistency: what are its necessary conditions, its typical failure modes, and the boundary cases where it collapses into one of the neighboring positions?
