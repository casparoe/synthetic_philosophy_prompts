For a dissertation chapter on democratic boundaries, I'm focusing on non-territorial climate-governance institutions—bodies that set carbon budgets or geoengineering protocols without being tied to a single state. The boundary problem asks who belongs to the demos, and the standard debate opposes the all-affected-interests principle to the all-subjected principle. It seems to me that for these institutions the two principles converge: anyone affected by a climate decision is also subject to its coercive economic and environmental effects, so the demos should be defined functionally by exposure rather than by citizenship. If that convergence holds, why do democratic theorists still treat territorial membership as the default and frame the inclusion of outsiders or future generations as an exceptional problem rather than the baseline? Is there a principled distinction—between causal affectedness and political subjection—that breaks this convergence, or does the persistence of territorial framing depend on an unstated normative commitment to state sovereignty that democratic theory hasn't defended?
