At a recent philosophy-of-morality reading group, two participants—Maya and Jonah—debated whether cross-cultural divergence in folk morality undermines moral objectivity. Maya opened by noting the familiar WEIRD/non-WEIRD pattern: Western-educated subjects tend to treat harm and fairness as central moral categories, while subjects in many small-scale societies weight loyalty, authority, and purity at least as heavily. She argued that if moral objectivity requires convergence on basic moral axioms, this divergence is fatal—there is no neutral standpoint from which to adjudicate whether loyalty should ever outweigh harm. Jonah replied that Maya confuses moral psychology with moral ontology; apparent divergence often dissolves once you distinguish moral output (the judgment) from moral input (empirical beliefs about social structure and coordination needs). On his view, different cultures apply universal cooperative principles to different environments, so the divergence is practical rather than metaphysical. Maya then pressed a more specific challenge that she called normative framing incommensurability: in some societies, refusing food to an in-group member is wrong because it violates a relational status-constituted duty, whereas in others the same refusal is wrong only if it produces harm. The moral kind “wrongness of refusal” is constituted differently—by relational position in one case, by consequence in the other—so Jonah’s “different inputs” model cannot capture it, because the very category shifts. Jonah countered with what he termed structural equivalence under normative translation: even if surface moral concepts differ, both cultures may be tracking violations of cooperative expectations indexed to different social architectures. He added that Maya’s incommensurability argument proves too much—by her standard, physicists using different coordinate systems would be describing different worlds. Maya responded that Jonah’s translation argument assumes a neutral metalanguage of cooperation, but that metalanguage is itself culturally loaded—specifically, it reflects the WEIRD liberal framework of impartial cooperation—so his resolution smuggles in the objectivity he aims to defend. They asked me to referee, and I could not decide. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, identify the specific point each gets correct, and state what both are missing—particularly whether there is a coherent middle position between universal axioms and radical incommensurability that can accommodate thick moral concepts without collapsing into relativism.
