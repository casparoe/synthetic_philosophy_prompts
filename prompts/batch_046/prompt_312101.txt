So I just finished a book on politcal ethics (retired biologist here, pretty new to philosphy) and I keep hitting this dirty hands problem -- the idea that leaders sometimes have to do morally ugly stuff, like lying or ordering force, to actually help people. The analogy everyone leans on is surgery: the surgeon gets covered in blood and breaks skin to save a life, so being dirty is just part of the job, and we forgive it because the outcome is good. My friend says that's the classic framing, but he pointed out that in surgery there's informed consent and a clear endpoint where you wash up, while in politics the citizens never signed a form, and the dirt -- like a lie told years ago -- doesn't wash off; it just sticks to the person's character. Can you lay out the mapping precisely (surgeon corresponds to ?, patient to ?, blood/guts to ?, washing up to ?), then show exactly where the analogy cracks, especially around consent, whether the harm is reversible, and whether the leader really knows what's healthy for the public? And once those cracks are on the table, does the conclusion still hold -- that we should excuse the politician's moral violations just because the result turned out okay? Or is there a better analogy that doesn't smuggle in the idea that the leader is a doctor and everyone else is the patient?
