After a long argument last night with a friend who insists that Strawson's reactive attitudes covertly require libertarian free will—she kept saying that resentment only makes sense if the agent genuinely could have done otherwise in some metaphysically robust sense—I am now preparing a twenty-minute talk for our philosophy-of-action workshop. My thesis is a modified Strawsonian view: reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation) are not emotional judgments about an agent's metaphysical freedom, but rather normative repair practices that stabilize cooperative expectations when they are breached, functioning analogously to error-correction protocols in distributed systems. I argue that this functional reconstrual preserves the reactive/objective distinction without smuggling in desert, and that it explains why we suspend reactive attitudes toward young children or the severely mentally ill—not because they lack free will, but because they fall outside the cooperative framework within which repair practices are meaningful. The audience will be mostly compatibilist metaphysicians with Kantian leanings (they will worry I am reducing morality to social engineering), one legal philosopher who defends retributivism (he will ask whether I have eliminated punishment's expressive value), and a moral psychologist who studies emotion regulation (she will ask whether I am describing actual emotional dynamics or an idealized functional model).
