Over the past month, since recovering from a neurological procedure that left me with prolonged depersonalization, I have been trying to reconstruct what justifies my belief that the person who entered the operating room is the same being who is now asking this question. In a recent conversation, a clinician dismissed my anxiety by appealing to the familiar slogan cogito, ergo sum — usually rendered as I think, therefore I am — treating it as if it settled both my present existence and the continuity of my identity across the episode. I suspect it does neither automatically, but I need help seeing exactly how many different claims are hiding in that sentence.

Can you lay out the precise theses this slogan might express, arranged from the thinnest to the thickest? I have in mind at least these candidates: a purely indexical, present-tense guarantee that there is a current locus of thought; a metaphysical proof that this locus is a non-physical substance; a claim that such a substance necessarily persists through any interruption of consciousness; a dismissal of psychological continuity as irrelevant to personal identity; and perhaps a performative demonstration that skeptical doubt about one's own existence is self-refuting. For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, whether it is defensible, whether it is false, and — most importantly — whether it actually addresses the specific worry I described: the fear that the pre-procedure subject and the present thinking subject might be numerically distinct despite my now reporting an uninterrupted stream of experience. I am not asking for a bibliography or a history of commentary; I want a disambiguated menu I can bring back to my own reflection, with clear verdicts on which readings would genuinely support the clinician's reassurance and which would merely change the subject.
