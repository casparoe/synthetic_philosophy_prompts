Here's an argument I built for a seminar and now find suspicious because the conclusion feels like a cheap trick. (1) Rationality means picking the option with the best expected payoff at every decision point. (2) If I'm hit by a first strike and I retaliate, I die; if I don't, I survive. So ex-post retaliation is never best. (3) So a fully rational agent never retaliates after being struck. (4) Oponents know this, so the threat is empty and deterrence collapses. (5) To fix this I have to automate retaliation—build a dead-hand system that fires without human input—so my future self literally can't choose not to. (6) But setting that up now is also a decision, and I do it because deterrence working is the best expected outcome. So I'm being rational now precisely to guarentee I'm irrational later. (7) If rationality can require constructing mechanisms that violate it, then rationality isn't a consistent property of an agent; it's just whatever strategy happens to win. (8) That collapses the distinction between rational precommitment and self-manipulation, and it seems like I could justify locking myself into anything. (9) So either nuclear deterrence is impossible for rational agents, or the concept of rationality is self-undermining. I want to keep (1) and (5), but I feel like there's a slight between (6) and (7) that I'm not seeing. Which step actually breaks, and what does rejecting it cost me?
