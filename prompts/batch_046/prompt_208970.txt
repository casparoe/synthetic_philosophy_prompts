A philosophy-of-science handout summarizes Kuhn's view of incommensurability as follows: "When a scientific paradigm shifts, the taxonomic categories scientists use change meaning, so rival paradigms do not merely disagree about answers but about what counts as a legitimate question. For example, Newtonian and Einsteinian physicists do not just assign different values to mass; they mean different things by 'mass' — invariant versus frame-dependent — and there is no neutral formal language in which to translate their equations without semantic loss. The incommensurability extends even to mathematics: Newton's calculus and Einstein's tensor calculus are, on this account, different formal languages rather than interchangeable tools applied to one reality. Because evidential standards and rules of inference are internal to each paradigm, there is no rational algorithm for adjudicating between them; scientists convert for psychological or sociological reasons, not because one view is closer to truth. Hence Kuhn denied that science is cumulative and claimed that cross-paradigm progress is not merely hard to measure but conceptually impossible."

How fair is this summary? Please audit it claim by claim: which parts reflect Kuhn's actual arguments about paradigm change and conceptual taxonomy, which parts exaggerate his thesis into the stronger "radical incommensurability" often attributed to him by critics, and which parts are straightforward misreadings. In particular, address whether Kuhn held that mathematical formalisms are themselves incommensurable, whether he denied all rational comparison across paradigms, and whether he treated cross-paradigm progress as conceptually undefined rather than locally evaluable. Also indicate whether the handout's conclusion — that Kuhnian incommensurability makes scientific choice essentially non-rational — survives these corrections.
