I'm preparing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of mind course, and I need one essay question on Daniel Dennett's framework of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—that students cannot answer by reciting standard examples like the chess computer or the thermostat. To push them past memorization, embed the following constructed scenario: imagine a synthetic predictive-processing agent implemented on neuromorphic hardware, organized as a hierarchical generative model that minimizes variational free energy. From the physical stance, it is only spiking silicon; from the design stance, it is only layered prediction-error minimization with precision-weighting. Yet its behavior invites intentional ascription: it appears to maintain false beliefs about hidden environmental states, to update those beliefs when precision is reallocated, and to act on them in ways that anticipate the behavior of other agents—including, in one variant, apparently deceiving a partner agent about a hidden state.

The exam question should ask students to address, in about 90 minutes and roughly 700 words, the following issues: (a) whether the intentional stance offers predictive leverage that the design stance cannot provide, or whether free-energy dynamics fully subsume intentional explanation; (b) whether Dennett's criterion for real patterns—that they support robust, projectible counterfactual predictions—applies to intentional patterns here, especially when the design-level dynamics are completely known; (c) whether this case supports eliminativism about intentional patterns, or rather a stance-relative realism in which intentional patterns are real but ontologically dependent on design-level dynamics; and (d) whether the appearance of deception changes the analysis, particularly if the design stance can only recover the behavior by importing intentional-level variables.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, clearly worded for students.
2. A model answer of approximately 700 words.
3. A marking scheme with five must-hit analytical moves, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically miss each move.
4. In both the model answer and the marking notes, clearly separate what is settled within Dennett's framework from what is genuinely contested in the philosophy of mind—do not present a contested claim, such as whether real patterns are stance-independent, as the consensus view.
5. Write in American English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the answer should rely on conceptual analysis available to a student without external sources.
