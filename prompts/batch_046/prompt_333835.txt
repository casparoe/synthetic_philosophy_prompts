I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern when people reason about specific individuals in hypothetical situations. First, someone considers an alternative scenario involving a particular person—asking whether Socrates might have fled at his trial, or whether I might have pursued medicine had I grown up elsewhere. In this stage, they speak as though the very same individual is present in that scenario, drawing conclusions about essential traits or deep preferences: that courage is not necessary to Socrates, or that my actual career reveals something fixed about me. But if pressed—if one points out that the same person cannot both flee and stand trial, or cannot both be me and have entirely different formative experiences—they retreat to saying that the figure in the scenario is not literally that person, but only an analogue, a "version," or a representation. The puzzling feature is that they do not treat this retreat as abandoning their original conclusion; instead, both moves are taken to support the same claim. They want the intuitive pull of direct presence to establish the point, and the representational escape hatch to avoid contradiction. Does this specific oscillation—between treating a hypothetical figure as the literal individual and as a mediated stand-in—have an established name in philosophical discussions of identity across worlds? How exactly does it map onto the dispute between views that permit one individual to exist in multiple worlds and views that deny this, allowing only analogues? If no precise term exists, should we classify it as an equivocation on "possible," as an ambiguity in how we refer across hypothetical scenarios, or as a distinct argumentative failure with its own structure?
