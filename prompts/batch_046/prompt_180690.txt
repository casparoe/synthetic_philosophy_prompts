Consider the institutional practice—adopted in various municipalities—of “counter-commemorative co-naming” for streets and monuments honoring figures whose records include serious moral wrongs. Rather than removing the statue or renaming the street, the city retains the original designation but adds an officially sanctioned parallel name honoring a victim, resistor, or excluded figure. The physical signage renders both names visible with formal asymmetry: the original appears in lighter type or smaller scale, while the counter-name carries primary visual weight, and an interpretive plaque frames the pairing as a “dialogue across history” rather than a replacement.

This practice coexists with legal doctrines that treat physical removal of monuments as “historical erasure” subject to stricter procedural review than the original erection, and with funding rules that bar public maintenance of monuments to figures convicted of specific crimes unless the monument is reclassified as an “educational installation” through added critical contextualization.

What philosophical commitments about collective moral responsibility, the ontology of public space, and the ethics of remembrance does this cluster of policies presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does retaining the original trace while visually subordinating it assume that moral wrongs remain permanently attached to commemorative objects, or that wrongs can be neutralized by juxtaposition rather than erasure? Does the asymmetrical typography presuppose that public space distributes honor as a zero-sum resource, or is it compatible with a non-competitive account of remembrance?

Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Is the cluster coherent, or do its parts—protecting monuments from removal, defunding uncontextualized monuments, and reclassifying them through interpretation—rest on inconsistent views about whether a monument’s meaning is fixed by its origin, by present interpretation, or by institutional intention? And if we replaced the implicit theory with one that treats public commemoration as purely expressive of present community values, with no special obligation to preserve historical traces, which specific mechanisms—review boards, signage protocols, funding conditions—would have to be abandoned or redesigned? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that rival theories of value and responsibility could license the same institutional form?
