I'm working through my position on health and disease for a philosophy of medicine reading group, and I want a consistency check before I present it. Here are my current commitments:

(1) I accept a version of Boorse's biostatistical theory for somatic conditions: disease is subnormal functioning relative to an age- and sex-defined reference class, where function is measured by contribution to survival and reproduction.

(2) For mental disorders, I reject Wakefield's evolutionary dysfunction requirement. I think practical or social dysfunction—an inability to meet socially expected roles—is sufficient for disorder status, even when the psychology in question is evolutionarily well-adapted.

(3) Nevertheless, I hold that evolutionary dysfunction, specifically a mismatch between evolved design and present environment, is a necessary condition for any state to count as a genuine disease, mental or physical. Without such a mismatch, statistical deviation is merely natural variation.

(4) I think the concept of health is essentially contested, largely because biological reference classes are pragmatically selected rather than naturally given; there is no unique biological fact about whether a given trait falls within normal limits.

(5) Consequently, I believe public health policy should be guided solely by descriptive biostatistical norms—aiming to reduce deviations from species-typical functioning—without appealing to normative ideals of flourishing or well-being.

Do these hang together? I am particularly unsure about the interaction between (1) and (3), between (2) and (3), and about whether (4) undermines (5). Please audit the set: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict, and rank the real tensions by severity. For each tension, specify the least costly revision and what philosophical price it exacts. Be precise about whether a given conflict requires abandoning naturalism, normativism, or the evolutionary framework, and avoid treating all inconsistencies as equally damaging.
