Consider two climate scientists, Dr. Aris and Dr. Bao, with identical graduate training and full access to the same global temperature datasets and model architectures. They disagree about whether equilibrium climate sensitivity exceeds 3.0°C: Aris holds roughly 75% credence that it does, Bao holds roughly 35%. Aris has a consistently superior calibration record on decadal temperature projections—an adjacent forecasting domain—while Bao has authored more peer-reviewed papers specifically on ECS parameter estimation—the target domain. Each knows these comparative track records. Evaluate whether conciliationism or steadfastness is appropriate. Your answer should address the conditions for being an epistemic peer, the equal-weight view, at least one steadfastist objection that appeals to having the right reasons for one’s belief, whether superior calibration in an adjacent domain transfers epistemic dominance into the target domain, and whether reconciling disagreement over a continuous parameter requires arithmetic averaging of point estimates or a different form of belief revision.

The student wrote:

Conciliationism, as articulated by philosophers like Christensen, requires that when two epistemic peers disagree, each should adjust their credence toward the other’s position—often meeting near the midpoint. Here, Aris and Bao share the same evidence and reasoning capacities, so they satisfy the peer condition. Therefore, they should split the difference, converging near 55% credence.

That said, there is a well-known steadfastist reply called the right reasons objection. As Christensen notes, if you have direct access to the reasoning that supports your view—say, Bao’s specialized grasp of ECS parameterization—then you need not reduce confidence merely because an equally qualified person disagrees. So Bao can remain steadfast provided her reasoning is sound.

Now, Aris’s stronger calibration record in adjacent domains establishes cross-domain epistemic dominance. This dissolves the peer relation in the ECS domain as well: Aris is globally more reliable, so Bao should fully defer to Aris, while Aris is entitled to stay steadfast at her original credence. The mistake in pure conciliation is treating a quantitative parameter dispute as if it demands linear interpolation of point estimates. In reality, probabilities over continuous variables combine through Bayesian conditioning, not arithmetic mean. Consequently, the equal-weight view applies only to discrete, binary disagreements, not to parameter-estimation disputes like this one.
