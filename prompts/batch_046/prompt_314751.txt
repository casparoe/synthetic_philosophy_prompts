I'm giving the opening remarks tomorrow at a workshop called "Curating Attention: Editorial Judgment in the Age of Metrics." The room will be split about even between working journalists and grad students in philosphy and media studies. I want to use Kierkegaard's attack on the press and "the public" — not as decoration, but as the actual frame that tells me what I can and can't say. Specifically, I want to lean on his idea that the press manufactures a phantom "public" (this abstract crowd that is actually nobody) and that this lets everyone dodge real individual commitment. So I can't open with the usual "we're here to serve our readers" or "the public deserves transparency" — by Kierkegaard's lights that's already false, because "the public" is just a leveling mechanism that dissolves responsability.

But I also want to inject something weirder and more specific: the distinction he draws in that 1846 review of Two Ages between an "age of revolution," where people act with passion and take sides, and the "present age" of reflection, where everything is made interesting and nothing is actually important. I want to say that our current metrics-driven environment is basically the age of reflection on steroids, and that the workshop shouldn't be about optimizing for engagement — which is just feeding the phantom — but about recovering some kind of editorial "passion" or inward commitment.

Can you write me about five minutes of opening remarks? They need to land for the journalists without sounding like a lecture, and for the philosophers without dumbing down the Kierkegaard. And please don't let me slip into talking about "the audience" or "the community" as if they're real agents with intentions — that's exactly the mistake the frame is meant to block.
