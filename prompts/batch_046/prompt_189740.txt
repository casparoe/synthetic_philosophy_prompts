As a novelist drafting an anachronistic chamber drama, I ask you to inhabit the following conceit: Blaise Pascal—restored briefly to intellectual life in 1670, confined strictly to the conceptual world he actually inhabited—is presented with a decision-theoretic paradox that bears his name but was articulated only in the early twenty-first century. The scenario, now called Pascal's mugging, involves an agent who assigns a non-vanishing credence to a cosmological or theological hypothesis that entails an unbounded reward for performing a trivial, nearly costless action; the agent refuses, invoking a threshold below which probabilities should not drive choice, yet the interlocutor insists that expectation—probability multiplied by utility—must dominate regardless of prior magnitude. Pascal may not invoke modern decision theory, Bayesian epistemology, or any commentator he did not know; he may draw only on his own mathematics of expectation, the theology of grace and hiddenness developed in his religious writings, the distinction between the esprit géométrique and the esprit de finesse, and his Jansenist conviction that human merit is negligible before divine freedom.

Predict, in precise reasoning, how Pascal would adjudicate the agent's refusal. Would he regard it as a legitimate exercise of the spirit of finesse—recognizing that practical reason possesses boundaries the geometric mind cannot compute—or as a corruption of the wager's logic, in which even an infinitesimal probability of infinite salvation justifies the smallest act of assent? Specifically, address whether his theology of hiddenness offers a principled reason to reject demands for action upon astronomically remote hypotheses, and whether his mathematics of expectation permits a rational cutoff rather than an unconditional multiplication. Reconstruct his response as an internal argument built solely from these resources, indicate precisely where those resources reach their limit, and avoid attributing to him any concept—such as explicit utility maximization or formal credence—that belongs to a later vocabulary.
