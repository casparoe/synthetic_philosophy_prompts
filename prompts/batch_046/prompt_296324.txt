In my ethics working group, people regularly compare navigating moral uncertainty to running a climate-model ensemble: each normative theory is treated like a different GCM parameterization, our credences across theories are like model weights, and practical decision-making is likened to a policy planner choosing a robust strategy that performs acceptably across the spread. The analogy is used to defend aggregating across moral frameworks with meta-level weights rather than committing to a single theory. Audit it precisely: lay out the correspondence table (what plays the role of the physical system, the parameters, the ensemble spread, the validation data, and the planner's objective function), then identify where the mapping collapses—particularly regarding whether moral theories generate empirically testable predictions, whether there is an analog to observational validation, and whether averaging moral prescriptions can yield a coherent action rather than a morally incoherent compromise. Finally, say whether the conclusion—that we should weight and aggregate—actually survives these breaks, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work, and suggest a more apt comparison if one exists.
