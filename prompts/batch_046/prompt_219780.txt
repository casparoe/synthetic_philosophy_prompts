I have been teaching a postgraduate seminar on comparative justice and the portability of institutional designs, and I keep noticing a recurring argumentative loop in discussions of whether the Nordic welfare state can be exported. The pattern is this. One party observes that the Nordic combination of high taxation, universal provision, and low corruption depends upon unusually high levels of social trust, normative consensus, and willingness to comply with fiscal obligations; consequently, one cannot simply transplant the tax-and-transfer architecture into a low-trust society and expect it to function. A respondent then points out that these very conditions—interpersonal trust, solidarity, acceptance of redistribution—are themselves partly cultivated and sustained over generations by universalist welfare institutions. Rather than treating this interdependence as a practical obstacle, the respondent presents it as a reason to import the entire configuration at once: the institutions and the culture must be adopted together, since each reproduces the other. Meanwhile, sceptics invoke the same circular relation to argue that the model is essentially sui generis and therefore non-exportable, because an external society lacks the requisite cultural substrate. In both directions, the mutual constitution of institutional framework and social precondition is acknowledged, yet it is never translated into an account of initiation—how a society lacking either element might begin the cycle, or whether partial adoption is systematically unstable. The loop seems to serve simultaneously as a defence of uniqueness and as a mandate for holistic replication, whilst leaving the bootstrapping problem entirely untouched.

I am not seeking a reading list or citations, but rather whether this specific pattern—the use of a circular, mutually sustaining relationship between welfare institutions and social trust both to resist partial adoption and to justify wholesale import, coupled with an absence of any entry mechanism—has an established name in philosophy of social science, institutional theory, or comparative politics. Is it best understood as a variant of path dependency, a case of institutional complementarity, or perhaps something closer to a 'social-capital bootstrap paradox'? If no precise term exists, I should like to understand exactly what distinguishes this loop from an ordinary circular explanation, and where its conceptual edges lie: for example, whether it requires genuine mutual causation or merely its appearance; and whether it is peculiar to normative institutions such as redistribution, or whether analogous loops appear in technological or organisational diffusion. I should be grateful for a response in British English.
