You serve on your university's Joint Research Funding and Ethics Committee, which must issue a binding decision on Proposal 2024-89: a $2.3 million request to study the "heritability of critical-thinking disposition" in adolescents using classic twin ACE models, with the explicit aim of using the resulting coefficient to advise public school systems to reduce investment in pedagogical critical-thinking interventions in favor of ability-based tracking. The principal investigator argues that a heritability estimate near 0.75 demonstrates the trait is "mostly genetic" and therefore resistant to classroom modification. One senior quantitative geneticist on the panel supports this, insisting that high heritability genuinely constrains expected environmental effect sizes and that rejecting the proposal on conceptual grounds would mistake scientific literacy for political preference. Draft the committee's decision memo—recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions—addressing with conceptual precision what heritability does and does not establish: distinguish population-level variance decomposition from individual-level determination; explain the reference-class dependence of heritability coefficients; address gene-environment covariance, including indirect genetic effects (sometimes called "genetic nurture"); and clarify why heritability estimates do not license inferences about the efficacy, mutability, or ethical priority of pedagogical interventions. Include an honest, rigorous account of the strongest consideration favoring the PI's position—specifically, whether and how high heritability can rationally inform resource allocation under budget constraints—then conclude with an actionable recommendation the committee can implement, specifying any conditions that would render the proposal conceptually sound.
