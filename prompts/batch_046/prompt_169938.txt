I've been in this philosophy of science reading group for like two years and we keep circling back to whether Gödel's incompleteness theorems actually tell us anything interesting about minds, law, or politics outside pure math. One camp keeps bringing up that if a system is consistent and strong enough there are unprovable truths, so human cognition must transcend any algorithm, or that every legal code has inherent gaps that judges must fill intuitively. The other camp rolls there eyes and says the conditions don't map—brains aren't formal axiom systms in the relevant sense, courts don't work like Peano arithmetic, and this is just a catagory mistake that sounds deep because the word 'incomplete' is scary.

We all understand the actual proof, nobody disputes the formal result inside mathematics, but the debate hasn't moved an inch in maybe a decade of our meetings. My working theory is that we're not disagreeing about Gödel at all, but about what counts as a 'mechanism' versus a 'system', and whether analogical reasoning from math to cognition is even licensed. But maybe there's a hidden empirical question—like whether human mathematical intuition can be captured in a specific formal framework—or maybe it's just bedrock intuition about whether the mind is a machine. I want an honest diagnosis: is this verbal, substantive, or two different questions wearing the same hat? And given that, what would actually count as progress—some sharper formal result about cognitive architectures, a conceptual distinction that blocks the analogy, or should we just admit nothing will move either side?
