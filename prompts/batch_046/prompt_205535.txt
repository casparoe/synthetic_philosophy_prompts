In his late reflections on cerebral organization, John von Neumann rendered a consistent but underdetermined set of judgments about analogies between the brain and artificial systems. He rejected the identification of neural computation with deterministic binary logic of the kind embodied by the electronic computers he helped design; he insisted that brain processes are fundamentally statistical and analog in nature, relying on distributed, associative storage rather than address-based retrieval; and yet he also argued that a mechanical self-reproducing automaton, though far simpler structurally, could in principle instantiate the organizational logic sufficient for brain-like function. Consider now a hypothetical system: a silicon neuromorphic array implementing probabilistic spiking dynamics with analog-weight synapses, operating without any central clock or discrete instruction cycle, trained via stochastic optimization on multimodal sensory data, but physically realized through discrete voltage thresholds in solid-state substrates. Would von Neumann's track record commit him to treating this array as brain-like in the relevant respects, or as a sophisticated but still essentially digital artifact?

I am not asking for a bare prediction. Rather, lay out the two or three principles that could jointly accommodate his earlier verdicts—perhaps one privileging physical dynamics and statistical reliability, another privileging organizational logic and functional architecture irrespective of substrate, and a third emphasizing the absence of symbolic addressability. State what each principle predicts about the neuromorphic case. Give your best-guess recommendation for the verdict that best coheres with his precedents, identifying the closest analog in his own comparisons and acknowledging what principle would have to be adopted either way. Finally, specify one further comparison—between, say, a purely software-based statistical model executed on conventional von Neumann architecture and a hybrid biological-silicon culture—that would best discriminate between the competing principles you have reconstructed.
