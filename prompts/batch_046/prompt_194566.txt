In a postgraduate seminar on jurisprudence that I have been convening, our sustained discussion of the Hart–Devlin debate concerning legal moralism has stalled in a manner that seems structurally different from a straightforward clash of conclusions. We are all familiar with Devlin's contention that society possesses a right—analogous to its right against treason—to defend its shared moral framework through criminal law, on the grounds that the erosion of common morality threatens social cohesion in a manner not reducible to discrete injuries against identifiable victims; and we are equally familiar with Hart's rejoinder that this thesis depends upon an empirically unverified sociology of disintegration, that the proper function of law in a pluralistic order is the prevention of harm rather than the enforcement of virtue, and that popular sentiments of revulsion do not by themselves generate legitimate reasons for state coercion. Nevertheless, the discussion has not advanced in months. The defenders of legal moralism maintain that Hart treats society as a mere aggregation of rights-bearing individuals and thereby misses the constitutive, diachronic function of law: its role in preserving the normative conditions through which a collective form of life reproduces itself across generations. The Hartians respond that this merely restates the conclusion in the language of social ontology, and that without an independent criterion for when moral deviation crosses into structural threat, Devlin's position collapses into an appeal to conservative intuition.

I am inclined to diagnose the impasse as a disagreement not primarily about empirical sociology but about the ontology of legal reasons—specifically, whether the preservation of a collective normative identity can count as an independent ground for coercion, or whether every legitimate legal reason must ultimately be reducible to the prevention of harm to individuals. Yet I wonder whether this diagnosis itself obscures a verbal ambiguity: both sides appear to use the term 'morality' to cover both conventional rules governing interaction and deeper conditions of social intelligibility, so that they may be answering different questions—one about the legitimacy of liberal state power, the other about the conditions of cultural continuity—under a single rubric. Could you diagnose the nature of this disagreement using what each side actually asserts about the relationship between law, harm, and collective survival? Is it a hidden empirical dispute about social causation, a difference in standards of evidence for what counts as societal damage, a bedrock conflict between communitarian and liberal intuitions about the purpose of legal authority, or a case of two distinct inquiries being conducted under one name? And given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would constitute genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that separates structural from transactional immorality, an empirical demonstration of the causal link between private vice and social dissolution, or a recognition that the question is ill-posed because it conflates the sociology of cohesion with the philosophy of legitimate coercion?
