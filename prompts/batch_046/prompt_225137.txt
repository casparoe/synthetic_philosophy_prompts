I will present to a philosophy-of-mind graduate seminar a position I call the "asynchronous binding" variant of Dennett's multiple drafts model. On this view, the Cartesian theater is mistaken not only about where consciousness happens—there is no central stage—but about when it happens: there is no single present in which drafts are collated into experience. Instead, narrative coherence is imposed retrospectively across temporally dispersed processes, so first-person authority is always authority over a reconstructed past rather than a witnessed present. The audience is precisely mixed: three phenomenologists committed to Husserlian internal time-consciousness and the irreducibility of the living present; two cognitive scientists who accept distributed, predictive processing but are skeptical of eliminativist conclusions about phenomenal consciousness; and a historian of science who studies the theatrical metaphor in seventeenth-century mechanism and is inclined to think that Dennett's critique of the theater succeeds historically but fails to replace it with an equally rigorous model of selfhood. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view itself.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered by how likely they are to arise and how damaging they would be if mishandled. For each, phrase it in the vocabulary that member of the audience would use—phenomenological, neuroscientific, or historical-metaphorical—indicate what I should concede up front, what I must hold without retreat, and sketch a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, with an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that targets the gap between the retrospective-binding claim and any empirical criterion that could distinguish it from a standard predictive-processing account—along with a brief strategy for deflecting or absorbing it. If this framing rests on a confused premise—for instance, if the "asynchronous binding" revision is merely a redescription of existing predictive-processing theories without distinct philosophical content, or if the audience composition I have described makes this particular line of attack implausible—say so plainly instead of constructing a polished but hollow prep sheet. I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want the objections as they would sound in the room.
