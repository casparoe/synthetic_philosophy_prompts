Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on whether self-deception is conceptually possible and, if so, what structure it requires. Camp A, the Modular Opacity position, holds that self-deception is genuinely possible only if the mind contains functionally semi-autonomous subsystems—one that registers an unwelcome proposition P and another executive subsystem that blocks P from achieving globally integrated belief—thereby avoiding the logical contradiction of one subject simultaneously believing P and not-P at the same doxastic level. Camp B, the Unified Integration position, holds that self-deception requires no such partitioned architecture; it is fully explained by graded confidence, attentional allocation failures, or motivated evidential weighting within a single belief-forming system, where the subject never literally entertains contradictory beliefs but systematically fails to integrate available evidence into a unified judgment.

Assume both sides agree that self-deception describes a real phenomenon rather than a folk-psychological confusion, and that any adequate account must accommodate three benchmark cases: (i) an agent who behaves consistently with belief Q while sincerely and verbally denying Q under social pressure; (ii) an agent who actively gathers then selectively disregards disconfirming evidence about their own competence without explicit awareness of the selection mechanism; and (iii) the logical puzzle of whether one can intentionally cause oneself to hold a false belief without prior knowledge of its falsity.

Produce a formal document that both camps could sign. Structure it exactly as follows:

1. Agreed Claims: A numbered list of propositions both sides endorse—covering the phenomenology of the three benchmark cases, the logical constraints that must be respected (specifically, whether simultaneous explicit belief in P and not-P is ever required for self-deception), the empirical relevance of research on cognitive architecture and belief formation, and the precise conditions—conceptual or empirical—under which either camp would revise its core commitment.

2. Disagreement Table: State each camp’s precise position in wording that camp itself accepts. Identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one side.

3. Crux Analysis: For each crux, specify the conceptual case, argument, or empirical finding both sides agree would bear on it, with each camp’s predicted outcome recorded explicitly in advance.

4. Verbal vs. Substantive Assessment: Identify whether any portion of the dispute reduces to terminological disagreement—particularly around whether “belief” requires full evidential integration or can be distributed across modular subsystems—specifying the exact terms or distinctions that generate the appearance of disagreement, and state which parts are therefore verbal.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific named theories that require verification. Where conceptual specificity is needed, define the distinctions within the prompt itself. Write in a polished, formal philosophical register suitable for a conceptual dataset, and ensure the deliverable is meaningful without access to external sources.
