I am preparing remarks for my graduate seminar in comparative philosophy, where we are working through Zhuangzi’s treatments of death—particularly the parable of the joyful skull, the butterfly dream, and the notion of death as part of the great transformation (dahua). I have been trying to formalize his view using concepts from contemporary metaphysics and philosophy of personal identity, but I keep running into a tension that I suspect is an artifact of my own framing rather than the text itself, and I want to check whether the confusion is mine.

Here is the difficulty. Zhuangzi describes death not as annihilation but as a change of form, comparable to waking from a dream. If this analogy is taken seriously, the boundary between life and death does not appear metaphysically sharp: there is no precise instant at which the living self becomes dead matter, only a continuous flow into other shapes. Yet to say that the boundary is not sharp seems to presuppose that there is still a hidden fact of the matter—however epistemically inaccessible—about when the transformation is complete. That sounds like importing an epistemicist account of vagueness into personal identity, according to which a precise cutoff exists but eludes us. At the same time, Zhuangzi’s broader critique of the chengxin—the heart or mind that fixes distinctions and clings to life—suggests he rejects all hidden essences and sharp cutoffs altogether. So his transformation view appears to oscillate between two incompatible positions: either it smuggles in a kind of mereological essentialism, according to which some core substrate of the self persists through every change of form, or it eliminates the self so thoroughly that the dream analogy collapses, since there is no longer a subject who could be either the dreamer or the butterfly.

My question, then, is whether this framing—reading Zhuangzi as caught between hidden essentialism and eliminativism about the self—is genuinely present in the text or is produced by forcing his parables into contemporary debates about vagueness and identity. More specifically, if Zhuangzi truly rejects every fixed distinction, can he consistently maintain that death is a transformation of something, or must he instead hold that what we call the self is nothing more than a temporary convergence of qi that disperses without remainder? I am not asking for a bibliography or for readings I should consult; I want to know whether the conceptual tools I have introduced—epistemicism about vague boundaries, mereological essentialism, and substrate-independent transformation—actually illuminate the parables or obscure their point about non-attachment.
