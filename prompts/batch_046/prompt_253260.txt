I am preparing a seminar defence of the claim that Dennett's idea of counterfeit people should be read as an institutional thesis rather than as an epistemic warning about consciousness: large language models are counterfeit people not because they lack phenomenal experience, but because they are embedded in social practices that ascribe responsibility without the structural conditions—normative authority, liability-bearing capacity, historical embeddedness—being satisfied. I therefore argue that AI ethics should shift its focus from consciousness-detection to the architecture of accountability. The audience is a philosophy of AI ethics reading group comprising mainly analytic philosophers of mind with functionalist or eliminativist leanings who will defend Dennett's original framing, two institutional ontologists influenced by Searle and Tuomela, and a visiting legal philosopher concerned with liability gaps; they are broadly sceptical of consciousness-first ethics but will press on whether my institutional relocation collapses Dennett's distinction or merely relocates it. Please prepare my Q&A sheet: list the objections I will actually face, ordered by how likely and damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary this audience would use. For each, provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute response, note precisely where that reply is weakest, indicate what I should concede up front, and identify the single reply that must land for the proposal to survive. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is particularly dangerous given my position. Write your response in British English.
