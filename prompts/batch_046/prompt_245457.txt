I am a high school student preparing a presentation for my philosophy elective on distributive justice, and I want to read Andrew Niccol's 1997 film Gattaca through the lens of luck egalitarianism. Specifically, I want to examine Ronald Dworkin's distinction between option luck and brute luck, together with the broader claim that morally arbitrary natural differences — in this case, genetic endowments — should not determine life prospects. The film depicts Vincent, an "in-valid" born with genetic imperfections, who assumes the identity of Jerome, a "valid" with elite genetics, in order to enter the space program. I am asking for a critical reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously: in which scenes does Vincent's deception and ambition vindicate the luck egalitarian argument that genetic destiny is unjust? At the same time, where does the narrative resist that framework — for instance, through Jerome's decision to sell his genetic identity, through the state's biometric surveillance, or through the film's treatment of Vincent's drive as a form of "choice" that complicates the brute-luck category? I am especially interested in whether the film inadvertently illustrates Elizabeth Anderson's democratic-equality critique: that by framing genetic disadvantage as an individual misfortune to be overcome through personal deception and merit rather than through collective restructuring of social relations, the story may stigmatize "in-valids" in precisely the way luck egalitarianism is accused of doing. Which scenes best expose this tension — for example, the swimming competition with Anton, the final flight sequence, or the institutional DNA checks — and what does the film's specific logic of passing, recognition, and gatekeeping add to the philosophical debate that abstract argument about resources and welfare alone does not capture? I want an analysis that avoids reducing the film to an illustration, and instead says honestly where the philosophical lens illuminates a scene and where the scene forces the philosophy to account for something it misses.
