As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in a systematic way, I have been trying to reconstruct Gödel’s modal ontological argument without relying on secondary summaries. I think I have followed the axioms correctly through the proof that a God-like being exists necessarily, but when I extend the framework with one additional principle I call modal saturation, I derive what seems to be an absurd result: not just that God exists, but that every genuinely contingent fact is either necessary or impossible, leaving no real contingency at all. I want to know exactly where the reasoning breaks, and I want to preserve as much of Gödel’s machinery as possible—especially the claims that necessary existence is a positive property, that positive properties are necessarily positive, and that consistency of the God-property implies actual existence in every world.

Here is the argument, numbered as it appears in my notes:

(1) In Gödel’s system, for every property P, exactly one of P and its complement is positive, and not both.
(2) If a property is positive, then necessarily it is positive.
(3) Necessary existence is a positive property.
(4) Being God-like—call it G—is the property of having all and only the positive properties.
(5) G is consistent; it is not self-contradictory.
(6) Therefore, by Gödel’s proof, there necessarily exists an entity x such that Gx.
(7) Now I add modal saturation: if x necessarily instantiates G, and omniscience (knowing every true proposition in one’s world) is among the properties entailed by G, then for any world-level state S, x’s knowing whether S holds in a given world entails that the world-property “S obtains here” is either positively entailed by G or positively excluded by G.
(8) Take a genuinely contingent state C—for example, that a particular star formed in a specific region of a specific galaxy at a specific time. Define the world-property Φ(C): this world contains that exact stellar formation.
(9) By (7), either Φ(C) or its complement is positively entailed by G, hence is a positive property.
(10) By (1) and (2), that world-property is either necessarily instantiated across all worlds or necessarily absent across all worlds.
(11) Thus C is either necessary or impossible; observed contingency is eliminated.
(12) But contingency is clearly observed.
(13) So the chain must fail somewhere between (6) and (11).

My suspicion is that (7) is the illicit move—perhaps I have confused properties of the being with properties of worlds, or I have misapplied closure-under-entailment across modal spaces. But I am not sure whether repairing (7) undermines (6), or whether Gödel’s original axioms already contain a hidden version of the same slide. If I must choose, I prefer to keep (1) through (6) intact and reject (7), but I want to understand the precise theoretical price: does abandoning modal saturation require me to restrict omniscience so that it does not range over world-properties, or does it require a broader limitation on how properties can be quantified across worlds? I also want to know whether this difficulty corresponds to a recognized objection—something in the vicinity of modal collapse—or whether I have constructed a novel misapplication that does not appear in the literature.
