After a long, exhausting argument with a friend about whether human extinction is bad mainly because of all the deaths it involves, or whether there is a deeper harm that has nothing to do with dying at all, I want to see whether my students actually understand Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture or merely recognize its label. My friend insisted that if humanity were replaced by entities that preserved every book, every formula, and every artifact, then nothing philosophically important would be lost; I think Scheffler's point is precisely that preservation of archives is not preservation of the "collective afterlife" that gives our present projects meaning. I need an exam that forces students to locate that distinction themselves, not recite it.

Please write one essay question for the final exam of my upper-level philosophy seminar on value and future generations. The exam lasts 90 minutes; students choose one of two essay questions. This one should be weighted at 40 marks out of 80, and students should write approximately 1,000 to 1,200 words. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; students will not have texts in front of them, and a model answer should reason from the idea, not invoke page numbers.

The question must be unanswerable by summarizing Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife. To achieve this, inject the following niche framing directly into the prompt given to students: present a scenario in which humanity is replaced—not annihilated—by non-conscious, highly efficient successor entities that preserve every human archive, scientific record, and artistic artifact, but have no inner lives, no projects, and no capacity for meaning. Ask students whether Scheffler's conjecture implies that this replacement is just as bad as extinction, and require them to identify the specific feature of his argument—namely, that present activities depend on the continuation of a shared human future of conscious, project-bearing beings, not merely on the survival of records—that decides the case.

Then provide:
1. The exam question itself, worded precisely as students will see it.
2. A model answer of roughly 600 to 800 words. It must contain the following specific moves, and a top-grade answer should be separated from a mid-grade answer by their presence or absence: (a) an accurate reconstruction of the conjecture as a claim about the dependency of present meaning on the assumed continuation of humanity; (b) a clear distinction between this "project-dependency" argument and consequentialist arguments about lost welfare, violated rights, or foregone goods; (c) an analysis of the successor-entity scenario showing why archive-preservation is insufficient; (d) an identification of the crux—whether the argument strictly requires conscious successors or merely the continuation of value-bearing practices that could, in principle, be carried on by non-conscious systems; (e) a critical evaluation that names a specific overreach, such as whether the conjecture wrongly assumes all valuable projects depend on distant futures, or whether non-human conscious beings could serve the same function.
3. A marking scheme that assigns marks to each move, separates an A answer (contains a, b, c, d, and e with d identifying the conscious-successor crux and e offering a targeted criticism) from a B answer (contains a and c but conflates project-dependency with welfare-dependency in b, misses the crux in d, or offers only generic skepticism in e). List specific mistakes that should cost marks: treating Scheffler as a utilitarian, confusing the conjecture with an argument for personal immortality, appealing to "what Scheffler says" instead of reasoning, or misreading the successor scenario as satisfying the conjecture because archives survive.

If this request rests on a confused premise—if, for example, the successor-entity scenario actually collapses into a different philosophical problem altogether, or if Scheffler's text cannot support the distinction between conscious successors and preserved practices that I am asking students to draw—then say so plainly instead of producing the exam materials politely. Do not invent quotations from Scheffler; paraphrase his view without quotation marks, and verify the book title and basic framing if you lean on them, but do not ask students to cite anything.
