I am a doctoral student in comparative literature and philosophical aesthetics, currently organizing a ninety-minute graduate seminar session for about ten peers working on hermeneutics and interpretation theory. We have been discussing the uneasy relation between anti-intentionalist commitments and what I have been calling the archival temptation—the pull we feel to treat an author’s private note or letter as settling a textual dispute, even when that note is ambiguous or self-contradictory. I want a structured in-class activity, not a lecture, that forces participants to generate and defend interpretations of a deliberately ambiguous text before being confronted with fabricated “authorial evidence,” so that the tension between communicative intent, artistic intention, and what might be called retroactive self-interpretation is felt as a practical problem rather than received as doctrine.

Please design the session around a short, invented three-paragraph prose fragment—let us call it “The Cartographer of Frozen Rivers”—that depicts an explorer attempting to map a waterway that repeatedly melts and re-forms. The language should be rich enough to sustain two robust but incompatible readings: one framing the passage as political satire about colonial cartography and the erasure of indigenous spatial knowledge, and another framing it as an autobiographical meditation on cognitive decline and the failure of memory. In the first phase, divide the group into pairs assigned roles—one “textualist” responsible for defending a reading grounded only in linguistic and structural features, and one “archivist” who will later advocate for revision based on external materials. After thirty minutes of interpretation and cross-examination within the pairs, distribute a fabricated “archival discovery”: two contradictory letters, both attributed to the same fictional author, one insisting the piece is exclusively political satire with no personal meaning, the other insisting it is purely private grief-work about dementia and should not be read politically at all. The rules should require each pair to produce a revised joint interpretation, but award coherence points (tracked visibly on a board) only when both members can justify the revision without simply dismissing either the text or the letters. Provide a precise timeline, necessary materials, and a payoff scheme using only social or symbolic stakes rather than money.

The debrief should map each phase onto the conceptual distinction between communicative intent, artistic intention, and retroactive self-interpretation; it should also predict what will reliably go wrong the first time—likely that one role dominates, or that participants treat the letters as automatically authoritative—and advise how to correct for it in real time. Crucially, include an honest admission of which feature of the intentionalist/anti-intentionalist debate this simulation cannot reproduce—perhaps the case in which a text systematically undermines every interpretation its author subsequently claims to have held, leaving no stable “intent” to recover—and explain how I, as facilitator, must supply that limit case verbally after the activity concludes. Please do not request bibliographies, real scholarly citations, or references to specific historical texts; the idea must emerge from the constructed materials and the rules themselves, and the prompt should remain fully meaningful to someone without web access.
