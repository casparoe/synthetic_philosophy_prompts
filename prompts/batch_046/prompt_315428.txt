I serve as a mediator for a regional health ethics board that resolves disputes over surrogate decision-making for adults with profound cognitive impairment. Our charter adopts a relational conception of personhood informed by Ubuntu: moral standing is constituted by networks of care rather than by individual rational capacity alone. I need a reusable procedural framework—ordered questions with stopping rules, value-judgment flags, and explicit misfire conditions—that my panel can apply in about thirty minutes to determine whether a proposed surrogate decision respects the patient’s relational identity rather than treating them as an isolated rights-bearer or a mere biological substrate.

The procedure must distinguish relationships that constitute the person from those that only affect them, and it must operate even when the network is fractured, contested, or includes members the patient previously excluded. It should specify what counts as sufficient evidence of relational constitution, where it halts with a negative determination, and where it yields to irreducible value judgment. Apply it to three disputes: (1) a daughter demands withdrawal of life support from an unresponsive parent who previously told only an estranged sibling that they wished to live; (2) a residential care collective—whose members regard a kinless patient as a founding figure—seeks to continue artificial nutrition over the objection of a distant legal guardian; and (3) a recent spouse requests aggressive intervention opposed by adult children whose lifelong relationships with the patient predate the marriage, with the procedure yielding a result that privileges the children’s claim despite conventional spousal priority.

State clearly what the framework cannot resolve—particularly when relational preservation conflicts with biological preservation or when the constitutive network itself is exploitative. Finally, indicate how confident you are in the framework’s fidelity to relational personhood and identify the single distinction or step about which you are least certain.
