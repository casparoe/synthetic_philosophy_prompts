I just finished a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether everyday conventions—tipping in restaurants, queuing for service, obeying pedestrian signals—constitute genuine moral obligations, and whether a visitor is morally bound to follow them even when they differ sharply from home practice. To stop us talking past each other, I want an adversarial collaboration document that both of us could sign: call the first position the Localist view, which holds that these norms generate real, if context-dependent, moral duties that apply to outsiders, and the second the Conventionalist view, which holds that they are coordination devices or local expressions of taste carrying at most prudential weight, so that departure wrongs no one unless direct harm results. Please write this entirely as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though composing a long letter, and begin with the descriptive and conceptual claims both sides can accept—about how these norms function, the distinction between moral rules and mere conventions, and the limits of our shared empirical knowledge about social breakdown—before moving to the precise disagreements phrased so that each camp would recognize its own view, particularly on whether refusing to tip in a tipping culture, bypassing a queue in a queuing culture, or jaywalking in a culture that treats pedestrian discipline as a civic example, constitutes an independent moral wrong. Then identify the cruxes, which I take to include whether local practice has intrinsic moral value, whether unconsented norms can generate duties, and whether there is a distinct phenomenon of normative friction—moral resistance rather than mere social discomfort—when one violates a convention; for each crux present a concrete case or argument both sides agree is relevant, such as the Berlin pedestrian who waits at an empty red light and is mocked by locals for excessive compliance versus the tourist who crosses and is reprimanded, or the refusal to queue in a society where queuing is framed as mutual respect rather than efficiency. For each case record in advance what the Localist predicts—whether wrong has occurred, whether guilt is appropriate, whether outsiders should adapt—and what the Conventionalist predicts on the same points. Finally, address honestly whether part of our disagreement is verbal—perhaps about whether strong social expectation just is a weak moral obligation—and conclude with a single flowing paragraph that states the residual dispute without resolving it, all without asking for citations, bibliographies, or outside sources, since the point is to map our reasoning from common ground.
