I'm writing up my preliminary position for a graduate seminar on reflective reasoning and artificial agency, and I'd like a consistency check before I commit to it in writing. The course centers on the so-called Löbian obstacle—broadly, the logical limits on a formal system's ability to validate its own reasoning—and I have tried to map out my own stance on what it implies for self-trusting agents, both human and artificial. Here is the package of commitments I have adopted. First, I accept that the obstacle is a genuine structural limit, not a technical artifact: a consistent formal system extending elementary arithmetic cannot establish its own global soundness in the manner that full reflective self-endorsement would require. Second, I take this to imply that any agent whose cognition is exhaustively formalized in such a system cannot rationally maintain complete, unqualified self-trust—that is, justified confidence in the reliability of its own future outputs—without violating consistency. Third, I nevertheless want to defend a practical stance I call functional self-trust: an agent may operate with bounded, context-local reliance on its own updates, provided this reliance is grounded in empirical track-record monitoring and architectural safeguards rather than in a global logical proof. Fourth, I think the obstacle applies narrowly to deductive, proof-theoretic reasoning and loses much of its force for probabilistic or inductive self-assessment, which means a Bayesian agent could in principle avoid the problem by updating on observed reliability rather than seeking a logical guarantee. Fifth, I apply the same structure to human cognition: if human reasoning is formalizable in the relevant sense, then our ordinary self-confidence is partly irrational; if it is not formalizable, then the obstacle offers no license to build formal artificial agents that claim a stronger form of self-trust than we possess. Sixth, I hold that the correct normative response is not global epistemic skepticism but what I term structured suspicion—treating one's own outputs as defeasible by default, with layered verification mechanisms that explicitly refrain from validating the system as a whole.

My concern is that these commitments may pull in opposite directions, and I am not sure which ones are genuinely incompatible versus only apparently so. Does my defense of functional self-trust in the third commitment collapse into a relabeling of the problem I acknowledged in the second, or can it be sustained as a distinct normative category? Does my claim in the fourth commitment—that probabilistic reasoning escapes the obstacle—undermine the relevance of the first commitment for practical AI design, given that most real-world inference is inductive? And does my fifth commitment about human cognition force me either into an implausible reduction of human reasoning or into abandoning the normative bite of the obstacle altogether? I would appreciate an audit that ranks the tensions by severity, identifies which pair is the closest analog to the others, states what principle I would be adopting if I retained functional self-trust while dropping the strong conclusion about rational self-trust, and names the further case—perhaps involving a self-modifying system that uses only inductive confidence tracking—that would best distinguish whether the obstacle should be treated as a universal constraint or as a domain-specific feature of deductive logic.
