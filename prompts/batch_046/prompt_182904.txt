Draft an adversarial-collaboration document for two opposing camps in the debate over dirty hands in politics. The first camp, the Role-Residue View, maintains that holding political office generates a domain-specific permission to violate ordinary moral prohibitions—such as deceiving the public or authorizing harm to non-combatants—without canceling the wrongness of those acts; rather, the wrongness persists as an irreducible moral stain or residue borne personally by the officeholder. The second camp, the Institutional-Design View, maintains that treating such violations as individual moral burdens is a category error: the phenomenon we label dirty hands is better explained as the predictable output of collective decision architectures that force binary choices between catastrophe and complicity, and moral analysis should therefore focus on restructuring institutional procedures rather than on cultivating individual conscience.

The document should have the following sections.

1. Agreed claims (numbered). Include at least: (a) a neutral, precise characterization of the phenomenon both sides recognize as dirty hands, specifying both procedural violations of democratic norms and substantive violations of individual rights; (b) agreement that democratic authorization, whether ex ante or ex post, does not fully dissolve the moral problem, though the camps interpret its significance differently; (c) agreement on empirical constraints—time pressure, incomplete information, and the practical limits of institutional redesign; (d) explicit agreement that both sides reject the identification of political morality with private morality, and both reject any pure consequentialist calculus that erases moral remainder entirely; (e) agreement on a concrete shared case: a democratically elected executive who, without legislative approval, authorizes a secret surveillance program that infringes privacy rights but is believed necessary to prevent an imminent attack.

2. A disagreements table. For each camp, present in wording that camp would accept for its own position: (i) the locus of the moral problem—individual psychological residue versus collective architectural failure; (ii) the interpretation of the shared case, distinguishing procedural dirt (bypassing legislative oversight) from substantive dirt (infringing rights); (iii) the role of the distinction between first-person dirty hands (the agent's subjective experience of moral taint) and third-person dirty hands (public attribution of blame independent of subjective state), noting which camp treats this distinction as conceptually central and which treats it as a red herring.

3. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one camp. I suggest two: (A) whether moral residue is conceptually coherent when the agent faced no reasonable alternative course of action; (B) whether institutional redesign can eliminate all forced complicity or can only redistribute it across different agents or procedures.

4. Tests and predictions. For each crux, specify: (a) one concrete argument or hypothetical institutional reform both sides agree is relevant; (b) each camp's advance prediction about how that argument or reform should be assessed.

Keep the formulation formal and analytical. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not quote named texts verbatim; paraphrase references to classic treatments without quotation marks. Ensure that any specific conceptual distinction you invoke—particularly the first-person/third-person separation—is defined precisely within the prompt itself.
