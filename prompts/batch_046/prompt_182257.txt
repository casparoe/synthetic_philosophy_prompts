I've been reading Quine's Word and Object since I retired from the lab—thirty-odd years calibrating interferometers, so I suppose I think about measurement the way other people think about language. I was chatting with my neighbour (he teaches logic) and we cooked up this argument that seems to bite its own tail, but I can't see the weak joint.

(1) Quine's gavagai example shows that if we only observe native speech dispositions, then 'gavagai' is equally well translated as 'rabbit', 'undetached rabbit part', or 'temporal stage of rabbitness'; all three manuals preserve every observable verbal behaviour. (2) Now, scientific measurement is just a kind of translation: the pointer on my old interferometer reads '3.2', and my calibration manual maps that onto 'the wavelength is 656.3 nanometres'. The manual connects observable instrument states to theoretical claims. (3) So apply Quine's reasoning inside the lab. For any calibration manual that maps pointer-state P to 'wavelength W', there should exist rival manuals that map P to 'wavelength-stage W', 'undetached spectral-part W', etc., all preserving every observable instrument behaviour and predictive success. (4) But if rival manuals are genuinely indistinguishable by experiment, then empirical science can never choose between the ontology of enduring wavelengths and the ontology of wavelength-stages. (5) That means scientific realism about the entities we measure is no better supported than realism about rabbits; we're just picking the manual that feels neat. (6) Yet in practice we treat calibration as converging on the true physical quantity, not as an arbitrary choice among behaviourally equivalent ontologies. (7) So either Quine's indeterminancy doesn't apply inside science—which looks like special pleading—or it applies everywhere and destroys the idea that experiment reveals real structure.

Where does this slip? Is step (2) a false analogy between language-translation and instrument-calibration? Or is the mistake in (4), assuming that behavioural equivalence of manuals blocks empirical adjudication when in fact theoretical virtues do the work? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) intact if possible, but I want to know exactly what I'm giving up. Please answer in British English, and don't bother with references or a bibliography—just work through the reasoning.
