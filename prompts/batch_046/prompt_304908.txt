I've been working through Kant's indirect-duty account of animals and I'm stuck on what seems like an internal tension with his aesthetics. In the Groundwork, the standard reading is that animals lack practical reason and therefore are not ends in themselves; cruelty to them is wrong only because it corrupts the moral character of the human agent. But in the Critique of Judgment, Kant claims that judging something beautiful requires presupposing a shared sensus communis among rational subjects—an intersubjective agreement about the judgment itself. When I judge a living animal to be beautiful, I seem to be treating my response as one that could be shared with other rational judges. Yet if animals are not members of the rational community, they cannot be parties to this shared judgment. Does that mean my aesthetic appreciation of animals is necessarily private and therefore fails to be genuinely beautiful? Or does the fact that nature's purposiveness—including the organized form of animals—gives us an intellectual interest in the beautiful imply that animals carry a normative weight in reflective judgment that Kant's moral philosophy explicitly denies? I'm trying to see whether the aesthetic theory forces Kant to grant animals a kind of objective standing that his ethics refuses them, without simply accusing him of inconsistency.
