In philosophy of science, the long-running debate over whether scientific disciplines carve nature at its joints—whether chemical elements, biological species, and psychiatric categories correspond to real, mind-independent divisions—has settled into a pattern of mutual acknowledgment without movement. Essentialists maintain that genuine kinds are defined by necessary properties or by homeostatic property clusters that exist independently of our classificatory practices; pluralists and conventionalists reply that graded membership, context-sensitive application, and the historical revision of taxonomies show that our categories are constructed for predictive and explanatory convenience rather than discovered. The arguments are familiar, the examples well-worn, and yet neither camp concedes ground.

My own suspicion is that the stall is not primarily empirical—no one expects a single experiment to settle whether "tiger" denotes a natural kind—but conceptual, and possibly verbal. Specifically, I think the two sides conflate two distinct demands that a kind-term might satisfy: kind-constitution, which concerns the conditions under which an entity belongs to the category, and kind-explanation, which concerns whether invoking the category licenses robust counterfactual and interventionist reasoning. Essentialists tend to treat satisfaction of the first demand as a prerequisite for the second, while pluralists allow the second to be met by stable causal structures even in the absence of shared essences. If that is right, then the disagreement may partly be about what "carving at the joints" is supposed to deliver—metaphysical insight about unified property bundles, or methodological reliability in explanation—rather than about whether science succeeds.

But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether there is a deeper, hidden disagreement doing the work: perhaps about the standards of scientific realism, about whether explanation requires unification or merely predictive success, or about whether the very question is ill-posed because "joint-carving" imports a spatial metaphor that misrepresents the ontology of processes and relations. I do not want a survey of essentialism, pluralism, or conventionalism. Instead, diagnose the structure of the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive but mislocated, or bedrock? Are the sides answering different questions—one ontological, one epistemological—under one heading? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question—say, about the stability of causal mechanisms across contexts—that could in principle resolve it? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a rigorous distinction between constitution and explanation, a reconceptualization of taxonomy in terms of interventionist rather than essentialist criteria, or an argument that the impasse is unavoidable and should be dissolved rather than solved?
