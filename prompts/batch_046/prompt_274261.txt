Here's an argument I cooked up about presentism that feels like it eats itself. (1) Every historical explanation has to decide what's relevant and what's just noise in the archive. (2) Those relevance-criterias can only come from the historian's own present framework — there's no view-from-nowhere to rank facts by. (3) So any explanation of a past event E is really an explanation of E-as-filtered-by-present-criterias. (4) If that's right, then saying the French Revolution was more violent than the American one isn't comparing two past events; it's comparing two present representions, because the very metrics of violence we pick are ours. (5) But then comparative history collapses into comparing our own shadows. (6) And here's the kicker: if escaping presentism means explaining E without present criteria, then (2) makes that impossible — you'd have no way to pick out E from the noise in the first place. (7) So presentism isn't a bias to overcome; it's the condition that makes history possible at all, and asking whether historians can escape it is like asking whether eyes can escape being eyes. (8) But that can't be right, because historians clearly do recover genuinely alien norms — think of how medieval penetential logic looks nothing like modern psychology — and they can be genuinely surprised by the past. Where's the slip? I want to keep (2) and (6) if I can, but I'm willing to ditch (4) if that's the price. Is there a hidden quantifier shift between "using present tools" and "only seeing present images"?
