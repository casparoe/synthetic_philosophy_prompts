I sit on a university research-integrity committee that meets monthly to adjudicate anonymous whistleblower reports alleging discrete errors in recently published papers—miscalculated standard errors, mislabeled control groups, duplicated gel images, inconsistent sample sizes, and the like. Each individual allegation, evaluated in isolation, usually reaches our “more probable than not” evidentiary threshold, yet accepting every allegation in a long report implies that the paper is largely erroneous, which clashes with our institutional default that peer-reviewed findings enjoy a presumption of soundness. To manage this tension systematically, I need a reusable decision procedure—a step-by-step flowchart expressed in prose—that operationalizes the logical structures of the lottery paradox and the preface paradox for practitioners who are bench scientists rather than logicians. The procedure must specify, in order: (1) the evidential standard and documentation required to register a single allegation as “individually credible”; (2) the aggregation rule that determines when a collection of individually credible allegations generates a collective “inconsistency flag” analogous to the preface case; (3) the stopping conditions—both the point at which the committee must recommend a formal investigation and the point at which it may dismiss remaining allegations without retracting its baseline confidence in the paper; and (4) the explicit junctures where logic ends and a value-laden institutional judgment (about reputational risk, resource limits, or due-process fairness) must take over, including who on the committee signs off at each gate. Apply the procedure to two contrasting reports: Report A, which lists twenty minor statistical discrepancies each supported by weak but non-negligible evidence; and Report B, which lists three serious image-duplication allegations, each ambiguous enough that reasonable experts disagree, but whose joint acceptance would undermine the study’s central claim. In particular, include one worked case—perhaps a report with seven credible but minor errors in a high-profile clinical trial—where the procedure mandates an investigation that the committee will find institutionally painful, and explain exactly why the logic of accumulation forces that result despite the cost. Finally, flag any step in the procedure that relies on a contested philosophical interpretation of either paradox, so that users know where they are importing theory rather than following neutral protocol.
