I am a doctoral student in international legal philosophy, preparing the conceptual architecture for a dissertation chapter on the moral ontology of atrocity naming. Before I commit to the argument, I need a rigorous consistency audit of the following positions, which I have developed independently but which may pull in directions I have not fully mapped.

My commitments are these:

(1) Lemkin’s original conception of genocide—which encompasses not only physical destruction but also the coordinated dismantling of cultural, political, and social institutions—is morally more adequate than the narrower formulation adopted in international treaty law, because a group’s continued existence can be annihilated without the death of every individual member.

(2) At the same time, I defend the treaty formulation’s restriction to national, ethnical, racial, and religious groups on metaphysical grounds. These categories identify what I call modal groups: collectivities whose members share properties that make them susceptible to a distinctive kind of collective harm that is irreducible to aggregated individual injuries.

(3) I maintain that the refusal to apply the label “genocide” to particular atrocities—such as the systematic destruction of indigenous pedagogical and linguistic institutions through state-mandated assimilation—is not a neutral legal technicality but a performative act that partly constitutes the moral status of the event. Naming generates specific obligations for third parties; withholding the name is therefore a normative choice, not merely descriptive omission.

(4) I am a moral realist about atrocity judgments: there are mind-independent facts about whether a given event is genocide, determined jointly by the perpetrator’s specific intent and by the objective structure of the harm inflicted.

(5) My method for hard cases is reflective equilibrium corrected by structural analysis: I begin with considered judgments about clear cases, but because cognitive and evolutionary biases systematically distort intuitions about group-based harm, I correct those judgments by analyzing the power relations and institutional architectures that shape both the violence and its naming.

(6) I reject cluster-concept or family-resemblance accounts. I believe genocide has necessary and sufficient conditions, but I hold that these conditions are normative rather than purely descriptive: to be genocide, an event must instantiate a specific wrong-making feature—namely, the targeted destruction of a modal group qua modal group.

(7) Finally, I distinguish between extensional politics—which groups fall under protection—and intensional politics—which kinds of harm count. I want to hold that cultural and institutional destruction can be genocide-level harm (intensional expansion) without abandoning the claim that only modal groups can be its proper targets (extensional restriction).

My worry is that these commitments harbor hidden tensions, especially between the performative dimension of naming (3) and my moral realism (4), and between the metaphysical defense of narrow group categories (2) and the moral endorsement of Lemkin’s broader harm conception (1). There may also be an apparent tension between my normative conditions in (6) and my reliance on bias-corrected reflective equilibrium in (5) that I am not seeing clearly.

Please audit this package. For each genuinely conflicting pair—not merely apparently so—specify the conflict, rank the tensions by severity, and indicate the least costly revision that restores consistency without collapsing the core project. Identify whether a single revision could dissolve the greatest number of conflicts. In particular, tell me whether holding both that cultural destruction counts and that only modal groups can be targets forces me to expand the group list in a way that undermines (2), or whether the intensional/extensional distinction actually stabilizes the package.
