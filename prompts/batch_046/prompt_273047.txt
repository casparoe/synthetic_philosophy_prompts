After a long argument with a friend about whether anyone possesses stable character or whether behavior is almost entirely determined by situation, I need to operationalize the debate rather than resolve it philosophically. I chair the ethics and professional-development review board at a 600-bed teaching hospital; every quarter we review eight to twelve cases in which a resident or fellow acted in ethically questionable ways—cutting corners on informed consent, being disrespectful to staff, failing to report errors, or manipulating research records. For each case the board must recommend either character-focused remediation (targeted mentoring, probation, dismissal) or situation-focused redesign (changing rotation structure, supervision density, checklists, or incentive schemes). We have ninety minutes per case, no moral philosophers on staff, and our recommendations require sign-off from the dean of graduate medical education. Please turn virtue ethics, corrected by the situationist critique, into a reusable decision procedure that someone without philosophical training can run. The procedure should be ordered: specific questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stop-points where the answer is “no—do not treat this as a virtue failure,” explicit hand-off points where the remaining question is one of values rather than facts and must go to the board’s judgment, and a clear statement of what the procedure cannot resolve. In translating the theory, preserve the distinction between possessing a virtue and having it activated, and introduce an operational concept—call it “virtue bandwidth”—meaning the range of situational stress within which this individual’s prior cross-situational behavior demonstrates that the virtue is actually operative rather than suppressed by pressure. The procedure must handle cases where the same outward behavior looks like cowardice in one context and reasonable self-preservation in another, and it must account for “trait activation thresholds” without requiring psychological testing. Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (1) a resident who has been consistently dismissive and interruptive toward nursing staff, but exclusively during high-census night shifts with reduced supervision; (2) a resident who fabricated a minor data point in a low-pressure, well-supervised outpatient study with no time constraints; and (3) a resident who failed to challenge an attending’s obvious clinical error after an explicit hierarchical threat, despite having challenged flat-structure teams in prior rotations. Ensure that one of these—likely the first—yields a recommendation you think I will resist, probably that the appropriate response is systemic redesign rather than individual punishment. Finally, state the limits: what categories of ethical failure this procedure is not designed to catch, and the conditions under which it will systematically misfire by either excusing bad character or pathologizing good people caught in bad systems.
