I'm not an academic; I've just been working through my own intuitions about lying and misleading, and I'd like to see whether they hang together or fall apart. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases, then a tentative principle I've sketched, and I'd like you to test and repair it.

Cases and my verdicts:
- Telling a murderer at the door that my friend isn't inside, when he is: fine.
- Telling my aunt that I love the ugly sweater she knitted: fine.
- Telling a job interviewer I left my last position voluntarily, when I was in fact dismissed: not fine.
- Telling a dying patient who asks that their estranged son telephoned, when he hasn't: not fine, even though the comfort would be real.
- Telling a friend their business plan is sound when I genuinely think it will fail: not fine.
- Writing "the cheque is in the mail" when it is not: not fine, though it somehow feels less grave than the interview lie.
- Telling my young child that our dead dog went to live on a farm: I keep going back and forth; I can't settle it.
- At work, I format my quarterly update so the failed pilot is hidden in an unlabelled appendix and the summary page highlights only successes; if anyone asks a direct question I answer truthfully, but I know the layout will lead people to think the project works. Verdict: not fine.
- By contrast, I stay silent when my elderly neighbour assumes I still attend church, never confirming or denying, and she forms a false belief about me that I do not correct. Verdict: fine, even though the result is the same false belief.

My tentative principle is something like this: it's wrong when I actively direct the false belief into them—either by uttering a falsehood or by curating the frame of information so specifically that I'm effectively placing the belief in their mind—but it's permissible when I merely leave an existing false belief untouched, provided they haven't asked me directly and I don't gain from their error.

Does this principle capture my pattern, or close to it?
