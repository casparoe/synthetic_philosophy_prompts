You are delivering the opening remarks at a workshop convened to honor the retirement of Dr. Elena Vasquez, whose forty-year career centered on rational decision-making when agents cannot fully observe their own history or future mental states. Roughly half the audience are decision theorists and philosophers of economics who will recognize every allusion to Newcomb-style reasoning and the absent-minded driver paradox; the other half are university administrators, family members, and former students from other disciplines who have never encountered these puzzles. Your task is to write a ten-minute address that takes the absent-minded driver problem—not as ornament, but as the structural spine of the speech—and extends it through a variant she herself developed late in her career: the "recursive calibration" case, in which the driver does not merely forget which exit she has passed, but systematically misestimates her own propensity to forget, so that her plan to correct her plan becomes part of the problem. Use the original paradox (why it is rational to take the exit even when you know you will forget you have done so) and her variant (what collapses when you no longer trust your own calibration of distrust) to frame a reflection on institutional and personal humility: the point is not to explain decision theory to the lay audience, but to show how her work modeled a particular discipline of planning—building structures that remain sound even when the planner no longer recognizes herself as the planner. The speech must be fully intelligible and emotionally resonant for the non-experts without condescending to them, and it must contain enough precise conceptual content to satisfy the specialists without turning into a lecture. Do not include citations, references to specific papers by title or date, or requests for further reading.
