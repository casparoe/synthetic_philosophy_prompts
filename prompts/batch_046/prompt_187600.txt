For a personal audit before a discussion group, I have sketched out my current thinking on interpretation—mostly shaped by my work on black-box systems and some scattered reading on Davidson's radical interpretation, though I have no formal philosophy training. I want to check whether the following commitments actually cohere, because I suspect I am importing engineering habits that conflict with the philosophical positions I think I hold.

My commitments are these:

(1) To interpret any agent at all—biological or artificial—we must apply a principle of charity that is constitutive, not merely heuristic: we have to treat the agent as largely rational and as holding mostly true beliefs about its environment, or else we have no basis for attributing content.

(2) Belief and meaning are holistically interdependent in the Davidsonian sense: fixing the content of one mental state or utterance requires fixing a broad network of others; there is no atomistic dictionary entry for an unknown system.

(3) In engineering practice, however, I regularly assign isolated, specific meanings to individual outputs of a model (for instance, tagging a particular activation vector as "danger-signaling") without having mapped the system's entire conceptual scheme, and I regard this as legitimate provided it yields reliable predictions.

(4) I accept a strong version of interpretive indeterminacy: if two translation manuals both render the agent fully rational and fit every observable behavioral datum, neither manual is uniquely correct; they are on a par.

(5) At the same time, when faced with two such manuals, I think we ought to prefer the one that maximizes agreement between the agent's attributed beliefs and my own beliefs about the shared environment, because charity demands convergence on truth, not just internal consistency.

(6) I hold that an agent's internal physical architecture—its weights, circuits, substrate—has no philosophical bearing on interpretation; meaning is fixed solely by observable input-output patterns and the rationalizing narrative we construct over them.

(7) Despite (6), in my debugging work I often revise an earlier interpretation of a system's outputs after inspecting its internal layers (e.g., finding a spurious correlation tied to a specific feature map), and I treat that revision as correcting what the system meant, not merely as updating my predictive model.

(8) Finally, if an agent consistently operates with a non-classical logic—say, maintaining both P and not-P in different contexts without inferential collapse—I think charity should permit us to attribute that reasoning structure directly to the agent, rather than forcing a classical reinterpretation, so long as its behavior remains structurally predictable.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, ordering them by severity. For each tension, say what has to give and what the cheapest revision would be. Also flag any pair that looks conflicted but actually dissolves once the distinction is made correctly. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or weakening one of these eight—would clean up the largest number of problems, and what it would cost me philosophically.
