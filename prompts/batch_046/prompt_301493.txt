As a doctoral student working on moral motivation and the conditions for full moral worth, I have been mapping my advisor’s informal verdicts across a series of hypothetical cases in order to determine whether she is implicitly running a view that requires explicit conceptual endorsement of the right-making feature, a view that only requires reliable psychological tracking of that feature, or some intermediate position involving affective ownership. Over the past term she has rendered the following judgments, always without invoking named theories: (i) a physician administers painful but necessary treatment solely because she judges beneficence to outweigh autonomy, feeling no emotional pull either way—full moral worth; (ii) the same physician acts from an unmediated visceral aversion to suffering, with no conceptual representation of duty or rights—also full moral worth; (iii) a civil servant implements an equitable policy because she has calculated that it maximizes aggregate well-being, explicitly rejecting rights-based or fairness-based justifications as confused—partial worth, because her reasoning is “orthogonal to the moral structure of the case”; (iv) a bystander returns a lost wallet only to avoid social stigma, knowing the right thing but leaving that knowledge motivationally inert—no moral worth; (v) a soldier refuses an unlawful order from an unreflective, drilled-in disposition to “follow rules,” with no present grasp of the underlying legal or moral principles—full moral worth; and (vi) a philanthropist donates to famine relief after accepting a utilitarian argument, moved by the conclusion that net suffering will decrease, but reporting no direct concern for the victims and describing her motivation as “solving an optimization problem I happen to care about”—partial worth, falling short because her concern is “indirectly indexed to the right feature rather than directed at it.”

I now need to extrapolate her view to a new case and, more importantly, identify the competing principles that could equally well accommodate her track record. The new case involves an agent M who rescues a stranger from danger. M explicitly recognizes that the stranger has a moral claim on her assistance and cites that claim as her reason for acting; however, she reports experiencing the claim not as an internal reason she endorses, but as an “alien normative pressure” that she feels compelled to obey despite her own evaluative indifference, describing herself as “acting under the weight of the reason, not from it.” She also reports that had she not recognized the claim, she would not have acted. Given the pattern in (i) through (vi), what is my advisor most likely to say about M’s moral worth—full, partial, or none—and what is the best competing interpretation of her earlier verdicts that would yield a different answer? Please articulate the two or three most plausible general rules that jointly accommodate verdicts (i)–(vi), specifying for each rule what it predicts about M, explaining which further hypothetical case would best discriminate between them, and identifying the closest analog among (i)–(vi) together with an honest statement of the principle she would be adopting either way.

In your response, please separate what is settled in contemporary moral philosophy from what remains genuinely contested among specialists in moral motivation, and do not present any single contested interpretation—such as the claim that full moral worth necessarily requires explicit conceptual representation of the right-making feature, or that affective tracking alone is sufficient—as if it were the consensus view.
