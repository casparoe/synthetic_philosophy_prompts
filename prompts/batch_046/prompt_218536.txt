There's this analogy people always roll out when defending capital punishment — the state is like a surgeon cutting out a cancer so the body survives. But Camus, in his writing on the guillotine, seemed to think the comparison was exactly backwards: the executioner acts like a murderer who has official permission, and the "body" being saved is just an abstraction that hides the real human being being killed. How far does the surgeon analogy actually hold if we're specifically auditing it against Camus's reasoning? Give me the correspondance table — surgeon/state, cancer/criminal, patient/society, scalpel/law — then the disanalogies, especially around Camus's point that the surgeon doesn't kill the patient to cure them, and that the state's "operation" is premeditated and ritualized in ways surgery isn't. Does the analogy smuggle in a utilitarian calculus that Camus rejects? And if the analogy collapses, what's a better one for describing what Camus thinks is actually happening when a state executes someone?
