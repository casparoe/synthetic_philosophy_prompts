I've been working through W. E. B. Du Bois's aesthetic philosophy for a graduate seminar on art and political commitment, and I keep circling back to what feels like a structural tension in his position. Du Bois is well known for arguing that all art is propaganda, and specifically that Black artists have a duty to use their work to combat racial injustice rather than pursuing beauty for its own sake. Reading this as a strict instrumentalism, it seems the value of an artwork collapses into the effectiveness of its political message. Yet in some of the secondary literature I've encountered, commentators introduce a kind of formalist correction: they suggest Du Bois operated with a double consciousness not just of racial identity but of aesthetic judgment, holding that a work must first satisfy an autonomous standard of beauty or formal excellence before its propaganda can be genuinely legitimate. If that reading is right, then Du Bois is committed both to the claim that art's value is essentially political and to the claim that art's value depends on a non-political aesthetic criterion. My confusion is this: if the formal standard is genuinely independent, then Du Bois's instrumentalism is undermined; if it is reducible to political effectiveness, then the formalist layer is redundant. So is there a coherent interpretation that reconciles these two commitments without contradiction? Or should I treat the formalist reading as a later sanitization of Du Bois's politics, and conclude that his view was genuinely tension-ridden? I'd appreciate help clarifying whether the contradiction is real or only apparent, and what principle, if any, governs the relationship between aesthetic form and propaganda in his thought.
