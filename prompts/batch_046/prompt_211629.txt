I have noticed a recurring pattern in recent regime transformations that does not align neatly with the standard vocabulary of democratic erosion, and I want to know whether it has a specific conceptual name.

The pattern runs as follows. A country retains genuinely competitive national elections: opposition parties win seats, form regional or municipal governments, and exercise formal authority within their jurisdictions. The judiciary remains structurally independent, and media outlets face no direct state censorship. Over successive electoral cycles, however, the governing majority enacts a series of legally valid framework statutes that grant the central executive broad veto powers over subnational policies, using categories—such as "economic coherence," "public morality," or "national security"—that are defined broadly enough to cover most significant regional decisions. At the same time, the legislature restructures oversight bodies—electoral commissions, audit offices, anti-corruption agencies—by replacing career civil-service leadership with politically appointed figures serving long, staggered terms, with each replacement conducted through constitutionally proper procedures. No party is banned, no election is falsified, and no state of emergency is declared. Yet the cumulative effect is that an opposition party can win a national or regional election and discover that the substantive policy domains available to it have been systematically preempted by legal architectures installed by prior majorities. The formal shell of democracy—elections, party competition, judicial review, parliamentary debate—remains intact and observable, but the range of outcomes that democratic contestation can actually produce contracts sharply.

This is often subsumed under "democratic backsliding," but that term appears to cover everything from court-packing to outright fraud and carries a normative charge that makes it difficult to separate structural description from partisan evaluation. "Competitive authoritarianism" typically requires direct manipulation of electoral fairness—skewed media access, candidate harassment—which is not the primary mechanism here. There is also talk of "abusive constitutionalism," but that usually centers on using formal constitutional amendment to dismantle institutional checks, whereas in this pattern the constitutional text itself may remain largely unchanged; the hollowing occurs through ordinary statutory preemption and administrative capture.

Does this specific configuration—preserved electoral competition, intact formal institutions, and systematic legal contraction of the opposition's governing capacity—have an established name in comparative politics? If not, how should it be precisely distinguished from ordinary democratic backsliding on one side and from competitive authoritarianism on the other? What boundary conditions and defining features would a concept need in order to capture the mechanism rather than serve as a partisan label?
