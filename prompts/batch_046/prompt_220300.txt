As a novelist, I spend more time with the architecture of character and contingency than with political theory, yet I keep returning to Machiavelli’s pairing of fortune and virtù—not the textbook version in which fortune is a river and virtù a dike, but something more structurally strange. Write a dialogue between two interlocutors who are both deeply versed in Machiavelli but who disagree fundamentally on the ontology of these forces. The first, advancing a performative and theatrical reading, should argue that virtù is essentially improvisational: the prince functions as an actor-manager who does not resist fortune so much as redirect its energy through staging, timing, and the manipulation of appearance; fortune, on this view, is not an external meteorological force but the unpredictable responsiveness of an audience that can be shaped but never fully determined. The second, working from institutional path-dependence rather than individual heroism, should argue that fortune is the accumulated inertia of prior institutional commitments—what political historians might call structural lock-in—and that virtù is therefore not an individual trait but a collective organizational capacity to reconfigure path dependencies under crisis. Both must be equally sharp, and they must respond to each other’s actual points: the theatricalist should press the institutionalist on how collective structures can generate the rapid, singular decisions Machiavelli celebrates; the institutionalist should press the theatricalist on whether a sovereign subject can truly “stage” outcomes that are already overdetermined by embedded commitments and sunk costs. The dialogue should not resolve neatly. End with each interlocutor identifying the precise point at which the other’s framework becomes either indispensable or irredeemable for making sense of Machiavelli’s text. Keep the tone formal and polished, and do not introduce citations, bibliographies, or appeals to authority outside the conceptual exchange itself.
