I have been working through Scanlon’s contractualist framework—specifically the claim that an action is wrong just in case it is permitted by principles that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement—and I have become convinced that the standard aggregation problem, in which contractualism appears unable to justify saving five lives at the cost of one because the single victim can always reject the principle allowing her sacrifice, can be resolved by distinguishing direct individual burdens from what I will call structural patterns of vulnerability. On this modified view, an agent does not need to aggregate the complaints of five separate people into a collective weight; rather, each of the five can reject a principle that permits them to be left to die, and the single potential victim can reject a principle that selects victims arbitrarily according to a structural feature—say, proximity or chronological order—rather than according to a reason that applies to her specifically. The result is that the principle permitting the rescue is not reasonably rejectable by anyone, without any interpersonal aggregation of harm taking place. I am trying to test whether this is a genuine solution or merely a relabeling of the aggregation problem, and I would value a careful assessment of its exposure.

Tell me what would count against this position—not only the familiar objection that the single victim still bears the full cost and can reject any principle that permits her death, but also which intuitions, which possible cases, and which results in other areas (say, about distributive justice or risk imposition) it stands or falls with. For instance, does the distinction between direct burden and structural pattern collapse in cases where the structural feature is itself a direct burden to the victim? Which formal features of contractualist reasoning—such as the requirement that rejection be reasonable rather than merely self-interested—would defenders invoke to protect the view, and which features expose it? I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: is there an observation about multi-party risk distribution that undermines the structural-pattern move entirely, or only a set of counterintuitive verdicts in trolley-style cases? Furthermore, I want to know what a committed defender of this modified contractualism would themselves concede as genuinely counting against them, as opposed to what critics claim but defenders can dismiss by appealing to the distinction between reasonable and unreasonable rejection. Finally, is the position well insulated—have its qualifications rendered it unfalsifiable in practice—and is that insulation a virtue or a vice?
