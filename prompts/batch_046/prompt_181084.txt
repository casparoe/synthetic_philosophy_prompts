I run a philosophy of mind seminar with about 12 graduate students in one 90-minute session. I want them to feel the epistemic force of the debate over whether dreams are experiences—specifically Malcolm’s verificationist worry that no criterion can distinguish a genuine nocturnal experience from a confabulated morning report, against Dennett’s view that the seeming experience is constructed upon awakening rather than occurring during sleep. Design an in-class role-play or structured simulation that makes this gap visceral before anyone names the philosophers. The setup: divide participants into sleepers and investigators. Each sleeper receives a sealed card—hidden from others—indicating either that they had a brief, fragmentary experience during REM or that they have only a post-waking narrative assembled from residual imagery; both groups are instructed to produce a waking report using identical emotional vocabulary and sensory detail. Investigators must devise and apply verification criteria to classify reports, committing to a protocol before seeing results. After classification is locked, reveal that one criterion either over-includes constructed narratives or excludes reports matching the genuine cards in ways that reproduce Malcolm’s problem, and that no available criterion resolves this without circularity. Include rules, role assignments, materials limited to printed cards and transcripts, a scoring mechanism that rewards accurate classification but penalizes overconfidence, a debrief that maps each failure mode onto Malcolm’s verificationism and Dennett’s construction view, and an explicit note on what will predictably go wrong the first time this runs—such as investigators demanding unavailable physiological evidence or sleepers over-performing emotional markers—and how to correct it in the moment. Also be frank about which element of Dennett’s position—his denial that dream experiences are temporally located during sleep—this simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Do not assign advance reading; do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists in the debrief.
