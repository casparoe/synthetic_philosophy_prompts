I have been working through the Stoic idea of the dichotomy of control—the distinction between what depends on our own judgment and what does not—mostly through modern books that apply it to everyday anxiety and productivity. I tried to extend it rigorously to collective action and moral cooperation, and I ended up with an argument that seems to show that consistent Stoicism makes shared moral work either irrational or hypocritical. Since I am not trained in philosophy, I am unsure whether the flaw is in my reasoning or in my understanding of the tradition. Here is the chain:

(1) The dichotomy divides the world into what is within my power—my intentions, my efforts, my responses—and what is not: other people's choices, external events, and the outcomes of shared endeavors.

(2) Modern Stoic writing uses this to argue that I should focus on my own actions and regard results with indifference, so that I am not disturbed by failure.

(3) Consider a collective moral task, such as organizing a local effort to protect a watershed. The success of this task depends on the participation of many people, on institutional decisions outside my influence, and on contingent factors like weather.

(4) Therefore, to care about whether the watershed is actually protected is to care about something that lies largely outside my control.

(5) If I genuinely care about that outcome, I violate the dichotomy and expose myself to the very anxiety and moral frustration the Stoics advise me to avoid.

(6) If, on the other hand, I genuinely treat the outcome as indifferent—caring only about my own contribution—then I have no reason to coordinate with others or to persist when cooperation is difficult. I could just as well cultivate my character in isolation.

(7) So rigorous Stoicism seems to dissolve collective moral action into either hypocrisy (caring about results while claiming indifference) or isolationism (true indifference that removes any motive for cooperation).

(8) Yet the Stoic tradition, including Marcus Aurelius, clearly treats civic virtue and justice as central duties rather than errors to be eliminated.

(9) Therefore, either the modern revival has quietly abandoned the core dichotomy, or there is a mistake in steps (3) through (6).

Where does this argument fail, if it does? I am especially reluctant to give up the intuition that we should not be enslaved to outcomes we cannot dictate, but I also do not want to conclude that working together on shared problems is philosophically irrational. If the error is in my framing of the dichotomy itself, I would like to know what a more precise distinction would look like and whether adopting it requires giving up anything essential to the Stoic view.
