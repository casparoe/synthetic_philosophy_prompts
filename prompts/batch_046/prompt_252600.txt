I'm delivering the opening remarks for a workshop titled "Recognition After Merger," convened jointly by two philosophy departments that were administratively consolidated six months ago despite sustained faculty resistance. The audience is divided: roughly half are philosophers, political theorists, and graduate students intimately familiar with Hegel's master-slave dialectic; the other half are university trustees, alumni donors, and senior administrators who have no philosophical training but hold strong views about institutional identity and autonomy. The philosophical idea must do real structural work rather than serve as ornament: I want the dialectic's logic—that self-consciousness arises only through a struggle with an other whose recognition is necessary yet never finally secured—to shape exactly what I can and cannot claim about the merger. This means I should present the consolidation not as an annihilation of independence but as an exposure of its pre-existing condition: the institution's sense of being "itself" was always mediated by recognition from students, peer institutions, and the broader academic community. Crucially, however, I must avoid the comforting narrative of resolution. I cannot say that recognition has now been achieved, that conflict has ended, or that the merged entity is "complete," because within the dialectic the struggle for recognition is perpetual and self-certainty remains structurally unstable. The address should run about eight minutes, maintain a formal but accessible tone for the mixed room, and rely on no citations, reading lists, or specific textual references—just the conceptual architecture itself.

Please write the speech in American English, ensuring that the philosophical framework determines both the argument and its necessary limits, so that non-philosophers can follow the reasoning while experts recognize that the dialectic is being taken seriously as a constraint rather than a metaphor. Keep the tone polished and occasion-appropriate: it is an opening address, not a lecture, but it should be impossible to deliver the same words without the Hegelian structure holding them together.
