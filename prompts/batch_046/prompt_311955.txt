I am a high school student preparing an independent philosophy presentation on collective cognition, and I need a rigorous diagnostic taxonomy rather than a list of labels. The case I am using is the ant colony that Douglas Hofstadter discusses in Gödel, Escher, Bach—often referred to as Aunt Hillary and the ant fugue—in which thousands of individually simple ants produce problem-solving behavior that looks like thinking, even though no single ant understands the colony's goals. Rather than asking an audience which theory they prefer by name, I want a decision tree that sorts philosophical positions according to concrete commitments they must accept or reject.

Please build a branching taxonomy that begins with the shared premise that ant colonies display genuinely adaptive, distributed problem-solving through chemical and behavioral signaling, and then divides by answers to the following four diagnostic questions—each phrased as a claim someone could agree or disagree with without already knowing the philosophical vocabulary:

1. Does having a mind require a single, unified first-person perspective, or can distributed awareness without one central point of view still count as consciousness?
2. Consider a modified colony in which every biological ant is replaced, one by one, by a silicon node that replicates the original ant's pheromone-response rules exactly, preserving all information flow and colony-level behavior but removing all biological tissue. At the end of this replacement, does the system still have a mind? (Please treat this substrate-replacement scenario as a serious branch, not a rhetorical trick.)
3. Is the absence of a brain-like central executive automatically disqualifying for mentality, or can purely functional organization across distributed agents be sufficient?
4. If two physically separated sub-colonies maintain continuous chemical linkage and shared problem-solving, are they one mind spread across space, or does spatiotemporal contiguity set a hard boundary?

For each leaf of the tree, provide the name of the position if it is established—such as emergent functionalism, biological naturalism, eliminativism about collective consciousness, distributed cognition without subjective experience, or unnamed if the combination is unexplored—and state its single most awkward philosophical commitment in one sentence. Also flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, especially if functionalist and emergentist views collapse onto the same answers here. The taxonomy should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need an instrument my classmates can use in discussion without consulting outside texts.
