I was recently discussing Kuhn's notion of incommensurability with some colleagues over dinner, and we kept circling back to a problem that seemed more concrete than the usual philosophy-of-science examples. Suppose you have a research institute where one faction treats cognition as essentially statistical pattern completion across enormous parameter spaces, while another faction maintains that genuine explanation requires explicit compositional structures with assigned semantic roles. They are jointly tasked with designing a single evaluation protocol for a proposed cognitive architecture, but they disagree not only on the results—what counts as understanding—but on what an evaluation should even measure. I want to understand the most important implications of taking Kuhnian incommensurability seriously in precisely this setting. Specifically, is there any genuinely neutral conceptual ground from which to adjudicate their competing standards of explanatory adequacy, or does the dispute collapse into a choice between irreconcilable frameworks? What practical consequences follow for peer review and funding allocation if grant panels contain members from both traditions? And should we regard this incommensurability as a temporary obstacle to be bridged, or as evidence that the field ought to split into separate disciplines with distinct evaluative criteria? Please address these points in British English, reasoning through the conceptual structure without relying on bibliographic references or lists of further reading.
