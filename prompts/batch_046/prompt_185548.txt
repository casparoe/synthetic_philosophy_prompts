I’ve been trying to make sense of what philosophers mean by “moral responsibility” without getting lost in debates about free will, and recently a friend summed up Peter Strawson’s well-known essay for me in roughly these terms. Strawson, my friend said, thinks the whole question of whether people are “really” responsible is misguided, because responsibility is not a metaphysical property that depends on determinism being false; instead, it is a social practice built around what he calls “reactive attitudes”—feelings like resentment, gratitude, and indignation that we direct at others as members of a shared moral community. According to this summary, Strawson treats these attitudes as essentially biological and unavoidable emotional reflexes, which is why the determinist versus libertarian dispute is moot: since we cannot help but feel blame, moral responsibility just amounts to whatever norms a given culture happens to enforce through these feelings. From this, my friend drew two further conclusions: first, that we should only ever ask whether expressing these attitudes is therapeutically useful, never whether they are genuinely justified; and second, that Strawson’s framework implies we could eventually replace human blame with algorithmic moderation without losing anything important, since the attitudes are really just tools for social control. I have also been wondering lately whether reactive attitudes can even be directed at non-human agents in a meaningful way, so that second conclusion strikes me as especially suspicious. Something about the summary feels like it captures Strawson’s rejection of metaphysics, yet I worry it makes his view sound far more deterministic about human psychology and far more cynical about morality than it actually is. Is this a fair account? Please separate what is accurate, what is a standard simplification, and what seems like a misrepresentation—particularly regarding whether Strawson thinks these attitudes are biologically fixed, whether he reduces morality to social utility, and whether his framework actually supports replacing interpersonal blame with automated systems.
