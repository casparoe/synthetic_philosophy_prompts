I'm serving as a teaching assistant for an upper-level philosophy of medicine seminar, and I'm grading midterm responses to a synthetic case I designed to test how well students apply naturalist and normativist frameworks beyond the textbook examples. The exam question asks students to consider a stable, heritable polymorphism in a human population that produces a chronically elevated baseline inflammatory response—statistically atypical when measured against the full species-wide distribution, yet evolutionarily maintained because it confers strong resistance to an endemic pathogen. The prompt then asks: How would Christopher Boorse's biostatistical theory classify this condition? How would Jerome Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis classify it? And can the concept of evolutionary mismatch—conditions that arise when environments shift faster than adaptive traits—be incorporated into either framework without collapsing into pure normativism?

Below is one student's answer. I need you to assign a grade (either a letter grade or a score out of ten) and provide specific, point-by-point comments justifying it. The response gets the broad architecture right—Boorse's statistical-functional account versus Wakefield's hybrid dysfunction-plus-harm model—but I suspect there are one or two subtle mistakes that a hasty grader might let slide, possibly involving how reference classes operate in BST, or whether evolutionary mismatch reasoning necessarily imports normative judgments about "proper" environments. In your assessment, please clearly separate what is settled in the relevant literature (for instance, that BST employs age- and sex-specific reference classes, or that HDA requires both an evolutionary failure of function and an evaluation of harm) from what remains genuinely contested (such as whether Wakefield's harm criterion is ultimately reducible to social values, or whether evolutionary medicine can remain purely descriptive). Do not present any contested interpretive claim—especially regarding the reducibility of harm judgments or the normative status of mismatch theory—as if it were the consensus view among philosophers of medicine.

Student answer: "According to Boorse's biostatistical theory, disease is a statistical deviation from the normal functioning of a part or process, where normal is defined by the species-typical contribution to survival and reproduction. Because this polymorphism is statistically atypical across the entire species, Boorse must classify it as a disease, even though it is protective against infection. Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis requires both a dysfunction—failure of an internal mechanism to perform its naturally selected function—and harm evaluated against social norms. Since the elevated inflammatory response is performing exactly its evolved protective function, there is no dysfunction, so HDA correctly excludes it from being a disease. The concept of evolutionary mismatch holds that diseases arise when modern environments differ from those in which our traits evolved, but this is ultimately a normativist move: it implicitly judges current environments as wrong or unnatural for our biology. Therefore, neither theory can accommodate mismatch conditions without smuggling in value judgments about the environments humans ought to inhabit."
