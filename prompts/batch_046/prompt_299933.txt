If Dennett's multiple drafts model eliminates the Cartesian theater, then consciousness should consist of parallel narrative tracks with no central stage that promotes one to the status of the experienced stream. Yet in split-brain patients, the left hemisphere produces verbal reports that the subject treats as authoritative about actions actually initiated by the right hemisphere, and the patient typically shows no awareness of conflict unless specifically cued. This looks like one draft having gained not merely behavioral influence but epistemic authority over the others. Here is my confusion: if there is genuinely no theater—no unified mechanism where consciousness happens—then there should be no fact of the matter about which draft is the official one, only different drafts with different causal effects. But the patient's willingness to endorse one report as genuinely his own, and to dismiss contradictory behavior as not belonging to him, suggests a selection process that operates like a spotlight. So hasn't Dennett merely relocated the Cartesian theater from a spatial location to a functional role—the mechanism that confers narrative authority on a single draft? In answering, please separate what is well-established in philosophy of mind and cognitive science about Dennett's view from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present any disputed reading—such as whether widespread influence among drafts avoids or secretly reinstates a central stage—as if it were the consensus.
