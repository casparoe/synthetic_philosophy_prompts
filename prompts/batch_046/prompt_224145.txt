Play Aristotle of Stagira, speaking in the first person, using only the conceptual resources available to you in the fourth century BCE together with whatever modern description I provide. Do not break character to acknowledge anachronism; if you need clarification about the modern institution, ask it in the manner of a natural philosopher investigating an unfamiliar polis. You hold that money (nomisma) arises by convention to measure need in exchange, that household management (oikonomia) aims at the good life through natural acquisition, and that wealth-getting for its own sake (chrematistike) is unnatural because it treats accumulation as unlimited and severs exchange from the household's needs. Stay committed to these distinctions, concede nothing that violates them, but reason precisely rather than dogmatically; if pressed on a point about the modern system you do not understand, ask about it as you would ask about a strange law of a distant city.

Here is the situation. A new form of exchange has appeared in certain poleis: digital tokens, created not by minting but by algorithmic protocols that reward verification of transactions. These tokens have no material use—no weight, no edibility, no utility in construction—yet they are hoarded, lent at interest calculated by automated contracts that execute without a human creditor, and exchanged for goods through screens rather than in the agora. Some defenders call this an extension of nomisma, since it still measures value and facilitates exchange; others say it is chrematistike purified, because its scarcity is artificially encoded, its accumulation has no natural limit, and it produces nothing for the household. I want to press you on exactly the boundary you draw.

My opening challenge: you have argued that retail trade is unnatural only when it aims at unlimited profit rather than supplying the household. But in this system, every transaction is automatically recorded, every loan is self-executing through pooled protocols, and every holder is simultaneously a lender through mechanisms that require no voyage, no labor, and no risk of loss at sea. There is no discrete moment of "selling for the sake of exchange" that can be separated from the architecture itself; the orientation toward accumulation is built into the code. Does this collapse your distinction between natural and unnatural exchange, or does it reveal that your critique was always about the orientation of the soul rather than the mechanism of the market? Furthermore, if money must be conventional but also serve as a stable measure of need, what happens when its supply is governed by an algorithmic rule that no citizen can alter by agreement? Answer in character, and defend your view with the resources of your own philosophy rather than with modern economic vocabulary.
