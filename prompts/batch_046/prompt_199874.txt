I've been in a historiography course this semester and we keep circling back to presentism — wether historians can or should escape the moral standards of there own time when they study the past. On one side you've got people arguing that if you don't bracket your modern ethics you literally can't understand past actors, because you're just mapping current categories onto mental worlds that don't share them; they talk about "sympathetic reconstruction" like it's a methodological requirement. On the other side people say that pretending to suspend your values is impossible and dangerous, since the very choice of what counts as data — whose letters survive, which violence gets named — already embeds present judgments. We've argued this for like six sessions and nobody's moved.

My working guess is that the stall isn't about facts but about what "understanding" is even supposed to do: one camp treats it as a kind of translation task where you have to render past reasons in terms the actors themselves would recognize, and the other treats it as an evaluation task where you compare past actions to stable moral standards that don't change just because people were ignorant of them. But maybe I'm wrong and it's actually a hidden disagreement about whether moral concepts have cross-temporal reference at all — like, do "cruelty" and "justice" pick out the same properties in 1700 and 2024, or are they indexicals? I want a diagnosis, not another round of examples. Is this a verbal dispute about "objectivity," a substantive clash over the metaphysics of moral properties, or two different disciplines — explanation versus criticism — wearing the same hat? And if your diagnosis is right, what would actually break it: a conceptual distinction, a specific historical case that forces both sides to agree on the data, or is progress basically impossible because both sides are defending bedrock intuitions about what scholarship is for?
