The only way to save the five is to actively discontinue treatment for the one. In a conversation about this scenario, someone dismissed the proposal outright by saying “the ends don’t justify the means,” treating the phrase as if it were a self-evident refutation. I feel the dismissal is too quick, but I cannot precisely articulate what the phrase claims or whether it genuinely applies here.

Could you disambiguate the slogan for me? Please lay out, as a menu of distinct readings, the precise theses it might be expressing—ranging from the tautological to the radically deontological. For instance: that good outcomes are never relevant to moral evaluation; that there is an absolute prohibition on certain kinds of actions regardless of consequence; that an agent’s direct intention makes a moral difference that a merely foreseen side effect does not; that aggregate welfare cannot override individual rights or institutional rules; or that “justification” concerns only the agent’s motives rather than the objective rightness of the act. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or merely ambiguous, and then specify whether that particular version actually supports the judgment that actively withdrawing the ventilator from one patient to save five is impermissible, or whether it leaves that judgment unsupported.

I am preparing to discuss this with friends and want to know exactly which claim I would be committing to if I agreed with the person who invoked the slogan.
