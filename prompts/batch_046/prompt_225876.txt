The exam question asked students to present the central argument in which Gongsun Long claims that a white horse is not a horse, then to discuss Hui Shi's reply, and finally to explain whether the disagreement is best understood as a confusion between compound properties and simple objects or as a deeper logical point about how terms combine. The prompt also instructed them to distinguish Gongsun Long's specific position from the broader tendencies of the School of Names and to avoid treating all Mingjia thinkers as if they shared a single unified doctrine.

Here is the student's answer. It begins correctly by stating that Gongsun Long separates the universal form horse from the compound determination white horse, arguing that since whiteness belongs to color and horseness belongs to form, their conjunction produces a different determination that cannot be subsumed under the simple term. The student also correctly recounts Hui Shi's counter-example, noting that if the addition of a color always dissolved membership in the original category, then a yellow horse would also fail to be a horse, which is plainly false. These are the visible strengths. But then the student writes that Hui Shi's response is best reconstructed in modern predicate logic as an appeal to the formal principle that for all x, if x is a horse and x is white then x is a horse, treating Hui Shi as if he were defending a logical identity of properties rather than offering an intuitive counter-instance about weighing similarities and differences together. That is a subtle distortion. Next, the student claims that Gongsun Long intended his argument as a reductio ad absurdum of the School of Names' own commitment to universal forms, suggesting that by pushing separation to an extreme he demonstrates the collapse of all universal terms. This inverts the standard reading: Gongsun Long is generally interpreted as defending separation, not undermining universals from within. Finally, the student concludes that the dialogue reveals an early version of the modern intension-extension distinction, applying this anachronistically to both figures without acknowledging that neither operates within a semantic framework that distinguishes sense from reference in anything like a Fregean manner.
