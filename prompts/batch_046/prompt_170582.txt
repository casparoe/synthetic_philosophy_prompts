My friend Leo and his cousin Nina spent most of last Saturday arguing about whether science actually solves the problem of induction or just hides it behind fancy language. Leo began by describing Nelson Goodman’s “grue” puzzle—emeralds that are green before some future date and blue afterward—but then he added a twist of his own. He asked Nina to imagine a climate variable he called “therm-green,” which behaves exactly like temperature until the year 2080 and then inverts its scale. He said climate scientists would reject any model built on “therm-green” not because it has already failed, but because the predicate is “gerrymandered”: it smuggles in a hidden cutoff date. Leo took this to mean that induction only works because we have hidden rules about which descriptions are “projectible,” and that those rules do all the real work.

Nina said that is not a solution; it is just a relabeling of prejudice. She argued that “gerrymandered” is defined relative to the conceptual scheme we happen to have inherited. If human cognition had evolved so that “therm-green” was our basic thermal concept and “temperature” was the derivative one that needed an explicit date-free definition, then “temperature” would look like the gerrymandered predicate. So picking “green” over “grue,” or “temperature” over “therm-green,” is just picking our scheme, not justifying induction.

Leo replied that there is an objective formal difference: “green” can be specified without mentioning any particular date, whereas “grue” and “therm-green” both contain a hidden temporal parameter baked into their definitions. That syntactic simplicity is a real feature of the predicate, not a cultural accident.

Nina came back that simplicity is always measured inside some formal system, and you can redesign the system—choose different primitives, different axioms—to make “grue” basic and “green” the complex derivative. So syntactic simplicity cannot break the tie unless you have already decided which formal system is the correct one, and that decision is exactly the circularity Hume identified.

Leo then shifted ground: even if conceptual schemes are tied, induction still functions because consistency demands that we stick to one scheme across time. The point is not to prove metaphysically that the future must resemble the past; it is to show that if you switch predicates arbitrarily—using “green” today and “grue” tomorrow—you contradict yourself.

Nina said consistency is cheap. If she consistently uses “therm-green” for every prediction, she will never generate an internal contradiction either; she will simply say, when 2080 arrives, that the definition has flipped according to its own terms. So consistency does not select the right scheme; it only punishes sloppiness within a scheme.

Leo’s next move was that scientific theories are empirically tested, and theories built on “temperature” or “green” keep passing tests, while theories built on “therm-green” do not. Therefore the scheme is selected by predictive success, not arbitrarily.

Nina answered that the testing process itself uses the predicates of the scheme being tested. You observe emeralds with eyes calibrated to register “green,” not “grue”; you measure heat with instruments calibrated to “temperature,” not “therm-green.” So appealing to empirical success to justify the scheme is like using a ruler to check whether the ruler itself is straight.

Then say what both are missing, particularly regarding whether induction needs a global justification or can operate with local, predictive criteria. Finally, indicate who is more right overall, or whether the dispute ends in a stalemate, and explain why. Do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I am trying to think this through on my own.
