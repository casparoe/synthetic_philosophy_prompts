I'm trying to clarify my own commitments about when an object genuinely possesses a disposition in the presence of finks, masks, or internal counteragents. My judgments so far are these. A sugar cube enclosed in a water-soluble film that delays dissolution for several minutes is still soluble when dropped into tea; the film is an external mask. A wire that conducts electricity unless touched, because an attached sensor instantly severs the circuit, is not live; the interrupter belongs to the system that determines the wire's state. A heart with arrhythmic tissue but a permanently implanted pacemaker that corrects every irregular beat is still disposed to arrhythmia; the pacemaker suppresses the manifestation without removing the tissue's tendency. A synthetic toxin chemically modified so that each molecule carries an intrinsic self-neutralizing group that activates upon tissue contact is not toxic; the neutralizer is part of the toxin's own constitution. And a match stored in an oxygen-free chamber is still flammable; the absence of oxygen is an external missing condition, not an interfering presence.

What principle, if there is one, captures this pattern? I am especially unsure whether the key distinction is spatial or functional—whether the interfering factor lies inside or outside the object's boundary—or whether it concerns whether the manifestation actually occurs even if immediately counteracted, or perhaps whether the interference is triggered specifically by the stimulus that would elicit the disposition. Please distinguish what is broadly settled in the philosophical literature on dispositions from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view as the consensus. Would I call the enzyme catalytic? Also tell me which one of my five listed verdicts your reconstructed principle must reject, and propose one further hypothetical case that would discriminate between the two most plausible principles you identify.
