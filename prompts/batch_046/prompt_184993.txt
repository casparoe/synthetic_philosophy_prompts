I am leading a ninety-minute seminar for second-year politics students on Simone Weil's argument that political parties ought to be suppressed. They have read her essay on the suppression of political parties but will arrive treating it as a historical footnote to post-war constitutional design rather than as a live critique of collective cognition. Plan the session's explanatory arc: what must come first—her distinction between justice as attention to particularity and the party as a machine for generating collective passion—so that the institutional conclusion follows rather than precedes the epistemic claim; the single carrying comparison or case study (not a generic party-machine example) that illustrates how collective allegiance corrupts individual judgment without relying solely on twentieth-century French politics; the predictable misconceptions in order—first that she is simply anti-democratic, then that she ignores legitimate pluralism, then that her proposal is utopian—and precisely where in the sequence each must be defused; one targeted question or brief exercise at the end that reveals whether students have grasped the corruption of attention rather than merely memorised the constitutional proposal; and which biographical or theological elements should be explicitly bracketed at this level so the argument remains philosophically sharp rather than hagiographic. Use British spelling and keep the focus on pedagogical dependency rather than on delivering the explanation itself.
