The idea I want to push is that knowing the distant past isn’t just about missing records, it’s about negative archives—when absence itself is structured by who had the power to write and preserve. Herodotus reports contradictory traditions without always decidin between them, Thucydides wants to cross-check and lock things down, but both still treat the past as something that either leaves traces or doesn’t. In the novel the library is literally a labyrinth designed to hide texts, and the whole plot turns on suppressing a lost Aristotelian manuscript. Could you read the book through that lens: how does Eco dramatize the claim that every archive is also an instument of concealment, and where does William’s rationalist, Thucydidean-style investigation run into walls that aren’t just accidents but built-in features? I’m especially curious whether the novel ends up closer to Herodotus’s openness, or to a deeper skepticism that undermines both, and where the work resists being turned into a simple illustration of source criticism—what does it actually add about the politics of preservation that straight philosophy misses?
