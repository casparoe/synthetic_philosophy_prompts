In my high school course on strategic reasoning and philosophical logic, we have spent several weeks on the centipede game and the backward induction paradox. The dominant interpretation presented by my instructor—and reinforced by the texts we have examined—is that the paradox dissolves once one accepts common knowledge of rationality together with the assumption that each player prefers ending the interaction one step earlier than being ended upon by the opponent. On this view, the rational agent should take the payoff immediately at the first node; the fact that experimental subjects routinely continue for multiple rounds is treated not as a counterexample to the logic itself but as evidence of cognitive limitation, emotional attachment to cooperation, or failure to perform the full recursive reasoning. I have come to think that this defense protects itself by treating any deviation from immediate defection as a departure from rationality, rather than allowing that rationality itself might be structured differently across sequential stages.

More specifically, I want to defend the position that backward induction in the centipede game is conceptually unstable rather than merely empirically violated, because the recursive step requires an agent at round n to reason about her own behavior at round n+1 using the same epistemic framework she uses to reason about her opponent's behavior—what I would call an illicit temporal symmetry. From the first-person perspective of the agent actually situated at round n, her future self is not an external mechanism whose actions she can predict with the same detachment she applies to the other player; rather, her future reasoning remains continuous with her present deliberation. If this asymmetry holds, then the induction fails not because agents are irrational, but because the model of rationality presupposes a kind of self-objectification that collapses when we take seriously the indexical nature of practical reasoning over time.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I am asking for an honest assessment of how exposed my view actually is: which formal results in game theory, which experimental designs, or which distinctions in the philosophy of action would genuinely undermine the claim that backward induction depends on an illegitimate symmetry between self and other? Would decisive counterevidence have to show that agents can consistently treat their future reasoning as an object of present calculation without contradiction, or is there a deeper conceptual point I am missing? I also want to know whether defenders of the standard backward-induction account themselves concede anything resembling this asymmetry—perhaps in discussions of precommitment, personal identity over time, or dynamic consistency—and whether those concessions are treated as peripheral qualifications or as serious structural problems. Finally, I worry that I have insulated my view by defining "reflective rationality" in opposition to "mechanistic rationality," allowing me to dismiss any successful backward-induction behavior as belonging to the latter category. Is that insulation defensible, or has it rendered the view unfalsifiable in a damaging way? Please distinguish the evidence that would be genuinely decisive from what would merely be awkward, and indicate whether there are live escape routes for my position or whether it risks collapsing into a rival account of sequential choice.
