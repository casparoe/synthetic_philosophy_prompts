At a philosophy-of-biology seminar, two colleagues—Mara and Leo—debated whether DNA is genuinely a "code" in any philosophically substantial sense, or whether the term is only a stretched metaphor. They went back and forth in front of the group, and the room split. Here is the exchange as reported by a bystander.

Mara opened by saying DNA is literally a code: triplet codons function as symbols standing for specific amino acids, and the mapping is arbitrary, as shown by variant genetic codes in mitochondria and certain ciliates. The ribosome, she said, acts as a decoder that reads these symbols and translates them into protein sequences, analogously to a CPU reading binary instructions.

Leo replied that this imports features of language and computation that do not apply. In a genuine code there is an interpreter functionally independent of the message medium; but the ribosome and tRNAs are themselves built from molecules whose structures are specified by DNA, so the "decoder" is just another part of the chemical system. Moreover, the mapping is not arbitrary: there are stereochemical affinities between certain codons and amino acids, and evolutionary optimization explains the rest. The "symbol" is not separable from its physical token—you cannot write the same codon in ink or sound waves, because the codon just is three specific bases in a chain.

Mara countered that circularity is not unique to DNA—human brains learn language using neural structures partly shaped by genetics, yet language remains representational. The existence of variant codes demonstrates contingency: if the mapping were chemically fixed, no variation would be possible. She added that physical embodiment does not eliminate representation—computer bits are voltage states, yet they carry semantic content about computations.

Leo said this conflates causal information with semantic information. DNA carries Shannon information—statistical correlations between sequence and protein—but there is no "aboutness," no interpretation, and no possibility of misrepresentation in the philosophical sense. A mutation does not "misrepresent" a protein; it simply causes a different chemical cascade. The ribosome does not understand or decode in any intentional sense; it reacts. To call DNA semantic is to anthropomorphize chemistry. The right distinction, he argued, is between causal-informational chains and genuinely representational ones, and DNA clearly belongs to the former.

Mara's final move was that Leo sets the bar for representation implausibly high. By his standard, even a thermostat might fail to represent temperature, and we might have to deny representation to all non-conscious systems. But philosophers of biology routinely treat DNA as carrying teleosemantic content—the gene "means" the protein it produces in a derived, evolutionary sense. The code metaphor captures this functional normativity: there is a correct and incorrect translation, just as there is correct and incorrect execution of a computer instruction.

Leo responded that teleosemantics is precisely the contested theory here, not a neutral description. Invoking it assumes what needs to be shown—that evolutionary history can ground genuine representational content rather than merely causal reliability. Without that assumption, all we have is a highly reliable biochemical correlation, not a code in any sense that distinguishes it from, say, the correlation between a key's shape and a lock opening.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Mara's and Leo's contributions, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right but incomplete, clearly right, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify the specific point—particularly around representational content versus causal information, and the role of teleosemantics—that both parties are missing or that should break the tie.
