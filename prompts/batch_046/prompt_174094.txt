I've been working through my own judgments about when moral norms bind an agent directly versus only through an idealized deliberative counterpart, and I'm trying to see if there's a coherent principle behind the pattern rather than a list of exceptions.

Here are my concrete verdicts. When a person is temporarily severely depressed and therefore incapable of weighing long-term consequences, I think norms against serious self-harm apply to them directly, not merely to a fully rational reconstruction of them. When someone is permanently and profoundly intellectually disabled, I think the same protective norms are in force but are mediated: we safeguard them not because they directly violate a duty, but because an idealized guardian deliberating on their behalf would will protection. If a trained professional uses a quick heuristic in a crisis and violates a norm that a fully deliberative version of herself would have observed, I think she is directly accountable for the failure in that moment. But if a sleep-deprived surgeon makes an analogous error during an operation, I think the direct norm is suspended in the moment and the failure is only indirect—located in her prior planning failure to avoid operating while exhausted. If someone has been systematically deceived about the consequences of their actions, I think they have done wrong directly, even though an ideally informed version of them would not have acted that way. Yet if someone acts from a deeply ingrained cultural bias that they genuinely cannot recognize or articulate, I think direct accountability is suspended and only the idealized, corrected version bears the normative burden. The case I keep reversing on is a person with moderate dementia who retains intermittent lucidity but cannot maintain consistent deliberation across time: sometimes I think direct norms apply only during the lucid intervals, other times I think they apply continuously through an idealized model of the agent's enduring practical identity.

Is there one account of indirect versus direct normativity—perhaps indexed to something like control, accessibility to correction, or the structure of the agent's practical reasoning—that captures all or most of these verdicts? What does the best such principle predict about the dementia case? And please state honestly which of my judgments it cannot accommodate without becoming ad hoc: the distinction between the crisis professional and the sleep-deprived surgeon, the asymmetry between deception-induced and bias-induced ignorance, or the split treatment of temporary versus permanent cognitive incapacity? If the principle requires dropping one verdict to stay simple, which one should go, and what does that imply about whether indirect normativity is fundamentally about the agent's actual capacities or about the counterfactual path of idealized deliberation?
