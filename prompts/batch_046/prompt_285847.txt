I cover science and ideas as a journalist, and I am designing an assessment for an upper-level philosophy seminar that will appear in a feature on conceptual puzzles that resist summary answers. The topic is self-deception: is it even possible? Rather than asking students to rehearse the standard taxonomy—Sartre on bad faith, Davidson on divided minds, or the intentionalist versus deflationary dispute—the exam should force them to confront a more granular crux: what I will call the problem of asymmetric evidential licensing. The question should present a carefully constructed case in which an agent suppresses decisive evidence for belief B, applies rigorous evidential standards to every other domain, and upon reflection sincerely endorses those standards as correct, all without revising B. Students should then be asked whether this structure is conceptually coherent. Specifically, they must evaluate whether maintaining B requires the agent to occupy two genuinely incompatible normative stances—one that licenses suppression and one that demands universal application—or whether it can be explained by a single stance with a localized, non-rational blind spot that does not amount to full-blown deception. The answer must address whether selective blindness collapses self-deception into ordinary weakness of will, and whether reflective endorsement of the evidential standard undermines the very possibility that the suppression is intentional rather than merely negligent.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. The question should be suitable for a 90-minute sitting. The model answer should not rely on citations, bibliographies, or named authorities; it should build the argument from conceptual analysis alone. The marking scheme should be out of 20 marks, organized around five must-hit points—each with its point value and a one-line description of how candidates typically miss it. The scheme should clearly separate an A answer, which resolves the licensing asymmetry with precision, from a B answer, which summarizes familiar positions but fails to explain how one stance can both endorse and violate the same standard. Do not include reading lists, requests for further sources, or any prompt for bibliographic verification.
