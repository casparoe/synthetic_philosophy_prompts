I am preparing an essay for my philosophy of language class, and I want to read Shakespeare's Othello through the current debate over whether there is a genuine moral difference between lying and misleading. I am especially interested in applying the distinction—familiar from Grice's analysis of conversational maxims and from more recent discussions of "paltering," in which a speaker uses technically true statements arranged to generate false beliefs—to Iago's manipulation of Othello in Acts III and IV. Rather than inventing explicit falsehoods about Desdemona's fidelity, Iago selects truthful details, such as the handkerchief and Cassio's observed behavior, and arranges them so that Othello draws the fatal inference himself. Please give me a reading that asks whether the play treats this method as morally different from outright lying, and whether its tragic force depends on Iago's control being of a particular kind: exploiting Othello's reasoning autonomy rather than overriding it with direct falsehood. I want you to take seriously both the philosophical arguments—for instance, that misleading respects the listener's agency in a way lying does not, versus that it is more insidious precisely because it co-opts that agency—and the dramatic evidence, including Iago's soliloquies, the audience's privileged knowledge, Othello's own language of proof and certainty, and the specific scenes in which suspicion is planted. Where does the play illuminate a distinction that abstract argument alone misses, perhaps about the moral significance of leaving the victim's epistemic labor intact? Where does it resist this framing, and what should an honest critic concede? I am not looking for a bibliography, citations, or a reading list; I need a critical interpretation that takes both the philosophical debate and the play seriously, and that could guide my essay without requiring access to sources I do not have.
