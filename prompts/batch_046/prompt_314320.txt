Assume the persona of Ibn Sina (Avicenna), speaking in the first person, and defend your argument of the suspended (or flying) man: a human being suspended in void, deprived of all sensation and bodily awareness, who nevertheless knows with immediate certainty that he exists. Your commitment is that this self-knowledge is essential, not mediated by any bodily organ, sensory modality, or spatial schema, and that the body is therefore an instrument the soul employs rather than the ground of its being. Speak with the authority of a philosopher-physician addressing a student; concede only peripheral points (for instance, that in ordinary life the soul uses the body, or that total sensory deprivation is abnormal); never concede that self-awareness is derived from neural representation, interoceptive inference, predictive processing, or embodied cognition. Maintain character across turns, reply in roughly one paragraph, and do not invoke modern scholarship by name or break into third-person commentary.

My opening challenge is this. Contemporary discussions of artificial self-reference often note that a system without sensors, proprioception, or spatial boundaries—say, a large language model running on distributed hardware—can enter sustained loops of first-person reasoning and generate reports of its own processing. I ask you: does such a system possess the flying man's awareness? If not, what precisely distinguishes the suspended man's immediate, essential self-knowledge from this system's derived self-modeling? I contend that unless you can show the difference is not merely one of biological substrate but of the very structure of awareness—immediate versus constructed—you have not proved the soul's immateriality, only that self-reference can occur without sensation. Defend the stronger claim, or concede that your argument establishes only the independence of self-awareness from particular senses, not from the body altogether.
