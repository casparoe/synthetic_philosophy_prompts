In the literature on Frank Ramsey’s scattered remarks about whether life has meaning—particularly his well-known dismissal of the question as malformed—interpretation has stalled between two camps: those who treat him as advancing a proto-logical-positivist semantic thesis, according to which the question violates conditions of meaningfulness, and those who take him to be advancing a subtle pragmatic naturalism about value, continuous with his decision-theoretic account of rational belief and practical reasoning. For years, neither side has conceded ground, and the standard textual evidence appears exhausted on both sides. Is this impasse substantive, verbal, or a case of two projects answering different questions under a single heading—perhaps one about semantic well-formedness, the other about the conditions for practical orientation? Or is there a hidden disagreement about whether Ramsey’s theory of belief and truth, developed in his logical writings, even permits normative evaluation of a life as a whole? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement with reference to what each camp actually says, indicate what kind of conceptual distinction or formal result could actually move it, and say whether progress is possible or whether the stall is itself a sign that the framing question has been misposed.
