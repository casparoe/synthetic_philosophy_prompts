Consider the institutional triad formed by German administrative data-protection law, American open-government doctrine, and Chinese social-credit architecture. In German federal practice, data-protection officers possess veto authority over state processing of citizen records, and the legal framework treats the disclosure of an individual’s address, employment history, or tax details to third parties as a presumptive harm requiring justification, irrespective of whether any material or psychological damage actually results; the protection is anchored in a constitutional conception of personality rather than in a risk-benefit calculus. In the American administrative tradition, by contrast, Freedom of Information statutes treat government-held records about citizens as presumptively public assets, and constitutional doctrine denies any reasonable expectation of privacy in information voluntarily conveyed to regulators for licensing, taxation, or welfare purposes, so that openness is the default and concealment requires exceptional justification. Meanwhile, Chinese social-credit systems aggregate behavioural data drawn from both public agencies and private platforms to generate composite reputation scores that determine access to transport, employment, and credit, operating on the explicit premise that transparency of conduct is a civic obligation and that informational opacity is itself a marker of untrustworthiness.

These three arrangements appear to rest on incompatible ontologies of the citizen in relation to the state: one conceives the person as an informational boundary whose integrity is violated by exposure regardless of consequences; another conceives the citizen as a data-point embedded in a public record that belongs, in principle, to the polity; the third conceives the individual as a reputation-bearing node whose social value is constituted by visibility to an evaluating system.

What philosophical commitments about personhood, harm, and the legitimacy of state knowledge does this triad strictly presuppose, and where is it merely compatible with rival views? Must the German position assume that informational boundaries possess non-instrumental value, or could it be reconstructed on purely consequentialist grounds without loss? Does American openness necessarily presuppose that citizens are transparent data-points belonging to the polity, or is it compatible with a robust theory of individual informational sovereignty that simply assigns different weights to publicity and privacy? And does the social-credit model commit its architects to a view of moral character as constituted by visibility, or can it be defended as a purely instrumental coordination mechanism that carries no ontology of the person? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind each practice, distinguish what each strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, identify where the three are philosophically underdetermined, and say what a coherent policy built on wild-animal-style informational vulnerability—treating opacity as a basic welfare interest—would have to change in each institutional context.
