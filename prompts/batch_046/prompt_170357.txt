I am in the final stages of revising a novel that opens with an epigraph drawn from a poem in a language I do not read fluently—an old dialect in which the verb for carrying a physical burden shares its root with the verb for sustaining an unspoken sorrow, and in which the grammatical case applied to the subject shifts depending on whether the action is performed with deliberate agency or endured as an involuntary experience. I fed the stanza into a statistical translation engine, and it rendered the line with perfect grammatical fluency: the speaker is said to “support a heavy structure,” using the agentive form. Yet the original context—a widow sitting motionless in an empty chapel—clearly demands the experiential form, “to bear a grief that does not diminish.” The engine selected the more frequent collocation in its training corpus, not the one that the poem’s emotional architecture requires. I corrected the line by hand, but now I am second-guessing myself. Am I merely being protective of my own interpretive authority as the author, or does the engine’s mistake reveal something deeper—namely, that it rearranges surface patterns without grasping the conceptual frames that make one meaning live and the other dead? More practically, if I include the corrected version in my book, am I attributing to the poem a meaning that exists only through my own analogical reasoning, and does that matter for a work of fiction? I do not ask for a bibliography; I ask for a clear judgment on whether I should trust the statistical output when it conflicts with my intuitive sense of the poem’s interior logic, and whether that conflict itself proves that translation requires something like understanding rather than mere pattern matching.
