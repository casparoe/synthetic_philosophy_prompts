Compose a take-home examination prompt (expected response: 1,500–2,000 words) for a final-year undergraduate seminar in epistemology, together with a model answer and a detailed marking rubric. The examination should center on the regress problem and the three classical responses—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—but it must be designed so that it cannot be answered by reciting textbook summaries. To that end, embed the following specific scenario in the exam prompt itself: a subject S holds belief B1, which is justified by B2; B2 is justified by B3; and so on in a linear chain. Crucially, B1 also possesses a non-inferential, fallible default entitlement that can confer weak warrant back onto B3 should B3's linear support fail, without B1 being inferred from B3. Ask the student to determine whether this structure (i) avoids the regress, (ii) collapses into one of the three classical positions under a different description, or (iii) generates a distinct, fourth problem—specifying exactly what that problem is and which of the three classical responses best addresses it. The model answer must identify the precise logical moves required for full marks: distinguishing circular reasoning from mutual support, analyzing whether the feedback loop is genuinely non-inferential, evaluating whether an infinite chain remains necessary despite the loop, and assessing whether the scenario ultimately vindicates a stratified foundationalism, a holistic coherentism, or an infinitist chain with local closure conditions. The marking scheme should allocate specific weight to conceptual precision (40%), argument reconstruction and evaluation (40%), and identification of the hidden tension between non-inferential entitlement and linear justification (20%). Specify common errors that should cost marks—e.g., conflating circularity with coherence, treating default entitlement as merely another inferential step, or asserting that the loop eliminates the need for any base without argument. The prompt must be self-contained: do not request bibliographies, citations, or external reading lists, and frame all necessary concepts within the exam text so that a student who has attended the seminar can respond without searching for sources.
