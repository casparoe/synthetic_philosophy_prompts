I am revising a chapter in which a retired moral philosopher delivers a lecture summarizing P. F. Strawson's account of moral responsibility, and I want to verify the conceptual architecture before finalizing the scene. A set of lecture notes I have been given summarizes the view in this way: Strawson holds that moral responsibility is constituted entirely by reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, and indignation—which function as essentially involuntary emotional reflexes triggered by perceived harm or benefit. According to the notes, Strawson concedes that if determinism is true these reflexes are epistemically unwarranted, yet he insists that human psychology is hard-wired for reciprocity, so we cannot genuinely abandon them; from this he concludes that determinism is probably false, or at least that belief in it ought to be suspended in ordinary interpersonal life. The summary further claims that Strawson distinguishes sharply between reactive attitudes and what he terms objective attitudes—the clinical or managerial stances reserved for children, the mentally ill, or machines—and that he believes a consistent determinist must adopt objective attitudes toward all adult peers. Finally, the notes assert that Strawson's framework provides no normative resources for distinguishing legitimate resentment from pathological bitterness, since both are merely reactive attitudes, and that this collapse undermines his defense of moral responsibility against the free-will skeptic.

Please audit this summary with a committed eye: examine each claim in turn, noting which parts a careful reader of Strawson would accept, which involve subtle conflations—perhaps between participant reactive attitudes and vicarious ones, or between the local suspension of an attitude and its global replacement by an objective stance—and which are flat misreadings. In particular, take a definite position on whether Strawson treats reactive attitudes as bare reflexes, whether he argues for the falsity of determinism, whether the objective attitude is intended as a universal substitute for interpersonal engagement, and whether the framework genuinely collapses normative distinctions within blame. I want your verdict delivered with confidence rather than as an even-handed survey of interpretive options: tell me precisely where the summary is distorted, where it is nearly right, and whether its concluding charge—that Strawson's view reduces to a psychological determinism about blame—survives once the misrepresentations are corrected.
