After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend last night about Quine's thesis on the indeterminacy of translation, I want to step back and see whether my own intuitions about particular cases form a coherent pattern or whether I am just contradicting myself. The argument kept returning to the gavagai example, but it also spilled over into other scenarios where two translation manuals seem empirically equivalent yet I want to treat them differently. I need a precise principle—perhaps involving behavioral evidence, ontological charity, notational variance, or rationality constraints—that captures my verdicts, tells me which ones I must abandon if they conflict, and predicts what I should say about a case I have not yet considered.

Here are my verdicts, in order.

First, the standard scenario: a linguist can build two empirically adequate manuals for an unfamiliar language. In Manual R the native utterance gavagai is translated as rabbit. In Manual P the same utterance is translated as undetached rabbit part, or as a temporal stage of a rabbit. I fully accept that no further behavioral observation decides between R and P. Yet I resist treating them as mere notational variants, like Celsius and Fahrenheit. It feels to me that R and P genuinely disagree about what the natives are referring to, even though neither is factually wrong. Verdict: there is real indeterminacy, but it is not reducible to a choice of notation.

Second, consider a language with no explicit plural marking. One manual introduces a plural operator in the metalanguage to handle talk about many rabbits; another keeps every noun singular and relies on context. Both are empirically adequate. Here my reaction is the opposite: this is pure notational variance. There is no deep philosophical indeterminacy, only a decision about how to regiment the target language. Verdict: not all empirically equivalent manuals reveal translation indeterminacy.

Third, imagine a ceremonial term, mava, used only in ritual contexts. Manual A renders mava as ancestor-spirit. Manual B renders it as social-role-occupied-by-elder-male-relative-in-the-lineage. Both fit every observed utterance and behavioral response. Nevertheless, I want to say that Manual A is wrong, not merely different, because it imports an ontological commitment to spirits that the behavioral data do not compel and that violates interpretive charity. Verdict: charity and ontological parsimony can exclude some empirically adequate manuals as mistranslations.

Fourth, consider a manual that systematically translates the natives' arithmetic assertions into a non-standard system—rendering two plus two as five—while making compensating adjustments elsewhere so that all behavioral predictions remain intact. I think such a manual is technically constructible, but I also think it is defective in a way that Manual P is not. I want to exclude it on rationality grounds: an interpretation that attributes massive arithmetical error is not a genuine translation, even if it saves the behavioral phenomena. Verdict: rationality constraints can rule out some empirically equivalent manuals.

My pattern, then, seems to be that I allow real indeterminacy for some empirical equivalents (R versus P), deny it for others (plural morphology), reject some equivalents on charity or ontological grounds (mava), and reject others on rationality grounds (the arithmetic case). Is there one coherent account of translation determination—perhaps a layered view that distinguishes ontological, notational, and rationality dimensions—that delivers all four verdicts? Or do I have to drop one? For instance, must I abandon my resistance to treating R and P as notational variants if I want to preserve the charity and rationality constraints?

Finally, apply your best principle to a discovery case I have not listed. Suppose we learn that native speakers consistently apply gavagai to living rabbits in every observed context, but consistently withhold it from detached rabbit parts—such as a severed ear—even when those parts are presented in circumstances otherwise identical to those involving whole rabbits. Would this behavioral asymmetry collapse the indeterminacy between R and P, or would it merely relocate the underdetermination to a different level of the manual?

Please state the principle precisely, map each clause to the verdict it explains, say honestly which of my four verdicts it cannot accommodate and must therefore be overturned, and give your prediction for the discovery case.
