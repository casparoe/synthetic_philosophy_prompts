I've adopted a strong version of the Frankfurt-inspired denial of the Principle of Alternative Possibilities: not merely that agents can be responsible without being able to do otherwise, but that the very notion of "modal access" to alternatives is a retrospective narrative construct with no causal-explanatory role in agency. On this view, responsibility tracks only "source-authorship" — whether an action issues from a coherent, non-manipulated practical identity — and this condition is fully compatible with determinism. The standard "flicker of freedom" objection is dismissed because even minimal unexercised alternatives (like the ability to form an intervention-intention) do not provide the robust modal access that PAP originally required. What would actually count against this position? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: which empirical findings in cognitive science (say, on choice blindness or post-hoc rationalization of decisions), which conceptual distinctions (between sourcehood and leeway conditions), and which legal or intuitive judgments (about duress, coercion, or addiction) would genuinely undermine it? Does the view risk being insulated by qualification into unfalsifiability — for instance, by redefining "manipulated" so broadly that any counterexample is absorbed? If it is well insulated, is that a problem for a metaphysical thesis of this kind, or an appropriate feature? Please distinguish what a committed defender of this source-only view should concede as damaging from what they could consistently dismiss, and say whether there is any observation that should move them to abandon the position entirely rather than refine it.
