I hold a range-sensitive population axiology in which welfare is weighted by a convex function of its distance from a neutral point defined not by felt indifference but by the absence of any preference-satisfying capacity; the weighting applies across lives rather than within them, so that a vast population of barely good lives is worse than a smaller population of flourishing ones, and the view aims to avoid both the repugnant conclusion and collapse into average utilitarianism. What would genuinely count against it? I want formal results showing that any convex cross-life weighting must violate separability or transitivity; conceptual arguments that such a neutral point is circular or arbitrarily fixed; and normative or empirical considerations about whether human valuation actually supports this shape of weighting. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—by adjusting the convexity parameter or relocating the neutral point to absorb any counterexample—and whether that insulation is a serious defect for an axiological theory. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, noting which ones a committed defender should concede as genuinely damaging and which they could reasonably dismiss.
