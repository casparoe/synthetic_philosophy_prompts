I hold a qualified Weberian position: the Protestant ethic did not cause capitalism in any straightforward linear fashion, but functioned as a selective causal filter—stabilizing rationalized capital accumulation among competing economic practices by conferring moral legitimacy on behaviors such as deferred gratification, systematic bookkeeping, and reinvestment that might otherwise have been abandoned as socially aberrant. On this account culture is not a prime mover but a retention mechanism; it does not push history forward so much as determine which economic trajectories survive institutional selection. What would count decisively against this view, and what would merely be awkward? I want the defeaters sorted by severity: for example, whether evidence of vigorous capitalism in non-Protestant contexts undermines the filter claim or merely demonstrates multiple causal paths; whether holding legal and market institutions constant eliminates the differential persistence of capitalistic practices in Protestant regions; and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability through appeals to functional equivalents that can always be postulated whenever Protestantism is absent. Please distinguish the objections that defenders of this filter interpretation would themselves concede as damaging from those they would dismiss as missing the point—particularly regarding whether culture can ever be said to cause anything if it only ever operates as a background selector rather than an initiating force. Finally, state how confident you are in this assessment and single out the point about which you are least sure.
