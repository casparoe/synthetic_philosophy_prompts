I serve on the Clinical Ethics and Policy Committee at a major academic medical center, and we face a concrete case that forces us to confront a philosophical distinction our institutional protocols have never addressed. A 34-year-old patient, eight months post-anoxic brain injury, meets standard behavioral criteria for a minimally conscious state; however, a recently piloted hierarchical representational decoding (HRD) protocol — developed within our neuroscience research division — detects robust second-order meta-representational activity in prefrontal-parietal circuits while showing no evidence of integrated first-order sensory binding. The family, interpreting this dissociation as proof of absent phenomenal consciousness, has requested withdrawal of artificial nutrition and hydration. The attending neurologist counters that these higher-order representational states constitute access consciousness sufficient for moral status, and urges continued life-sustaining care. Our current policy defines consciousness solely in behavioral terms and offers no guidance on cases where first-order and higher-order capacities come apart.

After a preliminary discussion with colleagues in neurology and philosophy of mind, I need a formal memo for Thursday’s meeting that recommends a specific course of action with a defined time frame — for example, whether to honor the request immediately, maintain care for a review period, or convene an independent assessment — and explains whether the HRD finding should alter our institutional evaluation. The memo should open with the bottom-line recommendation, then provide reasoning that treats both the family’s phenomenal-consciousness argument and the neurologist’s higher-order-access argument with equal philosophical seriousness, including an honest account of the strongest consideration favoring the side you do not recommend. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; the committee needs reasoning it can evaluate without external lookup. Keep the document to no more than 600 words — approximately one single-spaced page — and avoid inventing clinical data beyond what is described here.
