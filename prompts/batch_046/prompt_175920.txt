Write an essay on whether Hofstadter's idea of a distributed self can survive being split across multiple brains, not just mirrored in one. You know the basic picture: the "I" is less a soul and more a strange loop of self-reference that could in principle run on other neural hardware—like when someone knows you so well that parts of your pattern are literally running in their head. But push this further: imagine your self-model gets fractured across three people, each holding part of the loop but none having the full strange loop alone. Is "you" now spread across all three? Or does breaking the loop into pieces destroy the original "I" instead of extending it?

Weigh both sides seriously. The patternist view says it shouldnt matter—if the self is just information, then a cluster of brains running fragments of it is basically distributed computing, and your identity just becomes a network property. The opposing view says first-person ownership needs a single causal stream; split the loop and you've killed the subject, leaving only fragments that might form a new collective "I" but cant claim to be you.

Don't end with a shrug—reach an actual conclusion and defend it. (Saying "it depends on how you define self" doesnt count unless you actually pick a definition and stick with it.)

Write in American English please, and dont worry about being too formal—this is just a thought experiment.
