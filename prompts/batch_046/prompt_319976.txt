A speech-regulation board applies Mill’s harm principle: it permits restrictions on expression only when the expression causes direct harm to identifiable others, not merely offense, false belief, or self-injury. Its precedents are these. First, it permitted a forty-five-minute protest that blocked access to a government building, ruling that temporary physical obstruction is an inconvenience rather than the kind of harm that justifies interference. Second, it banned the distribution of a printed list naming specific private individuals, giving their home addresses, and urging readers to “make them pay,” ruling that this was direct facilitation of violence. Third, it permitted a university course that taught a widely discredited scientific theory, ruling that the propagation of false belief, without targeted action against particular persons, falls outside the principle. Fourth, it banned a commercial platform whose algorithm systematically pushed personalized defamatory content about private citizens into the feeds of those citizens’ employers, ruling that the platform’s structural design created a direct causal pathway to severe economic injury.

Now consider a new case: a closed, invitation-only professional network allows members to use a built-in synthetic-voice tool to generate audio replicating a colleague’s voice saying damaging falsehoods. The audio is not publicly indexed, but members share it within the network to influence hiring and promotion decisions about identifiable targets, who suffer tangible career harm; the platform provides the synthesis tool but no verification mechanism, and the targets are private individuals.

What should the board decide if it wants to remain consistent with itself? Identify the closest analog among the four precedents, and say whether the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome. If so, articulate at least two plausible rules that fit all four precedents equally well, state what each predicts about this synthetic-voice network, and specify one further case that would distinguish the rules. If you recommend a specific verdict, say what principle the board would be adopting with that choice and what principle it would be adopting with the opposite choice, and explain which analog makes your preferred reading more coherent.
