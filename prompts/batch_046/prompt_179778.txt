I'm preparing discussion materials for an advanced seminar on practical reason and metaethics, and a colleague observed that indirect normativity often sounds defensible in the abstract but collapses into something close to parody the moment you try to institutionalize it. With that tension in mind, please write a satirical employee onboarding manual—approximately 600 words, deadpan corporate-bureaucratic voice, formatted with numbered sections and bullet points—for a fictional consultancy called "The Institute for Idealized Deliberation and Indirect Governance." The firm sells normative compliance audits to government agencies and corporations, yet its charter contains an absolute prohibition on direct evaluation: it never assesses actions, outcomes, policies, or real-world behavior. Instead, it certifies whether a client's decision-making architecture would survive "idealized deliberation," defined in-house as a proprietary forty-seven-step reflective-equilibrium protocol that no actual human being has ever completed in full.

The manual should include the following elements, all rendered with straight-faced institutional seriousness. First, a "Direct Evaluation Prohibition" clause explaining why judging observed conduct is both epistemically unreliable and normatively illegitimate, since genuine norms apply only to the processes that generate behavior, never to behavior itself. Second, a "Second-Order Blame" policy describing how clients are to be held responsible not for what they actually did, but for failing—through the correct indirect pathway—to want to want to do otherwise; include a brief flowchart of how blame travels upward through successive orders of desire. Third, a refund and liability section addressing the case in which a client's certified "ideal self" diverges sharply from their documented real-world conduct, specifying whether the divergence voids the audit, triggers a mandatory re-idealization, or is treated as a non-actionable externality. Fourth, a sidebar announcing the firm's national holiday honoring the anonymous architects of the forty-seven-step protocol, with suggested ceremonial language for toasts.

The satire should exaggerate real philosophical commitments—the move from "what ought I to do?" to "what ought I to want to want?," the treatment of present suffering as merely an input to an idealized calculus rather than as a direct reason for action, and the risk that relentless idealization becomes a systematic excuse to disregard actual harm—without ever stating these as explicit objections or winking at the reader. A specialist should recognize that you understand why philosophers take indirect normativity seriously and what work it is meant to do; a non-specialist should be able to read the piece as absurd but coherent institutional jargon. Please do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or named references to specific living scholars; keep the manual entirely self-contained so that it stands on its own conceptual logic.
