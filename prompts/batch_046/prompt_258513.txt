I'm preparing remarks for a graduate seminar on historiography and have reconstructed an argument that worries me because its conclusion seems to swallow both Rankean objectivity and Carr's scepticism at once, leaving no stable ground for the discipline. Here is the chain as I have it written out.

(1) Every historical narrative selects a subset of past events and orders them into a coherent sequence. (2) That ordering always employs some explanatory framework—whether economic determinism, structural sociology, or what I have been calling configurational closure, the principle that any narrated causal chain must terminate in an event that is not itself explained within the narrative, thereby imposing an artificial boundary that the past as an unstructured totality does not possess. (3) So any history adds to the past a form of coherence, together with an explanatory closure, that the raw sequence of events lacks. (4) Now Ranke's ideal demands that we recover the past as it actually happened, without imposing our own frameworks or artificial termini. (5) Carr and Hayden White argue that such imposition is unavoidable: the historian's framework and configurational choices are constitutive of the account rather than mere packaging. (6) If (4) and (5) are both correct, then the historian is required to do what is impossible—to present a framework-free, closure-free account by means of an unavoidable framework and closure. (7) A normative standard that demands the impossible is not a genuine standard but an incoherent wish; therefore Ranke's ideal collapses. (8) But if we drop (4) and accept that all history is framework-imposed, then there is no principled distinction between a rigorously researched monograph and a well-constructed novel set in the past: both select, order, explain, and apply configurational closure according to an authorial design. (9) So either historical objectivity collapses into fiction, or Ranke's demand is incoherent. (10) Neither outcome seems right, yet each step looks defensible on its own.

I feel like the slide from (3) to (6) smuggles in something—perhaps an equivocation between explanatory framework and narrative form, or between unavoidable selection and unavoidable distortion—but I cannot locate the exact pressure point. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If I am forced to choose, I would rather preserve (4) in some qualified form rather than accept (8), but I want to understand precisely what that preservation costs and whether it forces me to abandon (5) entirely or only to restrict its scope.
