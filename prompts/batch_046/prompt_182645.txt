I'm preparing a closing exercise for a graduate seminar on non-Western epistemology, and we've spent the term working through the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda—the idea that reality is irreducibly complex (anekānta) and that any single predication captures only one aspect or naya. I want to end not with a summary but with a poem that enacts the logic structurally rather than merely naming it.

Write a poem of exactly seven stanzas, each corresponding in sequence to one mode of the saptabhaṅgī—the sevenfold conditional predication (syāt asti, syāt nāsti, syāt asti nāsti, syāt avaktavya, and the three combinations involving avaktavya). Open every stanza with the refrain "syāt" (perhaps / in one respect), so that the repetition itself performs the epistemic humility of the view. Take as your subject a deliberately liminal object: a synthetic protocell that metabolizes and replicates in a laboratory but carries no evolutionary lineage, no cellular ancestry, and no clear boundary between mechanism and life. Apply each of the seven qualifications to this object, letting the stanza's imagery shift with the mode—affirmation, negation, simultaneous affirmation and negation, inexpressibility, and the three mixed forms.

Let the form do philosophical work: use a repeating rhyme or rhythmic pattern that accumulates qualifications rather than resolving them, so that by the seventh stanza the reader feels every assertion made and every assertion withdrawn. The poem should not synthesize the seven modes into a single verdict or close with a moral about humility; it should end with the final "syāt" hanging open, leaving the protocell suspended in non-absolutism rather than skepticism. Be precise enough that someone familiar with Jain logic can map the stanzas onto the seven modes in order, but write verse good enough that a reader with no background in syllogistic logic still feels the weight of holding contradictory descriptions without collapse.
