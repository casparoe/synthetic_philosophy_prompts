A recent essay in a popular philosophy-of-science magazine summarizes the cosmological fine-tuning argument as follows: physicists have established that if the cosmological constant exceeded its observed value by roughly one part in 10^120, cosmic expansion would have outpaced gravitational collapse, preventing galaxy formation; if the strong nuclear force were approximately 2% stronger, stellar nucleosynthesis would have converted nearly all primordial hydrogen into helium, leaving no long-lived stars or liquid water. Across several independent parameters, the life-permitting ranges are said to be extraordinarily narrow—frequently cited as smaller than one part in 10^50—so that the actual values appear highly improbable unless explained. The essay presents the multiverse hypothesis as an attempt to dissolve this appearance by postulating an unobservable ensemble of universes spanning every possible parameter value, but dismisses it as violating Occam's razor by multiplying entities needlessly. From this, the author concludes that the constants appear deliberately calibrated for life, and that no non-theistic explanation survives scrutiny.

I am unsure how much of this characterization is accurate to the actual debate in physics and philosophy. Could you audit it point by point? Specifically, distinguish (i) claims that are broadly accepted in the relevant literature, (ii) standard popularizations that conceal important technical distinctions—such as the difference between fundamental constants and cosmological initial conditions, the problem of defining a probability measure over parameter spaces, and the distinction between conditions permitting any life versus conditions permitting observers—(iii) substantive misrepresentations, such as whether defenders of fine-tuning actually claim the values are "improbable" in a well-defined statistical sense, whether the multiverse is correctly characterized as an ad hoc escape rather than a consequence of some physical theories, and whether observer-selection effects are genuinely ignored rather than addressed by the argument's critics. Also clarify whether the conclusion of "deliberate calibration" follows from the premises as summarized, or whether the summary omits alternative explanations—such as a single-universe account that appeals to unknown physical law, or the objection that we should not be surprised to observe conditions compatible with our own existence—that a defender of the argument would have to engage with rather than dismiss.
