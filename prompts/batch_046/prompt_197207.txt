I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on vagueness and practical reason, and I'm preparing an upper-level seminar exam on comparative evaluation. I want one essay question that forces students beyond the standard sorites rehearsal on whether 'better than' is transitive. Specifically, I want the question to turn on a conceptual framework I'll call the cumulative epistemic margin view: the idea that each adjacent pair in a spectrum of small improvements carries an independent, non-zero margin of comparative error, and that transitivity fails not because value relations are metaphysically non-transitive, but because error margins compound across long chains, rendering distant comparative judgments rationally unreliable rather than false. The exam should ask students to assess whether this framework genuinely saves metaphysical transitivity while justifying its rejection in practical reasoning, and whether it collapses into a standard epistemicist account of vagueness. It should also require them to apply this to a concrete welfare case—comparing a population that receives imperceptible quality-of-life gains every generation for ten thousand generations against one that receives a single large improvement now—and to determine whether the intermediate non-transitivity blocks the overall evaluation.

Please provide the following, in this order:

1. The Exam Question (approximately 350-450 words): Frame it as a 90-minute essay for an upper-level philosophy seminar. Structure it into three parts: (a) reconstruct the spectrum argument against the transitivity of 'better than'; (b) explicate and critically assess the cumulative epistemic margin view, including whether it distinguishes metaphysical from rational failure of transitivity and whether it avoids collapsing into epistemicism; (c) apply the analysis to the intergenerational welfare case, stating whether the distant comparison is rationally blocked and why. Do not ask students to cite specific authors or texts; the evaluation should turn on conceptual precision alone.

2. Model Answer (approximately 900-1100 words): Demonstrate the moves a top answer must make. These include: a precise reconstruction of the spectrum argument with the transitivity premise explicitly identified; a clear distinction between metaphysical and epistemic or rational failures of transitivity; an explanation of how error margins compound rather than cancel; an evaluation of the epistemicism-collapse objection that addresses whether the view implies sharp cut-offs; a resolution of the welfare case that either endorses or denies the distant comparison with explicit reasoning about cumulative margins; and a brief conclusion on whether the framework succeeds.

3. Marking Scheme (approximately 400-500 words): Organize it around five must-hit points, totaling 25 marks, with specific allocations. For each point, state: the conceptual move required; the marks available; and a one-line note on the typical mistake that should cost marks (for example, conflating metaphysical non-transitivity with epistemic unreliability, treating error margins as additive in the wrong direction, or resolving the welfare case by mere intuition without reference to the compounding mechanism). End with a brief note on what separates an A-range answer (22-25) from a B-range answer (18-21).
