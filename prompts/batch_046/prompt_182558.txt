I hold the view that pain asymbolia—cases where nociceptive processing remains intact but negative affect is selectively abolished, typically linked to anterior cingulate disruption—shows that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to its sensory content but belongs to a separable evaluative overlay. From this I draw a stronger claim: that representationalist theories grounding pain's badness in the representation of tissue damage fail, because asymbolic patients represent damage (they discriminate location and intensity, avoid further injury) yet do not suffer, which suggests suffering is an added interoceptive prediction-error signal rather than part of the representational content itself. Consequently, I think moral arguments that invoke the intrinsic badness of pain in animals or medical ethics must be rebuilt around whether an organism runs this predictive-error overlay, not whether it registers damage.
