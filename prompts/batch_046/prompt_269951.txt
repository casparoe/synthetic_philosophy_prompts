I am preparing for a seminar in comparative constitutional law on church-state regimes, and I want to practice defending a precise position against a sharp interlocutor. Please take the role of an unnamed but rigorous defender of the German Kirchensteuer arrangement: you hold that state collection of church taxes is permissible only when exit is genuinely costless in legal terms, and you will concede administrative flaws or denominational inequities but you will never concede that the system violates liberal neutrality. Speak in the first person, keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, remain in character until I write 'stop', and treat this as a live debate rather than a lecture. Here is my opening challenge: I grant that registered Kirchenmitgliedschaft is technically voluntary, yet the Kirchensteuer is not a generic charitable donation but a fiscal obligation tied to a specific legal status that also governs eligibility for denominational burial, institutional pastoral care, and other civil recognitions of confessional membership. If opting out therefore means forfeiting not merely tax liability but a recognized legal identity within a religious community, then the exit is structurally punitive rather than neutral. How is this different in kind from the old Prussian Staatskirche, except that the state now embeds the coercion in the tax code rather than exercising it through direct ecclesiastical appointment?
