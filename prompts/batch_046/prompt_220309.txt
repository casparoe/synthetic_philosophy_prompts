A colleague is defending origin essentialism about gametes and offers the following reductio against psychological-continuity accounts of personal identity. (1) If psychological continuity determines identity, then gradual replacement of biological substrate does not break it, provided mental connections are preserved. (2) So imagine that every cell of the zygote that developed into you is replaced, one at a time, by cells from a second zygote formed from different sperm and egg, with the replacement program precisely mirroring normal development; the adult that results has your memories and character. (3) By (1), that adult is you; therefore you could have originated from that second pair of gametes. (4) Apply the same replacement reasoning recursively: the second zygote could have been replaced by a third, and so on, yielding an infinite chain of possible origins. (5) Modal accessibility is transitive across such chains: if origin O1 can lead to O2, and O2 to O3, then O1 can directly reach O3. (6) Among the origins reachable in this chain is a synthetic embryo engineered from non-human primate gametes but programmed to produce your exact psychology. (7) Hence, on the psychological view, you could have been a chimpanzee with human consciousness. (8) But essential humanity excludes that possibility. (9) So psychological continuity is false, and the particular sperm and egg that formed your actual zygote are essential to you.

He admits that (5) does most of the work, yet insists it is just standard possible-worlds logic. I think the argument slips somewhere between (3) and (6), but I cannot locate the exact equivocation or modal slide. Which step should be rejected? Explain why it seemed plausible, and then spell out what each of the main positions—psychological-continuity theory, origin essentialism about the zygote, and a hybrid biological-psychological view—must concede if they reject that step. I would prefer to keep (1) and reject (8), but I want to know what that costs.
