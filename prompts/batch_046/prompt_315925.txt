I was arguing until late last night with a friend — a political theorist who keeps insisting that I am smuggling modern institutionalism into ancient history — about the framing I intend to defend in a seminar paper I am presenting next week. My thesis is that the canonical democratic failures associated with Mytilene, the Sicilian Expedition, and the trial of Socrates should not be interpreted primarily as episodes of demagogic irrationality or popular cruelty, but as recurrent symptoms of what I am calling deliberative discontinuity: the absence in Athenian institutional practice of mechanisms to preserve, formalize, and integrate strategic objections across separate assemblies and votes. Because each crisis was deliberated in relative isolation from the strategic objections raised in prior ones, the democracy repeatedly reproduced structurally similar errors — overconfidence in present rhetoric, suppression of minority strategic assessments, and an inability to update on failure — despite possessing otherwise sophisticated norms of public reasoning. I am presenting this to a seminar audience composed mainly of classicists who know Thucydides and Xenophon intimately, political theorists skeptical of anachronistic vocabulary applied to the polis, and at least one historian of ancient political culture who will argue that systematizing these events erases their irreducible particularity.
