As chair of the Tri-County Civic Allocation Board, I must circulate a recommendation memo before Thursday's binding vote. We hold one legally indivisible legacy disbursement of approximately $4.2 million, and our evaluation protocol has tied two fully vetted proposals on every measurable axis—precisely because they embody goods our charter now treats as incommensurable.

Proposal A, the Shoreline Oral Archive Initiative, is a seven-year, community-embedded program to document and facilitate intergenerational transmission of an unwritten coastal historiography tied to a specific intertidal ecology. Climate projections indicate that landscape will be permanently submerged within fifteen years; the practice is non-replicable, and once dispersed, no substitute archive can recover the particular normative and ecological reasoning embedded in it.

Proposal B, the Regional Crisis Stabilization Network, is an immediately deployable, evidence-based mental-health first-responder program projected to reduce acute psychiatric morbidity by roughly thirty percent over five years across the same region, reaching an estimated twelve thousand people.

Our board adopted the Pluralist Evaluation Protocol last year. It explicitly rejects reducing all public goods to a single metric—whether economic return, preference satisfaction, or quality-adjusted life years—and requires that when values are genuinely incommensurable the board still render a concrete, time-bound recommendation, including an honest account of what is irretrievably lost on the rejected side. Several members argue that this case demonstrates pluralism collapses into paralysis, and that because suffering is universal while cultural particularity is contingent, Proposal B must prevail without further qualification. Others contend that selecting B implicitly establishes a lexical ordering in which welfare goods always dominate heritage goods, thereby violating the protocol's commitment to non-substitutability. A minority proposes deadlocking and returning the funds, though the donor agreement forbids that.

Please draft the formal memo I will circulate. It should recommend a specific course of action—approve Proposal A, approve Proposal B, or approve one subject to defined conditions and a clear timeline—together with reasoning that engages the strongest argument for the rejected proposal rather than treating it as a sentimental residual. In particular, address whether rendering any binary choice in this framework betrays value pluralism itself, or whether refusing to choose is its own morally consequential decision with a distinct cost. The memo should be self-contained and must not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
