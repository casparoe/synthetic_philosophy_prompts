I have been reflecting on Grice's distinction between speaker meaning and sentence meaning, and I find myself drawn to what seems like a strict implication of the Cooperative Principle. If implicature is recovered only when a hearer assumes that the speaker is trying to make his contribution one that observes the maxims—quantity, quality, relation, and manner—then it appears that genuine implicature requires an underlying cooperative intention on the speaker's part. Where that intention is absent—most clearly in adversarial legal cross-examination, in whistleblower communications designed to maintain plausible deniability, or in strategic deception—there should be no Gricean mechanism to generate speaker meaning beyond the literal content. Any hidden message in such cases would have to be a matter of conventional code or mere pragmatic inference, not true implicature. But this seems wrong: we do interpret adversarial utterances and deliberately ambiguous warnings as conveying precise, intended meanings that are not encoded in the words themselves. So I want a committed verdict: does Grice's framework genuinely restrict speaker meaning to cooperative contexts, and if not, where exactly does it locate the rational basis for recovering implicature when the speaker's interests are diametrically opposed to the hearer's? If the answer is that Grice must be supplemented, specify the minimal addition—without appealing to relevance theory or conventionalism—that saves adversarial speaker meaning within a broadly Gricean architecture.
