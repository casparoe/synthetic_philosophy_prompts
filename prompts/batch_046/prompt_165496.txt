I'm preparing the core argument for a seminar paper on moral epistemology and cross-cultural psychology, and I want a rigorous consistency check before I present it. I hold the following commitments: (a) moral realism of a broadly non-naturalist kind—there are mind-independent moral facts, though they are not reducible to natural properties; (b) that the WEIRD-psychology literature is essentially correct in showing that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic populations are genuine outliers in moral cognition, especially in weighting harm and fairness far more heavily than loyalty, authority, and sanctity; (c) that evolutionary and cultural-transmission explanations for these binding foundations support a partial debunking argument—our intuitive judgments are shaped by adaptive pressures and social transmission rather than functioning as direct perceptions of moral reality; (d) that reflective equilibrium is nevertheless the correct method in ethics, in which we begin with considered judgments and mutually revise particular verdicts and general principles; (e) that because of (b), a responsible practitioner of (d) must treat WEIRD moral psychology as a local variant rather than the universal default, actively giving weight to non-Western frameworks—for example, treating obligations of communal loyalty that conflict with individual harm-avoidance as genuine moral inputs rather than as cognitive biases to be filtered out; and (f) that the aim of this process is to approximate objective moral truth, not merely to achieve internal coherence or to broker an intercultural compromise.

I also tend to think that cross-cultural moral disagreement is sometimes not a disagreement about competing answers to the same question, but rather a disagreement about which social phenomena even fall within the moral domain in the first place—analogous to how different linguistic communities parse color space with different category boundaries—so that importing a non-WEIRD framework into equilibrium is not just adding a new data point but altering the taxonomy of moral issues under consideration.

My concern is that several of these commitments pull in opposite directions, but I am unsure which conflicts are genuine and which only appear so. Which pairs are actually incompatible—particularly where the standard motivations for holding each view conflict with one another—and which tensions dissolve once the commitments are properly understood? If I must make a revision, what is the least costly adjustment, and what exactly would it force me to abandon? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally damaging, and treat the audit as an examination of my position rather than a general lecture on metaethics.
