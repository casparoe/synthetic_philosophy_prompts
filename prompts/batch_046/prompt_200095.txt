I'm part of a long-running rationalist-leaning reading circle that is planning a six-month deep-dive into decision-theoretic paradoxes, and we are debating whether to allocate our sessions to superrationality—specifically Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery—or to treat them as well-explored curiosities and pivot to adjacent formal questions. Rather than a survey, I want a defended agenda: among the open conceptual issues these experiments genuinely raise—whether the sameness-of-reasoning condition can be reconstructed without circular reference to shared priors, how superrational strategies scale (or fracture) when moving from symmetric two-agent games to N-player populations with heterogeneous cognitive architectures, whether evidential conditioning survives intact once we allow correlated reasoning without explicit coordination, and whether any of this bears upstream on mechanism design for distributed multi-agent systems—which two or three deserve sustained philosophical effort, and which should be deprioritised?

My own suspicion is that the N-agent heterogeneous-cognition extension is the crux: if superrationality requires not merely logical identity but also uniform epistemic access, then it may collapse into a trivial special case of correlated equilibrium, and the normative punch of Hofstadter's puzzles evaporates. Conversely, if there is a non-circular formalisation of the sameness condition—perhaps using logical omniscience constraints or a particular interpretation of logical probability—then the Platonia dilemma might yield genuine constraints on belief revision that are independent of standard Bayesian updating. I am less convinced that applied questions about AI coordination are ready for philosophical input here, but I could be wrong.

Please rank these four directions—the formal reconstruction of reasoning-sameness, the heterogeneous N-agent scaling problem, the interaction with evidential versus causal decision theory, and the applied relevance to distributed coordination—by which offers the best ratio of conceptual payoff to tractability for a group without specialist game-theoretic machinery. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria: dependency on other open problems, whether resolving it would change our view of rational agency in symmetric situations, and what kind of argument or counter-example over the coming year would cause you to reorder them. I am writing this to structure our syllabus, not to generate a bibliography, so I care about the reasoning, not citation counts.
