I have spent several months trying to understand whether culture can actually cause economic change, specifically through the lens of Weber's thesis that Protestant ethics shaped modern capitalism. What strikes me is how consistently the conversation collapses into the same pattern: defenders cite the correlation between Calvinist communities and early industrial activity, critics respond that those same communities had pre-existing access to trade networks, and both sides then treat the dispute as if it were purely about which correlation is stronger. I have begun to suspect that the impasse is not empirical but structural — that both sides are forced to place culture on one side of a cause-and-effect line, when culture might instead work like a kind of stabilization filter that decides which economic shifts can persist without triggering social rejection. Under this view, the question is not whether values caused capitalism or capitalism caused values, but whether a particular culture acts as a gatekeeper that allows certain material changes to become durable. Is this a real alternative, or does it merely rename one of the existing positions? More broadly, is the long stall in this debate a sign that it is verbal — that "cause" means something different to each side — or that the two camps are asking different questions, one about historical origins and one about ongoing legitimacy? And if the disagreement is indeed about these different roles culture can play, what kind of progress would actually resolve it: a historical case study, a conceptual distinction, or an admission that the original framing was ill-posed?
