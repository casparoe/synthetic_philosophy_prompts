As a novelist drafting a philosophical dialogue, I listened to two scholars argue over whether al-Ghazālī's occasionalism can sustain any meaningful notion of internal causation, using fiction as an analogy. One said occasionalism resembles authorial causation in a novel: every event is ultimately caused by God, the author, yet within the story we correctly speak of characters causing outcomes through their choices, because God—like a consistent author—maintains regular patterns. The other replied that this misses the metaphysical point: occasionalism denies that fire contains any power to burn cotton, so there is no modal link for God to maintain; in fiction, by contrast, the author establishes and respects internal rules, so character actions genuinely produce effects within the narrative logic. The first countered that God's customary action is precisely such an established internal rule, giving occasionalist worlds the same derivative but real causal structure found in fiction. They asked me to referee: does occasionalism hollow out causation entirely, or does it preserve a coherent internal narrative of causes, and does my authorial analogy illuminate or distort al-Ghazālī's view? Please adjudicate each move—who is right about what, and what do both neglect? Say also what consideration would reverse your verdict and where your reasoning is weakest.
