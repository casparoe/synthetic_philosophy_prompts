In my philosophy of science seminar, the instructor has developed a clear but unstated pattern in judging whether a scientific thought experiment produces genuine knowledge or only reveals conceptual tension. Her rulings to date are these: Galileo's falling-bodies argument in a vacuum yields knowledge, because idealizing away air resistance reveals an actual law; Einstein's elevator equivalence principle yields knowledge, since it structurally isolates gravitation from inertia; Schrödinger's cat does not yield knowledge, being a reductio that highlights interpretive conflict without advancing quantum theory; Maxwell's demon does yield knowledge, despite violating the second law, because it exposed the information-theoretic foundation of entropy; Newton's rotating-bucket argument for absolute space does not, since it relies on metaphysical premises that empirical mechanics never resolves; and the twin-paradox derivation in special relativity does yield knowledge, as it produces testable predictions about clock desynchronization.

In this variant of the Boltzmann-brain problem, an eternally expanding de Sitter universe should, over infinite time, generate vastly more minimal conscious configurations—each a fleeting neural state that registers only a false, randomly generated star-field—than it generates evolved biological observers, and proponents use this not to attack cosmology generally but specifically to argue that any multiverse model lacking an evolutionary cutoff for observers is self-undermining.
