I'm in a philosophy-of-mathematics seminar and we've been tracking one colleague's implicit stance on choice principles. So far her verdicts line up like this: she accepts countable choice without reservation; she rejects the well-ordering theorem for the real numbers; she treats the Hahn-Banach theorem for normed spaces as perfectly legitimate and does not regard its proof as invoking anything objectionable; and she rejects the Banach-Tarski decomposition, holding that the principle licensing the breaking of a ball into finitely many pieces and its reassembly should be abandoned. I want to see whether she's running a consistent rule or just reacting case by case.

Next week she is going to consider free ultrafilters on ω. She knows that one can use them to extend counting measure to a finitely additive measure on all subsets of the integers, breaking σ-additivity, and that they underpin Banach limits extending the ordinary limit to every bounded sequence. She also knows they do not by themselves generate a geometric paradox like Banach-Tarski, though they sit close in logical strength to the principle behind Hahn-Banach. Do you think she'll allow the ultrafilter? I'm less interested in the bare yes or no than in the rule she's actually running—perhaps a strength-based cutoff near the Boolean Prime Ideal Theorem, or perhaps a pathology-avoidance rule that tolerates standard analytic tools but rejects anything that destroys measurability or additivity—plus what each rule predicts about this case. If the two rules diverge here, what's the cleanest further case to separate them: for instance, whether she accepts that every commutative ring with unity has a maximal ideal (pure BPI, with no geometric pathology) versus whether she accepts selecting representatives from the orbits of rational translations on the circle (yielding a non-measurable set, but using only choice over countable sets)?
