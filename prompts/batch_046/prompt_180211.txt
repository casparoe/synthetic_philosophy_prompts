Read Ursula Le Guin's story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas through the lens of the boundary problem — who belongs to the demos? I know people usually read it as utilitarianism with a twist, but I'm interested in the political membership angle. The citizens live in a perfect society that only works because one child is locked in suffering; that kid is physically inside the walls but totally outside the decision-making community. So is this a case for the all-affected interests view, where anyone affacted by collective choices should be part of the demos? Or for all-subjected persons, where only those actually under the authority of the collective count — which might actually exclude the child if they're not treated as a subject? And then there's the people who leave: are they rejecting the boundary line itself, or just this particular line?

Here's the niche part I want you to think about: what if we read the child not as an excluded citizen but as a stakeholder in a stakeholder-democracy model, where the suffering party doesn't need full membership but has a veto? Does Le Guin's story support that middle option, or does it show that any model that draws a line — even a stakeholder line — is just rationalizing the same violence? I want a reading that takes the story's details seriously — the locked room, the citizens who stay and rationalize, the ones who just leave without a plan — and also takes the democratic boundary literature seriously. Does the story actually illuminate the debate, or does it show the debate is missing something? Don't worry about citations, just argue from the plot and the concepts.
