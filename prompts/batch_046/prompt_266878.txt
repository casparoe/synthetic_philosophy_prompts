Please argue that anekāntavāda—the Jain doctrine of many-sidedness—should be reconstructed as an ontological thesis about standpoint-indexed predication rather than as an epistemic doctrine of partial knowledge. On this view, saying that a substance both possesses and lacks a given property is not a confession of cognitive limitation but a claim that properties are relationally constituted through specific naya, or standpoints of reference. Defend this interpretation by explaining how it resolves the tension between the Jaina commitment to absolute ahiṃsā and the unavoidable presence of harm in practical life: specifically, argue that moral predicates such as "violent" and "non-violent" apply relative to distinct reference frames rather than as contradictory univocal descriptions of the same act. In doing so, address the role of the seven-fold predication schema (saptabhaṅgī) not as a taxonomy of epistemic uncertainty but as a grammar of partial reference frames, and respond to the objection that relocating contradiction from logic into semantics offers no genuine philosophical advance.
