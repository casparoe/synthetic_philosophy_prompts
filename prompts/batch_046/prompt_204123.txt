I am convening a 90-minute interdisciplinary workshop for about eighteen postgraduate students—drawn equally from political philosophy and cultural sociology—on the moral psychology of achievement and social resentment. Rather than delivering a lecture on meritocratic celebration versus egalitarian restraint, I want a structured, three-phase simulation that makes the participants physically experience the tension between the American Dream and what Scandinavian and Australasian cultures call Jantelagen and the tall poppy syndrome, before either framework is named. I have been working with an informal framing I call “visibility economics”: the idea that public recognition functions not as an expandable symbolic reward but as a positional good with negative externalities for group cohesion, so that elevating one member necessarily diminishes others. I do not wish to present this theory up front; instead, the rules and pay-offs should generate the emotional dynamics organically.

Please design a runnable activity comprising, first, a competitive production phase of roughly twenty minutes in which groups of four generate a tangible proposal for a local public-good intervention, with individual contributions tracked via a hidden “distinction score” assigned by a silent auditor; second, a redistribution and recognition phase of about twenty-five minutes in which the whole workshop must allocate a fixed pool of “social capital” tokens between amplifying the highest-scoring proposal and distributing them evenly, with a concealed variance calculator that automatically triggers a “solidarity tax” if any single contributor receives more than thirty per cent of the total—thereby imposing a collective cost for visible excellence; and third, a forced-choice deliberation of about fifteen minutes in which participants must defend either amplification or the tax mechanism, without knowing which cultural norm they are representing, guided by a mediator who may invoke a hidden “normative charter” but risks collapsing into advocacy. Include precise rules, role descriptions (the auditor, the mediator, the token-keepers), the materials required (proposal templates, token sets, the concealed calculator), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a pay-off scheme using only symbolic stakes rather than money, arranged so that participants feel the sting of exceptional work being flattened and, separately, the discomfort of elevating one peer above the rest before any concept is introduced.

In the debrief, map each moment of the simulation onto the philosophical contrast: show where the American Dream’s assumption that recognition is expandable and deserved conflicts with Jantelagen’s premise that it is zero-sum and socially corrosive, and with the tall poppy syndrome’s specific resentment of visible success. Be explicit about which feature of these cultural phenomena the activity cannot reproduce—such as the historical sedimentation of norms in institutional practice, or the class-inflected dynamics particular to cutting down successful figures in liberal market societies—and tell me exactly what I must supply verbally to fill that gap. Also predict what will go wrong on the first run: whether groups will attempt to game the hidden variance threshold, whether the mediator role will destabilise into partisan advocacy, and how to correct these failures without breaking the simulation’s integrity or revealing the framing prematurely. Please write your response in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the design must be entirely self-contained for use without web access.
