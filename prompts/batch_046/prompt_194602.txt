I spent much of last evening arguing with a close friend about whether Humean regularity theories or anti-Humean governing accounts of natural law can be made consistent with our intuitions about engineered and derived regularities, and she offered a series of verdicts that I now want to test for coherence. She accepts that F = ma and the inverse-square law of gravitation are genuine laws; she accepts that the emerald-green regularity is law-governed, albeit derivatively; she denies that the universal absence of gold spheres larger than one mile is a law, treating it as an accidental boundary condition; she denies that a perfectly exceptionless pattern observed in a specific proximal-policy-optimization reinforcement-learning agent—where, across every training run with this architecture and seed range, the cumulative reward asymptotically approaches π multiplied by the step count divided by one thousand, irrespective of environment—is a law, despite its exceptionless character within that system; and she accepts that the elliptical orbits of our solar-system planets are law-governed, though only in a derivative, non-fundamental sense.

The case I want you to adjudicate is this: consider a crystal lattice that can form naturally only under extreme pressure, such that in every actual and nearby possible instance, a particular quantum-spin configuration occurs with a frequency that equals the constant e divided by the total number of nodes in the lattice, not because of any fundamental symmetry but because of an intricate, non-obvious interaction between boundary conditions and local electromagnetic fluctuations. Would my friend call this a law? I want you to treat her five verdicts as data and extract at least two competing principles—say, one privileging modal robustness across natural kinds, one privileging membership in the globally best axiomatic system regardless of domain, and one privileging direct governing relations among fundamental properties—that could jointly accommodate her judgments; for each principle, state its prediction about the lattice case; then offer your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reasoning that favors it; and finally specify the single further hypothetical case that would most sharply distinguish these principles from one another.
