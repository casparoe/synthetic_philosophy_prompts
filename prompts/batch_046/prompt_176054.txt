I am presenting a workshop paper titled "Institutional Echo and the Unresolvability of Macrohistorical Causation" at a joint session of economic historians, human geographers, and philosophers of social science, and I want to be ready for the question period rather than surprised by it. My core claim is that the familiar debate over whether institutions, geography, or culture best explains cross-national wealth is not merely empirically contested but methodologically stalled, because formal institutional metrics fail to capture what I call institutional echo—the way pre-existing informal coordination norms absorb, redirect, and sometimes nullify the functional effects of imported formal rules, rendering cross-national institutional comparisons incommensurable with geographic determinism within any stable counterfactual framework. The audience divides into three skeptical camps: economic historians committed to a colonial-origins framework who treat institutional quality as instrumentally identifiable and exogenous after initial settlement conditions; human geographers who regard climate, disease burden, and crop suitability as the deep causes and who will hear my concept as culture or social capital renamed to rescue institutionalism; and philosophers of social science concerned with overdetermination in large-N historical studies, who will worry that I have made my thesis unfalsifiable by expanding the domain of institutions to include precisely the mechanisms that make them appear to fail. Please prepare me with a speaker’s prep sheet: list the objections in the order they are most likely to arise, phrase each roughly as that camp would phrase it, and for each tell me what I should concede, what I should firmly hold, and where my reply is weakest so that I defend that point rather than hope they miss it. I especially need you to surface the objection I am most hoping nobody asks—namely, that if institutional effects are always mediated by unobservable normative substrates, then development economics has no legitimate basis for institutional-reform interventions—and suggest how to answer it within the brief reply window without overstating my negative thesis or collapsing into quietism. Present this as a prep sheet ordered by likelihood and damage, not as a full essay defending my position, and do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since I will supply all references myself.
