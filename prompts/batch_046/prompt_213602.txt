I have recently joined a committee revising our municipality's emergency housing allocation formula, and I need to pressure-test my normative framework before I present it to colleagues who keep raising leveling-down objections. Here is my current package: (a) I hold a prioritarian view—the moral value of a housing benefit increases continuously with the recipient's distance below a local, actual-population capability median; (b) I reject the leveling-down objection—since I deny that equality has any intrinsic value, reducing welfare among the well-off without helping anyone worse off is not good, and prioritarianism does not entail it; (c) I accept responsibility-sensitive weighting—those who fall below the median through voluntary, foreseeable risk should receive a discounted prioritarian weight, though not reduced to zero; (d) I treat equality of capability outcomes as purely instrumental; (e) I want the allocation rule to direct resources to the worst off, but not to cap resources for those near or above the median if doing so yields no housing gains for those below. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear so, and what is the least costly revision? In particular: does using an actual-population median create instability under crisis conditions that collapse the reference point itself? Does combining continuous cardinal weights with a categorical responsibility factor require a common metric that my view does not supply? And is my denial of equality's intrinsic value sufficient to ground my rejection of leveling-down, or does it smuggle in an unstated premise about the structure of well-being? Rank the tensions by severity, identify the cheapest fix for each, and note any apparent conflict that dissolves on inspection.
