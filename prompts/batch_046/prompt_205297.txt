As a retired field ecologist who has only recently begun working through philosophy of biology in earnest, I was struck by a disagreement reconstructed for me at a seminar between two specialists—call them Dr. Voss and Dr. Chen—concerning what philosophers call the species problem. Rather than rehearsing the familiar textbook cases, they discussed a hypothetical but biologically realistic scenario they termed a “broken-ring archipelago”: a chain of island populations in which intermediate populations have gone extinct after a localized volcanic event, leaving only the two terminal populations. Genetic archives (fossil and subfossil DNA) show that gene flow was once continuous through the now-vanished intermediates; the surviving terminals are morphologically distinct and reproductively isolated in controlled sympatry experiments, yet each retains the genetic architecture for the intermediate phenotypes. Voss argued that these are clearly one species—one evolutionary lineage—because the reproductive isolation is an artifact of recent geographic disruption, not an intrinsic biological boundary. Chen countered that once the intermediates are gone, “potential interbreeding” becomes a counterfactual fiction, and treating the terminals as conspecific ignores the ecological and evolutionary processes now operating independently on each island. Their exchange proceeded through four moves:

(1) Voss claimed that species membership ought to track historical continuity of gene flow, not present reproductive barriers, since evolutionary lineages are defined by their causal-historical connections rather than by current interaction potential.

(2) Chen replied that this makes membership depend on accidental historical contingencies—had the intermediate islands never existed, the same two terminal populations would count as different species—so Voss’s criterion fails to capture any stable biological reality.

(3) Voss responded that Chen’s own process-based criterion suffers an inverted contingency problem: ecological roles can shift overnight after a disturbance, so if species are defined by current ecological function, taxonomic stability is impossible.

(4) Chen concluded that this reveals not a flaw but a feature—species are not natural kinds with essential properties but “explanatory stations” that scientists stabilize for particular purposes, and recognizing this pluralism is the only intellectually honest resolution of the species problem.

Please adjudicate this disagreement move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then state who is more right overall and why. I am asking for a committed verdict—do not merely lay out options, but take a definite position on each move and on the dispute as a whole, giving your reasons plainly. Also indicate whether either party misses something important that someone with a scientific background but new to philosophy ought especially to notice.
