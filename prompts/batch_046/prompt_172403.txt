In Tolstoy's What Is Art? he proposes that art works like an infection: the artist experiences a feeling, and the work's purpose is to transmit that feeling to others so that they experience it too. The strength of the infection is said to be the sole measure of artistic excellence. Today this analogy is often revived in discussions of digital culture, where "viral" content is treated as the highest form of art precisely because it achieves mass emotional contagion, and recommendation algorithms are defended as optimizing for Tolstoyan infection. I want you to audit the infection analogy rigorously. First, lay out an explicit correspondence table: what in the artistic process plays the role of the pathogen, the host, the vector, the immune response, and the epidemic? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for the normative conclusion that art should be judged by how successfully and widely it infects, and separate these from disanalogies that are merely decorative. In particular, address whether infection's typical involuntariness, its lack of interpretive mediation, and its usual harmfulness map onto art in ways that undermine using contagiousness as an aesthetic criterion, and whether concepts like aesthetic distance or interpretive resistance function as a kind of immune system that good art actually requires rather than suppresses. Third, assess whether the modern application—using the analogy to justify algorithmic amplification of emotionally contagious media—survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative load all along. Finally, if the infection model fails as a normative framework, suggest a better analogy for how art relates to its audience—one that preserves the idea of transmission without treating the audience as passive tissue—and say whether that replacement supports or undermines the algorithmic-viral defense.
