I am preparing a unit on metaphysics for advanced undergraduates, and I want to use Franz Kafka's The Trial not as a mere illustration but as a genuine interlocutor for the problem of universals. The novel presents Joseph K. as accused of an unspecified crime, with guilt appearing to attach to him through the actions of various bureaucrats and courtrooms rather than through any particular deed he can identify. I would like you to read the novel through the lens of this debate, distinguishing carefully among three accounts of the property "being guilty" as it operates in the narrative.

First, consider the Aristotelian reading: guilt as an immanent universal that inheres in K. only through particular instantiations—specific accusations, judgments, and institutional acts directed at him. Second, consider the Platonic reading: guilt as a transcendent form that the Court and its officials imperfectly participate in, so that K.'s particular situation is merely an approximation of an abstract, pre-existing Law. Third, consider a less conventional option—that guilt functions here as a structural or relational universal, existing not in K. as a substance nor in a separate realm, but only in the network of relations among K., the various courts, and the unseen authority that binds them together.

Please address the following in your response. For each of the three readings, identify at least one scene or narrative pattern—the initial arrest, the lawyer's monologue about the court's inaccessibility, the cathedral encounter with the priest, the execution—that either supports or resists that interpretation. Be precise about what feature of the scene does the metaphysical work: is it the repetition of identical procedures across different locations, the inability to locate the source of authority, or the way guilt seems transferable between institutions?

Then, adjudicate: which account best captures the ontology of guilt in the novel, and where does each account fail? In particular, does the novel ultimately undermine realist positions altogether, perhaps suggesting that guilt is nothing more than a nominal label applied to a consistent pattern of bureaucratic behavior? If so, does Kafka's narrative add anything to the philosophical debate about universals that a purely conceptual argument—say, the standard objections to Platonic forms or to Aristotelian inherence—cannot establish on its own?

Please treat the strongest version of each position fairly, and conclude by saying what, if anything, the work teaches us about the limits of applying metaphysical categories to institutional and moral experience.
