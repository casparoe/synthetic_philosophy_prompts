My study group cooked up this argument about punishment using counterfactual welfare accounting (CWA) and now I think it proves retributivism either never applies or demands totally wild sentences, but I can't spot the leak. Here are the steps:

(1) Retributivism says punishment is justified only if it gives the offender exactly the suffering they deserve, where desert tracks moral gravity of the wrong.
(2) Moral gravity is fixed by victim harm, measured counterfactually: it's the drop in the victim's wellbeing relative to the world where the offender never acted.
(3) So the deserved quantum of suffering is just that counterfactual welfare gap, call it G.
(4) The state inflicts suffering S via punishment, and for proportionality S should equal G.
(5) But S is also counterfactual: it's the offender's wellbeing in the punished-world minus their wellbeing in the world where they go free.
(6) For S to equal G, the offender's baseline wellbeing (without punishment) must match the victim's baseline wellbeing (without crime), otherwise the same absolute suffering creates different welfare drops.
(7) In reality offenders and victims have different baselines—health, wealth, social support—so S=G is either impossible or requires tuning the sentence untill the welfare drop matches, which usually means way harsher sentences for wealthy offenders and lighter ones for poor ones.
(8) But retributivists clearly don't think sentencing should depend on wether the offender was already rich or sick.
(9) So either retributivism can't be applied consistently, or it secretly requires massive sentencing disparities based on pre-existing welfare.
(10) Therefore retributivism must be false, or at least it can't use counterfactual victim harm as its metric.

Which premise should I give up? Explain why it looked okay, and then say what pure desert theorists, mixed/hybrid theorists, and consequentialist revisionists each have to pay if they reject that premise instead of another one. I'm especially reluctant to drop (1) or (8).
