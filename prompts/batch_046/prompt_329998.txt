I'm in this philosophy of mind seminar and we keep circling back to that strange inversion Darwin and Turing both pulled — getting correct, adaptive behavior out of mechanisms that dont understand a thing they're doing. My professor has been ruling on whether various systems count as competence without comprehension and I can't tell if she's running one rule or smuggling in three.

So far she said yes for Darwinian selection building the vertebrate eye, since there's no designer grasping optics; yes for a basic Turing machine running a sorting routine, since the tape doesnt represent numbers; yes for bacterial chemotaxis, where flagella just respond chemically with no internal map; and yes for old chess programs that search trees without knowing chess strategy. But she said NO for human implicit grammar learning — she called that comprehension in larval form, not its absence. She also said NO for pocket calculators, arguing they're prosthetic extensions of human reason, not independent cases. She was ambivalent about bees but seemed to think homing pigeons have cognitive maps, so probably no for them.

Would she call that competence without comprehension?

I'm trying to see if her rule is about missing representations, missing evolutionary selection, or missing meta-cognitive flexibility.
