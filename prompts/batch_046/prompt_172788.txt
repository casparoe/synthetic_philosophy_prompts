Write an essay on whether internalism about practical reasons can survive what I'll call the "grafted-desire problem"—a hypothetical I want you to treat seriously, not just dismiss as sci-fi. Imagine a neuroscientist can implant in you a fully articulated desire-set, say a deep care for distant strangers, that isn't derived from any current motivation of yours. Once implanted, though, it functions identicle to your native desires: you deliberate with it, feel guilt when you ignore it, and can refelctively endorse the considerations that support it. The internalist, in something like Williams' spirit, says reasons must link to your actual motivational set; the externalist says reasons exist regardless of what you happen to want.

Weigh both sides, but focus hard on this crux: if the grafted set gives you full rational access to its supporting considerations, and you genuinely endorse them, do the reasons they generate become "internal" now? Or do they stay external because their origin is alien? Pay special attention to whether an internalist can draw a stable line between "organic" and "implanted" motivations without falling into a kind of motivational essentialism that can't explain ordinary value change—like growing out of old tastes or acquiring new ones through conversation. Don't just summarize the usual internal/external debate; use the grafted-desire case to push it.

Reach and defend a verdict. Is internalism salvageable if we shift the boundary from causal origin to reflective endorsement? Or does the case show the distinction needs to be redrawn entirely, maybe around something like "ownership through rational integration"? Don't end non-committal—pick a side and justify it, including an honest treatment of the strongest objection to your view.
