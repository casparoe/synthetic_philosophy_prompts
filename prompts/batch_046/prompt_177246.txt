As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of action and moral psychology, I have been tracking one seminar instructor’s judgments across a series of Frankfurt-style cases concerning moral responsibility and the principle of alternative possibilities. Her verdicts so far are these.

Case One: The classic Frankfurt scenario—an external controller monitors the agent and stands ready to intervene should she fail to perform X, yet she performs X for her own reasons and the controller never activates. Verdict: the agent is morally responsible.

Case Two: A preemptive-overdetermination variant—the controller actually activates and causally forces the agent’s action, overriding her own deliberative process. Verdict: not responsible.

Case Three: A flicker-of-freedom variant—the controller will intervene only if the agent attempts to refrain entirely; she retains the ability to execute the act with her left hand rather than her right, though either execution counts as performing X. She chooses the right hand on her own reasons and the controller does nothing. Verdict: responsible.

Case Four: A prior-sign variant—the controller detects a specific neural precursor that reliably predicts the agent’s choice of X; if the precursor occurs, the controller ensures X, but because the agent chooses X for her own reasons the precursor never appears and there is no intervention. Verdict: responsible.

Case Five, the new case: A citizen, Alex, lives in a society monitored not by an external agent but by an internal neural implant installed at birth. The implant continuously records cortical activity and is programmed to override any decision violating a legal code, yet in this instance it never activates. Neuropsychological studies—known to Alex—show that the implant’s mere presence subtly suppresses risk-taking by altering baseline dopamine signaling, producing what might be called a structural chilling effect. Aware of the implant, Alex nevertheless deliberates and decides to commit a minor fraud. The implant records the decision but does not intervene, and there is no detectable precursor that would have triggered an override. Is Alex morally responsible?

Please predict what the instructor would say about Case Five if she aims to stay consistent with Cases One through Four. Identify the closest analog among them, and state honestly whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. If they do, lay out the distinct principles that could be generating her pattern, say what each principle predicts for Case Five, give your best-guess verdict with reasons for preferring it, and name one further case that would best tell the competing principles apart.
