I'm writing for a philosophy of physics course on thermodynamics and information, and I've grown tired of the textbook Maxwell-demon examples that every student has already seen. I want to test whether three conceptual frameworks genuinely diverge in their physical verdicts or only in their vocabulary, but I need a case engineered to force them apart rather than letting them all retreat to "the second law holds because information has a cost." Please construct a one-paragraph thought experiment—call it the Subspace Filter Demon—meeting these specifications. The setup: a cavity contains pairs of identical particles prepared in maximally entangled spin-singlet states. A control mechanism embedded inside the cavity measures not individual energies but only the exchange symmetry of each pair—symmetric triplet versus antisymmetric singlet. Based on this binary classification, the mechanism applies a potential that allows only antisymmetric pairs to accumulate in region A and only symmetric pairs in region B. Because the two subspaces carry different statistical weights at thermal equilibrium, a macroscopic temperature differential appears to emerge without direct mechanical work being applied to the particles themselves. Constraints: use only standard quantum statistical mechanics, with no hidden variables or post-quantum dynamics; the apparatus must have finite resolution and be physically embedded (no omniscient external observer); and the scenario must avoid perfect predictors, exotic spacetimes, or practical engineering feasibility—the point is purely conceptual.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate what each framework delivers. First, the classical information-theoretic account in the Landauer-Bennett tradition: locate the entropy cost in the erasure of the symmetry-classification record. Second, the thermodynamic-interactionist account: locate the cost in the physical coupling required to apply the symmetry-selective potential, insisting that measurement without interaction is impossible. Third, introduce and apply a view you should call procedural functionalism: the claim that the demon's decision rule is itself a physical state with an entropy budget, so that "knowing which subspace" is not external commentary but an internal correlation structure that must be included in the total entropy balance. Specify that this third view should either save the second law by a different route or fail to save it, but in either case its verdict must depend on whether there is a principled boundary between the rule-state and the particles. Then do the design check: state the belief (that the second law holds), the method (symmetry-filtering with embedded measurement), the nearby worlds (those in which the rule-state is or is not counted in the total entropy), and why each framework gives the verdict it does. Identify the single structural feature—likely the absence of a sharp observer-system boundary—that makes procedural functionalism peel away from the other two, and explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse back into a familiar information-paradox, and if so, which one? Finally, say how a defender of procedural functionalism would attack the case, and be explicit about what argument or physical consideration would change your mind regarding whether the view is genuinely distinct from the information-theoretic account. Note where your reasoning is most likely to fail: in assigning an entropy to the decision procedure, or in assuming the symmetry filter is physically realizable without hidden work costs?
