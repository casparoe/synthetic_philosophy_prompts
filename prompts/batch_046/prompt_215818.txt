I'm writing a chapter on how comparative cognition draws the line between mechanism and agency, and I'm trying to excavate the philosophical commitments buried in a standard classification practice. In both the ethological literature and philosophy-of-mind pedagogy, the digger wasp Sphex functions as the canonical case of sphexishness: the wasp checks its burrow, drags prey to the entrance, checks again, and—if an experimenter has displaced the prey—repeats the check rather than adjusting its sequence. Researchers routinely run a standardized perturbation protocol (displace the prey, observe repetition, assign a graded flexibility score) and then treat that score as diagnostic: low flexibility is taken to indicate not merely a different cognitive architecture, but the absence of practical reasoning, representational updating, or even the kind of agency that could ground moral consideration.

What philosophical commitments does this practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does treating repetition-under-perturbation as a deficit commit one to the view that agency necessarily requires the capacity to revise action-plans in response to changed circumstances? Is the protocol implicitly wedded to a deliberative model of practical reasoning—one that distinguishes behavior guided by updatable representations from behavior that is merely causally rigid? Or could a compatibilist about free will, or an enactivist who rejects internal representational states altogether, accept the same experimental observations while denying that they license any conclusion about the absence of agency?

Separate, carefully, what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. For instance, does assigning a low flexibility score entail that metabolic efficiency can never explain rigidity, or only that rigidity is insufficient for agency? And if the underlying presupposition—say, that adaptability to perturbation is necessary for free will—were replaced by an account on which agency consists in the coherence of an organism's behavioral economy rather than its responsiveness to disruption, what exactly would have to change in the scoring rubric, in the interpretation of the wasp's loop, and in the philosophical conclusions drawn from it?
