I've spent the past year, without any formal training, trying to understand why made-up scenarios—what philosophers call intuition pumps—feel so convincing. One pattern I keep noticing is that the most persuasive examples don't just present a logical puzzle; they paint a situation so concretely that the listener stops checking whether the situation could actually occur. I don't have an academic term for this, but it feels like a kind of "vividness capture," where imagination locks onto sensory or narrative details that aren't actually relevant to the argument being made. At the same time, I can see that these tools are often indispensable for clarifying concepts, so the problem isn't that they exist, but that we lack clear rules for when they're doing honest work versus smuggling in hidden premises.

Given that, I'm trying to decide where the real open work lies. If someone had a small research group or about three focused years—without a laboratory, working mostly through reasoning and observation—which questions about intuition pumps should they actually pursue? I'm torn among three directions, though I may be missing better ones: first, whether we can build a rigorous taxonomy of vividness capture—what specific structural features of a scenario cause people to skip feasibility checks; second, whether intuition pumps fail in systematically different ways across cultures, since what feels "obviously right" may depend on background norms about agency, harm, or social roles that I, as one observer, can't fully see; and third, whether there's any principled criterion for distinguishing a legitimate intuition pump from a misleading one, rather than relying on post-hoc disagreement.

But I don't want a survey or a reading list. I want a defended prioritization: rank these lines of work (and any others you think I'm neglecting) according to what deserves limited effort. For each, explain why it's upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for someone working outside institutional philosophy or cognitive science, who it actually serves—philosophers, educators, people designing reasoning systems that rely on analogy—and what kind of result over the next few years would cause you to reorder them. Be explicit about your criteria: importance, tractability, neglectedness, and dependency on other answers.

Also, which alleged implications of this debate are overrated? For instance, does repairing our use of intuition pumps genuinely change how we practice ethics, law, or public reasoning, or is that downstream effect smaller than it appears? Rank the implications by how much they should actually guide where effort goes, and tell me which ones don't follow at all. I want an agenda, not a defense of any particular thought experiment.
