I'm preparing a short position paper for a graduate seminar on institutional ethics and intertemporal decision-making, and I would welcome your comments on whether the conceptual framing is accurate, whether the argument is genuinely interesting rather than merely paradoxical, and specifically where the analogy I am drawing might break down.

The core idea is this. Most philosophical treatments of pure time preference treat it as an individual attitude: one cares less about one's own welfare at age eighty than at age forty solely because the former is farther in the future, with no appeal to risk, uncertainty, or changing preferences. I want to apply this structure to collective agents—specifically, universities—making tradeoffs across generations of beneficiaries. Consider an endowment committee deciding whether to fund an expensive archival digitization project that will primarily serve researchers fifty years hence, or instead to expand mental-health services for current students. If the committee applies a pure temporal discount rate to the welfare of future scholars—discounting them only because they are temporally distant—this seems structurally parallel to the individual case. Yet there is a potential disanalogy: unlike a single person, the institution lacks continuous psychological identity, so future beneficiaries are not "part of" its present self in any obvious sense. If pure time preference is rational only when there is such personal continuity, then institutions ought to reject it entirely and weight future welfare equally. But if pure time preference is rational even without continuity—say, as a structural feature of any rational planning agent—then universities should systematically underfund preservation and overfund present consumption, which appears to undermine their defining purpose as intergenerational custodians of knowledge. I want to suggest that this tension renders pure time preference unstable when applied to collective agents: it either collapses to zero for institutions lacking personal identity, or, if applied consistently, generates an institutional self-undermining presentism.

My notes remain rough, particularly on whether I have quietly smuggled in risk-based considerations—archival projects can fail, technologies become obsolete—when I claim to be isolating pure temporal distance. I also worry that my appeal to "institutional identity" does too much unexplained work, since corporations and states face analogous puzzles without having the same custodial obligations. Could you comment on whether the analogy between individual and institutional discounting holds, whether the distinction between pure and risk-based discounting is maintained clearly enough in this framing, and whether there is a more precise way to articulate the identity condition without invoking metaphysical theories of group personhood that I have not actually developed? I am not looking for citations or a reading list, just an assessment of whether the conceptual machinery is coherent and where it might be sharpened or abandoned.
