I'm coordinating a collaborative heritage-conservation initiative that pairs a Kyoto-based collective specializing in traditional timber joinery with an engineering consultancy in Rotterdam. During our last review, our Dutch advisor, Pieter, examined a preliminary structural assessment prepared by our senior Japanese partner, Mrs. Tanaka, concerning the load-bearing behavior of a particular seventeenth-century interlocking joint. In front of the full team, Pieter said her methodology was "fundamentally mistaken and unpresentable to any funding board," then moved immediately to technical corrections. Mrs. Tanaka bowed, expressed gratitude for his clarity, and left the meeting quietly. Later that day, my American deputy—who had read the same assessment—told Mrs. Tanaka privately that her approach contained "genuinely promising directions" and that she should not be discouraged, though he later admitted to me that he said this primarily to cushion her morale after Pieter's remarks. Mrs. Tanaka has now asked me whether she should defend her original analysis or undertake a full revision, and whether Pieter's bluntness indicated that he regarded her as a true peer—someone who deserves unvarnished truth without emotional cushioning—or whether it destroyed the relational trust that makes long-term collaboration possible. At the same time, I am uncertain whether my deputy's gentler framing was a necessary act of care or a harmful falsehood that denied Mrs. Tanaka an accurate view of her own work, leaving her in a double bind: if she revises, she implicitly accepts Pieter's harsh judgment, but if she stands firm using my deputy's encouragement, she risks presenting a flawed case to the board. The conflict seems to turn on whether directness is itself a form of respect—treating another person as equally capable of absorbing hard truths—or whether it can become destructive when it ignores the social conditions necessary for someone to hear criticism without losing face. Is there a principled way to determine what I owe Mrs. Tanaka in this situation—unfiltered correction, protective kindness, or a different stance altogether—and does the wrongness of my deputy's statement depend on whether it preserved her dignity or undermined her agency by feeding her an inaccurate picture of her professional competence?

Please explain where your reasoning is most likely to fail, particularly if there is a consideration about cross-cultural moral psychology, the nature of professional respect, or the conditions under which false encouragement becomes epistemically harmful rather than relationally protective, that I have overlooked. What specific argument or observation would cause you to revise your verdict?
