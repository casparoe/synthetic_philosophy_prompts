I sit on the grant-allocation committee of the Whitmore Charitable Foundation. We are reviewing a proposal for a public-health intervention that, according to its own risk-assessment model, is expected to prevent approximately forty serious illnesses across the general population, but will, with near-certainty, inflict severe and irreversible harm upon exactly three named individuals who have already been enrolled in the trial cohort. The applicants defend the project by arguing that the aggregate welfare gain plainly outweighs the concentrated loss, and that refusing to fund it on account of the three named participants amounts to arbitrary boundary-drawing: treating persons as separate containers of utility rather than allowing benefits and burdens to cancel across the group. One of my fellow committee members has raised what she terms the separateness objection, contending that our charter requires us to regard each beneficiary as a distinct centre of moral value, not as an interchangeable fraction of a collective sum, and that aggregating harm and benefit in this way violates the very idea of respecting persons. I need a formal written opinion recommending whether we should approve the grant, reject it, or approve it subject to conditions, together with clear reasoning that addresses the researchers' aggregation argument directly, treats my colleague's concern with genuine seriousness rather than dismissing it, and sets out concrete criteria we can apply to analogous applications in the future. The recommendation should reach an actionable bottom line that the research team could actually respond to.
