I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science studying scientific rhetoric. Over the past year I have repeatedly observed the same discursive configuration in peer-reviewed literature and conference Q&A sessions, yet I cannot locate an established label for it within speech-act theory or science studies. The pattern is this: a researcher reports an empirical result using strictly descriptive language—for instance, stating that a particular model yields the correct prediction under condition C—but the framing simultaneously establishes an unstated methodological norm, such that any subsequent study that fails to meet condition C is treated as methodologically deficient, even though no explicit directive was ever issued. When challenged on the normative load, the speaker insists that the utterance was purely factual and that any normative consequence is an unintended interpretation by the audience. This is not a standard Austinian performative, because no recognized conventional procedure is being executed; it is not a Searlean declaration, because the institutional fact is not being created by explicit fiat; and it differs from ordinary conversational implicature, since the normative constraint is structural to the disciplinary practice rather than calculable from context alone. Concrete instances include climate scientists using the phrase robust projection to settle acceptable parameter ranges, and neuroscientists invoking replicable effect to foreclose debate on a paradigm—each case functioning descriptively while quietly fixing evidentiary standards. Is there an established term—perhaps in the sociology of scientific knowledge or in post-Austinian pragmatics—for this specific hybrid of descriptive report and hidden normative institution? If the concept does not exist as such, please define it precisely, distinguish it from strategic ambiguity, hidden performativity, and indirect speech acts, and identify the boundary conditions that separate it from ordinary disciplinary rhetoric.
