I want to teach them judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma, not as an abstract puzzle but as a structural problem that also arises when a literary jury attempts to reach a collective verdict on a prize manuscript. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself.

Please lay out the dependency order: which conceptual component must be firmly in place before the discursive dilemma can appear to them as a genuine structural tension rather than as a simple mistake or failure of good faith? Choose one concrete carrying scenario—preferably a jury of three readers evaluating two submitted novels against interdependent criteria such as narrative coherence, ethical complexity, and formal innovation—and specify exactly what that scenario must include and what it must exclude so that the conflict between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation becomes visible without collapsing into “the group is being irrational” or “democracy does not work.”

Then map the misconceptions these writers will predictably bring or form: for instance, that majority rule is self-evidently correct in every context, that an inconsistent collective judgment proves someone was dishonest, that aggregating premises is obviously more rational than aggregating conclusions, or that this phenomenon is merely the familiar “wisdom of crowds” failing. Design one brief check—perhaps a single question I can pose near the end—to verify that they have grasped the structural conflict itself rather than merely feeling uneasy about disagreement. Finally, tell me which standard formal apparatus—named paradox labels, axiomatic representation results, and references to the specialist literature—I should explicitly set aside at this level, and say so openly.
