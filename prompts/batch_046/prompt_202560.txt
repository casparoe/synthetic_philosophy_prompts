Next month I'm giving a seminar paper to our interdepartamental history/philosphy working group on whether the Fischer thesis actually commits us to "blaming Germany" for WWI, or whether that's a category mistake.

My line is that Fischer's archival work on the September Program gets misread as moral indictment because people conflate causal explanation with collective culpability, and that once you separate institutional responsibility from national guilt, the controversy stops being about "was Germany guilty" and becomes about whether any collective agent can bear responsibility for a war started by bureacratic drift. The audience is mostly diplomatic historians who've taught the Fischer controversy for years, two philosophers of collective action who think I'm dodging moral accountability, and a grad student who keeps citing Christopher Clark's Sleepwalkers to argue that blame is always distributed. I need the objections in the order they'll actually come, not in logical order—starting with the historian who'll say I'm reading Fischer anachronistically, then the collective-action people, then the Clark fan. For each: phrase it like they'd say it, what's the two-minute reply, where the reply is weakest, and what I should concede vs hold. Also include the question I'm praying nobody asks about whether this lets the Kaiser off the hook, and how not to sound like I'm defending revisionism.
