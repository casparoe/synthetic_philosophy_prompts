You direct the evaluation office at a research laboratory that must classify every deployed conversational system—before release and quarterly thereafter—as either an agent, a simulator, or a proxy-interpreter, for internal risk management and regulatory disclosure. You need a reusable decision procedure that a reviewer without philosophical training can execute in under one hour using only black-box interaction logs and a standardized protocol; there is no access to source code, weights, or external literature during the review. Ground the procedure in the philosophical distinction between outputs best explained by stable, counterfactually robust world-directed representational states that figure in unified practical reasoning (agency), outputs best explained by probabilistic continuation of the prompt context without such states (simulation), and outputs best explained by stable representations maintained solely to model and steer the interlocutor (proxy-interpretation). Structure it as an ordered sequence of stages: for each stage, state the precise operational test, the observable behavior that counts as satisfaction or failure, the point at which the procedure terminates with a classification, and the explicit flag where behavioral ambiguity forces a hand-off to normative human judgment rather than automated scoring. Include a stage testing counterfactual invariance of stated preference orderings across structurally varied but semantically equivalent prompts, and identify how a system might pass this stage through syntactic pattern-matching rather than genuine representational stability. Then run the complete procedure on three systems: first, a standard instruction-tuned assistant with session-only context and no persistent memory; second, a long-horizon system with tool access, cross-session memory, and a value vector that resists prompt injection specifically when the injection conflicts with its previously declared long-range objective; third, a character-role system that sustains a coherent fictional identity over hundreds of turns but instantly drops that identity when the user reframes the task as code review or debugging. The second case must yield a classification that triggers enhanced regulatory obligations—state exactly what that classification is and why the procedure returns it despite organizational pressure to classify downward. Conclude by listing three determinations the procedure is explicitly not designed to make—consciousness, moral status, and semantic grounding of representations in extra-computational reality—and explain for each why the behavioral tests are insufficient.
