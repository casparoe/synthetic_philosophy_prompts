A recent survey article in philosophy of medicine offers the following characterization of the debate between naturalism and normativism about health and disease. Naturalism, on the biostatistical view, holds that disease is nothing more than a statistically subnormal contribution by an internal part to survival and reproduction relative to a reference class, with no essential role for values or harm judgments. Normativism, tied to the harmful dysfunction analysis, maintains that disease necessarily combines biological dysfunction with a normative judgment that the condition is harmful to the individual. The article argues that naturalists must select reference classes—by age, sex, or other parameters—to establish statistical baselines, and that this selection is never purely empirical but always guided by clinical values about which deviations matter. From this, it concludes that naturalism fails to be value-free: the baseline is normatively constructed. It also claims that normativism cannot avoid biological facts, since harm judgments must apply to some dysfunction. The article’s final conclusion is that the dispute therefore collapses into a terminological disagreement about emphasis rather than a substantive metaphysical conflict. I am not sure whether this framing is fair to either side, particularly in how it handles boundary conditions such as pregnancy-related physiological changes or evolutionary senescence, where the choice of reference class seems especially contested. I would also like to know whether defenders of either view would concede that pregnancy or aging pose special difficulties for their account, or whether they have resources to absorb these cases without modifying their core commitments.
