For the past semester my philosophy reading group has been deadlocked over Wittgenstein’s family resemblance inside language games—specifically whether it genuinely eliminates shared essences or merely disguises them behind overlapping similarities. One side argues that if concepts like “game” lack necessary and sufficient conditions, then categorization must still depend on a hidden universal—resemblance itself—without which the chain of similarities would never stabilize across different language games. The other side insists that resemblance is not an ontological ground but a practical capacity exercised within specific forms of life, and that demanding a further explanation is like asking what holds the earth up once we learn it rests on another body. We agree on Wittgenstein’s standard examples and on the descriptions in the relevant passages, yet we cannot determine whether our disagreement concerns metaphysics, the standards of conceptual explanation, or whether “family resemblance” is even the right name for what happens when rules are followed in practice. I hypothesize that we are answering different questions under one phrase: one about the ontology of universals, one about the pragmatics of explanation. Is that right, or is there a hidden empirical claim—perhaps about how children acquire categories across language games—that one side is smuggling in? Could a conceptual distinction, say between horizontal and vertical resemblance, or a formal result about similarity clustering, actually break the stalemate, or does the impasse show that the original question is ill-posed? I want a diagnosis with reasons, supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of Wittgenstein scholarship, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a distinction, an experiment, a change of question—could resolve it, or why no progress should be expected.
