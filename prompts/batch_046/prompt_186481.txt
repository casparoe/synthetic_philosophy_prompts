Please design a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with four binary branch nodes—that classifies philosophical positions on whether culture or values can be genuine causes in historical explanation, using Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism as the central test case. Each branch node must be labeled by a concrete diagnostic claim or counterfactual scenario on which the positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that someone could answer it without knowing the names of the positions it leads to. The root question should ask whether a specific theological interpretation, such as the doctrine of predestination read as a mandate for worldly ascetic labor, can make a causal difference to sustained capital reinvestment, holding institutional frameworks, legal property rights, market access, and resource endowments constant across the comparison.

The four branch nodes should cover the following splits in order:

1. The isolation test: If two societies share identical economic structures but differ only in whether their dominant theology sacralizes worldly success or condemns wealth accumulation, and only the first exhibits long-term capital accumulation, is the divergence best explained by the theological framework being a direct cause?

2. The mechanism test: For those who answer yes, does Weber's concept of elective affinity identify a content-specific psychological mechanism (for example, salvation anxiety generating compulsive labor and consumption restraint) or only a functional form—deferred gratification coupled with status denial—that any secular or religious belief system could reproduce?

3. The substitution test: If secular state ideologies or non-theological ethical systems in other historical contexts produce behaviorally identical patterns of accumulation and deferred consumption, does this show that Weber's specific cultural content was causally inert, or does it merely demonstrate that the same causal structure is multiply realizable?

4. The failure-mode test: For those who reject causal attribution at the root, is the rejection ontological (values or ideas are not the right kind of entities to enter causal relations with behavior) or epistemological (we cannot measure theological commitment independently of the economic behaviors it purports to explain, making the attribution circular)? Within the epistemological path, include a further split on whether the circularity is fatal, or whether it can be broken by a second-order reflexive mechanism in which values influence behavior only when agents explicitly interpret and monitor their own values as binding obligations.

Every leaf must carry either a named position—such as strong Weberian elective-affinity causation, functionalist structural substitution, ontological anti-idealism, fatal methodological behaviorism, or the reflexive hermeneutic view—or be marked unnamed/unoccupied, with one sentence diagnosing why that combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. In particular, include the reflexive-efficacy position, where causal power depends on second-order self-interpretation, even though it is not a standard entry in Weber commentary, and flag any pair of leaves that the tree's logic cannot genuinely separate. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or specific author-date citations; identify every position solely by its conceptual commitments. Assume the audience is a graduate seminar in philosophy of social science that understands Weber's thesis in outline but should not need to recall specific secondary literature to navigate the tree.
