Consider the gender-equality paradox: societies with stronger formal gender-egalitarian institutions and norms frequently exhibit greater occupational segregation by gender than less egalitarian societies. Interpretations diverge—some take this as evidence of deep-seated, possibly innate gendered preferences; others treat it as proof that egalitarian conditions merely allow socially constructed preferences, previously masked by economic coercion, to become visible.

Argue for the following position: the paradox is best explained not by the origin of gendered preferences (whether innate or socialized), but by a mechanism we can call structural preference visibility. Under conditions of economic constraint and institutional exclusion, gender-correlated career preferences are suppressed—not necessarily absent, but unobservable—because survival demands override expressive choice. When egalitarian policies remove these suppressors, the underlying distribution of preferences becomes observable, creating the illusion that equality has produced divergence rather than revealed it.

Then defend the further normative claim that this visibility mechanism generates a specific, underappreciated obligation of justice. If a society eliminates coercive economic barriers, it acquires a positive duty to redesign the architecture of career choice—through instruments such as anonymized apprenticeship matching, delayed specialization tracks, or decoupled credentialing pathways—so that revealed preferences are not mistaken for fully autonomous choices, particularly when those preferences may reflect gendered schemas internalized under prior conditions of structural inequality. Address the objection that such architectural redesign constitutes illegitimate paternalism, and explain why distinguishing preference suppression from preference formation undermines that objection without committing you to any particular view about the naturalness or cultural malleability of gender.

In developing this argument, do not invoke bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid appealing to highly specialized empirical literatures you cannot reconstruct from first principles. Build the case conceptually.
