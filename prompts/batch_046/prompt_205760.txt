Since my mother's recent diagnosis with frontotemporal dementia and the unexpected activation of an advance directive she composed fifteen years ago—before she could have conceived of this particular cognitive trajectory—I have taken on a more formal role with our hospital's clinical ethics consultation service. We now convene twice weekly to adjudicate conflicts in which a patient's present expressed preferences, emotional responses, or apparent personality structure diverge sharply from the conditions and instructions in an earlier document. I need a reusable decision procedure that our three-person team can execute in roughly forty-five minutes per case, yielding recommendations that must be co-signed by the attending physician and a family-designated surrogate, but that do not depend on anyone having read specific philosophical literature or having access to external references. The stakes are consistently high, the emotional temperature is elevated, and we have no staff to verify historical attributions or bibliographic details.

The procedure should be grounded in the philosophical problem of diachronic authority—under what conditions a statement made by an earlier psychological stage binds a present subject who may no longer share that stage's values, memories, or conceptual framework—without reducing the issue to a simple autonomy-versus-best-interests calculus that any model could recite from memory. To keep the framework conceptually rigorous yet operationalizable, I want it to incorporate a specific distinction I have been developing: between an Orwellian revision, in which the documentary record of the directive or our interpretation of its conditions has been subtly altered, misapplied, or selectively remembered, and a Stalinesque revision, in which the patient's present mental economy is genuinely transformed such that the earlier self's evaluative framework no longer maps onto the current subject's experience. The flow must ask, in strict order, whether the present patient is ontologically the same authority-bearing subject the directive addresses; whether the triggering conditions are satisfied in a manner undistorted by either kind of revision; and, only if both prior gates are cleared, whether honoring the directive nonetheless violates a residual moral constraint that should trigger a values-based override rather than a procedural halt. At each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what to do when information is genuinely inaccessible—for instance, when the patient cannot communicate and family members offer contradictory accounts of prior values—and flag explicitly which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedural checkpoints.

Please deliver the procedure as a numbered flowchart in words, with clear stop points that output honor the directive, suspend pending further evaluation, or defer to direct ethical reasoning that this framework cannot settle. Then apply it to three cases: first, a patient with moderate Alzheimer's who now refuses all oral nutrition but whose directive explicitly rejects artificial nutrition and hydration, where the family insists she would not want to suffer and the consultant suspects the procedure will mandate withholding intervention in a way that feels morally wrong; second, a patient with severe frontotemporal degeneration who expresses cheerful contentment and new religious commitments that directly contradict her earlier secular, autonomy-centered directive, where neuropsychological assessment indicates a near-complete discontinuity in personality structure; and third, a minimally conscious patient following traumatic brain injury whose directive was written specifically for persistent vegetative state but whose current neurological status is ambiguous and for whom no family member can confirm whether present responses indicate any retained psychological continuity. In the first application especially, state precisely what the procedure mandates and explain why the resulting discomfort does not indicate a procedural failure. Conclude by specifying what kinds of conflicts lie outside the procedure's scope and under what conditions a consultant should abandon it for unmediated ethical judgment.
