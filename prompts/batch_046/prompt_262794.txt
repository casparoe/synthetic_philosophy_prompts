So here's the thing. I've been thinkin about why debates over corruption always seem to stall out right when they get interesting. On one side you've got the Western standard-bearers — corruption is abuse of public office for private gain, full stop, and if you're handing out jobs to your cousins or accepting 'gifts' that are clearly just payments in disguise, that's corruption regardless of local custom. On the other side you've got the critics — often anthropologists or comparative political scientists — who say that framing is just exporting a particular contractual, individualist morality. They point to clientelism, reciprocal gift-giving, even patronage networks that actualy deliver welfare in weak-state contexts, and say calling all that 'corruption' is like calling a family dinner 'theft' because nobody paid market price.

The debate never moves. Universalists cite rule of law; relativists cite social embeddedness. Both sides agree on the descriptive facts — yes, the minister gave the contract to his brother-in-law; yes, the bureaucrat recieved an envelope — but they can't agree on whether those facts are corruption.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't actualy about culture versus universals. I think it's a disagreement about what kind of wrong corruption is in the first place. Maybe one side sees it as a breach of institutional role-obligation (you promised to act as an office-holder, not a clan-member), and the other sees it as a distortion of allocative fairness (resources went to the wrong people regardless of the social meaning). And then there's this third layer I've been playing with — call it 'ontological corruption,' where the wrong isn't in the outcome or the role-breach, but in treating a relationship that should be governed by gift-logic or kin-logic with the wrong kind of metric entirely, like applying contract-accounting to reciprocity.
