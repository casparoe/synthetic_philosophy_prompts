Below is an exam question from a course on democratic ethics, followed by a student’s answer. You are grading the submission as an instructor or TA. Do not signal your own view of the correct answer; evaluate the argument strictly on its own terms.

Question: Consider a large-scale election conducted by ranked-choice voting, in which a voter judges both leading candidates to be seriously flawed, though one is clearly less harmful than the other. The voter also believes her ballot is causally negligible: the probability that her specific ranking alters the outcome is effectively zero. Is there a moral duty to rank the lesser-evil candidate above the other? Answer by distinguishing instrumental reasons (causal efficacy) from expressive reasons (what the act communicates), and address the following: (a) whether an expressive justification can support ranking a candidate one finds morally objectionable; (b) whether the dual-track framework—where Track A concerns causal impact and Track B concerns contribution to the democratic mandate’s perceived legitimacy—alters the analysis of abstaining or leaving ranks blank; and (c) whether the “clean hands” objection, which treats ranking as a form of complicity rather than mere preference-ordering, undermines any purported duty to rank the lesser evil.

Student answer: Yes, there is a clear duty to rank the lesser-evil candidate first. Instrumentally, because the election is large, a single vote cannot change the result, so there is no instrumental reason to rank anyone. On Track B, voting contributes to democratic legitimation, so participating is important. Since ranking expresses one’s values, the voter should rank the less harmful candidate first, because that expresses her preference for less harm. Leaving a rank blank is just abstaining, which withholds legitimation and is therefore wrong. The clean hands objection does not apply here because ranking is not endorsement; it is only ordering preferences. So the voter has an all-things-considered duty to rank the lesser evil, and leaving blanks or abstaining is morally wrong because it fails to express any preference and undermines legitimation.
