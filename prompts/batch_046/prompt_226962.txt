The poster summarized the view as follows: prioritarianism is the thesis that social welfare is calculated by applying a strictly concave weighting function to individual well-being, where the moral weight of an additional unit of welfare decreases continuously as a person's baseline welfare increases. From this, the poster argued that prioritarianism is vulnerable to a version of the leveling-down objection: because the weight assigned to a unit of welfare is determined relative to others' welfare levels, any action that lowers the well-being of the best-off—without changing anyone else's—must increase the aggregate weighted sum, since the remaining distribution is more equal and the worst-off now receive relatively higher weights. The poster then claimed that prioritarians typically respond by denying that equality has intrinsic value, but that this response fails because the weighting function itself encodes equality in its calculus. Finally, the poster added that prioritarianism has no resources to distinguish sufficientarian thresholds—discrete welfare floors below which weights become constant—from smooth prioritarian curves, and therefore collapses into a form of threshold egalitarianism once basic needs are met. I am skeptical of this account, but I do not have the technical background to locate every error. Could you go through it point by point? Specifically: is the characterization of the weighting function correct, or does it smuggle in assumptions about interpersonal comparison? Does the leveling-down objection apply to prioritarianism in the form given, or is it being misappropriated from egalitarian critiques? Is the claim about the standard response being self-undermining fair? And is the alleged collapse into sufficientarianism a genuine structural feature, or a confusion of two separate distributive principles?
