As a novelist drafting a philosophical dialogue for a character who is an evolutionary biologist turned philosopher, I need a precisely engineered thought experiment that I can embed in the scene without relying on citations or external references my readers will not have at hand. The dialogue concerns the divergence between etiological—selected-effect—and causal-role—system-capacity—accounts of biological function, and I want the biologist character to present a naturalistic, single-paragraph scenario in which the two theories deliver opposite verdicts about the proper function of a specific anatomical trait, not because the theories have been twisted, but because a particular feature of the evolutionary history genuinely isolates them.

Please construct such a case to the following specifications. The organism and trait should be drawn from realistic evolutionary biology—no science-fictional organisms or engineered chimeras—but the evolutionary trajectory should be hypothetical, injecting the somewhat niche idea of a developmental spandrel that has become load-bearing for a novel physiological system after the original selective environment disappeared. Specifically, design the scenario so that the trait was originally selected for function A, such as structural reinforcement during a specific developmental phase; that this original selective pressure has been completely removed for many generations; and that the trait now makes an indispensable contribution to a completely different system-level capacity B, such as thermal regulation in a changed habitat, while its original contribution to A is now either absent or physiologically irrelevant. Keep the scenario to one paragraph and avoid stipulating the verdict; instead, let the divergence fall out of how each theory defines function.

After presenting the scenario, walk through the verdict each theory delivers and the reasoning behind it: what the etiological account says about whether the trait's function is A, B, or none; what the causal-role account says about whether it is B, A, or plural; and why. Then identify explicitly which feature of the case is doing the philosophical work—likely the decoupling of selection history from current system contribution—and explain what happens to the divergence if that feature is removed, for instance if the original selective pressure were still present. Finally, include an honest note on how a defender of the account that comes out counterintuitively—probably the etiological theorist—would attack the scenario, perhaps by challenging the claim that selection for A has truly ceased or by appealing to functional pluralism. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the scene must stand on conceptual reasoning alone. I am asking for this because the dialogue needs to feel intellectually rigorous yet entirely self-contained, allowing a reader with no web access to follow every move.
