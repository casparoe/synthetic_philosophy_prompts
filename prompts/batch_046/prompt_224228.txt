As a novelist constructing a revolutionary protagonist for a historical novel, I have been framing her relationship to the past through Nietzsche's three modes—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—only to find that they seem to entail she cannot possess a coherent psychology. The argument runs as follows. (1) Monumental history serves life only by extracting heroic models from the past while suppressing awareness of historical difference; it requires selective forgetting. (2) Antiquarian history serves life only by preserving the past in its full particularity without judgment; it requires total remembrance. (3) Critical history serves life only by judging and dissolving parts of the past to liberate the present; it requires active condemnation. (4) Psychological coherence in a fictional character requires a unified temporal orientation—what I call the triune-past constraint: one consciousness cannot simultaneously suppress, preserve, and condemn the same past. (5) Therefore any psychologically coherent character can adopt at most one of the three stances. (6) Yet to render a believable revolutionary, I must show her inspired by past rebels (monumental), anchored in family tradition (antiquarian), and breaking with oppressive structures (critical). (7) Hence any believable revolutionary protagonist violates the triune-past constraint. (8) So either historical fiction is impossible, Nietzsche's framework is inapplicable to individual psychology, or one of my premises fails. (9) I reject the first disjunct, and I would prefer to keep both (4) and (6).

Where exactly does this go wrong? Is the error an equivocation between collective cultural uses of history and an individual's psychological stance, a scope mistake about what "unified" requires, or a false mapping from my compositional needs onto my character's mind? If I must abandon one premise among (1)–(3), which costs the least for the craft of writing historical fiction?
