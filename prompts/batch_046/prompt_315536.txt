Question: Kant is often interpreted as forbidding every lie, even to save an innocent person from a murderer asking at the door. Now consider a variation: instead of saying anything false, you open the door wide and say, literally, "Come in and search—he is not hiding here," using a tone and gesture that you intend the murderer to read as ironic proof of absence, though the words themselves are technically true. Is this kind of indirect, performative deception permitted on Kant's view? Defend your answer with reference to perfect duty and the universalizability of maxims.

Student answer:
Kant clearly holds that lying is always wrong, even in the murderer-at-the-door case, because truthfulness is a perfect duty. In his discussion of lying out of benevolence, he argues that if you falsely tell the murderer your friend is absent and the murderer then finds him, your false statement breaks the social trust that makes communication possible, and you share responsibility for the harm. So an explicit verbal lie is definitely forbidden.

But in my scenario there is no false proposition at all. I literally invite the murderer inside to search, and I never utter the sentence "he is not here" as a factual claim; any misunderstanding comes entirely from his own interpretation of my tone. Since Kant's argument targets false assertions, this indirect deception falls outside the prohibition's scope.

Next, my maxim is "protect an innocent life by allowing a wrongdoer to draw a false inference from a literally true invitation." If everyone acted on this maxim, literal invitations would still be understandable, so universalizing it produces no contradiction. By the universal law formulation, the act is therefore permissible.

Finally, I do this solely out of respect for the victim's humanity, not from self-interest. Kant teaches that a good will is the only unconditional good, so because my motivation is pure, the act itself must be morally correct regardless of its external form.
