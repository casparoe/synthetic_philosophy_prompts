I have been puzzling over Buridan's ass—the case of a donkey that starves between two identical bales of hay because it lacks any reason to prefer one. In a recent conversation, two acquaintances debated what this actually demonstrates about rational choice. I am recounting their exchange as a neutral bystander and want an outside assessment.

(1) The first said that Buridan's ass proves rational choice requires a pre-existing preference ordering. If two options are genuinely equal in value, there can be no rational ground for selecting either, so the agent is necessarily paralyzed. Hence every actual choice reveals that the options were never truly equal—there was always a hidden preference.

(2) The second replied that in ordinary life people resolve ties constantly—choosing one of two identical coffee shops on a whim, or flipping a coin—without judging themselves irrational. Therefore preference need not precede choice; selection can occur through noise, habit, or arbitrary mechanism in the absence of any prior ranking.

(3) The first responded that relying on a coin or whim is not rational choice but outsourcing the decision. The ass possesses no external mechanism to break the tie. Consequently, if rational agency demands that one be able to offer reasons for one's selection, then tie-breaking by whim abandons rationality. That people do it in practice merely shows that we are not fully rational agents on those occasions.

(4) The second countered that this argument smuggles in an unstated premise: that rationality must be reasons-responsive in every single case. But rationality might instead be a matter of long-term coherence. If I choose the left bale today and the right bale tomorrow with no reason for either, my preferences remain consistent precisely because I am genuinely indifferent. The paralysis arises only if one insists that every choice must carry a narrative justification, which is an unrealistic standard. The genuine problem, therefore, is not choice without preference, but the expectation that all choice must be accompanied by a story.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Say also what, if anything, both parties are overlooking. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then present the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
