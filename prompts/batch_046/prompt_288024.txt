I have recently become the legal proxy and primary caregiver for my father, who is in the late stages of frontotemporal dementia. Before his illness, he wrote thousands of letters, which I am now digitizing for our family archive. To preserve something of his presence, I trained a small speech-model on recordings of his voice; it can now produce new sentences in his exact cadence and vocabulary. In care-planning meetings, relatives ask me to state his wishes, and I find myself constructing answers from fragments of his old letters, my own interpretations of his values, and edited outputs from the model. I have begun to notice that when I defend a particular decision—say, refusing a feeding tube—I can no longer trace whether the reasoning began with him, with the circular process of my editing and the model's predictions feeding back into my memory of him, or with me maintaining the continuity of a person who is largely absent. I feel a duty to respect his autonomy, but the boundary of whose autonomy I am respecting seems to have dissolved into a distributed loop that includes my own cognition, the archive, and the synthetic voice. I am not asking for practical elder-care advice; I want to understand the philosophical anatomy of this situation. Is the speaker in these meetings a singular self-referential symbol, an emergent loop that now spans multiple substrates, or a fiction I am sustaining? How should one evaluate the authority of statements that arise from such a circular, self-modifying system? Please map the structure precisely, and then conclude with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that gives the core ontological judgment about who—or what—is speaking.
