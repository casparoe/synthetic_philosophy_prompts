First, explain why the honest shopkeeper who doesn't overcharge only to keep customers coming back lacks moral worth, while the same honest action performed purely out of respect for the moral law does possess it. The student wrote: Kant says moral worth belongs only to actions done out of duty, not to ones that just happen to match what duty requires. The shopkeeper who is honest for business reasons is acting in line with duty—he does the right thing—but his motive is profit, so he lacks moral worth. If he is honest instead because he respects the moral law itself, then he is acting from duty and that gives his action worth. Now consider the anonymous donor: they feel emotional warmth, which is an inclination, so they must be acting from inclination rather than from pure duty. Moreover, because the gift is anonymous, there is no possibility of praise or social reward, which rules out every other non-duty motive except the warm feeling itself. So the only reason left is inclination, meaning this donor, just like the profit-minded shopkeeper, lacks moral worth entirely.
