Effective-altruist cause prioritization and several animal-welfare grantmakers now rely on “moral-weight” coefficients—continuous values (for instance, on the order of 10⁻³ for insects, 10⁻¹ for chickens, and 1.0 for humans)—to scale expected welfare adjustments when comparing interventions across species. The framework treats sentience and moral status as gradated rather than binary, yet it draws a hard practical line that excludes clearly non-sentient artifacts like thermostats, weather systems, or simple feedback loops, assigning them no weight whatsoever. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory that licenses this specific gradient: does it presuppose that consciousness itself is a scalar property with measurable intensity or integration levels, or is the continuity merely an epistemic smoothing over discrete, unknown thresholds? Is the lower boundary—excluding thermostats—grounded in a metaphysical claim about the absence of integrated information or subjective experience, or is it a tractability constraint disguised as ontology? Furthermore, examine whether this scalar practice is consistent with the same organizations’ legal and advocacy efforts, which often demand that certain animals be recognized as rights-bearing entities rather than as units in a weighted welfare calculus. Can a genuinely gradated theory of moral status accommodate rights-language without contradiction, or does it implicitly commit its practitioners to a purely consequentialist calculus in which rights are at best heuristic approximations? Reach and defend a specific conclusion about what view of sentience—from strict binary patienthood to fully continuous moral density—the institutional use of moral weights actually requires, and specify what elements of current practice would have to be revised if that underlying view turned out to be mistaken.
