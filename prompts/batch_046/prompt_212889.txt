In my upper-division epistemology course we are working through cases of higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. To motivate the idea that belief can be akratic in the same way action can, my instructor uses an analogy: practical akrasia—knowing you should not eat the dessert because it violates your diet, feeling the pull of taste, and eating it anyway—is structurally parallel to epistemic akrasia, where you hold a first-order belief (say, that a statistical model is reliable) while possessing higher-order evidence that you are biased in evaluating it, yet you maintain the belief anyway. He extends the comparison by saying higher-order evidence functions like a warning light on a dashboard: the first-order belief is the engine running, the higher-order judgment is the blinking signal, and akrasia is continuing to drive without pulling over. The analogy is meant to license the conclusion that epistemic akrasia is not only possible but no more puzzling than ordinary weakness of will, and that we should treat belief-revision failure as a mundane psychological fact rather than a distinct rational failure.

I am skeptical about whether the dashboard image does the work he wants it to. Can you lay out the correspondence table explicitly—what corresponds to the diet, the taste, the act of eating, the warning light, the decision to pull over, and the engine? Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion: for instance, whether belief is sufficiently voluntary to make pulling over a coherent option, whether the warning light has normative authority over belief that it lacks over action, and whether the analogy quietly smuggles in a picture of the mind as divided into a rational pilot and an irrational machine. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that epistemic akrasia is unproblematic and structurally identical to practical weakness—survives once these breaks are visible, or whether a different analogy (perhaps to perceptual illusion or to memory distortion) would be more appropriate. Please also state what would change your mind: which specific feature of belief-formation or which counter-argument about the normativity of belief would lead you to think the analogy holds firmly, and where in this audit your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
