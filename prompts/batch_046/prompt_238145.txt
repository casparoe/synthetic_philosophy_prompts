I am a graduate student in moral philosophy currently serving as a grading assistant for an introductory ethics course. Our rubric requires distinguishing, in student essays, between identifying a genuine moral bias—such as framing dependence or in-group favoritism—and merely expressing a contrary moral preference, such as weighing autonomy over welfare. I need an operational decision procedure I can apply in about five minutes per essay, grounded in the philosophical distinction between preference and bias rather than empirical psychology alone. Structure it as a sequence of no more than five tests: for each, state the precise question, what counts as a sufficient answer, and the exact point where the procedure stops with a "no" or hands the decision to human judgment because the dispute concerns values rather than detectable error. The procedure should flag cases where an essay mistakes second-order disagreement about normative standards for first-order cognitive distortion, and it should be explicit about what it cannot settle. Then apply it to three student claims: (a) that a deontologist's rejection of utilitarian calculus reveals a bias toward rule-following; (b) that stronger moral condemnation of harm to in-group members reveals a loyalty bias rather than a preference for partiality; and (c) that insisting on procedural fairness over aggregate efficiency in resource allocation is an egalitarian bias. Include one case where the procedure returns an answer that conflicts with the grader's likely intuition, and conclude by stating the procedure's hard limits.
