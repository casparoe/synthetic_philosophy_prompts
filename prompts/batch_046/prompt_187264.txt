I have been working through John Dewey’s experimentalist conception of democracy as a mode of social inquiry, and I have reconstructed the verdicts that a consistently Deweyan institutional review body might have rendered across five prior cases of democratic design. In the first case, the body endorsed a municipal water council that treated policy as a revisable experiment, with citizens jointly framing problems, testing interventions, and altering course on the basis of observed consequences, whilst explicitly rejecting a competing proposal that relied solely on expert hydrologists delivering a final plan. In the second, it rejected a direct popular referendum on complex biochemical regulation, arguing that democracy as inquiry requires the articulation and examination of problems rather than a binary vote on pre-formulated options. In the third, it approved participatory budgeting on the condition that residents could revise allocations after an initial pilot period, thereby treating fiscal distribution as an ongoing investigation rather than a single authoritative decision. In the fourth, it permitted a neighbourhood assembly to delegate technical measurement to specialists, provided that the specialists reported back in publicly accessible terms and the assembly retained authority over which problems were worth investigating. In the fifth, it rejected a predictive governance model that used statistical forecasting to pre-empt citizen deliberation about future needs, on the grounds that inquiry must begin with lived experience rather than algorithmic anticipation.

The new proposal before the body is an “algorithmic deliberation protocol” in a medium-sized city. Under this design, an artificial intelligence system aggregates resident preferences and generates a menu of policy options together with projected outcomes; citizens then vote only on whether to accept or reject the system’s recommended option, without participating in the framing of the problem, the selection of variables, or the interpretation of results. The system is fully transparent about its data sources and reasoning, includes immediate feedback loops that allow policy revision every six months, and recruits exclusively from the general adult population without vulnerable-group exclusion. The proponents argue that it preserves experimental revisability and eliminates expert domination, whilst critics contend that it severs the connection between democratic participation and problem-formation that Dewey regarded as essential.

If one wishes to remain consistent with the body’s earlier rulings, what should it decide about this protocol? Which of the five precedents offers the closest analog, and where do the prior verdicts genuinely underdetermine the outcome? In particular, if the body approves, what principle about the delegation of problem-framing would it be adopting; and if it rejects, what principle about the locus of inquiry in democratic life would it be affirming? Furthermore, identify the specific further case—perhaps involving a different form of technological mediation or a different division between framing and selection—that would most clearly distinguish the two competing interpretations of the body’s own track record.
