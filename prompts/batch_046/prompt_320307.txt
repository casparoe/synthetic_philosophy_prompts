Draft the joint statement that a Frame-Priority camp and an Inner-Content camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the location of meaning in layered message architectures, specifically in light of the frame / outer / inner message distinction associated with Gödel, Escher, Bach (GEB) and applied to the following constructed protocol.

The constructed case—call it the Reflexive Triad—works as follows. Every transmission contains three co-present layers. The frame layer specifies the interpretive rules for decoding the transmission, but it includes a reflexive clause: if the inner layer exhibits a specific structural signature (for instance, a non-decodable periodic redundancy or a syntactic anomaly that violates the frame's initial grammar), the frame updates its own rules to treat that signature as semantically weighted, thereby altering how the outer layer is parsed. The outer layer carries explicit propositional content. The inner layer consists of patterns—rhythmic, statistical, or syntactic—that are not mapped by the frame's initial rules and are not part of the outer layer's explicit propositions. The central question is whether meaning is primarily constituted by the frame (which authorizes what counts as meaningful), by the outer message (the explicit content), or by the inner message (the structural patterns that may trigger frame revision).

The two camps are defined precisely:
- Frame-Priority theorists: meaning is constituted by the frame. Without an authorizing frame, inner-layer patterns are mere noise or artifact, and outer-layer content is underdetermined. The reflexive clause is best read as the frame's self-correcting mechanism, not as evidence that inner structure carries independent semantic authority.
- Inner-Content theorists: meaning can reside in the inner layer independently of frame authorization. Frames are pragmatic conventions that can be erroneous or bypassed; the reflexive clause demonstrates that inner-layer structure causally forces frame revision, which shows that the frame is tracking meaning that already exists rather than creating it.

Produce the collaboration document in four parts.

1. Numbered agreed claims. List the claims both camps can accept, including about the formal structure of the Reflexive Triad; about what needs explaining (e.g., whether frame revision is genuinely caused by inner-layer detection or is merely correlated with it); about the distinction between misinterpretation (applying the wrong frame) and correct interpretation; and about the limits of what either camp can claim without empirical or conceptual support.

2. Disagreements table. Present the remaining disagreements in a table with two columns—Frame-Priority and Inner-Content. For each row, state the disagreement in wording that the respective camp would accept for its own view. Separate substantive disagreements from disagreements that are merely verbal or terminological, and for any verbal disagreement, state explicitly which part dissolves once terminology is fixed.

3. Cruxes. List the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. State each crux in neutral terms, without presupposing either camp's framework.

4. Evidence and predictions. For each crux, specify one concrete case, argument, or hypothetical result involving the Reflexive Triad that both camps agree is relevant (for example: a scenario in which the reflexive clause activates but the outer-layer content becomes contradictory; a comparison to biological signaling where a frame-like decoding rule is overridden by a structural pattern; or a formal analysis showing that frame revision is underdetermined by inner-layer features alone). Then record, in advance, each camp's prediction: what the Frame-Priority camp expects that case to show, and what the Inner-Content camp expects it to show.

The document should be written so that either camp could endorse the agreed-claims section and its own column of the disagreements table, with no straw-man formulations, and so that the residual disagreements are rendered as exactly as possible. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further literature.
