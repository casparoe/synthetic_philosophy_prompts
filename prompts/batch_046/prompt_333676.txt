My friend and I spent much of last evening arguing about Moritz Schlick’s account of life’s meaning and reached an impasse that feels structurally odd: we both accept his distinction between purpose-driven activity and activity valued for its own sake, and we both agree that modern civilization tends to instrumentalize human existence, yet we cannot agree on whether his conclusion is substantive or dissolutive. I maintain that Schlick offers a genuine axiological claim—that significance resides in play-like engagement and is eroded not by the absence of divine teleology but by the social compulsion to treat every action as a means—while my friend insists he is merely performing conceptual therapy, redefining “meaning” away from the metaphysical question rather than answering it. The debate stalls because every example we invoke—youthful sport versus adult labor, artistic creation versus factory production—seems to support both readings at once. Is this disagreement verbal, turning on ambiguous uses of “meaning” and “value”? Or does it conceal a substantive dispute about whether teleology is a necessary condition for significance? Perhaps we are answering different questions under one heading: one normative, about how life ought to be structured, and one descriptive, about the psychology of satisfaction. Diagnose the stall using what Schlick actually says about play, purpose, and the corruption of activity by civilization, not by surveying secondary literature. Tell me whether the disagreement concerns hidden empirical claims about motivation, divergent standards for what counts as a successful answer, bedrock intuitions about naturalness that neither side can argue for, or an ill-posed original question. And given your diagnosis, what—an empirical result, a conceptual distinction, or a deliberate change of question—would actually count as progress?
