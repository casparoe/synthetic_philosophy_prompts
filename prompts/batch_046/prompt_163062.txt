I'm preparing a seventy-five-minute philosophy seminar for advanced undergraduates who have encountered Hart's theory of legal rules but have no background in formal logic or constitutional law. The session aims to teach the specific problem of legal self-reference that arises in self-amending constitutional provisions—not as a variant of logical paradox, but as a structural feature of normative authority. To ground the discussion, I intend to use a hypothetical constitutional clause, Article Ω, which permits constitutional change exclusively through an amendment procedure whose own legal authority is defined solely within Article Ω itself.

Rather than explaining the concept to me, I need a teaching plan that lays out the dependency structure: which distinctions—such as that between a rule's content and its conditions of validity, or between a textual provision and the social practice that recognizes it—must be secured before self-reference becomes genuinely puzzling rather than merely circular; what Article Ω must include and what it must deliberately omit so that it functions as a carrying example rather than an obvious contradiction or an empty tautology; the sequence of misconceptions students typically generate—conflating logical paradox with legal indeterminacy, assuming self-reference necessarily yields contradiction, treating the rule of recognition as a single self-referential sentence rather than an institutional practice, and importing physical-world analogies of reflexivity that misapply to normative systems—and the precise point in the arc where each should be anticipated and defused; an explicit strategy for distinguishing this problem from the liar paradox without dismissing the analogy outright; what technical or historical material—perhaps Ross's formal paradox in full symbolic detail, or extensive comparative constitutional examples—I should openly exclude at this level and name as excluded; a check-for-understanding question that distinguishes genuine comprehension of the structural dependency from the superficial repetition that laws can amend themselves; and finally, please state clearly what argument or empirical finding about legal interpretation or institutional practice would cause you to revise your assessment that this pedagogical sequence is sound, and indicate where in the reasoning the failure risk is highest.
