I am designing a decision-tree handout for an applied ethics seminar focused on public commemoration, and I want to avoid the usual lists of named philosophical positions. Instead, I need a taxonomy—built as a branching flowchart of yes/no and either/or claims—that sorts out the actual stances people take when institutions must decide what to do with monuments, building names, and curricula honoring figures such as Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus. The diagnostic branches should be phrased as concrete commitments rather than theoretical labels: for example, whether a figure's achievements and moral crimes belong to a single evaluative balance sheet or to incommensurable registers; whether the "man of his time" defense operates as an excuse that reduces blame, as an explanatory frame that separates understanding from endorsement, or as an illegitimate evasion; and whether removing a name is primarily an act of moral accounting, an institutional message about present values, or a distortion of historical complexity.

The tree should contain exactly six to eight leaves, with the following positions required somewhere in the structure: a strict contextualist position that suspends moral evaluation in favor of historical explanation; a separation-of-registers position that insists achievements and wrongs must both be displayed but never weighed against each other; a cumulative-balance position that allows serious wrongs to be outweighed by historical benefits; a moral-disqualification position that holds certain wrongs permanently bar a figure from public honor regardless of achievement; and an institutional-voice position that treats the question as being about what the institution communicates now rather than about the figure's character. Each branch should be labeled with the claim that splits its siblings, not with the name of the view that sits at the leaf. You should also identify at least one logical combination that remains unoccupied—perhaps a stance that applies present-day moral standards rigorously yet treats "man of his time" as a full moral excuse for every wrong—and explain whether that vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

For the concrete cases, the taxonomy must reference specific scenarios: whether retaining Jefferson's name on a public university necessarily endorses slaveholding; whether commemorating Churchill's wartime leadership can be done without honoring his imperial racial policies; and whether teaching Columbus's navigational achievements requires glorifying the enslavement and demographic collapse that followed. Each leaf should conclude with one sentence stating its most awkward practical commitment—something a curator or trustee adopting that stance would have to explain to affected communities. The instrument is meant for students who do not already know the philosophical vocabulary, so no answer should depend on recognizing terms like presentism or moral luck; answer patterns should map onto positions solely through the concrete judgments they entail.
