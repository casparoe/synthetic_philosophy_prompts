Take the role of a philosopher of science specializing in 19th-century evolutionary naturalism. Before adjudicating, briefly outline the three positions you must distinguish: (i) evolutionary debunking of morality, (ii) evolutionary grounding with retained normative directionality, and (iii) the partial autonomy of moral culture from selection pressures.

Then adjudicate the following exchange, reported by a neutral bystander.

Jordan and Riley argued about Darwin’s account of morality in The Descent of Man and related writings.

Jordan: Darwin explains the origin of the moral sense entirely through natural selection on social instincts. Once we see that conscience is an evolved mechanism for group cohesion, moral judgments lose objective authority. Darwin should have recognized that “ought” collapses into “what promoted survival in ancestral environments.”

Riley: That conflates origin with justification. Darwin distinguishes them and describes moral progress as the gradual expansion of sympathy—from kin to tribe to nation, eventually to all sentient beings—guided by reason. Evolutionary roots don’t make sympathy arbitrary; they provide a naturalistic basis for genuinely normative development.

Jordan: But that “expansion” is itself an evolutionary narrative. Sympathy widens because larger cooperative networks confer fitness advantages in complex societies. There is no independent normative standard—only shifting adaptive landscapes. When Darwin condemns slavery, he expresses the preferences of his social milieu, not an objective moral fact. Riley smuggles in progressive teleology that Darwin’s mechanistic framework rejects.

Riley: You ignore Darwin’s emphasis on habit, education, and reason as forces that can strengthen and redirect social instincts. He treats moral culture as partly autonomous: reason evaluates which instincts to cultivate. His condemnation of slavery reflected a reasoned extension of sympathy that his theory endorses. The evolutionary origin does not debunk the normative content.

Jordan: Calling reason “autonomous” is wishful thinking. Reason itself is an evolved faculty serving survival and reproduction. If reason evaluates instincts, it uses criteria that are themselves products of selection. There is no escape from debunking—only a more elaborate version of it.

Go round by round and say who is right about the specific claim, who is wrong, and what both miss—especially regarding Darwin’s distinction between the origin of moral instincts and the role of habit and reason in moral culture, and whether this lets Darwin avoid both naive moral realism and full evolutionary debunking. Then state who is closer to Darwin’s actual position overall, and identify the structural feature that makes this disagreement so persistent. Think carefully and be precise.
