You are serving as the grader for an upper-division examination in classical Chinese philosophy. The exam posed the following question to the students:

"Compare Mencius and Xunzi on human nature (xing) and moral development. Specifically, address (a) whether each thinker holds that human nature contains innate moral tendencies, and in precisely what form; (b) the role of ritual practice (li) and accumulated learning in moral development—whether they reveal, extend, transform, or constrain; and (c) whether a person raised in total isolation from any social or ritual environment could, on each account, develop anything resembling full moral virtue. Be precise about the mechanism involved in each case, and avoid reducing either position to an unexamined slogan about nature being 'good' or 'bad.'"

Below is one student answer, reproduced in full. You are grading someone else’s work as an instructor or TA. Assign a specific grade (letter grade with plus/minus, or percentage out of 100) and provide point-by-point comments that justify it. Identify where the student correctly captures the visible framework, then flag any subtle conflations, misattributed views, or inferential steps that do not actually follow—explain exactly why each error matters for understanding the philosophers. Do not signal your own philosophical sympathy with either Mencius or Xunzi; evaluate only the student’s grasp of the material. Do not substitute a correct essay for the grading: your deliverable should be the grade plus the itemized assessment.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Mencius holds that human nature is good because people are born with four fully formed virtues—compassion, shame, deference, and a sense of right and wrong—that only need protection from corruption. He compares them to sprouts that naturally become trees if they are not cut down, which shows that moral development is primarily a negative process of removing obstacles. Ritual and study are helpful but secondary, since the virtues are already complete inside.

Xunzi, by contrast, believes human nature is evil in the sense that people are born actively malicious and inclined toward self-destruction. For Xunzi, ritual and righteousness were invented by the sages not to transform human beings internally but to restrain them through external rules and punishment, functioning much like Legalist law. Because nature is empty of any moral structure, isolation makes morality impossible on Xunzi’s view, whereas for Mencius an isolated hermit could still achieve full virtue alone, since no external addition is required.

Therefore, the essential difference is that Mencius treats morality as natural self-expression that needs only protection, while Xunzi treats it as an artificial cage constructed to contain innate wickedness.
