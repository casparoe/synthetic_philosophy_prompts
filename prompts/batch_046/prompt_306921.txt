My proposed project concerns the concept of collapse itself, specifically in relation to three well-known historical cases: the disruptions of the Late Bronze Age, the transformation of Classic Maya polities, and the deforestation and demographic decline on Easter Island. In my former scientific work, we carefully distinguished a system that had undergone a critical transition to an alternative stable state—often simpler, with lower energy throughput and reduced structural complexity—from a system that had genuinely lost all functional integrity. Historians and archaeologists, however, routinely describe all three of the above cases as “collapses,” implying catastrophic failure, disappearance of civilization, or breakdown of social order. Yet in each instance, human populations persisted, some institutional forms survived in altered shapes, and ecological conditions eventually reached a new equilibrium rather than remaining in ruins. This suggests a conceptual ambiguity: are we describing an objective trajectory of societal change, or are we smuggling in a teleological assumption that societies have a single proper form they ought to maintain?
