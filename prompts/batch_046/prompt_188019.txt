I had a lengthy argument with a friend last night about whether being a hypocrite actually strips you of the standing to blame others, and I am trying to work out what I genuinely think rather than just repeating the usual lines about people in glass houses. Here are my verdicts on six concrete situations; I want to know whether there is a single principle that delivers all of them, or whether I have to sacrifice one or two for the sake of coherence.

First, a surgeon who once operated while exhausted and caused harm — for which she was disciplined and has since undergone mandatory retraining — now reports a junior colleague for operating while visibly fatigued. I think she retains full standing; her past failure, properly acknowledged and repaired, gives her criticism weight rather than undermining it.

Second, a local MP who has never paid council tax on his second home publicly condemns a constituent for the same omission. He has never acknowledged it. I think he has lost his standing completely; the hypocrisy is direct, ongoing, and unaddressed.

Third, a philosopher who writes on the ethics of deception but once told a compassionate lie to a dying relative now blames a graduate student for fabricating data. I think her standing is intact; the lie was minor, domain-distant, and morally justified, whereas data fabrication violates a specific institutional duty.

Fourth, a climate activist who takes four long-haul flights a year for holidays criticises a neighbour for driving a petrol car to work because there is no public transport. I think this is murky: the activist's behaviour is worse in aggregate, yet the neighbour's choice is unnecessary within a local system. I am unsure whether the activist retains partial standing, none, or whether the comparison should be ignored altogether.

Fifth, a victim of burglary who has never stolen anything blames the burglar. Clearly fine — standing is unquestioned.

Sixth, a former embezzler who served his sentence, made restitution, and was formally welcomed back into his professional community now blames a younger colleague for a similar offence. Here I hesitate: part of me says he has earned the right to speak through repair, but another part feels that having committed the very wrong removes the moral position regardless of subsequent forgiveness. I go back and forth.

Is there an account of standing-to-blame that captures these six — perhaps one that distinguishes between repaired failure in the same domain, unacknowledged ongoing complicity, domain-distant minor transgression, structural versus individual contribution, and formal moral restoration? Or do I need to overturn one of my verdicts — most likely the surgeon or the embezzler — to avoid an ad hoc list? Please state the principle precisely, show what it predicts for two cases I have not mentioned (for instance, a recovered addict who advises on drug policy while secretly using, and a teacher who was once bullied at school now blaming a student bully), and be honest about which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate without distortion. I am not asking for a literature survey; I want a defended agenda for what actually matters in the concept of standing, so that I can decide whether my argument with my friend was about a real disagreement or just about which examples we picked.
