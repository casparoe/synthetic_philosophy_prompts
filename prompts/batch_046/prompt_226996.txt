As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I have been trying to map the philosophy-of-emotion debate onto system architectures I understand: low-level sensor telemetry, interpreted event logs, and high-level diagnostic judgments that assign normative weight. The literature I have encountered frames the dispute over what emotions actually are in three broad camps—cognitive judgments (emotions are evaluative appraisals that can be true or false), perceptual representations (they are non-conceptual registrations of bodily or environmental conditions), and bare phenomenal feelings (they are qualitative experiences with no essential intentionality or normative content). Write an essay that engages this controversy directly, weighing the strongest considerations for each view.

Your essay should address specific pressure points: for instance, whether recalcitrant emotion—feeling afraid while sincerely judging that one is safe—undermines the judgment theory; whether emotional behavior in pre-linguistic infants and non-human animals shows that conceptual appraisal is unnecessary; and whether emotions can be directed at particular objects in a way that raw feelings apparently cannot. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it—perhaps that one layer is primary and the others derivative, that emotions are irreducibly hybrid, or that the tripartite taxonomy collapses once we distinguish functional role from phenomenology. You may use the distributed-system analogy—distinguishing raw signal, interpreted log entry, and alert classification—to clarify what is at stake in assigning emotions to one category rather than another, but do not let the metaphor substitute for philosophical argument; the verdict should stand on its own reasoning.
