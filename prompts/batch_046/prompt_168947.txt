Please describe the most important implications of tranquilism — the position that only the absence of craving and suffering possesses intrinsic moral value, whilst the presence of pleasure, desire-satisfaction, or flourishing contributes nothing — for the ethical assessment of affective nullification interventions in psychiatric practice. By affective nullification I mean a hypothetical therapeutic approach that does not restore positive affect or fulfil desires, but permanently suppresses both aversive craving and positive desire, leaving the patient in a sustained state of tranquil absence without either distress or enjoyment. I want you to consider, in particular: first, whether such an outcome ought to be classified as a complete therapeutic success or as a subtle form of harm, given that it eliminates suffering but also removes the structural conditions for flourishing; second, how tranquilism distinguishes morally between this state of conscious tranquil absence and death, since both entail the absence of suffering yet differ in the persistence of subjective awareness; and third, whether a clinician operating under tranquilist commitments should prefer nullification over conventional treatments that preserve the capacity for desire but carry a risk of relapse into suffering. Please treat this as a conceptual exercise rather than an empirical review of existing medical procedures, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
