What must any adequate theory of moral worth deliver to resolve the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem? Rather than another taxonomy of motives, I want the full desiderata for an account that applies even to agents who analyse their own reasons through second-order decision procedures rather than transparent introspection.

Specify the requirements: the canonical cases any theory must classify correctly (the prudent merchant charging fair prices, the agent who applies a moral rule for coherence with her self-image, the case where the right act is performed but only because an instrumental reason happens to align); the distinctions it must preserve, such as between being moved by a moral reason and merely having it in one's causal history, or between first-order moral belief and meta-level endorsement; and any constraints from moral psychology or decision theory it cannot contradict. For each, note whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Then identify any jointly unsatisfiable subset — especially if the demand that moral worth be fully transparent to the agent conflicts with allowing motivation that is partly instrumental — and say which single desideratum eliminates the most popular theories. Briefly analyse how Kantian, Humean, and decision-theoretic coherence accounts fare against the list. I am drafting a seminar outline, not asking for references.
