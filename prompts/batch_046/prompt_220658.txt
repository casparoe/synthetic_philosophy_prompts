On the other hand, I want to examine a contrasting framework—call it heterarchical meta-representation—which denies that self-awareness requires any centrally closed loop; instead, it proposes that conscious self-reference emerges from distributed, mutually correcting sub-systems that generate partial self-models in perpetual competition, never achieving the syntactic closure that Gödel's proof exhibits but nonetheless sustaining a functional sense of self.

Please compare these two accounts—Hofstadter's strange-loop interpretation of the Gödelian analogy and the heterarchical meta-representational alternative—focusing specifically on three issues. Second, evaluate whether either account can preserve the phenomenological unity of the self without collapsing into either Cartesian dualism or an eliminativist denial of subjective experience. Third, consider which framework better respects the distinction between propositional knowledge-that and practical knowledge-how in self-cognition, and whether that distinction undermines the formal analogy altogether. I do not need a bibliography or list of further readings; I am interested purely in the conceptual architecture of the comparison.
