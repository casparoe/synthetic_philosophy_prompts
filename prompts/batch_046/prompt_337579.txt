As a retired experimental physicist now studying classical Indian epistemology, I am struck by how closely the Nyāya defense of the pramāṇas resembles the problem of calibrating instruments without an external standard. I want to run a ninety-minute seminar exercise for six to eight philosophy-of-science graduate students in which they live through that calibration loop before anyone names it. Design a structured activity, with explicit rules and assigned roles, in which the group must determine the state of a sealed system using four distinct information channels that analogize to perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. The rules should be arranged so that checking the reliability of one channel necessarily invokes another, producing a regress that blocks unanimous agreement on any foundational reading. The participants should construct the sequence of checks themselves and only recognize the circular dependency near the end. Include a timeline, materials, and an incentive structure that rewards convergence but makes convergence impossible without breaking a rule. Then provide a debrief that maps each stage onto the corresponding pramāṇa relation, notes what will predictably fail the first time the exercise is run and how to manage it, and states honestly which feature of the Nyāya resolution—specifically, the claim that each pramāṇa is authoritative within its own proper domain and does not require external validation—the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply in discussion afterward. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the design should stand on its own.
