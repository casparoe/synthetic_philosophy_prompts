I'm the clinical AI governance lead for a multi-site hospital network, and every quarter I must sign off on whether each diagnostic AI tool remains in unrestricted use, is restricted to advisory-only mode, or is suspended. The governing distinction is between process-based supervision—auditing data provenance, clinician-in-the-loop protocols, drift monitoring, and reasoning-pathway documentation—and outcome-based supervision—tracking error rates, demographic parity, harm clusters, and predictive accuracy. I need a reusable decision procedure my audit team can run on any tool without philosophical training: ordered questions in sequence, conditions that count as a sufficient answer at each step, explicit hard-stop points, and clearly marked flags where judgment cannot be replaced by computation. The procedure must include a regress-breaking clause that addresses the circular dependency between process verification and outcome signals—specifying which audit layer is treated as primitive when the two conflict—and it must incorporate a test for process modal fragility: whether the system’s reasoning pathway remains invariant under counterfactual perturbations to clinically irrelevant input features, and whether this test is mandatory or optional depending on the clinical stakes.

Ground the procedure in the actual philosophical distinction between process and outcome evaluation, but translate it into operational language. Apply it to three cases: (1) an imaging AI with perfect demographic parity and low error rates, but an unaudited training dataset with unreported confounders; (2) a sepsis predictor with fully documented data provenance, complete clinician-override logs, and transparent feature weights, but a recent spike in false negatives confined to one ethnic subgroup that the process audit attributes to a documented, non-discriminatory data-sparsity mechanism; and (3) a dermatology classifier that passes all process checks and shows stable reasoning pathways under perturbation, but is supplied by a vendor that refuses disclosure of the feature-extraction architecture.

In your applications, include at least one case where the procedure recommends suspension or restriction despite strong outcome metrics that my clinical staff will want to keep, and one case where it permits continued use despite a documented outcome disparity. Clearly identify which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Finally, state honestly what the procedure cannot settle—for example, whether a sufficiently robust process can ever normatively excuse a persistent bad outcome, or whether any outcome failure retroactively invalidates the process audit—and separate what is settled in the philosophy of AI supervision from what remains genuinely contested among practitioners and theorists, without presenting any contested view, such as whether process integrity carries independent normative weight or is merely instrumentally valuable, as if it were consensus.
