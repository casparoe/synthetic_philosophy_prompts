I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have taken up philosophy of logic and mathematics with some seriousness. I now sit on my university’s interdisciplinary curriculum committee. A colleague in cognitive science has proposed a required general-education course titled “Gödel’s Theorem and the Non-Mechanical Mind,” whose central claim is that Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem proves human mathematical intuition necessarily transcends any formal algorithmic system, and therefore that artificial general intelligence is impossible in principle. Several committee members with scientific backgrounds are skeptical of this interpretation, yet they are unsure whether their objection is philosophical or merely disciplinary territoriality; others worry that rejecting the proposal amounts to enforcing a materialist orthodoxy. I have been asked to draft the committee’s decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—for circulation before next Thursday’s vote. The memo must recommend a concrete, actionable course of action and provide reasoning the faculty can actually respond to. Specifically, it should distinguish precisely what Gödel’s theorems establish about formal systems possessing recursive axiomatizability, sufficient arithmetic strength, and appropriate consistency conditions from what they do not establish about minds, brains, or machines in general. It should engage seriously with the Lucas-Penrose line of reasoning as the strongest argument for the course’s claim, explaining exactly where that reasoning oversteps Gödel’s results without misrepresenting the theorems themselves. If the recommendation is conditional approval, the conditions must specify concrete curricular requirements—such as mandatory modules on formal-system prerequisites or on the distinction between syntactic incompleteness and epistemic transcendence—rather than vague cautions. The memo should treat the concern about disciplinary bias honestly and conclude with a clear bottom line the committee can vote on.
