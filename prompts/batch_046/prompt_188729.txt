I have been trying to understand why the Löbian obstacle is not treated as a straightforward impossibility result for reflective artificial agents. As I understand it, if an agent's belief system satisfies the Hilbert-Bernays derivability conditions and can represent its own proof predicate, then adopting the self-trust principle—if my future self proves φ, then φ is true—allows the derivation of every sentence via Löb's theorem, rendering the system inconsistent. This suggests an exhaustive disjunction: either the agent abandons at least one derivability condition, thereby forfeiting standard deductive closure, or it retains them and must reject all forms of epistemic deference to its successors. Yet discussions of staged self-modification and reflective oracles often proceed as if partitioning the proof predicate across temporal stages could navigate this constraint. But if the derivability conditions must hold globally for the agent to be rational, wouldn't any such partitioning merely relocate the contradiction rather than resolve it? Where exactly does this reasoning go wrong—does the obstacle apply only to a specific modal framing of self-trust, or is there a substantive relaxation of the derivability conditions that preserves recursive reasoning without triggering the collapse?
