Write a poem of exactly fourteen lines in unrhymed iambic pentameter—blank verse—structured as a single grammatical sentence that does not reach a full stop until the final word of line 14. The speaker should be a normative reason itself—specifically, the reason not to desecrate an unmarked grave in a world from which all human reasoners have disappeared—not personified as a ghost or agent, but rendered as a structural feature of the situation that persists without an audience. Organize the poem around a volta between lines 8 and 9: in the first eight lines, establish the reason’s content and weight (the wrongness remains intact, the soil is undisturbed, nothing violates it) while deliberately withholding any “for whom” it binds; at line 9, executed through a sharp enjambment from line 8, turn to the problem that without an addressee the reason cannot call, command, or be answered, yet something about its normative pull survives that absence. The philosophical rendering should be precise enough that someone familiar with debates about free-floating rationales—reasons that retain force without reasoners—would recognize the position, while the verse should work as poetry for a reader unfamiliar with the concept. After the poem, append a brief note of no more than one hundred words identifying where the poem takes liberties with the idea of agent-independent reasons or relies on a contested reading—particularly on whether normative force requires an essential relation to a potential subject. Conclude by stating what argument or consideration would lead you to change your mind: for instance, a convincing account showing that all normative relations are necessarily directed at a reasoner, or that reasons are reducible to properties of mental states, which would collapse the possibility of genuinely free-floating rationales.
