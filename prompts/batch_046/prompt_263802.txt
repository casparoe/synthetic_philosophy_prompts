I am a retired experimental biologist who, in retirement, has taken up philosophy of mind with some seriousness, and I now consult informally for a neuropsychology research group. I have been thinking about epiphenomenalism and the difficulty—often discussed as the paradox of phenomenal judgment—that if phenomenal states are causally inert, then our first-person reports about them seem inexplicable, since the reports are physically caused yet purportedly about something outside the causal chain. Rather than leave this in abstraction, I want it operationalized.

You should act as an expert philosopher of mind and methodological consultant with experience translating metaphysical claims into empirical audit rules. Before presenting your final protocol, please take a moment to think carefully about how causal-exclusion reasoning can be turned into a practical checklist without collapsing into behaviorism or mysticism, and briefly brainstorm the conceptual tensions—especially around confabulation and qualitative excess—that any usable translation must preserve. My deliverable should be a reusable decision procedure, which I will call the Phenomenal Report Admissibility Protocol (PRAP), that a research data auditor—holding a master’s-level neuroscience background but no philosophy doctorate—can run repeatedly in about fifteen minutes per case. The auditor evaluates whether a subject’s verbal report about a subjective experience should be admitted as valid phenomenal data in a study protocol. The stakes are moderate (study validity, not clinical care); the principal investigator must sign off whenever the decision turns on values rather than facts; and the procedure must include ordered questions, explicit criteria for sufficient answers, clear stopping points yielding a “do not admit” result, and marked handoff points to the PI.

The PRAP must preserve two philosophical distinctions in practical form: first, the difference between reports produced through an intact direct sensory pathway and those arising from confabulatory reconstruction; second, what I will call phenomenal leakage—descriptive qualitative details in the report that exceed the information content of the known physical stimulus and its neural correlates, implying tracking of something beyond the physical causal chain. Present the protocol as a stepwise flow in words, suitable for a printed checklist. Then apply it explicitly to three cases: (1) a standard psychophysics participant who reports a crimson afterimage lasting about two seconds after a bright green stimulus, with full sensory pathway integrity; (2) a patient with partial blindsight in the left hemifield who, during a forced-choice task, reports “a vague feeling that something is present on the left,” with documented absence of primary visual cortex activation in that field—design the protocol so that this case yields a negative, exclusionary outcome, since a researcher eager to include subjective data would find that result unwelcome; and (3) a deeply sedated patient with no external emotional stimulus who spontaneously describes an intense affective state “like profound grief” but denies all perceptual content. Finally, state clearly what the PRAP cannot do—its blind spots, risks of false positives and false negatives, and the philosophical commitments it smuggles in despite its practical framing. Keep the language precise but accessible to someone without formal philosophical training.
