I'm writing a novel where one character is convinced her recurring nightmare about drowning is literally an experience she lived through in some past life, and her therapist keeps citing philosophy of mind to say that's incoherent — dreams aren't experiences, they're just playback. I went deep into the Malcolm-Dennett rabbit hole and I'm kind of stuck. Malcolm's argument that you can't experience something without being able to report it in the present tense makes intuitive sense, but Dennett's cassette theory — that the dream narrative gets assembled at the moment of waking, not lived through during sleep — feels like it's solving the wrong problem. What's frustrating is that every discussion I've had with writer friends and one philosophy grad student stalls in the exact same spot: one camp points to the vivid phenomenalogical reality of being-in-the-dream, the other points to the absence of concurrent access or verification, and nobody shifts an inch.

I'm wondering if this stalemate is even about facts. Maybe it's a verbal disagreement about what counts as an 'experience' — whether it needs cognitive accessability or just phenomenal presence — but both sides talk past each other because they load different conditions into the same word. Or is there a hidden empirical question about whether there's any neural signature of conscious experience during REM that's independent of waking recall? But I also started thinking the real axis might be something like 'phenomenal ownership' — whether there's actually a subject undergoing the dream in real time — which neither Malcolm's reportability condition nor Dennett's playback model really addresses. If that's the hidden disagreement, does it mean the whole debate is misframed?
