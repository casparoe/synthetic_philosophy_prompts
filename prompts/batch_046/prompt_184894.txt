I spent my career as an evolutionary ecologist studying phenotypic plasticity, reaction norms, and niche construction in non-model organisms, and since retiring I have turned to philosophy, particularly to the debate over whether documented cross-cultural variation in folk moral judgment—especially the divergence between WEIRD populations and small-scale societies—poses a genuine challenge to moral objectivity. I ask you to restate the two principal positions in that debate—the universalist claim that observed variation is phenotypic noise around a stable, species-typical moral genotype, and the relativist claim that it reflects deep, allopatric divergence with no underlying universal—using exclusively the conceptual vocabulary I actually used in my research: adaptive landscapes, founder effects, sampling bias in ecological field studies, niche construction, canalization versus plasticity, and reaction norms. For each position, provide an explicit mapping of its philosophical claims onto these biological terms. Then deliver a residue report: identify which philosophical distinctions—such as that between descriptive regularity and normative justification, or between causal explanation and rational warrant—have no genuine counterpart in this scientific vocabulary; state whether their absence indicates a limitation of the biological framework or reveals that those distinctions were carrying argumentative weight that the empirical data alone do not bear; and assess whether introducing a founder-effect model of normative transmission, in which extreme WEIRD moral phenotypes arise from institutional demographic bottlenecks rather than from ecological adaptation, introduces anything philosophically substantive that the standard universalism-versus-relativism framing overlooks. I am working without access to external literature, so please do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want to see what survives pure conceptual translation.
