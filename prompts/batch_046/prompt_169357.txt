In my school's advanced philosophy seminar we have spent the past term on Strawson's distinction between reactive attitudes and the objective attitude, with particular attention to whether determinism undermines moral responsibility. A specific disagreement has persisted for months without resolution, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on which side is right.

The dispute concerns whether determinism undermines not only personal resentment and gratitude but also what I will call institutional reactive attitudes—the structured practices of blame, accountability, and corrective expectation maintained by courts and communities on behalf of victims who never encounter the offender. One camp holds that these institutional practices are continuous with face-to-face reactive attitudes and therefore share the same immunity to determinism: they are embedded social practices that do not depend on a metaphysical belief in libertarian freedom. The opposing camp argues that institutional reactive attitudes implicitly presuppose that offenders are genuinely open to choice in a strong sense; if causal determinism is correct, they claim, these practices should be replaced by purely corrective or therapeutic mechanisms—Strawson's objective attitude—applied systematically rather than selectively.

We agree on the empirical premises: that human behavior is causally influenced by genetics and environment, that social accountability sometimes rehabilitates, and that direct resentment is psychologically costly. Yet we disagree about whether the rational vulnerability of an attitude depends on its being first-person or institutional, and about whether the objective attitude can be adopted piecemeal or must be adopted wholesale. One student suggests our stalemate is verbal: we use responsible to mean either embedded in a practice of accountability or deserving of suffering for freely willed wrongs. Another suggests we are answering different questions—one about the evolutionary function of blame, the other about its normative warrant—under the single heading of moral responsibility.

Can you diagnose the actual structure of this disagreement? Is it a hidden empirical dispute about whether reactive attitudes can survive explicit determinist belief, or about whether institutional analogues share the psychological structure of personal ones? Is it substantive but masked by ambiguous terminology? Or are the sides genuinely addressing different problems—psychological explanation versus normative justification—while treating them as one? Is there a hidden formal question about what could have done otherwise means in institutional contexts? Given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress: a precise conceptual distinction between participant and institutional attitudes, an empirical finding about how belief in determinism affects blame behavior, a formal clarification of the conditions for appropriate accountability, or an argument that the framing itself is confused and should be abandoned? I ask for the diagnosis and the conditions of progress, not a survey of interpretations or a list of further readings.
