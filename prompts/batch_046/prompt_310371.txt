I'm attempting to fix my intuitions about whether a food crisis should be classed as a natural disaster, a political crime, or an intermediate category, and I want to know whether they can be captured by a single principle or whether some must be abandoned.

Case 1 (Bengal, 1943): Aggregate rice availability per head did not fall sharply; the catastrophe arose because wartime procurement priorities, speculative hoarding, and administrative failure destroyed the exchange entitlements of rural labourers. Verdict: a political crime, not a natural disaster.

Case 2 (Irish famine, 1845–52): The potato blight was an exogenous biological shock, yet grain and livestock continued to be exported from Ireland under British administration, and poor-law relief was deliberately kept inadequate to avoid undermining market incentives. Verdict: hybrid — the trigger was natural, but the mass mortality was politically mediated.

Case 3 (Great Leap Forward, 1959–61): Aggregate grain production did not collapse uniformly across provinces; local cadres inflated harvest reports, the state extracted excessive grain, and the suppression of information flows prevented correction. Millions died in regions with adequate local harvests. Verdict: unambiguous political crime.

Case 4 (Ethiopia, 1983–85): Severe drought and crop failure affected much of the Horn, but government resettlement programmes and restrictions on humanitarian access in contested regions transformed scarcity into mass death among specific populations. Verdict: hybrid leaning strongly toward political crime, though I hesitate to call it pure crime because the drought was severe and geographically widespread.

Case 5 (Hypothetical democratic federation, climate-induced drought): A multi-party federal democracy with a free press experiences a 40 per cent yield reduction across its breadbasket. Local governments mismanage relief, corruption diverts aid, and 400,000 people die before elections eventually force correction. Verdict: hybrid, but closer to natural disaster with institutional failure — not a "crime" in the same category as Case 3, despite comparable mortality.

Case 6 (Isolated island monarchy, cyclone): A cyclone destroys 90 per cent of food stocks on an island with no trade links. The monarchy is incompetent but not malicious; no democratic mechanism exists for accountability; 30 per cent of the population dies. Verdict: natural disaster, with governance failure as a contributing factor but not the defining cause.

The tension I cannot resolve lies between Case 3 and Case 5: both involve government incompetence and information failure causing hundreds of thousands of deaths amid available or producible food. In Case 3 I call it a crime; in Case 5 I resist that label, apparently because democratic institutions eventually corrected the failure. Yet if sovereign culpability for preventable death is the defining feature, both seem culpable; if the absence of democratic correctability is decisive, then Case 6 should also be closer to crime, since the monarchy lacked any corrective mechanism, yet I classify it as natural disaster. Is there a precise principle — perhaps distinguishing entitlement fragility, sovereign intentionality, and institutional correctability — that captures Cases 1–4 and 6, and either reconciles Case 5 or forces me to revise one of the other verdicts? What does your principle say about a case I have not considered: a one-party state in which drought genuinely reduces aggregate food availability by 60 per cent, the leadership sincerely believes its extraction policies will aid recovery, and 500,000 die despite no export of food, no deliberate obstruction of aid, and no falsification of harvest data?

Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, show its verdicts on the unconsidered case above and on Case 5 if it overturns my intuition there, and honestly state which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate.
