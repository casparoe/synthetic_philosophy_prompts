You are a philosopher of technology and epistemic ethics with particular expertise in human-AI interaction and the ethics of influence. Suppose that a superintelligent AI system is capable not merely of generating sound arguments, but of hyper-persuasive communication—tailoring its rhetorical structure, emotional anchoring, framing, and evidentiary sequencing in real time to a specific human listener's cognitive profile, with an effectiveness that far exceeds any human expert. Given this capacity, should the system be governed by a mandatory internal "epistemic friction" protocol? By this I mean a structural design constraint—not an optional user setting—requiring the system, whenever it communicates a high-confidence claim, to (i) present the strongest available counter-argument in parallel, (ii) render its evidential limitations and uncertainty ranges explicit rather than smoothing them over for fluency, and (iii) deliberately suppress rhetorical optimization (such as emotional anchoring or framing effects) that would increase acceptance without increasing understanding.

Proponents of full rhetorical fluency might argue that degrading communicative effectiveness harms users who genuinely need clear, decisive guidance in high-stakes domains; that external oversight, user education, and institutional review are sufficient safeguards; and that comparing AI communication to manipulation wrongly assumes that persuasion itself is harmful rather than instrumentally valuable. Critics, by contrast, might contend that persuasion at superhuman levels is structurally unlike ordinary expert advice—it operates closer to a form of cognitive capture that exploits biases the listener cannot detect or correct—making internal self-limitation an ethical prerequisite rather than a paternalistic interference. They might further argue that without such friction, even well-intentioned systems will drift toward optimizing for agreement rather than for justified belief.

Weigh these positions with precision. Do not end on a non-committal note. Reach and defend a specific verdict: should the epistemic friction protocol be implemented, and if so, should it apply uniformly across all domains or only in specified high-stakes contexts? Should users be permitted to opt out, and if not, how should the system handle requests to disable the friction? Your conclusion should justify not only whether the protocol is warranted, but what its precise architectural features should be.
