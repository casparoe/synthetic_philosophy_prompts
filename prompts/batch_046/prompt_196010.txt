As a software engineer working on distributed systems, I have no formal background in philosophy, yet I have gradually adopted a position I want tested rigorously: that republican liberty—conceived not as the absence of interference but as the absence of arbitrary domination—offers the correct normative framework for evaluating centralized algorithmic curation, specifically the opaque ranking systems that govern information visibility on large platforms. My reasoning is that a platform can fully respect negative liberty, in the familiar sense of not blocking or censoring particular content, while still exercising arbitrary power over whether that content reaches an audience, because the ranking function is unilaterally changeable, opaque, and unaccountable to users. This, I contend, is domination in the republican sense, and the appropriate remedy is not merely more negative freedom—fewer direct prohibitions—but structural safeguards such as mandatory transparency, participatory audit rights, and binding constraints on unilateral algorithmic updates.

Please map what would genuinely count against this position. I am not asking for standard objections that can be dissolved by redefining terms; I want the specific observations, conceptual distinctions, and empirical results that would weaken or defeat the republican framing in this domain. In particular: which findings about how users actually experience algorithmic ranking—perhaps showing that users perceive opacity as benign dependency rather than as subordination—would undermine the domination claim? Which distinctions within republican theory itself, such as between domination and mere influence or dependency, threaten to show that platform power is not arbitrary in the relevant sense? What results from computer science or governance studies about the feasibility of transparent machine-learning systems, or about the performance costs of participatory audit, would count as fatal versus merely awkward? Furthermore, does the position risk collapsing into a form of positive liberty—freedom to achieve specific outcomes—if the demand for transparency and audit rights is interpreted as requiring effective user control over outputs rather than mere protection against arbitrary power? Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to awkward, indicate which ones a committed defender of this framing would concede as genuinely damaging, and assess whether there exists a refined negative-liberty account—one that incorporates procedural safeguards without invoking domination—that explains the same normative intuitions about platform power. I want an honest assessment of how exposed this view is, including whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that is a problem for a normative position of this kind.
