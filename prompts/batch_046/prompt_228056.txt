In philosophical discussions of pain asymbolia — patients with intact nociception who accurately report injury location yet deny suffering, display no protective withdrawal, and show no emotional distress — a long-running disagreement has hardened into stalemate. One side treats these cases as proof that pain is not intrinsically bad: the sensory-discriminative component can occur without the affective-evaluative overlay that supplies normative weight, so badness is contingent on a separable mechanism that can malfunction. The opposing side rejects this, arguing that asymbolia is misclassified nociception rather than genuine pain; on this view, unpleasantness is essential, and the patients’ first-person reports about the absence of hurt are unreliable about affective presence, not authoritative about pain’s absence. For years both camps have repeated the same clinical descriptions without convergence. Is this deadlock substantive — about whether affect is part of pain’s essence — or is it verbal, turning on whether “pain” should be defined by sensory profile or phenomenal quality? Or is there a hidden disagreement about the scope of first-person authority — whether it extends to evaluative contents or only to sensory ones — with both sides answering different questions, one metaphysical and one epistemic, under a single label? Offer a diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, say whether a new conceptual distinction, an empirical result about nociceptive pathways, or a reframing of the question could move it, and tell me honestly whether progress is possible or the impasse should be expected.
