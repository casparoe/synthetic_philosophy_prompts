I've been arguing with my friend about this pilot policy our departement is running: students can use LLMs to generate literature summarys and even draft the transitional paragraphs between sections, but they have to write the "interpretive core" by hand—the part where you explain why the evidence actually supports your conclusion. The official reasoning is that understandng lives in interpretation, not in assembly, so outsourcing the scaffolding is okay as long as you own the insight. But this seems weird to me. If I read five AI-generated summarys and then construct my interpretation from those, haven't I already let the model shape what counts as relevant evidence? What theory of epistemic ownership or mental transparency does this policy presuppose—something like a Cartesian boundary between my mind and the tool, or maybe a division between mechanical labor and real epistemic labor? And if we replaced that hidden theory with a fully externalist view where understandng is just a functional relation to reliable information sources, wouldn't the line between drafting and interpreting totally collapse? I'm trying to figure out whether the practice is actually coherent or if it's just smuggling in an outdated picture of the mind without admitting it.
