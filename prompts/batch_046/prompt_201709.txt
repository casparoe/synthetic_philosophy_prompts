I've been helping out with the allocation committee for our neighborhood shared-equipment co-op — wheelchairs, portable generators, that kind of thing — and every month we have to decide who gets priority when stock runs low. Lately I've gotten obsessed with moral uncertanty, like I genuinely can't tell if the right call is always to maximize total welfare, protect the worst-off first, or stick to some first-come-first-served rule, and I don't want to just pick one theory and pretend I'm sure. So I want an actual proceddure I can run in about fifteen minutes during our meetings: the questions in order, what counts as enough of an answer at each step, where it stops with a hard no, and where it has to hand things off to a person because it's a values call not a fact. 

The twist is I read about Aunt Hillary and the ant fugue — the idea that an ant colony might be a mind even though no single ant sees the whole picture — and it made me think moral frameworks might work like that: no one theory has the full view, and maybe a decision is only "solid" when the different frameworks line up in some kind of counterpoint, not just when they happen to agree on the result. So I want the checklist to include a step where I check not just "what does each theory say about the outcome?" but whether they agree on the structure of the reason, and I want you to tell me exactly what counts as that kind of agreement. Then apply it to three cases: the last generator going to a family with a sick kid, an elder care home running low on heat, and a neighbor who reserved it first and has no special need. I lean hard toward care ethics, so one of these should come out in a way I personally won't like, and I need you to flag where the proceddure will misfire for a small group like ours and what it absolutely can't settle.
