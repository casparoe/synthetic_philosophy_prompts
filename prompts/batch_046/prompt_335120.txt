I'm taking a course on philosophy of psychiatry, and we've been working through Thomas Szasz's well-known argument that mental illness is largely a myth—by which he means psychiatric diagnoses are not descriptions of underlying biological diseases analogous to diabetes or infection, but rather value-laden labels applied to problems in living and social deviance. In our seminar discussions, I've kept noticing a particular argumentative configuration that seems to accept Szasz's anti-essentialist premise and then derive from it a conclusion that is almost the opposite of his own, but I cannot tell whether this inversion has an established name in philosophy of medicine, sociology of diagnosis, or epistemology, or whether it is simply a local confusion.

Here are two concrete instances of the pattern. First, in debates about ADHD in schools: one speaker argues that there is no clear biological boundary separating pathological inattention from ordinary childhood restlessness, so the diagnosis is a social construct rather than a discovered natural kind. Rather than concluding that we should therefore reduce labeling and coercive intervention, the interlocutor says that precisely because the category is invented and flexible, educators ought to apply it more generously, using the label as an administrative tool to distribute accommodations, resources, and legal protections fairly. The construct-status is treated not as a defect to be corrected but as a functional feature that makes the institution useful. Second, in discussions of depression and grief: a critic observes that the DSM pathologizes ordinary bereavement, treating normal sadness after loss as a medical disorder. The reply does not defend the biological reality of depression; instead, it insists that without the diagnostic label insurers will refuse to cover therapy for grieving people, so the "myth" must be preserved and even expanded because it operates as a necessary ticket to care and social recognition. In both cases, the speaker openly grants that the psychiatric category is not a literal disease entity, yet uses that very granted premise to justify broader institutional adoption rather than dismantling or restricting the category.

This feels distinct from ordinary medicalization, where a phenomenon is wrongly treated as an objective disease and should be returned to the social or moral domain; here the category's inventedness is acknowledged and then instrumentalized in favor of expansion. It also feels different from simple reification, since the construct is not being mistaken for a real thing, but is being deliberately maintained as a fiction for bureaucratic ends. Is there an established concept that captures exactly this move—perhaps something in the literature on strategic classification, institutional function creep, or the bureaucratic management of "problems in living"? If there is, I want a committed answer: identify the precise concept, explain exactly how the described phenomenon fits its necessary conditions, and defend why the nearest alternatives—such as treating it as mere medicalization, reification, or a misreading of Szasz—fail to capture it, specifying the boundary cases that distinguish them. If there is no adequate established term, then do not merely list possibilities and leave me to choose; coin a specific term with explicit necessary and sufficient conditions, defend its boundaries against the closest existing concepts, and tell me exactly what the phenomenon is not and where its edges lie. I am not asking for a bare name; I want the carving—the exact shape of the configuration, its limits, and the reasons for placing them there.
