Hey — quick question that's been bugging me since I taught privacy last week. There's this pattern I keep seeing in how data-protection rules and workplace norms treat information: if I literally tell my boss I'm pregnant, that's protected info they can't ask for, but if an algorithm infers it from my pharmacy purchases and the company buys that inference, it's somehow not the same legal violation. The practice seems to treat disclosure as the harm, not the knowing itself. What theory of privacy does that presuppose — is it purely about controlling who asks you, or about controlling what can be known about you? And is it consistent with medical-privacy norms, where the whole point is that the doctor knows? There's also this weird asymmetry where we ban direct questions but allow proxy inference through behavorial tracking, which feels like it assumes privacy is about social rituals of asking, not about actual epistemic access. Can you reconstruct what implicit theory makes both rules hang together, or show that they pull in opposite directions? I'm not after a bibliography, just want to know if there's a named framework for this specific tension between disclosure-privacy and inference-privacy, and whether the practice is philosophically coherent.
