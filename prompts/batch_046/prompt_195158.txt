I serve on the governing council of a tight-knit residential collective that expelled one of its members after he deliberately caused a fatal harm to another resident. The council is now drafting a formal, irrevocable sanction: a ritualized, permanently enacted severance of all ties and recognition, designed with full deliberation and ceremony, intended both to honor the victim and to serve as an absolute boundary that no future violation could cross. Most members argue that only such a calculated, irreversible collective response can preserve our moral authority and deter anyone who might consider a similar breach; they say that institutional authority transforms what would be individual vengeance into legitimate justice, and that refusing this line is a failure of collective responsibility.

I have argued that there is a structural parallel that should trouble us: by calmly planning and ceremonially executing the total, permanent annihilation of one person’s place in our world, we are reproducing the very architecture of the original harm—premeditated, irrevocable, and total—except that we cloak it in procedural legitimacy. My colleagues reply that this is a category error, conflating malicious individual intent with the protective necessity of a community, and that justice requires exactly this kind of clear, unambiguous boundary. I want to know whether my objection—that a planned, collective, irreversible destruction of a human bond mirrors the moral logic of the crime it punishes—is philosophically coherent, or whether the distinction between institutional defense and personal malice really dissolves the parallel.

Please take a committed position: is my structural objection sound, and does it imply that communities should reject such absolute, pre-planned exclusions even for the gravest violations? Give your reasoning directly rather than laying out options neutrally. End with a single line that reads exactly: Verdict: [your concise judgment].
