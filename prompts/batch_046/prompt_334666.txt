I am a retired natural scientist—forty years in condensed-matter physics—who has only recently begun reading philosophy with any seriousness, and I find myself returning to the problem of Buridan's ass not as a medieval logical puzzle but as a case of spontaneous symmetry breaking without a mechanism. In my former field, a system poised between two identical energy minima can settle into one state through an infinitesimal fluctuation that carries no intrinsic causal weight; in practical reason, however, such a perturbation seems to violate the principle that choice ought to be grounded in preference. I should like you to write a poem for a small interdisciplinary reading group—scientists and philosophers together—that renders this conceptual collision structurally rather than didactically.

Please compose a poem of exactly thirteen lines, arranged as two stanzas of six lines separated by a solitary seventh line that stands alone at the axis. The second stanza must be a precise lexical reversal of the first—line one matching line thirteen, line two line twelve, and so on, with line six matching line eight—so that the poem is perfectly symmetric except for that central seventh line, which alone may differ. That deviation should embody the arbitrary break in equilibrium: the ass stepping left, the microscopic fluctuation, the tie that is not dissolved but cut. The surrounding lines should evoke the two identical bales, the paralysis of perfect indifference, and the absence of any reason that could justify the turn. The poem should not moralise or resolve the paradox; rather, the form itself should show that symmetry is violated from without, not dissolved from within. Write in British English, with language precise enough that a philosopher of action will recognise the problem of preference-independent choice, yet open enough to stand as poetry for a reader unfamiliar with Buridan.
