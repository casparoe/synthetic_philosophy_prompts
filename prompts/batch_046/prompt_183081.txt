Here are my verdicts about aesthetic judgment across several domains, and I want to know whether one principle can hold them together or whether some must be abandoned.

When a trained conservator and a layperson disagree about the beauty of a damaged fresco—the conservator seeing harmony in the fragment, the layperson seeing only ruin—I think the conservator is closer to getting it right, yet the layperson is not mistaken in the way one is about a historical date.

When a culture gradually comes to see a once-derided architectural form, such as Brutalist concrete, as genuinely beautiful, this strikes me as closer to acquiring taste for a complex wine than to shifting fashion: they appear to discover a property that was present all along.

When two mathematicians disagree about whether a particular proof is beautiful—one praising its economy, the other condemning it for an ad hoc lemma—I take them to be arguing about real structural features, with one possibly wrong about the proof's elegance.

When someone calls a self-sacrificial act "beautiful" but would withdraw that judgment upon learning the act was actually cruel and misguided, the aesthetic appraisal seems to depend on prior moral evaluation; yet if one learns a painting is a forgery, its sensory appearance usually remains unchanged, and so does its beauty or lack thereof.

When viewers call a false-color astronomical image beautiful, despite knowing the colors map arbitrarily to non-visible wavelengths and have no sensory basis in direct observation, I still think the judgment can be correct—which suggests beauty need not reside in raw sensory appearance.

These five verdicts sit uneasily together: cases two and three treat beauty as objective and correctable, case one allows expert-lay divergence without outright error, case four ties aesthetic judgment to non-aesthetic evaluation in a way that case five (representational beauty with no sensory ground) seems to deny.

Is there a unified account—perhaps a modified standard of taste that accommodates structural and representational beauty—that delivers all five? If not, which verdict should be overturned and why?
