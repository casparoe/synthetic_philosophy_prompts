I am trying to determine whether there is a unified principle that captures my judgments about cognitive extension, and if not, which of my judgments ought to be revised. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, the classic notebook case: an individual with severe memory impairment constantly consults a handwritten notebook to navigate daily life. Verdict: this clearly extends his belief system; the notebook plays the same functional role as biological memory and is continuously coupled to his reasoning.

Second, a surgeon wearing an AR headset that highlights tissue anomalies via computer-vision algorithms she does not understand. She treats its outputs as perceptual data but routinely overrides them on the basis of her own clinical judgment. Verdict: this extends her perceptual cognition.

Third, a mathematician who can recall a complex formula only by singing a private mnemonic jingle she composed. Verdict: this extends her memory, even though the storage is auditory and embodied in her vocal apparatus rather than in neural tissue.

Fourth, a research collective of five specialists, each mastering only one module of an interdependent proof, reasoning jointly through a shared database and publishing under collective authorship. No single member can reproduce the others' steps. Verdict: this constitutes an extended cognitive system at the group level, not merely five aided individual minds.

Fifth, a proprietary brain-computer interface implanted to regulate emotional arousal. It stimulates the amygdala according to opaque biometric algorithms, occasionally suppressing fear during objectively dangerous situations. The user experiences the calm but cannot understand, predict, or correct the algorithm's interventions. Verdict: despite being physically implanted and continuously coupled, this does not extend her emotional cognition; it operates as an internalized external regulator.

Sixth, a writer who uses an LLM autocomplete trained on her prior drafts. She accepts approximately ninety percent of suggestions without conscious review, and the model occasionally introduces factual errors and stylistic choices she would not endorse were she to notice them. Verdict: despite deep functional integration, this does not extend her reasoning or authorship.

The case I keep oscillating on is the following: a mathematician relies on formal verification software to check complex proofs. She understands the logical framework but not the low-level implementation, trusts its outputs in routine work, yet can suspend its judgments when they conflict with her intuitive structural grasp of a proof. Is this extended reasoning, or is it an external instrument analogous to a calculator?

Please propose the principle (or a small set of competing candidates) that best captures these verdicts, stating it precisely and identifying which clause is motivated by which case. Apply the principle to the verification-software case and to one scenario I have not listed: an individual with perfect biological recall who stores all long-term memories in an encrypted external drive, retrieving them solely through biometric authentication with no conscious awareness of the retrieval mechanism. Finally, be explicit about which of my six verdicts, if any, the principle fails to accommodate, and whether that failure is serious enough that the verdict should be overturned rather than the principle complicated.
