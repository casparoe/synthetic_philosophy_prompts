I have been examining the risk-taxonomy documents produced by several prominent AI governance bodies and safety institutes, and I notice a consistent pattern: the frameworks classify threats according to whether they could cause human extinction, widespread societal collapse, or severe economic disruption, yet they almost never include a distinct category for scenarios in which advanced systems generate or sustain enormous populations of sentient beings—whether biological or digital—whose lives contain far more suffering than well-being. This kind of outcome, often referred to in longtermist literature as a suffering risk or s-risk, does not necessarily entail the disappearance of humanity; one could imagine a stable future civilization that deliberately or accidentally maintains vast numbers of conscious entities in conditions of chronic distress for strategic, economic, or computational reasons. Given that these taxonomies are not accidental but reflect deliberate choices about what counts as a catastrophic harm worth monitoring, what philosophical commitments does the exclusion of s-risks presuppose?

Reconstruct the implicit theory—regarding moral status, the comparability of suffering across different substrates, the relationship between tractability and moral urgency, and the purpose of risk governance—that makes it coherent to treat extinction-level events as categorically central while treating large-scale engineered suffering as either impossible, unquantifiable, or outside the scope of institutional responsibility. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely happens to align with it: for instance, is the omission compatible with a thoroughgoing utilitarianism that simply judges s-risks less probable, or does it tacitly assume that only biological human welfare defines the boundary of moral concern? Finally, outline how a risk-assessment institution that took s-risks to be morally on a par with existential risks would have to alter its evaluation metrics, its criteria for model evaluation, and its allocation of oversight resources.
