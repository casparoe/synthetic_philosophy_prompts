Instead, he constructs an unstable comparative field: the Roman figure expresses the same ethical quality (ambition, rhetorical power, civic courage) through institutional and cultural forms that are incommensurable with the Greek expression. The reader is therefore not invited to imitate either figure, but to experience the friction between them. This makes Plutarch’s method structurally closer to hagiography than to classical exemplarity, except with a crucial inversion. Hagiography (as in late antique and medieval saints’ lives) typically resolves moral tension by transcending nature—the saint embodies virtue in a form that exceeds ordinary human capacity, offering a unified, aspirational endpoint. Plutarch, by contrast, leaves the tension structurally unresolved: neither the Greek nor the Roman version is privileged, and no synthesis is offered. The moral learning happens not in identification but in the sustained discomfort of comparison. I am tentatively calling this mechanism comparative moral friction, and I want to use it to rethink biography as a philosophical genre—not as instruction but as an exercise in holding contradictory ethical manifestations together without collapsing them into a single model.
