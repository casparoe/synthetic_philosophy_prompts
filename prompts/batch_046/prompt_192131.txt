I've been going in circles on whether Solomonoff induction is actually doing the philosophical work people claim for it. The view I'm tentatively holding is that Kolmogorov complexity gives us the only objective sense of 'simplicity' that doesn't smuggle in human cognitive biases, and that Occam's razor is therefore not just a heuristic but a consequence of the universal prior — even if we can never compute it exactly. I think the uncomputability objection is overblown, since normativity doesn't require tractability, and I also think the constant-factor dependance on the choice of universal Turing machine wash out in the limit for any non-pathological data stream.

But I want an honest map of what would actually count against this position — not the standard 'it's uncomputable so we can't use it' line, but deeper defeaters. For instance, what if there are empirical domains where systematically more complex hypotheses outperforms simpler ones in prediction, not just because of noise but because the world actually has high Kolmogorov complexity at the base level? Or what if the universal prior, once you allow for self-referential or adversarial programs, assigns non-trivial weight to 'demon' hypotheses that predict our observations perfectly but also entail that we shouldn't trust our reasoning — does that undermine the update rule itself, or is it just a weird edge case? There's also this worry I'm not sure about: if you move to resource-bounded agents, different universal machines might not just differ by a constant but actually reorder which hypotheses are 'simple' within the agent's lifetime, which would mean the prior is hardware-dependent in practice. Would that be fatal, or merely awkward? I'd like the defeaters ranked — which ones a committed Solomonoff advocate would actually concede hurt the view, and which ones they'd dismiss as missing the point. Is there any formal result in learning theory that shows this whole approach collapses into standard Bayesian model selection with an implicit simplicity bias that's culturally contingent? I want to know how exposed the position really is, and whether it's well-insulated in a way that's healthy or just dogmatic.
