Consider the following argument, which I take to show that causal exclusion eliminates far more than mental causation, but I cannot locate the mistake. (1) If mental property M supervenes on physical base B, and B is physically sufficient for effect E, then M is causally excluded: either B alone causes E, or we face systematic overdetermination, which is implausible. (2) The same structure applies to any macro-property: chemical kind K supervenes on microphysical realization R, and R is sufficient for chemical reaction C, so K is excluded. (3) By downward iteration, only the most fundamental physical properties possess genuine causal powers; neuroscience, biology, and chemistry are all epiphenomenal. (4) Yet interventionist accounts of causation treat macro-properties as difference-makers precisely because they support invariant counterfactual dependencies under interventions—dependencies that vanish if only fundamental properties cause anything. (5) Therefore the exclusion principle in (1) must be rejected. (6) But if we reject (1) generally, mental properties are rescued only by dissolving the mental-physical asymmetry that originally made mental causation seem specially threatened; the rescue looks ad hoc unless mental and chemical properties share the exact same metaphysical status. (7) Hence mental causation is either impossible—if we keep (1)—or preserved only at the cost of abandoning the very distinction that motivated the exclusion problem. I suspect an equivocation lurks between metaphysical sufficiency and interventionist invariance, or perhaps a hidden scope shift in the iteration from (2) to (3), but I cannot name it.
