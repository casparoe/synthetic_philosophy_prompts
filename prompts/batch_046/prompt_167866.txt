I'm trying to understand a preservation practice I've seen in urban planning guidelines but haven't fully grasped philosophically. In several municipalities with layered zoning histories, heritage impact assessments apply a different burden of proof depending on whether a district's form is "organic" or "planned." For pre-modern or pre-zoning neighborhoods—roughly those developed before widespread modern planning codes—a developer seeking to alter street patterns, demolish mixed-use structures, or change plot ratios must demonstrate that the proposed change improves on the existing layout's "latent functional value," even though the city maintains no archive explaining why the streets curve as they do, why certain buildings face particular directions, or why plot sizes vary irregularly. Here, the absence of documented design intent is treated not as ignorance to be overcome but as evidence that the arrangement embodies distributed practical knowledge that should not be second-guessed. By contrast, for districts built under documented master plans—post-war modernist estates, garden-city extensions, or other rationally designed quarters—the burden is reversed: alterations are permitted unless they violate the explicit, archived intentions of the original designers, and current welfare can easily override those intentions if the documents show they rested on outdated assumptions.

This looks like an institutionalization of Chesterton's fence—do not tear down a barrier until you understand why it stands—but applied selectively. It protects undocumented organic form with a presumption of hidden wisdom, while treating documented rational design as more easily revisable. What philosophical commitments does this two-tier practice presuppose? Must it assume that tacit, historically evolved social knowledge is epistemically superior to explicit expert planning, or can it be justified by something thinner, like risk aversion, path dependence, or democratic deference to inherited settlement patterns? Does it commit to a Burkean view that prescription and "prejudice" carry authority precisely because their origins are forgotten, and if so, is it consistent to deny that same authority to the documented intentions of modern planners? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories of value, knowledge, and political authority? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say what institutional changes would be required if we replaced its underlying view with one that judged all urban form solely by current, measurable welfare outcomes, treating both old and new districts as equally revisable.
