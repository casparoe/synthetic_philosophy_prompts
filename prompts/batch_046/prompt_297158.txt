Exam question: Apply the doctrine of double effect to the standard switch case, the footbridge case, and the loop-track variant where diverting the trolley onto a side track kills one person but then the trolley loops back and kills the five unless that body blocks it. For each case say whether the action is permitted under DDE, and specifically discuss whether the bad effect is merely foreseen or is actually being used as a means to the good effect. Student answer:
The doctrine of double effect was first worked out by Aquinas in Summa Theologiae for self-defense situations, and it has four conditions: the act itself must be good or neutral; the bad effect must not be intended; the good effect must not be produced by means of the bad effect; and there must be proportionality between the good and bad outcomes. In the switch case, pulling the lever is neutral. The death of the one worker is foreseen but not intended — it's just a side effect of saving the five. The good comes from the lever being pulled, not from the death, so condition three is satisfied. Proportionality is satisfied because five lives outweigh one life, so it's permitted.

In the footbridge case, pushing the fat man is not permitted. Here the agent uses the man's body as a means to stop the trolley. Even if the agent doesn't specifically want him dead, his death is the mechanism by which the five are saved, so the bad effect is a means to the good. That violates condition three. Proportionality still holds numerically, but it doesn't save the action.

In the loop-track variant, this works just like the switch case. The agent pulls the lever to divert the trolley onto the side track. The one person's death is unfortunate but only foreseen. Because the agent's plan is just the diversion, and the saving comes from that diversion rather than from the death, it's permitted. The fact that the body blocks the loop afterwards is not part of the agent's intention — it's an accidental feature of the track layout — so condition three isn't violated. Proportionality works the same way: 5 is greater than 1, so the good is proportionate.

Also, Foot applied DDE to trolley problems in her classic essay, but she actually rejected the means/ends distinction altogether and said we should just ask whether the harm is direct or indirect. So in some ways DDE doesn't apply to these cases the way Aquinas originally meant.
