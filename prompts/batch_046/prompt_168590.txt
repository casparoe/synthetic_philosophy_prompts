I recently faced a professional decision about whether to suppress a methodological flaw in a collaborative study because exposing it would undermine a cause I support, and I realized the difficulty was not the cost-benefit calculus itself but whether allowing that calculus to mediate my commitment to intellectual honesty was itself the failure. Reading Bernard Williams's discussion of integrity and his well-known objection involving one thought too many made me see my own dilemma differently. I am now preparing the final examination for my upper-level moral philosophy seminar, and I want an essay question that forces students to apply Williams's framework to a case that is not the familiar rescue scenario, so they cannot simply recite the standard distinction between personal projects and impersonal morality.

Please design the exam question, model answer, and marking scheme according to the following specifications.

The question should present a detailed hypothetical: Dr. Elena Voss, a historian of colonial resistance, has been asked to curate a museum exhibition. She discovers that the exhibition's centerpiece—a first-person account of a rebellion—was likely forged decades later, though it has become symbolically central to the descendant community. The museum director urges her to include it with appropriate caveats, arguing that the larger truth of historical oppression outweighs the document's questionable provenance, and asks whether she can in good conscience present it. The essay should ask students to analyze Voss's position through Williams's integrity objection and specifically through the lens of the one-thought-too-many argument.

The question must require students to address three specific issues: first, whether Voss's commitment to archival accuracy can be understood as a ground project in Williams's sense; second, whether her deliberation about whether to include the document involves one thought too many if she weighs the symbolic and emotional value to the community against her professional standards; and third, whether the director's reasoning represents precisely the kind of impersonal utilitarian mediation that Williams rejects, and whether the presence of collective identity—rather than purely individual life—alters the force of the objection.

Please provide a model answer of approximately 600 words. It should correctly distinguish Williams's concern about the form of deliberation from any simple prohibition on weighing consequences, apply the framework precisely to Voss rather than reverting to the standard rescue case, address the collective-identity complication without conflating the community's interests with Voss's own integrity, and offer a brief critical evaluation of whether Williams's critique succeeds when ground projects involve professional roles tied to communal history.

Please also provide a marking scheme suitable for a 40-minute upper-level exam essay worth 40 percent of the exam grade. The scheme should separate first-class answers in the A-range from upper-second answers in the B-range by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain—particularly the correct identification of the deliberative structure that constitutes having one thought too many—and should list the specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as treating Williams as merely anti-consequentialist about outcomes, conflating Voss's integrity with the community's symbolic needs, or applying the familiar rescue example without translating its logical structure to the archival case.
