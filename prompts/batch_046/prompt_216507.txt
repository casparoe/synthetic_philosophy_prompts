I've been arguing with my roomate about the death penalty, specifcally about how to weigh the risk of killing someone innocent against the usual justifications people throw around like deterrence or retribution. I came up with this framing I'm calling "institutional moral contamination" — basically, the idea that a system that accepts a nonzero risk of executing an innocent person isn't just making a statistical error, but is corrupting its own moral authority in a way that can't be fixed by lowering the error rate. It's not exactly the same as the standard debate over whether punishment should match the crime.

Can you compare that framing with the standard retributivist view that punishment should be proportional to the offense? I'm especially interested in whether my contamination idea is actually distinct from retributivism's own concerns about justice, or if it's just smuggling in consequentialist worries about institutional trust. Also, where does the risk of executing the innocent fit in each framework? I don't want a survey of every philosopher who's written on capital punishment — just a focused comparison between these two ways of thinking about the problem, and whether they can be combined or if they pull in opposite directions when you actually try to design a policy.
