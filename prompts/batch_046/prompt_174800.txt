I’m a retired biologist—spent thirty years in a molecular genetics lab—who recently started sitting in on philosophy seminars at the communtiy college, mostly because my wife said I was turning into a crank arguing about ethics at the dinner table. I’m still trying to figure out whether corruption is one thing everywhere or whether Western anti-bribery rules are just one local moral economy pretending to be universal.

Could you write a dialogue for me? I want two people who are actually sharp and respond to each other—maybe a political philospher who thinks corruption is basically the violation of impersonal institutional rules, and an anthropologist who argues that labeling something a bribe already smuggles in a specific, Western-style split between personal relationships and public roles. Have them wrestle with a concrete case I keep thinking about: in some East Asian research cultures, senior scientists routinely gift expensive reagents or equipment to junior labs as part of long-term patronage networks, and in the West we’d call that influence-peddling or grant fraud, but locally it’s seen as building obligation and trust. The anthropologist should push that if you can’t see this as hospitality embedded in a social relationship, you’re just applying your own culture’s accounting rules. The philosopher should counter that if every exchange can be re-described as gift-giving, then corruption loses all normative bite and we can’t condemn anything. Make it real back-and-forth, not strawmen—each should feel the force of the other’s point. And maybe have them circle back to whether an old lab rat like me should feel bad about the days when we swapped chemicals between universities with nothing but a handshake and a promise—was that corruption, or just how science actually worked before compliance offices existed? I’d like the tension left open, not wrapped up with a neat answer, since that’s honestly where my reading group ended up.
