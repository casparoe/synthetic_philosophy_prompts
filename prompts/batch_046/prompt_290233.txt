Case 2: The same municipality declines a large grant that would have restored the site as an educational memorial with a mandatory school-curriculum link. I also think this refusal is permissible, though it feels different from Case 1.

Case 3: The municipality accepts the grant, restores the site, and requires every local student to visit annually and write a reflective essay using a prescribed framing. This feels wrong to me—not merely imprudent, but morally off.

Case 4: A survivor of state violence asks that her testimony in a public oral-history archive be anonymized; the archive anonymizes the transcript but preserves the original audio in sealed storage with restricted access. I think this is the right balance.

Case 6: A government digitizes every bureaucratic file from an authoritarian era—names of victims, informants, and bystanders alike—and publishes them in a fully searchable open database with no contextual framing or redaction. This strikes me as wrong.

Case 7: The same government publishes only aggregated statistical findings and seals individual files for seventy-five years, allowing scholarly access under review. This strikes me as acceptable.
