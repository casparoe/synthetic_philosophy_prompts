A colleague presented me with the following chain of reasoning about rational choice and institutional tie-breaking, and I cannot locate the error, though the conclusion seems clearly wrong. Here are the steps:

(1) Rational choice requires that an agent's selection be responsive to the reasons that apply to the options.
(2) In a true Buridan's-ass situation—two outcomes with identical merit—no first-order reason distinguishes them; reason-responsiveness at the first order is impossible.
(3) Therefore, any actual selection in such a case is not responsive to a first-order reason favoring the chosen option over the other.
(4) Any tie-breaking mechanism—whether a coin flip, a deterministic hash of a patient's record number, or an unexamined preference—similarly fails to be responsive to first-order reasons.
(5) Consequently, once perfect equipoise is established, no subsequent mechanism can restore first-order reason-responsiveness; the choice remains arbitrary with respect to the options' merits.
(6) Now consider a hospital allocation committee that adopts an explicit protocol: when two patients have exactly equal clinical priority, assignment is made by a deterministic hash of their medical record numbers, applied uniformly.
(7) The committee defends this as rational because it prevents paralysis, eliminates human bias, and applies a transparent rule.
(8) But by (5), the hash is no more responsive to first-order clinical reasons than the chair's hidden preference for taller patients; both substitute a non-merit feature for the missing reason.
(9) Hence the committee's protocol is not genuinely rational; it is merely systematized arbitrariness disguised by procedural formality.
(10) Yet this proves too much. If correct, it would condemn not only this protocol but also lotteries in fair division, alphabetical ordering in peer-review panels, and rotational scheduling—practices that seem rationally defensible precisely because they replace hidden bias with explicit, ex ante fair procedures.
(11) So one step must be wrong. I am inclined to protect (7)'s intuition that procedural fairness can supply a second-order reason, but I am unsure whether that saves (6) or requires abandoning (1) altogether.

Please identify the weakest step—likely an equivocation between orders of reason, though I am open to other diagnoses—explain why it looked plausible, and specify what giving it up costs for our understanding of rational agency and institutional design. In particular, address whether a second-order procedural reason can genuinely justify a first-order arbitrary selection, or whether that move collapses the distinction between rational choice and mere rule-following.
