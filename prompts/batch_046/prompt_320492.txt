I have been studying Aristotle’s sea-battle argument together with contemporary open-future theories in the philosophy of time. The classical puzzle is that if the statement “there will be a naval battle tomorrow” is already true or false today, then fatalism seems unavoidable: should it be true now, then no present action could prevent the engagement. Open-future responses deny bivalence for future contingents, holding that prior to the event there is no determinate fact of the matter about whether the battle occurs; instead, reality branches, with both outcomes equally unsettled. I find this metaphysical picture compelling, yet it creates a tension for the institutional forecasting board on which I serve. Our charter obliges us to issue calibrated probabilistic forecasts—assigning, for instance, a 65 percent likelihood to a geopolitical conflict within a twelve-month window—so that policymakers can weigh risks rationally. But if the open-future view is correct, there appears to be no present state of affairs that makes the conflict 65 percent likely rather than 35 percent likely; the branch in which it happens and the branch in which it does not are both ontologically live, with no hidden fact selecting between them. Are we therefore measuring nothing more than our collective epistemic credence? And if ontological openness genuinely removes the fact-to-be-predicted, should our board abandon probabilistic forecasting altogether in favor of scenario planning that treats every branch as equipossible, or does the absence of a determinate future fact not actually undermine the coherence of calibrated institutional prediction?
