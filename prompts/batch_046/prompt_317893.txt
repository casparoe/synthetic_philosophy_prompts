I am preparing a diagnostic classification instrument for a graduate seminar in moral philosophy, specifically on suffering-focused ethics and its extension to borderline cases. I want a decision tree—built from the logical space of commitments rather than from a catalogue of author names—that sorts positions according to four binary splits. Each split should be phrased as a concrete case or claim that a respondent can answer without prior knowledge of philosophical labels.

The four splits must address: (1) whether suffering in merely potential or dormant entities—such as a synthetic neural substrate engineered to experience pain if activated, but never actually activated—carries any moral weight; (2) whether the naturalness of a suffering-causing process, for instance predation within wild ecosystems, diminishes our obligation to intervene technologically; (3) whether the prospect of astronomically large future suffering—through the colonisation of other planets by wild animals, or through the widespread creation of artificial minds—generates a stronger reason to prevent the existence of such beings altogether than to ameliorate suffering among those that already exist; and (4) whether the sheer scale of present wild-animal suffering makes direct, individual-level intervention practically impossible, leaving only systemic or extinction-level responses as morally obligatory.

Please present the tree so that each branch is labelled by the claim that separates it from its sibling. At every leaf, state the name of the position if it is recognised in the literature (for example, 'strong wild-animal interventionism', 'ecological laissez-faire within suffering-focused ethics', 'anti-natalist suffering-focused ethics', or 'pro-mortalist voluntary extinctionism'), or mark it 'unnamed' if the combination is not defended. After each name, provide exactly one sentence describing the position's most awkward commitment—the consequence that adherents find difficult to accept or that critics regard as its greatest liability.

Next, identify all combinations of answers that remain unoccupied. For each empty branch, offer a brief diagnosis of whether the gap arises from logical incoherence, from the view's being plainly unattractive, or from mere philosophical neglect. Finally, flag any two named positions that the tree fails to separate; explain which shared answers place them on an identical leaf, and specify the further distinction that would be required to divide them.

The response should be written in formal academic British English, suitable for distribution as seminar material. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the instrument should function for a reader with no access to external sources.
