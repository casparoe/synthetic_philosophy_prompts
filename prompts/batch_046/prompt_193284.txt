I've noticed a strange institutional pattern in how the law treats mental creations. You can copyright the exact wording of a book but not its underlying plot; you can patent a practical process for twenty years, but only if you fully disclose it, while trade-secret law protects the same kind of knowledge indefinitely so long as it stays hidden, yet offers nothing once someone independently figures it out; and no one can own a mathematical truth or a law of nature, regardless of the labor that went into finding it. What philosophical view of ownership, value, or the nature of ideas does this three-part framework quietly presuppose? Does it assume that ideas are genuinely non-rivalrous in a way physical goods are not, or that there is a deep distinction between discovering what already exists and inventing something new? Is the system philosophically consistent—copyright rewards expression without disclosure, patent demands disclosure for a temporary monopoly, and trade secret rewards concealment—or are these just practical compromises with no shared theory? And if the underlying assumption turned out to be wrong—say, if ideas are not actually non-rivalrous, or if the discovery-invention boundary collapses—what parts of this practice would have to be rebuilt rather than merely adjusted?
