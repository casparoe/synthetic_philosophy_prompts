Please help me understand the logic of the following passage from David Hume's "Of Miracles," section X of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748):

"When anyone tells me, that he saw a dead man restored to life, I immediately consider with myself, whether it be more probable, that this person should either deceive or be deceived, or that the fact, which he relates, should really have happened.... If the falsehood of his testimony would be more miraculous, than the event which he relates; then, and not till then, can he pretend to command my belief or opinion."

"Nothing is esteemed a miracle, if it ever happen in the common course of nature. It is no miracle that a man, seemingly in good health, should die on a sudden: because such a kind of death, though more unusual than any other, has yet been frequently observed to happen. But it is a miracle, that a dead man should come to life; because that has never been observed in any age or country."

My specific difficulty concerns how this reasoning relates to debates about testimonial justification—specifically the disagreement between reductionism and anti-reductionism. Reductionists hold that rational acceptance of testimony must be grounded in independent, non-testimonial evidence about a speaker's reliability or about the prior plausibility of the content. Anti-reductionists maintain that testimony carries a default epistemic entitlement, functioning as a basic and structurally independent source of justification that does not need to be derived from other evidential sources.

With this distinction in mind, does Hume's principle that belief ought to be proportioned to evidence, together with his weighing of the probability of deception or error against the probability of a violated law of nature, implicitly presuppose a reductionist framework? Or could an anti-reductionist consistently accept Hume's probabilistic conclusion—that testimony to a miracle should be rejected—while still insisting that, in ordinary cases, testimony provides a non-derivative evidential contribution that is then overridden by stronger counter-evidence? In other words, is Hume treating the witness's report as an item whose epistemic weight is fully reducible to prior probabilities about human fallibility and natural regularity, or does his reasoning leave conceptual space for testimony to operate as a basic source of belief that is subsequently defeated?
