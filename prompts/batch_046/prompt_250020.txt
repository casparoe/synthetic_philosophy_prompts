Here is an argument I constructed for a workshop on contractualist ethics, and I worry it collapses into an implausible demand for total self-sacrifice. I want to know exactly where the slide happens and what repairing it costs.

(1) Scanlonian contractualism judges a principle impermissible if it permits actions that violate a rule no affected party could reasonably reject.

(2) In standard aggregation cases—saving five strangers versus saving one—we intuit that the five should be saved.

(3) To justify this, contractualism must allow that the reasons of multiple people combine: five individuals each have a reason of strength R to be saved, and together these generate a collective reason stronger than the single person’s reason of strength R.

(4) Once cross-person aggregation of reasons is permitted, there is no principled barrier to aggregating burdens in the same structural way.

(5) Consider a collective scheme—say, a mandatory global carbon-rationing treaty—that imposes a small burden b on each of N citizens and prevents a catastrophic harm H distributed across all N.

(6) By (3), the N citizens’ collective reason to avoid H aggregates to outweigh each citizen’s individual reason to avoid b.

(7) So each citizen can be compelled to bear b.

(8) But b is not atomic: it decomposes into temporal and resource sub-burdens—hours worked, goods foregone, opportunities lost—across a lifetime.

(9) Applying the same aggregation logic to these decomposed sub-burdens, every waking hour and every marginal dollar a citizen could contribute becomes part of an aggregated collective reason to prevent H.

(10) Therefore contractualism requires each citizen to devote every available resource to the scheme, up to the point where further contribution yields no benefit—total self-sacrifice.

(11) That is implausible.

(12) So either contractualism cannot save the five (denying aggregation) or it demands total self-sacrifice.

I feel like something slips between (6) and (9), but I cannot name it. Is the error in allowing aggregation at all? Or in treating decomposed burdens like discrete persons? Or in assuming that what works for rescue cases transfers to policy?

Explain why that step looks initially plausible, especially given contractualist talk of relevant reasons and the idea that bare numbers should not matter in themselves.

Repair options (about 300 words): For the step you identify, explain what rejecting it costs. If we block the decomposition in (8), can we still aggregate in rescue cases? If we deny (3), do we lose the intuition about the five? Discuss at least two repair strategies and what each forces contractualism to concede about aggregation, individual exemption, or the scope of reasonable rejection.

Bottom line (about 100 words): State which premise you think is least costly to abandon, and sketch what the repaired contractualist view looks like—particularly whether it can preserve both the anti-utilitarian structure and our intuition about saving the many.
