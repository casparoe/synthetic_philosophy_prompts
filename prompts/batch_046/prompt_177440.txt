You are a professional philosopher of law with particular expertise in penal theory, including classical retributive and consequentialist frameworks as well as more recent expressive and hybrid accounts. Before formulating your answer, please take a moment to brainstorm the core distinctions—culpability versus harm, act versus risk, desert versus deterrence, and punitive versus restorative aims—that might organize a set of intuitive judgments about punishment.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. I want to know whether they cohere around one principle, or whether I must abandon some.

1. A driver in a state of intentional rage drives onto a sidewalk and kills a pedestrian. I judge that a lengthy prison sentence—around twenty-five years—is warranted.
2. A driver in the identical mental state performs the identical action but, by pure chance, kills no one. I still think prison is warranted, but my intuition is that the sentence should be noticeably shorter—perhaps fifteen years—not equal to case 1.
3. A factory manager knowingly ignores a lethal safety defect; a worker dies. I think a serious prison term is appropriate.
4. The same manager ignores the identical defect, but an unrelated event keeps all workers away from the danger that day; no one is harmed. Here I think a heavy fine, professional disqualification, and perhaps a brief custodial sentence are appropriate, but a long prison term feels wrong, despite the culpable mental state and risk being exactly the same as in case 3.
5. Two people perform the same reckless, life-endangering act; one causes death purely by luck, the other does not. I think both deserve punishment, and the one who caused death should face a somewhat heavier sentence, but the gap should be modest—certainly not the difference between prison and a mere fine.
6. A sentencing algorithm predicts with ninety percent accuracy that a particular offender will commit a violent crime in the next five years, and the judge extends the current sentence to cover that future risk. I regard this as impermissible preventive detention, not punishment, regardless of accuracy.

These verdicts pull in different directions: cases 2 and 4 treat realized harm as aggravating prison severity, yet case 5 limits how much it can aggravate, and case 6 rejects extending punishment solely on predicted future harm. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps one that separates a baseline punitive desert calibrated to culpability from a separate, harm-responsive restorative or reparative obligation—that can accommodate most of them? Or must I drop one verdict?

Please state your best principle precisely, identify which of my six judgments it cannot save, and apply it to a case I have not listed: a surgeon performs an unnecessary but profitable operation. In scenario A the patient dies on the table; in scenario B the patient survives but suffers permanent disability. According to your principle, how should the punishment differ, if at all?
