I work in software engineering and have no formal philosophy training, so when I read about Rome I tend to frame the question in system terms—state persistence, backward compatibility, breaking changes, graceful degradation—rather than through the usual historiographical narratives. I want to know whether my intuitions about when a civilization has genuinely "fallen" versus merely transformed hang together, or whether I am inconsistently applying some hidden criterion. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

1. The deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476 CE, followed by Odoacer's rule in Italy with Roman tax records, legal courts, and administrative titles continuing largely unchanged: this is transformation, not fall. The political sovereignty layer was swapped out, but the institutional state services kept running with backward compatibility.

2. The Roman Senate in the 6th-century West, still convening and appointing consuls under Ostrogothic and later Lombard rule, but with no independent treasury, no command over armies, and no capacity to enforce decisions outside the chamber: this is the case I keep reversing on. Sometimes it looks like a zombie process—an institution with no backend—which suggests fall. Other times it looks like a deprecated compatibility shim that outlived its dependencies, suggesting transformation.

3. The collapse of Mycenaean palace administration in Greece around 1200 BCE, with Linear B writing disappearing, centralized redistribution ending, and urban centers abandoned, followed by centuries without recovered literacy or state-level coordination: this is a fall. The knowledge infrastructure itself was lost, not merely migrated.

4. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 and the subsequent Ottoman adoption of Byzantine bureaucratic, tax, and legal structures through the millet system, with Greek officials continuing prior functions: transformation. The governing protocol changed ownership, but the data and processes migrated intact.

5. The end of Roman Britain around 410 CE, with the withdrawal of imperial coinage, the disappearance of urban administrative life, and the replacement of villa economies by materially different Anglo-Saxon settlement patterns: fall. The core dependencies—monetized taxation, urban literacy, long-distance trade integration—experienced breaking changes that were not replaced.

6. The open-source fork of a major proprietary software monolith after the original firm discontinues support: the brand and original development team vanish, but the codebase, documentation, and user ecosystem persist and evolve under new governance. This is my engineering control case—clearly transformation, not fall—because the executable institutional knowledge survives the loss of the original authority.

Is there one precise principle that delivers verdicts 1, 3, 4, 5, and 6, and that resolves case 2 in one direction? I am especially interested in whether the distinction tracks something like "discontinuity in institutional knowledge versus discontinuity in political sovereignty," or whether it requires a different axis—perhaps "whether core dependencies experience breaking changes with no backward-compatibility layer."

If you propose a principle, state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, state its verdict on case 2, and apply it to a case I have not listed: the transition from the Roman Republic to the Principate under Augustus, where republican magistracies and the Senate persisted but real decision-making authority migrated to the imperial household. Finally, be explicit about which of my listed verdicts—particularly the pairing of 3 and 5, which both involve the loss of urban and administrative infrastructure—the principle cannot accommodate without forcing me to revise one.
