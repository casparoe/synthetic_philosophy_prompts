I have recently taken up a role in local government whilst also finding myself drawn to direct-action campaigns, which has made questions of political obligation and civil disobedience personally urgent rather than merely academic. Here are my commitments.

First, on obligation: I hold an associative view — I owe obedience not because I have consented, but because I belong to a political community that aims at justice and treats its members with equal concern. Second, I think civil disobedience must meet the classic conditions: it should be public, non-violent, appeal to shared principles of justice, and accept legal consequences. Third, I accept a procedural-taint exception: if a particular statute is enacted through a process that systematically excludes affected parties (for instance, extreme partisan gerrymandering), my associative obligation to obey that specific law is suspended, even though my general obligation to the institution remains intact. Fourth, I hold a duty of institutional forbearance: citizens ought not disobey merely because a policy is inefficient or mildly unfair; disobedience is only warranted when a serious threshold of injustice is crossed. Fifth, I maintain a motivational-purity condition: the dominant motive for justified disobedience must be principled, not economic self-interest.

Now my verdicts on cases. I think a covert, anonymous tax boycott organised by wealthy professionals to protest loopholes is unjustified — it fails publicity, it is self-interested, and the grievance, though real, does not cross the seriousness threshold for them. But I think an identical boycott by low-paid workers protesting regressive taxation is justified, despite being anonymous and partly self-interested, because the injustice is severe for them and their standing is different. Finally, I think climate activists who secretly damage fossil-fuel infrastructure are engaging in justified disobedience, even though the acts are covert, do not accept punishment, and appeal not to the current community's shared principles but to obligations owed to future generations who are not yet members of our association.

Identify any pair that looks tense but is actually compatible on closer inspection. Then tell me which single revision — giving up one commitment or one verdict — would clean up the most inconsistencies, and which remaining tension would still be the most stubborn. Please also predict what this repaired position says about a case I have not mentioned: a public, non-violent protest by citizens who have been disenfranchised by gerrymandering, aimed solely at overturning the gerrymander itself, but carried out with no intention of accepting arrest.
