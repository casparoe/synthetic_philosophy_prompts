Specifically, I am trying to understand whether casting a ballot for an imperfect candidate expresses a kind of moral complicity with that candidate's wrongs, or whether it can be defended as a purely strategic preference ordering with no endorsement attached.

Write a dialogue between two interlocutors—one who holds that voting carries an irreducible duty of democratic participation whose expressive significance does not depend on being the decisive vote, and one who maintains that in large-scale democracies voting is almost entirely instrumental, so that the lesser-evil choice is best understood as harm-reduction rather than expression, and that this instrumental framing actually exposes the ballot as a poor vehicle for moral testimony. Each speaker should respond directly to the other's arguments about complicity, the statistical irrelevance of individual votes, and whether abstention or blank-ballot submission can serve as a morally cleaner alternative when both available options violate core principles. Ground the exchange in concrete but hypothetical institutional designs—such as a compulsory-voting regime that permits formal blank ballots, or a referendum that couples an attractive welfare provision with an ethically objectionable enforcement mechanism—without referencing specific contemporary politicians, parties, or recent electoral events. The dialogue should present both positions as equally rigorous, allowing for partial convergence if warranted, but avoiding straw-man representations of either the deontological or the strategic view.
