I spent last evening in a long argument with a friend about whether multi-causal explanations in history are genuinely explanatory or just arbitrarily long lists. She kept using the collapse of the Western Roman Empire around 476 CE as her example—climate stress, barbarian migration, bureaucratic overextension, religious transformation—and insisted that if every factor is genuinely causal, then selecting which ones to emphasize is pure narrative preference. I think there is a real philosophical distinction between an event being causally overdetermined and an explanation being redundant, but I need that distinction turned into something I can actually use.

I serve as the senior editor of a quarterly review that publishes brief causal explanations of historical turning points for an educated general audience. I receive about four submissions each week, I have roughly forty minutes to evaluate each piece, I do not possess specialist expertise in every period, and my decision—accept, revise, or reject—must be defensible to the author, our editorial board, and our fact-checker, who knows the sources but not the philosophy of causation. I need a reusable decision procedure: an ordered checklist or flowchart in words that my team can apply to any submission claiming to explain an event through multiple causes.

The procedure should be grounded in the philosophical analysis of overdetermination and multi-causal explanation—specifically, the distinction between a factor being counterfactually redundant for the event (token-causal redundancy), belonging to a class of which at least one member was necessary (type-causal redundancy), and being included for narrative completeness without adding causal information (explanatory redundancy). It should also address diachronic compensation, in which removing an early factor in a long causal chain does not change the outcome because intermediate causes adapt. Do not cite specific texts, scholars, or works; do not ask for a bibliography or reading list.

The deliverable should include:

1. The principle translated into practice: the ordered tests or questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure issues a clear reject, where it issues a clear accept, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the dispute concerns interpretive weight or values rather than fact. Mark every judgment point explicitly as such.

2. Provisions for hard cases: explanations in which every listed factor is redundant but the piece still feels empty; explanations in which one factor is clearly load-bearing but the author distributes causal weight evenly; and explanations involving long diachronic chains with compensatory intermediate steps.

3. Three worked applications:
   a. A submission explaining the collapse of the Western Roman Empire through climate stress, barbarian migration, bureaucratic overextension, and religious transformation.
   b. A submission explaining the wave of revolutions in 1848 across Europe through economic crisis, liberal ideology, and improved communication—where the procedure should reach a verdict I will dislike (specifically, accepting the explanation's even distribution of causal weight despite my strong intuition that economic crisis was dominant).
   c. A third case of your choosing involving a long causal chain—such as a submission on the abandonment of Classic Maya lowland cities around 900 CE attributing collapse to drought, endemic warfare, and agricultural overextension, with the added complication that paleoclimatic records indicate drought was spatially uneven.

4. An explicit statement of what the procedure cannot do: which explanatory disputes it will not resolve, where it is likely to misfire, and which judgments remain irreducibly subjective.

5. Honesty about which steps are genuinely procedural and which are judgment calls dressed in procedural language.
