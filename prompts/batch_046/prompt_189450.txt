I'm working through the regress problem for a seminar paper, and my study group constructed what feels like a trap that catches all three standard positions at once. The argument runs like this, with premises we initially accepted without resistance:

(1) A belief is epistemically justified for a subject only if that subject can discharge what I'll call the citation challenge for it: if pressed with "what makes this rational for you?" they can supply a consideration that is not identical to the belief itself.

(2) Foundationalism holds that basic beliefs are justified without support from other beliefs—so for a basic belief B, the subject has no distinct consideration to cite in B's defense.

(3) Therefore foundationalism permits beliefs that are "justified" yet leave the citation challenge for B permanently unanswered.

(4) Coherentism holds that B is justified by B's membership in a maximally coherent belief system. But coherence is a global, holistic property of the system; citing it answers "why maintain this network?" rather than supplying a consideration specific to B itself.

(5) So coherentism also permits beliefs for which the specific citation challenge for B goes unanswered.

(6) Infinitism requires an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons R₁, R₂, R₃, ... for B. A finite subject can cite R₁, then R₂, but can never cite the complete chain; and citing only R₁ leaves R₁'s own citation challenge open.

(7) Thus infinitism, too, permits beliefs for which the subject can never fully discharge the citation challenge.

(8) So all three theories allow "justified" beliefs that violate the accessibility condition in (1).

(9) But if we reject (1) and allow justification without any answerability, then justification collapses into mere causal reliability or psychological comfort, defeating its normative force.

(10) So either we accept that no belief is ever genuinely justified, or one of our moves in (2) through (7) is wrong.

We keep feeling the conclusion in (10) is far too strong—surely at least one position survives—but we cannot locate the leak. Which step should we reject, and what does giving it up cost each of the three theories? In particular, does the argument smuggle in an equivocation about what "answering the challenge" requires, or does one premise impose a demand that no theory of justification was meant to satisfy?
