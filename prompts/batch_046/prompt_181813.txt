I was discussing this with my partner after our cooperative’s monthly meeting, and I keep returning to a discomfort I cannot quite name. We are a small housing cooperative—twelve units, collectively owned—whose founding charter emphasizes mutual obligation rather than market exchange. One of our original members, who has lived here for over thirty years, recently suffered a spinal injury. To remain in her home she needs structural modifications: widened doorways, a retrofitted bathroom, and a ramp that will consume part of our shared garden. The cost is significant relative to our reserve fund, and the board must vote next week.

The board member leading the discussion is someone I respect—he cares genuinely about our resident—but he has consistently framed our decision in terms that feel conceptually askew. He argues that we should approve the renovation not because she has a claim rooted in her long membership and our collective commitment to her, but because refusing would expose us to liability, depress adjacent property values, and damage our reputation among potential future members, making recruitment harder. He insists this is not merely an additional consideration but the real justification: the moral reason is reducible to these prudential ones, since the cooperative’s survival depends on financial health. I feel that treating her continued presence as a fortunate side-effect of risk management misses the point of the obligation entirely—like using the wrong currency to pay a debt—but I cannot articulate exactly what distinction is being violated, whether my discomfort is philosophically defensible or merely sentimental, and whether recognizing the distinction should change our vote or only the language in which we justify it. Could you help me see the structure of this disagreement?
