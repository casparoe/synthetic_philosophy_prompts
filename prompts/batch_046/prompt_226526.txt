I am writing the final examination for a graduate-level philosophy of science seminar, and the problem of verisimilitude has become personally urgent rather than merely academic: a close family member recently received two competing prognostic models for a serious condition, both claiming closer approximation to the truth of the disease trajectory, yet they share nearly identical predictive success for observable symptoms while diverging radically in their commitments to unobservable pathological mechanisms, leading to incompatible treatment recommendations. I want the exam to treat this not as an abstract puzzle about falsehood-counting but as a live problem about how we compare theories that are all false.

Please construct one essay question for a ninety-minute final exam, worth fifty marks, with an expected answer length of about seven hundred words. The question must be unanswerable by summarizing Popper’s logical definition of verisimilitude. Instead, present students with a self-contained hypothetical: Theory A and Theory B are both false; they entail exactly the same true observational consequences; but Theory A’s false consequences concern macroscopic measurable parameters, whereas Theory B’s false consequences concern microscopic entities that are currently untestable. Ask students to determine whether Popper’s criterion—more true consequences and fewer false ones—can distinguish the two, and then require them to examine whether any purely logical repair can do so without importing ontological or pragmatic judgments about which kinds of error matter. They must also address the structural tension that adding content to increase true consequences typically increases false ones as well, and explain why this makes verisimilitude an unstable comparative notion rather than a simple ordering.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, clearly worded, specifying the word limit, and instructing students not to recite a summary.
2. A model answer of approximately seven hundred words that demonstrates the specific conceptual moves required for top marks: identifying the symmetry problem in the logical account, showing why shared language is a precondition for comparing consequence-sets, exposing the smuggling of ontological weight into apparently formal comparisons, and articulating why predictive success does not map neatly onto proximity to truth.
3. A marking scheme out of fifty that separates an A-range answer (forty-five to fifty) from a B-range answer (thirty-eight to forty-two) by naming the precise arguments each must contain—such as the distinction between content-increase and truth-approach, or the failure of logical verisimilitude to handle incommensurable falsehood-types—and listing the specific mistakes that should cost marks, including conflating verisimilitude with empirical adequacy, treating false-consequence counting as value-neutral, or ignoring the problem that both theories may have infinite false logical consequences.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not invoke specific named theories or figures beyond the core logical account of verisimilitude that students have encountered in the course. Keep all scenarios self-contained so that the question can be answered without any external research.
