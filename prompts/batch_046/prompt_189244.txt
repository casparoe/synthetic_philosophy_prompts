I am revising the syllabus for my upper-division seminar on deception and moral responsibility, and after a frustrating discussion with colleagues about whether misleading is merely a derivative wrong—parasitic on the norms of truthful assertion—I want to test the distinction against a literary case rather than another hypothetical. For the unit on lying versus misleading, I plan to assign Shakespeare's Othello, since the tragedy is driven almost entirely by Iago's manipulation of belief without relying on a simple catalog of false statements. I want a philosophical reading that takes both the play and the conceptual distinction seriously: distinguish the moments where Iago asserts outright falsehoods from those where he induces false belief through selective disclosure, conversational implicature, or strategic silence—particularly in the handkerchief episode and in the conversation about Cassio's supposed dreams. Does Othello treat these mechanisms differently? Is Iago's culpability heightened by the fact that he rarely asserts what is false, instead forcing Othello to assemble the deception himself from true fragments?

The reading should not reduce the play to an illustration of analytic philosophy of language, nor should it distort the philosophical distinction—say, by claiming that all deception is ultimately lying—to preserve the dramatic unity. The play's own preoccupations with racial otherness, marital possession, and the fragility of reputation must remain visible, and if the drama resists the lie/mislead distinction—if, for instance, Othello's own deceptions to the Venetian senate about his marriage blur the boundary—then the honest critic should say so and explain what that resistance teaches us. I am especially interested in the claim that misleading is morally worse than lying because it exploits the victim's rational agency, turning him into an accomplice in his own deception; does the play support that view, or does it suggest that the damage is identical regardless of the linguistic form? Finally, what does Othello add to the philosophical debate that a clean conceptual argument about assertion and implicature cannot capture? I intend to use this for students who have already read the standard literature on deception but who still treat explicit lying as the paradigmatic moral failure; my aim is to complicate that assumption without letting them think the distinction is merely verbal. Please avoid citations and do not quote the text verbatim—paraphrase scenes—since I want the class to work from memory and see whether the philosophical structure survives without textual crutches.
