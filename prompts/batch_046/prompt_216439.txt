I'm presenting a job-market paper to a philosophy department colloquium composed mainly of philosophers of action and moral responsibility: roughly half are compatibilists who take reasons-responsiveness to be sufficient for free will, a quarter are libertarians who think agent-causal power is necessary, and the rest work in moral psychology and are suspicious of any view that sounds like it collapses agency into mechanism. My thesis is that sphexishness—the mechanically rigid, invariant repetition characteristic of the sphex wasp—has been misread in the literature as the antithesis of free will. I defend what I call "binding rigidity": the claim that a specific form of mechanical fixity, in which prior commitments become causally locked so that deviation requires not mere flexibility but a breakdown of the self's structure, is actually a precondition for genuine agency rather than its enemy. Without such rigidity, I argue, flexible choice is just noise without an author; with it, determinism is compatible not merely with responsibility but with the kind of robust selfhood that makes responsibility meaningful. I develop this by contrasting Frankfurt-style hierarchical models, where higher-order volitions are supposed to stabilize desire, with a model in which stabilization happens through irreversible binding that makes certain future paths structurally unavailable.

Prepare me for the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is. For each objection: phrase it in the vocabulary they would use—such as reasons-responsiveness, agent causation, hierarchical identification, the disappearance of the agent, or the difference between being caused and being compelled—say whether it carries real force, what I should concede up front, and how to reply within the time available in a Q&A. Identify the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, explain why it is especially dangerous to my argument, and note precisely where my reply is weakest. Separate what is settled in the free-will literature from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present my binding-rigidity view as if it were the consensus.
