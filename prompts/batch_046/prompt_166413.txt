Exam question:
In Russell's essay "On Denoting," he argues that definite descriptions are not referring expressions but quantificational phrases—"incomplete symbols" that disappear upon logical analysis. First, explain Russell's analysis of the sentence "The present King of France is bald," with particular attention to why he thinks it avoids ontological commitment to a non-existent monarch. Second, analyze "The present King of France is not bald": does Russell treat this as having a single, unambiguous logical form, or does he recognize an ambiguity in the scope of the negation? In your answer, distinguish Russell's notion of an incomplete symbol from Frege's distinction between sense and reference, and briefly assess whether Russell's quantificational treatment captures the intuition that in using such a sentence we seem to be speaking about a particular individual.

Student answer:
Russell holds that "the present King of France" is not a proper name but an incomplete symbol that vanishes upon logical analysis. For "The present King of France is bald," Russell offers the analysis: there exists an x such that x is the present King of France, for all y if y is the present King of France then y is identical to x, and x is bald. Because there is in fact no present King of France, the existence clause is unsatisfied, so the statement is false. This is advantageous because we can deny the sentence without being forced to admit that there exists a king who happens to lack the property of baldness.

When we turn to "The present King of France is not bald," Russell applies the same pattern but places the negation inside the predicate, yielding: there exists an x such that x is the present King of France, x is unique in this respect, and x is not bald. Since no such x exists, this statement is also false. Thus both the affirmative and the negative sentences come out false, which allows us to reject them both without ontological commitment to a non-existent king. The intuition that we are talking about a specific person is therefore a grammatical illusion; logically, both sentences are general claims about uniqueness that fail to be satisfied. Russell's view that descriptions have meaning only within a complete proposition parallels Frege's sense-reference distinction, except that for Russell the description contributes only quantificational structure rather than a Fregean sense. Consequently, the analysis succeeds in eliminating the king from our ontology while preserving precise logical form.
