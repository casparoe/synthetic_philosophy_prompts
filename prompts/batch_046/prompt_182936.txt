Next week I present to our philosophy of mind and language seminar a thesis I call layered institutional derivation. I grant Searle's claim that original intentionality requires specific biological causation and that bare syntax lacks intrinsic semantics, but I argue that Dennett's two-bitser framework — the distinction between the design stance and the intentional stance — reveals an unacknowledged category in Searle's taxonomy. In multi-layered institutional artifacts such as binding contracts, constitutional provisions, or currency regimes, derived intentionality can be recursively stacked so that the predictive and explanatory patterns generated by adopting the intentional stance become structurally and causally inseparable from those we attribute to conscious agents. If Searle classifies these as merely derived, he must either deny that social institutions carry any genuine intentional properties, undermining his own account of collective intentionality, or concede that functional convergence can bridge the syntax-semantics gap he treats as absolute. The audience is about twelve people: mostly Searlean biological naturalists and phenomenologists who treat consciousness as a non-functional substrate and regard the Chinese Room argument as decisive, plus two functionalists well-versed in Dennett who are sympathetic but worry that applying the stance framework to legal and economic institutions overextends it. They care deeply about preserving the original-derived boundary without collapsing into eliminativism or behaviorism.
