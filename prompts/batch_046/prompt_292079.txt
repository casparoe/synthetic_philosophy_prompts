In a philosophy-of-biology seminar, a colleague routinely deploys the line "spandrels are only adaptations whose stories we haven't finished telling" to shut down non-adaptive explanations. She used it most recently to reject my proposal that humans' compulsive drive for narrative closure in causal reasoning might be a spandrel—an architectural byproduct of selection for language capacity—rather than a direct adaptation for explanation itself. I want to know exactly what work that slogan could be doing.

Can you lay out the precise, distinct theses it might express? I am looking for a menu: the purely epistemic reading (we currently lack the adaptive narrative), the ontological reading (every structural feature ultimately traces to some selective history), the methodological reading (adaptive hypotheses should be privileged by default), the taxonomic reading ("spandrel" is just a temporary placeholder in evolutionary classification), and any stronger or weaker variants between these poles. For each, indicate whether it is trivial, whether it is defensible, whether it is false, and—most importantly—whether it actually licenses dismissing my narrative-closure example as "merely an adaptation with an incomplete story."

Please also say which of these readings, if any, remain consistent with Gould and Lewontin's original critique of the Panglossian paradigm, and identify the kind of further case—perhaps a clearly deleterious trait that persists solely through genetic linkage to a beneficial allele—that would force a choice among the remaining readings.
