I am a retired physicist who has recently begun organizing a monthly philosophy workshop for a group of about twelve retired scientists and engineers; we meet for ninety minutes, have just completed introductory readings in formal social choice theory, and are now turning to its applications in democratic design. Please design an in-class deliberative game—complete with assigned roles, symbolic payoff points, printed materials, and a structured timeline—that makes the participants feel the practical force of Arrow's impossibility theorem within the context of direct democracy and multi-issue referendum bundles, analogous to California-style ballot propositions or Swiss federal popular initiatives. The setup should place the group in the role of a constitutional convention that must rank three interdependent policy packages—energy infrastructure, water rights, and climate adaptation—using only majority-rule aggregation over pairwise comparisons. Crucially, the rules must be arranged so that participants construct the aggregation mechanism themselves in round one; then, in round two, a fourth "hidden" option that was previously off the table is revealed, causing the collective ranking they produced to cycle or reverse in a way they did not anticipate. Before anyone names Arrow's conditions, the group should experience the failure of transitivity or the emergence of a de facto dictator—for example, through agenda-setting power assigned to one role. Include a debrief that maps each round onto unrestricted domain, non-dictatorship, Pareto efficiency, and independence of irrelevant alternatives; tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—especially the tendency of scientifically trained participants to propose a weighted scoring rule as an escape—and how to manage that reaction; and be frank about which feature of Arrow's result the activity cannot fully reproduce, specifically the formal guarantee that the impossibility holds for all possible preference profiles rather than merely for the single profile the group happens to generate. The participants should come away seeing that the impossibility is not an abstract mathematical curiosity but an unavoidable design constraint whenever a direct democracy attempts to bundle interdependent policies into a single referendum ranking. Please provide the deliverable in American English.
