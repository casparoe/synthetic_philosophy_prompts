I'm preparing a graduate seminar on philosophy of education, and I've been working through Alfred North Whitehead's account of learning as a rhythmic movement through stages of romance, precision, and generalization, along with his concern that ideas received without active application become inert. I'm trying to apply this not as an abstract schema but as a critical lens on a specific cultural text, and I want to avoid the usual readings that treat the philosophy as a mere backdrop.

Read the film Dead Poets Society through this framework. The narrative centers on a group of students at a rigid preparatory school who form a clandestine poetry society led by an unconventional teacher, Mr. Keating, who urges them to seize the day and think for themselves. One student, Neil Perry, discovers a passion for acting but ultimately takes his own life when his father forbids it. How might Whitehead's three stages illuminate the boys' experience? Does Keating's pedagogy remain stuck in the stage of romance—offering emotional awakening and collective enthusiasm—without guiding the students through the disciplined work of precision and the integrative capacity of generalization? Examine Neil's tragedy in particular: is his idea of artistic self-expression an inert idea, received with passion but never developed into a practical, disciplined capacity that could have enabled him to negotiate with his father rather than collapse before him?

At the same time, I want the reading to be faithful in both directions. Where does the film resist Whitehead's optimism about educational freedom? The secret society's rituals—standing on desks, meeting in caves—suggest a collective freedom, yet they occur entirely within and against the institution rather than transforming it. Does this imply that romance without structural change becomes reactive and dangerous, and if so, what does that contribute to Whitehead's own argument that genuine education requires both liberty and rigorous form? I'm writing this for students who know the film well but are unfamiliar with Whitehead, so please develop the conceptual analysis clearly without relying on specific textual citations or page references; the argument should stand on its philosophical coherence rather than scholarship.
