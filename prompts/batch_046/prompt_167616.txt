Next month I present a chapter from my dissertation to an interdisciplinary philosophy of science workshop, defending what I call the taste-model of Kuhnian incommensurability. My central claim is that the deepest disagreements between paradigms are not failures of semantic translation—scientists can usually learn each other’s vocabulary, formalisms, and predictive algorithms well enough to communicate—but rather a pragmatic incommensurability in how shared epistemic values are weighted. I argue that simplicity, predictive scope, and explanatory coherence function structurally like aesthetic criteria: each paradigm assigns them different relative priorities, so that when a chemical-revolution-style shift occurs, the dispute is closer to a disagreement about whether a painting’s boldness outweighs its technical precision than to a disagreement about bare observable facts. This means paradigm choice retains an irreducibly evaluative dimension that empirical anomaly resolution alone cannot settle, without collapsing into irrationalism or mere sociology.

The audience is precisely mixed and will come at me from different angles. There are two analytic philosophers of language who treat Quinean translation manuals as sufficient for scientific communication and who think Kuhn overstated semantic barriers; a historian of science specializing in the chemical revolution who believes Lavoisier’s victory was driven by uncontroversial measurement improvements rather than by shifts in value-weighting; a Kuhn scholar who insists that incommensurability is fundamentally taxonomic—world-change through altered kind-structures—not about preference ordering among shared virtues; and a science-studies scholar concerned that framing science as taste-based removes its normative authority and licenses a damaging relativism. I was discussing this framing with colleagues last week and realized I have not fully anticipated how the aesthetic analogy will land with people who care about scientific objectivity.

Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each of these audience members would actually phrase them, not as generic strawmen. For each objection, indicate what I should concede, what I must hold, and where my reply is weakest—particularly on the point that value-weighting sounds subjective even if I deny it is arbitrary. Include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody raises, explain why it is the most damaging to the taste-model specifically, and then give me a committed defense rather than a menu of escape routes: take a definite stand on whether the aesthetic analogy undermines scientific objectivity, argue for that stand with explicit reasons, and do not simply list competing interpretations and leave the choice to me. I need a prep sheet, not a survey of options.
