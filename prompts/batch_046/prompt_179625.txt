I'm convening a monthly seminar for advanced undergraduates and local civic organizers who have read some introductory political theory but have no specialized training in philosophy of religion or constitutional law. For our next meeting I want to guide them through the problem of public reason and religious arguments in democratic politics—not as an abstract doctrine, but as a practical question about what kinds of reasons can legitimately support coercive law in a plural society. I need a teaching plan for a single two-hour session, structured as an explanatory arc rather than a lecture.

Specifically: what conceptual distinction must be firmly in place before the normative constraint can even appear coherent to them—for instance, the difference between the shared political forum and the background culture, or between justification and motivation—so that they do not mistake the issue for mere etiquette; what single concrete scenario should carry the entire session, such as a municipal land-use conflict in which both a religious congregation and a secular environmental coalition invoke comprehensive moral visions about human flourishing and the common good, and what that scenario must include and exclude so that it illuminates the structural problem rather than reducing it to identity politics or group conflict; the predictable misconceptions they will form in sequence, such as thinking public reason demands that citizens suppress their faith entirely in private life, or conversely that sincere conviction alone makes any argument publicly acceptable, together with the precise point in the arc where each misconception should be anticipated and defused; a brief check—one targeted question or small-group task—that would reveal whether they have grasped the dependency structure rather than merely adopted a posture of inclusive tolerance; and, finally, what portion of the broader theoretical debate I should explicitly set aside as beyond this session's scope and name as such. The audience will arrive assuming the debate is simply about being respectful to believers; the plan should show them why the problem is about the legitimacy of coercion in conditions of reasonable pluralism.
