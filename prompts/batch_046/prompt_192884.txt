I'm planning a single 50-minute session for first-year philosophy-of-mind students who understand basic neural-network intuition but have no background in 1980s cognitive science. The goal is to make Hofstadter's careenium and symmball problem feel urgent rather than quaint: do symbolic patterns push the physical substrate around, or is substrate causation always doing the work with symbols only in the eye of the observer?

Plan the explanatory arc in this order and justify the dependencies: first establish uncontroversial substrate causation (marbles or voltage states bumping into neighbors), then show how stable high-level clusters emerge and get read as symbols, then run the green-light cascade that produces an "utterance," and only then pose the pushing question. Specify the one carrying example you will build on—what physical substrate it must display, what symbolic pattern must be readable within it, and what it must deliberately lack (no homunculus, no post-hoc interpreter, no non-physical causal bridge). Map the misconceptions students will bring in the sequence they typically arrive—reification of symbols as tiny objects, confusion of descriptive levels with causal powers, the dualist assumption that "dumb" matter needs symbolic organization, and the eliminativist leap that if symbols don't push then intentionality is illusory—and state exactly where in the arc each should be defused. Include a modern injection: use a synthetic analogy comparing the symmball cascade to next-token prediction in a transformer, but flag its limits so it doesn't smuggle in anthropomorphism. Give the check for understanding that distinguishes grasping the idea from mere discomfort or parroting—specifically, ask them to redescribe the cascade using only substrate vocabulary, with mental-state terms banned. Explicitly name what you will leave out and admit it (the full self-reference discussion of "I," formal symbol-grounding theorems, consciousness debates). Keep the response to no more than 350 words. At the end, state how confident you are in this plan and single out the one step—likely the modern token-prediction analogy—where you are least sure it clarifies rather than confuses the substrate-symbol distinction.
