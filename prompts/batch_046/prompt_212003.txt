As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy of biology, I keep encountering the slogan that DNA is the code of life—in bioinformatics documentation, in synthetic-biology marketing, and in popular arguments about whether organisms are literally programmed or whether genetic reductionism is justified. In each setting the phrase does different work: sometimes it licenses treating genomes as readable data structures; sometimes it is used to claim that development is algorithmic; sometimes it is invoked to suggest that DNA carries semantic meaning about the organism in the same way source code carries meaning about a program.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could express? I want a menu of readings—such as a literal programming-language analogy (with syntax, semantics, and an interpreter/compiler), an information-theoretic encoding in the Shannon sense, an architectural blueprint metaphor, a cryptographic cipher that requires a decoder, and any other philosophically relevant variant. For each, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false; and say which version is actually being invoked when people use the slogan to defend genetic determinism, to justify synthetic biology, or to resist the idea that DNA alone fixes phenotype.

In particular, I want to know whether any defensible reading requires attributing semantic content or intentionality to the transcription machinery—treating the ribosome as an interpreter that decodes meaning—or whether it can be understood as purely mechanistic, without a philosophically loaded "decoder." Which of these readings genuinely supports the argumentative moves people make with the phrase, and which ones collapse once the analogy is pressed?
