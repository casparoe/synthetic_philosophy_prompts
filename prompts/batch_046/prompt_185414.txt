People who defend ultra-formal translation often compare it to architecture: the rhyme scheme and meter are the load-bearing walls, and the literal meaning is just furniture you can shift around. They point to experiments like Le Ton beau de Marot, where keeping the "tone" meant rewriting almost every word but preserving the form. I want to check how far that building analogy goes — what's the wall, what's the furniture, and where does it break, especialy since in poetry the "walls" are built from sounds that carry meaning themselves? And if we imagine an added constraint where each rhyme-word must be a false cognate of the original (same root, opposite sense in the target language), does the analogy still support keeping the rhyme at all costs, or does it show that form and content aren't seperate materials you can just rearrange?
