Having retired from experimental physics and only recently begun studying philosophy of history, I am struck by how conceptually unclear it is to “blame a nation” for the First World War. Please write a formal dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors. Dr. A defends a refined Fischer-style view: that the German leadership’s deliberate risk-acceptance in July 1914, together with documented expansionist planning, makes the Reich primarily causally and morally responsible. Dr. B rejects this as a category error, arguing that nation-states lack the unified intentionality required for moral blame, that collective responsibility cannot be distributed across bureaucratic institutions in the way Dr. A suggests, and that structural dynamics—alliance rigidity, institutional path dependence, and miscommunication—explain the crisis better than singular agential choice. They should respond directly to each other’s actual points, especially on whether counterfactual analysis of the July Crisis supports singular attribution and on whether “Germany” can be treated as an intentional agent. Please also separate, either in the dialogue or in a brief framing note, what is historically settled (e.g., the chronology of the crisis and the existence of expansionist documents) from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present either the Fischer interpretation or its structuralist rejection as the undisputed consensus.
