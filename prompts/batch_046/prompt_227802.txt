I am drafting a 90-minute essay examination for a graduate seminar on the sociology of moral norms. I need a single question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme out of 20, that forces students to go beyond summarizing the honor-based account of moral change associated with Bernard Williams and the interest-and-argument thesis associated with Kwame Anthony Appiah regarding practices such as dueling and slavery. To prevent mere recitation, the scenario must be engineered rather than historical: ask students to explain the disappearance of a realistic institutional norm—call it public debt-shaming by craft-guild authorities in a mercantile economy—in which economic interests and honor codes point in opposite directions. Specifically, the guild authorities retain a direct economic incentive to maintain the practice, yet the social meaning of compliance has inverted: where once enduring public shaming signaled honorable stoicism, it now signals low status and exclusion from honorable networks. The exam prompt should require students to determine whether the practice ends through internal rational argument, shifting economic interest, or honor-structure collapse, and—crucially—to identify the inversion of the honor signal as the structural feature that separates an honor explanation from an interest explanation in this confounded case. The model answer must reconstruct both frameworks with precision, apply them to the engineered scenario, show why the economic-interest explanation fails despite the presence of incentivized actors, and critically evaluate whether the inversion feature demonstrates that honor explanations are irreducible to interest-based ones. The marking scheme should organize evaluation around five must-hit points—accurate reconstruction of the honor mechanism; accurate reconstruction of the dual-track model; correct application to the engineered case; correct identification of the inversion feature; and a critical assessment of reducibility—assign specific marks to each totaling 20, and for each point note the typical student error that should cost marks, such as conflating honor collapse with mere preference change or treating economic incentive as sufficient despite the signal inversion. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to specific works by page number. Keep the scenario ordinary and realistic—no exotic physics, no omniscient observers, no perfect predictors—so that a student without web access could answer it entirely through conceptual analysis.
