Design a 75-minute seminar activity for about 14 philosophy undergraduates that makes them feel the performative contradiction hidden in Benjamin Franklin’s quantitative system for moral perfection—specifically, the tension between rigorous self-surveillance of virtue (including humility) and the inevitable generation of pride through the very diligence of tracking one’s failures. Give the students paper “moral ledgers” listing Franklin’s thirteen virtues with columns for daily incidents, self-scores (0–3), and one-sentence justifications; provide five brief social scenario cards describing small moral choices (e.g., correcting an error, refusing to boast, admitting fault publicly). In Round 1 they complete entries for three scenarios, rating themselves on all virtues including Humility based on their awareness of other failures. In Round 2 they continue with two more scenarios, but now the facilitator introduces a hidden “Industry Score”: a visible token or badge awarded to the student whose ledger shows the most entries, the most detailed justifications, and the most consistent tracking—without warning that this rewards the opposite of the virtue being pursued. In Round 3 students exchange ledgers with a partner and are instructed to hunt for linguistic signs of “false humility” or self-congratulatory diligence in the partner’s Humility row and justification sentences, marking suspected phrases. Only then does the facilitator reveal that Franklin admitted he never achieved perfect humility and that others perceived his attempt as prideful; the debrief should map (a) the ledger mechanics onto Franklin’s “little book” accounting method, (b) the hidden Industry Score onto the structural incentive for visible moral labor rather than genuine improvement, (c) the partner review onto Franklin’s observation that external observers saw his pride, and (d) the students’ own surprise at finding boastful language in their humility entries onto the second-order problem of tracking a virtue that is undermined by awareness of one’s tracking. Include explicit rules for materials and payoffs (paper, pens, scenario cards, a non-monetary token), a 15-minute debrief timeline, an honest prediction of what will go wrong on first run (students initially write sincerely humble entries and miss the contradiction until forced to read their own justifications aloud as if they were another person), and a frank statement of which feature of Franklin’s view the activity cannot reproduce—namely, the lifelong temporal arc and the deistic framework in which he believed divine approval was the ultimate audience for his moral accounting—so the facilitator must supply that context verbally rather than through the game mechanics. The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should avoid verbatim quotations; paraphrase Franklin’s admission about humility and pride without quotation marks.
