I've been trying to work through the tension between Cartesian transparency and Williamson-style anti-luminosity, and I keep returning to a hypothetical stress-test. Suppose Descartes had lived into the early 2000s and encountered the argument that mental states fail to be luminous—that one can know p without being in a position to know that one knows p—supported specifically by sorites-style reasoning about incremental shifts in confidence that escape first-person detection. Given his commitment that clear and distinct perceptions carry a special kind of self-certifying authority, how do you think he would have responded? Would he have tried to restrict luminosity to a narrow class of maximally clear perceptions, conceding that ordinary knowledge lacks second-order accessibility? Or would he have rejected the graded-vagueness premises driving the anti-luminosity result, insisting that the mind's access to its own states is discrete and binary rather than susceptible to imperceptible erosion? I'm asking less for a historical reconstruction than for a conceptual probe: if we grant Descartes his core epistemology but deny the KK principle, which of his foundationalist commitments survive intact, which ones must be modified, and does the failure of luminosity undermine the very idea of a self-certifying cogito?
