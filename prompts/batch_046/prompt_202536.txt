I am preparing a proposal for a three-month independent study fellowship, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a rigorous, tractable research plan. The domain is Buddhist anatta (no-self) and its relationship to contemporary analytic philosophy of personal identity. My suspicion is that the debate has stalled because philosophers treat anatta either as a straightforward metaphysical denial of a Cartesian substance — which makes it compatible with psychological continuity theories — or as a purely soteriological exhortation to detach from ego, which makes it philosophically inert. I think this misses the specific reductionist ontology found in Abhidharma thought: the claim that experience is constituted by discrete, momentary mental events (cittas) with no enduring owner or substrate. I want the proposal to ask whether this reductionism, taken seriously rather than watered down, can be reconciled with a 'thin' account of personal identity if we borrow the phenomenological concept of 'mineness' — the pre-reflective sense that mental states are experienced as belonging to one without belonging to a substantive self. Specifically, can such a framework preserve enough continuity to make sense of moral responsibility and the intelligibility of karma and rebirth without smuggling in a transmigrating soul?

The proposal should contain: a precise statement of the research question; an explanation of why existing positions in both Buddhist studies and analytic philosophy leave this particular formulation open; a clear method (conceptual analysis of anicca, dukkha, and related terms in accessible translations, combined with engagement with reductionist and narrative-identity accounts) with a realistic phase-by-phase plan for a twelve-week project; a description of what a successful result would look like (for instance, a coherent 'thin-continuity' model) and what failure would look like (for example, the framework either collapsing into pure elimination of agency or covertly reintroducing a substantial self); and an honest assessment of scope, including the risk that the conceptual distance between phenomenology and Abhidharma exegesis proves too great for a single researcher. Please write in British English, do not include bibliographies or reading lists, and ensure the framing does not depend on access to obscure primary sources or specialised philological training.
