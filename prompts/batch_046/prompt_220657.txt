In constructing the moral architecture of a novel set within a fully deterministic causal order, I find myself delivering conflicting verdicts about responsibility that I would like to see systematized. Here are my judgments. A character who, after sustained reflection, endorses a harmful act as morally correct and executes it without coercion is fully responsible, even if the values guiding her were formed in a childhood she never chose. A character who performs an identical act because a concealed neural implant issues an irresistible impulse she never endorses is not responsible. A character who acts heroically against her deepest inclinations because she actively embraces the reason to save a stranger is responsible, and indeed more fully so. A character living in a universe where every brain state was fixed at the origin of time deliberates, weighs reasons, and chooses wrongly; provided she was not externally manipulated, I still hold her responsible despite the physical inevitability of her act. Finally, a character whose every decision was designed by an engineer with perfect foresight, yet who experiences her deliberation as genuinely open, strikes me as responsible, though I am less certain here.

These cases seem to demand that determinism does not excuse, while hidden manipulation does, yet I cannot state the boundary precisely. Is there a single principle—perhaps grounded in reflective endorsement, in the absence of alien causal origins, or in the transparency of one's reasons to an internal narrative perspective—that captures all five verdicts? If not, which judgment should I surrender? And what would the resulting principle predict for the case I keep revising: a figure whose reasons are entirely authentic, but whose brain was assembled neuron by neuron by a benevolent architect who foresaw every choice?
