I've been trying to work out, on my own, whether a government can claim real legitimacy simply because it delivers prosperity and public order, even if it never allows citizens to vote out its leaders. The examples of China and Singapore come up constantly in these discussions, but I find myself stuck on a distinction I keep encountering in passing: the difference between what some call "output legitimacy"—being justified by results—and "input legitimacy"—being justified by the consent of the governed. Before I can think clearly about authoritarian success stories, I need to understand what follows philosophically if we take performance legitimacy seriously rather than treating it as a cynical excuse for maintaining power.

In particular, I'm interested in three implications that seem somewhat hidden beneath the usual debate. First, if performance legitimacy is genuinely a form of moral justification and not just stability by another name, does it entail that the regime must be replaceable should its performance collapse, or can a one-party state claim a kind of permanent moral credit based on past growth and order? Second, I have a more specific worry about epistemic responsiveness: if delivering the right goods requires knowing what citizens actually need, then regimes that suppress information and dissent might undermine their own legitimacy claim even while maintaining high growth and low crime. Does this mean performance legitimacy secretly depends on democratic feedback mechanisms, or can an authoritarian bureaucracy be epistemically responsive without political competition? Third, I wonder whether we need to distinguish between "thin" order—the mere absence of violence and chaos—and "thick" order, which includes protections for rights and dissent; if prosperity is paired only with thin order, does that weaken the justification, and where exactly should the line be drawn?

I'm not trained in political philosophy, so I may be conflating legitimacy with mere effectiveness, or I may be smuggling in democratic assumptions that don't properly belong in this framework. If my framing rests on a confused premise—say, if performance legitimacy is not actually a coherent philosophical position but only a descriptive label, or if I'm treating prosperity as sufficient when it can only ever be necessary—please tell me plainly rather than answering politely within the confusion. I want the most important implications of taking this idea seriously, including any that show why it might fail on its own terms.
