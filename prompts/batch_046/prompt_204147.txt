I have observed a recurring structural configuration in debates about the boundaries of the democratic demos that does not map cleanly onto familiar distinctions. Consider cases where a polity considers extending participatory rights to a proxy group—such as youth delegates meant to represent future generations, or resident advocates meant to represent non-citizen migrants—on the grounds that the absent or not-yet-existing parties are affected by present decisions. Once seated, however, these proxies typically exercise independent judgment and pursue their own present interests rather than acting as bound fiduciaries for the absent principals. Consequently, the boundary is expanded by appealing to an external-affect principle (those outside are impacted and therefore merit representation), yet the internal functioning of the expanded body operates on an autonomy principle (members decide according to their own preferences). The result is a kind of boundary logic that borrows legitimacy from one criterion and exercises authority according to another.

This is not the standard non-identity problem, which concerns harm across possible worlds, nor is it a conventional trustee or delegated-representation arrangement, because the independence of the proxy is structurally embedded rather than a failure of execution. Is there an established concept in democratic theory that captures this precise mismatch—where inclusion is justified by the interests of non-members, but the included members behave as ordinary autonomous stakeholders? If no such concept exists, can you articulate a rigorous definition that distinguishes it from stakeholder pluralism, from delegated authority, and from the all-affected-interests principle itself, specifying both its core conditions and its boundary cases?
