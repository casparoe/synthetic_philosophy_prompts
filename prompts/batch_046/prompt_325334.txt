I was discussing with a friend late last night after a long seminar on philosophy of science, and we ended up in a heated disagreement about Milton Friedman’s so-called F-twist and how it applies to contemporary climate modeling. My friend insisted that Friedman’s defense of unrealistic assumptions entails a stronger thesis than is usually recognized: not merely that false premises are tolerable if the predictions are accurate, but that the descriptive falsehood of an assumption is itself a positive methodological virtue, because it demonstrates that the model is operating at the correct level of abstraction rather than being contaminated by empirical noise. I pushed back by saying that Friedman only argued for instrumental evaluation—judge the theory by its predictions—but my friend claimed this distinction collapses once you realize that all successful scientific theories employ deliberate simplifications, so the degree of unrealism directly tracks theoretical depth.

Specifically, we were considering an integrated assessment model that predicts global temperature trajectories by treating regional ocean-atmosphere feedback loops as homogeneous boundary conditions—an assumption that is demonstrably false at local scales. My friend argued that according to the F-twist, the more flagrantly this assumption violates empirical meteorology, the more it confirms the model’s scientific legitimacy, because the falsehood proves the model is not overfitting to local anomalies. I think this reverses Friedman’s logic: he said unrealistic premises don’t invalidate a theory if its predictions work, not that unrealistic premises cause or certify predictive success. Yet I couldn’t quite articulate why applying the F-twist to climate science doesn’t turn descriptive inaccuracy into an explanatory resource. Could you clarify whether there is any coherent reading of Friedman on which the empirical falsity of a model’s core idealizations actually contributes to—rather than merely coexists with—its predictive power, particularly in cases where the omission of real-world heterogeneity is a deliberate tractability move rather than an empirical claim?
