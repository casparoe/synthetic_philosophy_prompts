I've been trying to make sense of why I feel I should follow most laws even when I disagree with them, yet I also think there are moments when deliberately breaking a law—openly and for a principled reason—isn't just wrong but actually part of being a good citizen. The standard views I've heard seem to split between saying we always have a strong duty to obey unless the law is monstrous, and saying there's basically no special duty to obey at all. I want to defend a different picture: think of political obligation as a kind of running moral account. Every time a citizen complies with a burdensome law they could have evaded—paying taxes they resent, serving on a jury, obeying a regulation they think is foolish—they earn a kind of credit. Those credits don't cancel the general duty, but they do mean that when something genuinely important is at stake, a person can "spend" some of that accumulated standing to break a specific law in protest, accept the legal consequences, and still remain within the bounds of a justified political relationship. Please argue for this "moral-account" view of obligation and disobedience. Show why it handles the intuition that civil disobedience is different from ordinary crime, explain why it doesn't collapse into "break any law you dislike," and defend it against both the strict-obedience view and the no-obligation view. I'd like the answer in American English, and please don't include bibliographies or citations—I'm asking for the reasoning itself, not a literature review.
