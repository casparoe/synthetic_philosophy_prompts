As a retired physicist who has lately taken up philosophy, I have been thinking about Nietzsche's threefold distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical uses of history, particularly as it might apply to scientific practice rather than to culture generally. I would like you to argue for the following position: in contemporary experimental science, institutional mandates for exhaustive data preservation, open replication archives, and the permanent retention of negative results have made the antiquarian mode pathologically dominant, and have done so by fusing it with the critical mode into a hybrid practice—let us call it archival criticism—where every preserved failed replication serves simultaneously as historical record and as direct verdict against the original study. Defend the claim that this fusion suppresses the monumental mode, since genuine theoretical innovation requires selective forgetting and the reconstruction of exemplary achievements rather than exhaustive documentation, and that it also misdirects critical attention away from conceptual frameworks and toward mere empirical discrepancies. Please do not offer bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; build the case from the logic of the distinction itself and from general knowledge of how scientific institutions operate. In your answer, indicate how confident you are in each major step, and single out the specific claim about which you are least sure.
