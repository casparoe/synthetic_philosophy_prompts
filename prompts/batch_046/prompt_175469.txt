In our philosophy of politics course, two classmates argued about whether there is a fact of the matter about global violence declining. Maya cited the well-known per-capita declines in homicide and battle deaths, insisting that absolute numbers are misleading because population has grown, and that expanding "violence" to cover structural harm or ecological damage makes the claim empirically untestable—so the reduction is real on the only precise metric. Jordan replied that per-capita framing is itself normative: it treats a death in a larger population as less significant, and that excluding institutional and ecological harms arbitrarily restricts the concept to direct physical assault. Maya answered that epidemiology uses per-capita rates precisely to avoid such distortions, and that without a bounded definition the thesis becomes unfalsifiable. Jordan countered that falsifiability is not the only virtue—if the concept genuinely includes systemic harms, then a precise but false metric is worse than a broad true one. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate point by point: is the per-capita metric normatively neutral, does broadening the definition necessarily destroy empirical tractability, and is the disagreement conceptual or factual? Finally, say whether there is genuinely a fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, or whether the answer depends on framing choices that are not themselves resolvable by evidence.
