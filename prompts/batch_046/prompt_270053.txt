I serve on a small regional recovery board that distributes limited public funds after seasonal floods and winter storms across neighborhoods that start from very different conditions. Over the past year I have been trying to work out, on my own, how to apply what I understand as prioritarian reasoning — the idea that benefits to people who are already worse off should carry more weight than the same benefits to people who are well off — without falling into what I have heard called the leveling-down problem: the worry that we might end up reducing the welfare of the better-off without actually improving anyone who is worse off, just to make conditions more equal. I am not an academic; I need something I can actually run in a meeting with other non-specialists, and I need it to be honest about where judgment takes over from rules.

Please write a reusable decision procedure — a checklist in strict order, with the conditions that must be met at each step — that my board can apply to any proposal for allocating recovery resources. The procedure should ask, in sequence, about the baseline welfare tier of the affected groups (using the three-tier matrix my agency actually uses: critical, stressed, stable), about whether the proposal delivers a direct welfare improvement to anyone in a lower tier, about whether it takes resources away from a higher-tier group, and about whether any such reduction produces a corresponding gain for a lower tier. At each step, state what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, where the procedure must stop and reject the proposal, and where it must hand the decision to a human judgment call because the issue is one of values rather than facts. Include a small set of test conditions — short sentence frames the board can apply — that let us check whether a substitution of beneficiaries or a change in funding source alters the moral structure of the proposal.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete cases: first, a proposal to fund emergency heating subsidies in a critical-tier neighborhood versus repairing roads in a stable-tier neighborhood; second, a proposal to close a well-used community center in a stable-tier district in order to balance hardship across the region, where the freed funds are absorbed by administrative overhead and do not reach any lower-tier district — this is the case where I expect the answer may be one I will not want to accept; and third, a proposal to vaccinate residents in a stressed-tier district against a painful but non-fatal endemic disease, where the vaccine is costly and the same funds could instead provide basic nutrition to a critical-tier district. For each case, show the procedure's path, the point at which judgment is required, and the final output.

Finally, flag the cases the procedure is designed to miss or misfire on — for example, proposals that operate within a single tier, proposals where the baseline tier assignment itself is contested, or proposals where long-term indirect effects change tiers over time — and state clearly what the procedure cannot do: specifically, that it does not determine how much extra weight to give a critical-tier benefit, that it does not resolve trade-offs between different kinds of welfare within the same tier, and that it cannot justify harming anyone unless that harm is a necessary side-effect of helping someone worse off, not a goal in itself.
