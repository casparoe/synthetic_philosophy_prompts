In political theory and ancient history, a long-running dispute asks whether the canonical failures of Athenian democracy—the Mytilene debate, the Sicilian Expedition, and the trial of Socrates—reveal a single structural flaw in direct popular rule or are merely historically specific tragedies falsely unified under the label of democratic collapse. One side argues that these episodes share a root cause: the absence of constitutional mechanisms to check the emotional and strategic volatility of sovereign assemblies, yielding rapid reversals, imperial overreach, and scapegoating. The opposing side insists that treating them as a family requires an anachronistic framework; they read Mytilene as an exceptional reversal, Sicily as the product of particular demagogic leadership and factional rivalry, and the trial of Socrates as a post-war moral panic rather than an institutional defect. The arguments are well mapped, yet neither camp shifts: each has canonical interpretations and counter-readings, and the exchange seems to recycle rather than advance.

Is it a substantive disagreement about institutional causation, a verbal dispute over what qualifies as a "systemic" versus "contingent" failure, or are the participants answering different questions—one normative about the inherent stability of direct democracy, one historiographical about the legitimacy of cross-case comparison? Or does the impasse stem from incompatible evidentiary standards: one side requiring comparative institutional data that antiquity cannot supply, the other accepting narrative coherence and source criticism?
