As a journalist covering philosophy and its applications to institutional design, I am drafting a feature on how abstract metaphysical problems are being taught through simulation rather than lecture. For this piece, I need a fully specified, runnable classroom activity designed for a graduate workshop on metaphysics and normativity—twelve participants, ninety minutes—that makes the group feel the conceptual pressure of free-floating rationales: normative or explanatory reasons that operate without being grasped, held, or represented by any reasoning subject. To keep the exercise from collapsing into familiar examples like evolutionary adaptation or legal systems, frame the simulation around a distributed algorithmic governance architecture in which a resource-allocation rationale is encoded across protocols, audit logs, and feedback loops but never fully instantiated in any single human or algorithmic node. The rules and roles—for instance, Protocol, Auditor, Feedback Node, and Silent Constraint—should be arranged so that participants construct the decision pathway themselves and then discover they are responding to reasons that none of them possesses. Specify a timeline for the ninety minutes, the materials required, a payoff scheme that uses only collective points tied to a shared outcome (activating if the distributed reason is honored, collapsing if any node claims ownership of it), and a debrief that maps each role and decision point onto a component of the idea. The debrief should also identify the predictable failure mode—likely that participants will anthropomorphize the architecture or insist that one node "really" holds the reason—and advise how to handle it. Finally, include an honest note on which feature of free-floating rationales, specifically the complete absence of any representational substrate whatsoever, the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator. The point must emerge from the dynamics and incentives, not from any front-loaded explanation.
