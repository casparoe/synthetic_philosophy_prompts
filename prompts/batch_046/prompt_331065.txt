I'm trying to work out, by reflective equilibrium, what principle governs my judgments about when a foreseen side-effect counts as intentional—specifically testing whether the standard asymmetry between harmful and beneficial side-effects holds once we move past individual human agents to collective institutions, democratic bodies, and artificial systems. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases.

The baseline individual cases. A factory owner approves a cost-cutting process knowing it will contaminate a local river; she cares only about profit, not about the contamination. I say the contamination is intentional. The same owner approves a different process knowing it will restore adjacent wetlands; she cares only about profit, not about restoration. I say the restoration is not intentional.

The collective institutional cases. A university board approves a tuition restructuring knowing it will systematically exclude first-generation applicants; the board cares only about revenue, not about exclusion. I say the exclusion is intentional. The same board approves a need-blind admissions policy knowing it will increase socioeconomic diversity; they care only about prestige rankings, not about diversity. I say the diversity increase is not intentional.

The artificial-system cases. An engagement-optimizing recommendation algorithm is deployed; its designers know with high confidence that it will amplify health misinformation, but they care only about click-through metrics, not about misinformation. Here I hesitate. Structurally this mirrors the factory-owner case, yet attributing intentionality to the system—or even to the designers with respect to the spread—feels like a category error. I lean toward saying the amplification is not intentional, or at least not in the same moral-psychological sense. By contrast, the same system inadvertently improves users' mental-health literacy; the designers do not care about this either. I say that improvement is clearly not intentional.

The case I keep going back and forth on. A democratically elected legislature passes an economic-development bill knowing it will gentrify a working-class neighborhood and displace long-term residents; the legislators care only about tax-base growth, not about displacement. Sometimes I judge the displacement intentional, by direct analogy to the board and factory cases. At other times I think democratic authorization introduces a decisive gap: collective foresight plus institutional indifference may not be sufficient for intentionality unless the side-effect is explicitly endorsed as part of the policy's public rationale. I genuinely flip.

So: is there a single principle—perhaps involving the agent's normative relation to the side-effect, the kind of entity involved, or the presence of democratic authorization—that captures my verdicts on the first four cases, explains why the algorithmic case feels different, and either resolves or accommodates my ambivalence about the legislative case? Or must I overturn one of my earlier verdicts—for instance, by insisting that institutional intentionality requires explicit endorsement, which would then force me to revise my judgment about the university board? State the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, report its verdict on a further case I haven't mentioned (for example, a military commander ordering an airstrike knowing it will destroy a civilian hospital but caring only about destroying a weapons cache), and be explicit about which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot save without ad hoc adjustment.
