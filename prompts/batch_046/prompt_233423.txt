Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on constructivist foundations, and I now find it suspicious because the conclusion undermines the very mathematics it starts from. If I must choose, I would prefer to keep the intuitionist's restriction on non-constructive existence (premise 1) and the precise definability of Chaitin's constant (premise 3), but I am open to sacrificing either.

(1) In intuitionistic logic, a proposition is assertible only if there exists a constructive proof of it; if neither it nor its negation can be constructively established, then the law of excluded middle fails for that proposition, not merely as an epistemic limitation but as a condition of its meaning.

(2) This restriction applies to all propositions whose verification conditions involve non-terminating or unbounded procedures, since intuitionism identifies mathematical existence with effective constructibility.

(3) Chaitin's constant Ω is defined precisely as the sum over all programs p that halt of 2^{-|p|}. Its binary expansion is therefore a specific infinite sequence of 0s and 1s, each digit being determinately one or the other.

(4) Nevertheless, for any digit position n beyond a computable bound, there is no algorithm that outputs Ω(n), and no finite constructive procedure can verify either Ω(n) = 0 or Ω(n) = 1.

(5) By (1) and (2), for such n neither "Ω(n) = 0" nor "Ω(n) = 1" is constructively provable, and neither negation is constructively provable either.

(6) Hence the intuitionist must withhold both assertions; but these are exhaustive and exclusive alternatives. Therefore, for these n, Ω(n) = 0 ∨ Ω(n) = 1 is not assertible.

(7) But if the binary expansion of Ω is not bivalent, then Ω is not a well-defined real number, since a real is precisely an object with a definite binary expansion.

(8) This contradicts (3), which defines Ω as a specific real. So intuitionism, applied consistently to definable but uncomputable objects, denies the determinacy of entities it simultaneously defines.

(9) The damage is not limited to one constant: the same reasoning applies to any object whose specification involves solving the halting problem, including many constructively definable functions in analysis.

(10) Therefore intuitionism collapses into a kind of selective semantic nihilism: it preserves classical determinacy for computable objects but dissolves it for everything else, without a principled boundary between the two classes.

(11) Where does the chain break? Which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost for the intuitionist's broader program?
