In my philosophy reading group everyone keeps comparing Machiavelli's fortune and virtù to a poker game: fortune deals the cards (the political situation you inherit), virtù is knowing when to hold 'em and when to fold (tactical adaptability), and the prince is just a good player trying to maximize expected value. They use this to argue Machiavelli is basically a proto-rational-choice theorist who thinks human agency is mostly reactive optimization. But I'm not convinced -- especially when I think about Machiavelli's claim that fortune only governs about half our actions and that founding a state takes something closer to violent artistic creation than to betting well. Can you lay out the mapping for the poker analogy precisely -- what corresponds to the deck, the pot, the other players, the rules of the game -- then list the disanalogies, particularly around whether virtù can actually reshape the table itself rather than just playing inside fixed rules? And tell me whether there conclusion that Machiavelli is an optimist about human control survives once those breaks are visible, or whether a different analogy -- maybe a sculptor facing resistant marble where the grain is fortune and the chisel is virtù -- captures the tension better without smuggling in false optimism?
