As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy, I have been puzzling over whether there is a unified principle behind our judgments about bluntness, indirectness, and white lies. I want to test my own tentative idea, and I need help seeing where it breaks.

Here are four cases with my verdicts.

Case one: A Dutch colleague tells me directly that my reasoning contains a serious error. I think this is not wrong; in fact, it seems respectful.

Case two: A Japanese colleague, in the same situation, never states disagreement outright but lets me infer it from prolonged silence and a comment about the weather. I think this indirectness is also not wrong; it operates within an accepted social frame.

Case three: A physician tells a terminally ill patient that the prognosis is good, knowing it is false, in order to preserve hope. I think this is wrong.

Case four: Asked whether I like a friend's painting, I say only that the colors are bold—a literally true statement that deliberately avoids my negative judgment and is intended to let the friend believe I approve. I think this is permissible, perhaps even kind.

The difficulty is that cases three and four both involve the speaker intentionally inducing a false belief in the listener about a matter within the speaker's knowledge. In case four, however, I do not feel the same condemnation. My tentative principle is this: an utterance is wrong when it violates an implicit frame-contract that grants the listener special epistemic authority over the domain in question—health belongs to the patient and scientific reasoning belongs to the interlocutor—whereas in aesthetic or social domains the listener has consented to a cooperative fiction. Meanwhile, indirectness in case two is permissible because the frame itself permits temporary misinterpretation that is eventually corrected without deception.

But this principle feels ad hoc. Can it be repaired so that it saves all four verdicts? Or must I overturn one—perhaps accepting that case four is actually deceptive and wrong? Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause handles each case, and tell me what it predicts for a new case: a government spokesperson who uses a standard diplomatic formula, that the proposal is being given full consideration, when the decision to reject has already been made and the formula is universally understood within the profession to allow exactly that meaning. Is this more like case two or case three?
