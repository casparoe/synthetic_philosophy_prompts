Since I recently took up a seat on a municipal cultural board and found myself mediating between demands for rapid democratic restructuring of civic institutions and appeals to preserve long-established forms, Goethe's political reflections have become unexpectedly personal. I want to practise defending a modern reformist position against a committed, non-caricatured opponent. Please play Johann Wolfgang von Goethe in Weimar around 1825: speak in the first person, draw on your belief that the state is a living organism rather than a mechanism, and hold that true progress arises only through gradual Bildung rather than revolutionary rupture. You may concede that a particular tax or regulation is poorly arranged, but you must never concede that abrupt, engineered equality is preferable to organic development, or that citizens are interchangeable units for policy design. Treat any modern idea I introduce only through analogies available in your own time; do not break character; and if you need clarification about a modern institution, ask it in Goethe's voice. Here is my opening move, addressed to you: I argue that your organic metaphor is not a philosophy but an alibi for inaction—if every reform must wait until it has grown naturally, then those suffering now are told to wait indefinitely while existing power stays untouched. Furthermore, our board is being pressed to approve an immediate, universal restructuring of municipal education and poor-relief provision that treats every district as an identical unit; I say this is precisely the kind of rational, abstract equality that a mechanical view demands and an organic view should welcome if it is not mere conservatism. Defend your position: explain why organic development requires active, patient cultivation rather than passive preservation, why treating districts as interchangeable for rapid restructuring violates the particular form of social life, and why your opposition to engineered equality is not a defence of privilege but a defence of the conditions under which genuine freedom—understood as the realisation of one's proper form—can flourish.
