I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and in my department's ethics workshop we've spent months circling a specific formulation of the paradox of deontology: the claim that a strict prohibition on using someone as a mere means seems to entail that, in cascading rights-violation scenarios, an agent must stand by and allow more violations rather than intervene in a way that makes them complicit in one. The workshop's interventionists treat this as proof that deontology is self-defeating; the defenders reply that rights have lexical priority over aggregate welfare and that the appearance of paradox comes from smuggling in consequentialist aggregation. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about those cases at all but about a deeper disagreement over whether moral reasons must be interpersonally comparable within a single metric before they can genuinely conflict: one side treats incommensurability as a theoretical failure, the other as a structural feature of agency.
