Compare Avicenna's flying-man thought experiment with Descartes's cogito in Meditation II, focusing not on their surface similarity—both claim an immediate awareness of the self independent of sensory input—but on their divergent ontological ambitions. Does Avicenna intend the suspended man to establish only the epistemic priority and non-empirical character of self-consciousness, or does he mean it to demonstrate the substantial immateriality of the knowing soul? And does Descartes, by contrast, seek to establish the real distinction between thinking and extended substance rather than mere epistemic privilege? Assess which interpretation of the flying man is more textually defensible, whether either argument survives the modern physicalist reply that first-person reference can be explained without a non-physical subject, and whether conflating the two arguments into a single "Cartesian" proof of immateriality obscures a meaningful difference in their philosophical targets.
