I'd like to read William Golding's Lord of the Flies through the lens of evolutionary cooperation theory—specifically the one-shot versus iterated prisoner's dilemma and its extensions—rather than through the usual Hobbes-versus-Rousseau framing. The boys are stranded in a closed system: they cannot leave the island, and they must repeatedly interact over food, shelter, signal-fire maintenance, and defense against an imagined beast. In standard evolutionary models, cooperation can stabilize through direct reciprocity when the shadow of the future is long enough, or through network reciprocity when cooperators cluster spatially. But there's a less-discussed structural variant: the voluntary-participation or opt-out model, in which agents can decline interaction altogether. In those dynamics, cooperation survives not primarily because defection is punished, but because cooperators avoid defectors, leaving defectors trapped in mutually destructive pairings. The island, however, is a closed population with no credible exit—every boy must interact with every other, and the game's horizon is truncated and uncertain because rescue is unpredictable.

Read the novel with this constraint in mind. How does the absence of an exit option shape the rapid collapse of Ralph's conch-based institution? Does Jack's hunting faction represent a defection strategy that thrives precisely because the boys cannot avoid it, or does it succeed through a different mechanism—charismatic authority, status competition, or the transformation of the game into something closer to a dominance hierarchy? Where does Simon's altruism fit: does he behave as a cooperative player in an iterated game, as a "loner" attempting to opt out of interaction altogether, or as someone who refuses to treat the others as rational agents with payoffs? And does the ending—Ralph's recognition of the darkness in human nature—imply that cooperation is impossible without external enforcement, or rather that it is structurally doomed specifically when interaction is mandatory, horizons are short, and exit is impossible?

I'm preparing this for a discussion group interested in rationalist-style models of social dynamics, so I want a reading that takes both Golding's narrative and the evolutionary logic seriously: don't reduce the boys to payoff matrices, but don't use the philosophical frame as mere decoration either. Which scenes best illuminate the tension between one-shot defection and iterated cooperation—perhaps the breaking of Piggy's glasses, the murder of Simon, or the final hunt for Ralph—and where does the novel resist being mapped onto the model in ways that reveal what evolutionary game theory omits about violence, authority, and the psychology of groups?
