Consider the published case judgments of a philosopher defending strict tranquilism: only the absence of craving and suffering has intrinsic value, and no mental state—pleasure, knowledge, affection, or self-awareness—adds to it. Her rulings on proposed conditions are: (1) a permanent vegetative state with no cortical activity and thus no experience of craving or suffering—she writes that this achieves "complete tranquility," though she notes "there may be no subject present to bear it"; (2) a sustained conscious meditative state with minimal cognition, zero craving, zero suffering, and faint calm—she judges it "equal in value to (1), since the calm adds nothing"; (3) a fully active life with deep attachments and occasional mild suffering, but no unsatisfied craving—"deficient, because suffering remains"; (4) a life in which craving is always satisfied but never eliminated, with zero suffering—"deficient, because craving itself is present"; (5) a life maintained by an irreversible neural modification that permanently disables the mechanisms of craving and suffering, leaving full consciousness but permanently removing the capacity for pleasure, love, or curiosity—she has not ruled, but distinguishes in her work between "extinction tranquility" (mechanisms removed) and "satiation tranquility" (all wants met).

The new proposal is (6): a reversible 48-hour cyclical protocol. Every other day the subject receives temporary suppression of craving and suffering mechanisms; on those days they retain full consciousness but experience blank, affectless awareness with neither pleasure nor pain. On alternate days the suppression ends, and they experience ordinary life with typical craving and suffering. The cycle can continue indefinitely.

What should she rule about (6) to remain consistent with (1)–(5)? Lay out at least two distinct principles that could organize her earlier verdicts—paying special attention to whether consciousness, mechanism, or temporal continuity matter—state what each predicts about the cyclical proposal, offer your best-guess verdict with reasoning, and identify the single further case that would most clearly separate the two principles.
