I'm drafting a novel about a cartographer who suspects that every landscape conceals a hidden order beneath its apparent blur. For one scene I need a sestina—six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi—spoken in her voice as she attempts to fix the exact line where a salt flat becomes a marsh. Use these six end-words, rotating through the standard sestina pattern: boundary, shadow, grain, precise, fades, knows. Arrange the rotation so that "boundary" and "precise" never stabilize in the same stanza twice, tracking the idea that admissible precisifications multiply without a single correct one; meanwhile let "knows" grow more insistent with each appearance, tracking the rival idea that ignorance, not language, obscures a sharp line. In the envoi, position the words so that the grammar permits two readings: either she has discovered the boundary or she has projected it onto the land. Use only the salt-flat setting—avoid the familiar examples of baldness and heaps—so that someone familiar with the vagueness literature recognizes both positions without either being named. The poem should work as poetry even for a reader who knows nothing of the debate.
