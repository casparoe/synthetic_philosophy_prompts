I am serving as a teaching assistant for an undergraduate epistemology seminar, and the subject has taken on an unexpected personal weight. Until recently I believed my grandmother had emigrated in 1952 on the strength of a misdated family letter; I have now learned that she did in fact emigrate that year, but through an entirely unrelated route I had never considered. The belief was true, my reasoning was grounded in real documentary evidence, yet the evidential path and the truth were causally disconnected. This left me wondering whether I had genuinely known anything at the time, and it has made me unusually cautious about grading student work on Gettier problems.

Here is an exam question I composed, together with one student response. I am grading it and want an external assessment.

Exam question: Dr. Chen, a specialist, believes her patient has Syndrome X because she correctly observes three symptoms that are reliably correlated with the condition. In fact, those particular symptoms were caused by an unrelated drug interaction, but the patient does have Syndrome X—due to a separate congenital mutation that produces no observable symptoms. Dr. Chen's belief is true, and her clinical justification is sound (the symptom-correlation is medically valid). Is this a Gettier case? Does it undermine the analysis of knowledge as justified true belief? In answering, distinguish carefully between (a) a problem arising from false premises in the justification, and (b) a problem arising from an accidental connection between the justification and the truth, regardless of whether any premise is false.

Student answer:

Yes, this is clearly a Gettier case. Dr. Chen possesses a justified true belief—her training supports the symptom link, and the patient genuinely has the syndrome—yet she lacks knowledge because her justification points to one causal pathway (the observed symptoms) while the truth depends on an entirely separate pathway (the hidden mutation). This accidental connection between justification and truth shows that knowledge requires more than justified true belief.

That said, I think we should be precise about the taxonomy. In Gettier's original examples, the problem always involves deriving a true belief from a false premise. Here, however, Dr. Chen's evidential premise—that these symptoms indicate Syndrome X—is actually true; the correlation is real. What is false is only her implicit causal explanation. So this is not genuinely a Gettier case, but rather an instance of environmental epistemic luck, closer to Goldman's fake-barn scenario, where the environment happens to contain the fact that makes the belief true despite the believer's reasoning being misaligned with reality.

Consequently, the JTB analysis can survive if we supplement it with a "no false premises" condition. Once we require that every premise in the justification chain be true, cases like Dr. Chen's are excluded, because the false causal explanation is not part of the evidential chain proper. The belief is justified by true premises, and it is true; there is therefore no gap for knowledge to fall through. What philosophers need is not a fourth condition, but stricter attention to the internal coherence of justification rather than its causal history.
