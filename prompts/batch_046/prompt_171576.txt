Beatrice Webb was a leading British social reformer whose work shaped early welfare-state debates about poverty, moral judgment, and structural economic causes. Imagine she is alive today and confronted with a new administrative technology called predictive conditionality: welfare eligibility is no longer checked only against declared income or job-search logs, but is continuously recalibrated by machine-learning models that ingest real-time behavioral signals—geolocation traces, social-network density, and retail transaction patterns—to generate dynamic risk scores predicting future benefit fraud or work-avoidance. These scores automatically trigger graduated sanctions, from reduced payments to temporary suspension, without human caseworker review. Drawing on Webb's well-known skepticism toward moralistic distinctions between the deserving and undeserving poor, her commitment to universal social provision over punitive poor-law administration, and her belief that poverty is primarily a structural failure rather than an individual character defect, predict her assessment of this system. Would she condemn predictive conditionality as a mechanized revival of Victorian moral surveillance, or might she accept it as an administratively neutral tool that removes subjective human bias from eligibility decisions? Formulate and justify a clear verdict, addressing both the continuity with historical workfare logic and the distinctive feature that the judgment is now rendered by algorithmic correlation rather than by explicit moral categorization.
