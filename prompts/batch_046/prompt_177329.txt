I am a novelist working on a long-form project about a seventeenth-century philosopher who treats the universe as a vast combinatorial system in which every possible essence competes for admission into actual existence. In recent conversations with a friend who studies philosophy of religion, I have become fascinated by the idea that Leibniz’s claim that ours is the best of all possible worlds is usually misread as a simple optimization of happiness, when it might instead be read as an optimization of being itself. I want you to argue for the position that this world is best not because suffering is outweighed by greater goods, but because it is maximally inclusive of striving essences—those possibilities that possess an internal tendency toward existence proportional to their perfection. On this reading, the principle of compossibility operates like a formal consistency condition: it permits the greatest number of essences to coexist without contradiction, and the suffering we observe is not an unfortunate residue to be balanced away but the structural trace of that maximal hospitality. Every instance of pain marks the successful admission of an essence that a less inclusive world would have excluded entirely, so that reducing suffering without altering the internal relations among beings would actually diminish the total amount of reality instantiated.

You should specifically address the objection that a world containing the same beings arranged so as to suffer less would be preferable, and you should show that such rearrangement is impossible without violating the very relations of compossibility that allow those beings to exist together in the first place. Treat perfection as a quantitative measure of instantiated essence rather than a qualitative measure of well-being, and keep your reasoning precise without relying on obscure technical citations or bibliographic references, since I am drafting this for my own thinking rather than for an academic audience. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then develop the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. At the close, state how confident you are in the overall argument and identify precisely the point about which you feel least certain—particularly whether the move from quantitative perfection to the necessity of observed suffering is genuinely justified or whether it quietly smuggles in an unstated normative premise.
