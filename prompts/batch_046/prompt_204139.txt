My sister died three weeks ago, suddenly, at forty-three. She was an architect who loved concrete and trees in equal measure, and she held no religious belief; she once said that when she died she wanted to become soil for an oak. I am giving her eulogy tomorrow at a memorial where half the room is family who need comfort and half are her colleagues, several of whom teach Chinese philosophy and will recognize every conceptual move I make. Since her death I have been reading Zhuangzi — not as a scholar, but because I needed a language that did not pretend she had moved somewhere to watch over us, and also did not treat her absence as a simple mistake in nature. Please write a five-minute eulogy that takes Zhuangzi's view of death as its structural backbone rather than decoration. Specifically, build the speech around the idea that mourning, in Zhuangzi's framework, risks becoming a form of small knowledge — a fixation that keeps the dead and the living locked in separate, unchanging identities — and that the more difficult task is to allow the grief itself to undergo the kind of great transformation (hua) that applies not only to bodies but to the attachments of the heart-mind. The speech must not claim she is watching over us, must not invoke an afterlife, and must not dismiss the family's sorrow as mere confusion; but it should also avoid consolations that treat death as a failure or an enemy. It needs to be fully speakable to people who have never read Zhuangzi, yet precise enough that philosophers present see you understand the difference between bodily decay and the transformation of emotional attachments, and that you are not using the tub-drumming anecdote as a sentimental prop. I want the audience to leave feeling that her death belongs to a process they can respect without denying that she is gone. Separately, please include a brief critical note stating how confident you are in the interpretation of Zhuangzi you have relied on, identifying the specific point about which you are least sure, and flagging where you have taken liberties with the texts or leaned on contested distinctions between the inner and outer chapters.
