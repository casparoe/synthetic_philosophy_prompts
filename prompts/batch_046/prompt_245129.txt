My partner and I run a bakery with a strict dough-rest protocol: exactly forty-five minutes at standard kitchen temperature, then shape immediately. Yesterday the kitchen was unusually cold, so I proposed extending the rest to preserve the intended biochemical result. My partner refused, insisting that the protocol be executed identically regardless of conditions, because allowing even one exception would dissolve the discipline that makes our product consistent. I countered that the rule was adopted to serve the bread, not the reverse, and that repeating the same temporal step when the underlying conditions have clearly changed is like performing a verification that has already been satisfied. She replied that my willingness to adjust is not judgment but a failure to commit to any rule at all. I want to understand whether there is a philosophically defensible distinction between rational adherence to a procedure and a mechanical repetition of steps that no longer serve their original purpose — and whether the capacity to suspend a rule precisely when its rationale has disappeared marks genuine flexibility, or merely exposes an inability to follow through. Does her refusal represent a virtue of discipline, or a rigidity that undermines the very goal the discipline was meant to achieve, and does my preference for adaptation indicate real judgment rather than simple inconsistency?
