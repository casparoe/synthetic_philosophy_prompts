For a workshop on Spinoza's political philosophy I am drafting my position paper and want a consistency audit before circulating it. Here is where I stand: (1) Spinoza's democracy is the absolute form of government because sovereignty is never alienated—the multitude retains its collective natural right, now ordered by common law, rather than transferring power to a separate ruler. (2) The exclusions in the Political Treatise—women, dependents, foreigners, and those convicted of crimes—are consistent with democratic equality because they apply the sui iuris criterion: citizenship requires being under one's own power, not another's, and these exclusions track real social dependencies in Spinoza's context rather than arbitrary prejudice. (3) I read Spinoza's political rationalism strictly: the state's purpose is to guide citizens by reason toward freedom, and democratic institutions should approximate rational agreement rather than aggregate mere passions. (4) At the same time, I accept an affective-democratic interpretation: democratic legitimacy derives primarily from shared joyful passions that increase collective power, and stability depends more on affective alignment than on rational conviction alone. (5) From (3) and (4) I conclude that a genuinely Spinozist democracy should actively engineer civic rituals and education to cultivate common affects aligned with reason, but may legitimately suppress speech that produces collective sadness or fragmentation, since such speech undermines both the affective bond and the rational order. Which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which tensions are only apparent, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity and indicate, for each, which single commitment would have to be weakened or abandoned.
