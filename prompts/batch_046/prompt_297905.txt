I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately taken up comparative philosophy, and next month I am leading a single ninety-minute session for a small group of fellow retirees—mostly scientists and engineers—on Jain anekāntavāda, specifically the syādvāda doctrine of conditional predication and its seven-fold scheme, saptabhaṅgī. The audience knows Western logic and some philosophy of science but has no background in Indian philosophy; they will almost certainly arrive assuming that “many-sided truth” is just a familiar form of relativism or pluralism.

Plan the session’s explanatory arc: identify the prior conceptual distinction—between denying absolute exclusivity and affirming contradiction—that must be secured before syādvāda sounds coherent rather than self-refuting; choose one concrete carrying example, preferably drawn from scientific observation or ordinary experience, that can sustain all seven predicative modes without collapsing into nonsense, and specify exactly what properties it must possess and what it must lack; map the misconceptions they will form in sequence—for instance, that anekāntavāda licenses straightforwardly contradictory belief, that it expresses mere epistemic humility about human cognitive limits, or that it parallels quantum complementarity in a direct one-to-one fashion—indicating precisely where in the arc each misconception should be intercepted and how; propose one diagnostic question or brief exercise at the end that reveals whether they have grasped the logical structure of qualified predication rather than merely adopted a tolerant attitude; and state explicitly what technical apparatus—such as the full taxonomy of nayas or the metaphysics of jīva and ajīva—must be left out at this level and openly acknowledged as omitted.

Keep any comparison to scientific frames of reference precise and brief, but do not let the analogy become the subject; the goal is for them to understand anekāntavāda on its own terms, not to reduce it to an illustration of modern physics.
