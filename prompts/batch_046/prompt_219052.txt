I hold that when forecasting the long-term stability of a complex polity—specifically a large federal republic facing rapid demographic aging, extreme internal inequality, and high ethnic diversity—the outside view ought to dominate: one should begin with base-rate survival curves drawn from the reference class of comparable historical regimes, and treat inside-view arguments about institutional adaptability, technological innovation, or exceptional cultural cohesion as adjustments that should rarely move the forecast more than a small margin. What would genuinely count against this methodological commitment? I want the arguments and evidence that should weaken it, sorted by how decisive they would be: specific historical episodes where reference-class forecasting failed systematically for political entities; formal or empirical results showing that inside-view institutional variables can overwhelm base-rate predictions in this domain; and conditions under which the reference class itself is mis-specified or too heterogeneous to be useful. I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders of this outside-view approach have qualified it—adding post-hoc rescue conditions for outliers—into a framework that risks nothing, and whether that insulation is a genuine defect for a forecasting methodology rather than merely a rhetorical weakness.
