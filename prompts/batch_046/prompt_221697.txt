I am designing a single ninety-minute graduate seminar for philosophy MA students who have solid grounding in Aristotle's account of philia and Kant's theory of aesthetic judgment, but who have never encountered G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica. The session's aim is not historical survey but conceptual reconstruction: to teach Moore's specific claim that friendship and the appreciation of beautiful objects are the only things possessing intrinsic value, with all other goods being either instrumental to these or constituted by them through organic unity. Because students will immediately assimilate this to familiar frameworks—including, more recently, to relational-goods theories that treat value as structurally derived—I want the session built around a dependency map rather than exposition. Plan the teaching sequence precisely: first, the order in which the components must be introduced, specifically why a non-utilitarian framing of intrinsic value must be secured before organic unity is presented, and why the distinction between goods that are parts of an organic whole and goods that are merely instrumentally related must come before either friendship or beauty is discussed; second, the one carrying example or constructed scenario that should anchor the entire session—design one that involves both collaborative interpersonal engagement and aesthetic experience, specify exactly what properties it must possess (it must not reduce to mutual pleasure, to virtue-exercise, to disinterested contemplation, or to the satisfaction of independent preferences) and what properties it must lack (it should not be analyzable into separately valuable parts whose sum explains the whole's value), and explain why this particular structure is needed to make Moore's point visible; third, the misconceptions that will arise at each stage—particularly the assimilation of Moore's friendship to Aristotelian virtue-friendship, the assimilation of his aesthetic view to Kantian disinterestedness, the belief that organic unity is merely quantitative addition of values, the belief that Moore is advancing an empirical claim about human flourishing, and the assimilation of his view to contemporary relational-goods frameworks—indicating the exact point in the sequence where each should be anticipated and defused; fourth, a single diagnostic question that would reveal whether a student has actually grasped Moore's specific position or has merely mapped it onto a pre-existing ethical or aesthetic framework; and fifth, what elements of Moore's broader ideal-utilitarian context, including his treatment of moral rules as rules of thumb for producing intrinsic goods, should be explicitly bracketed and acknowledged as outside the session's scope. The plan should assume students have no access to external texts during the session, and it should not include bibliographies, assigned readings, requests for citations, or appeals to sources they could not access.
