For my upper-division seminar on philosophy of history and international-relations theory, I am trying to formalize the conceptual architecture behind the “lessons of history” that get invoked in crisis deliberation—specifically analogies to Munich, Vietnam, and the so-called Thucydides trap. Rather than summarizing existing literature, I want the specification of what a normative theory of historical analogy in this domain must be able to do if it is to count as adequate for guiding policy judgment without collapsing into either deterministic prophecy or empty contextual particularism.

Write the full list of desiderata that such a theory must meet: the phenomena it has to explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the errors it must avoid, and the consistency conditions it must satisfy. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind it, and flag whether it is non-negotiable or a camp-specific preference. The purpose is a theory adequate for deliberative guidance—one that tells us when an analogy genuinely instructs rather than misleads—rather than one that delivers predictive certainty.

In particular, your list must include and justify requirements that address the following:

(1) The Munich analogy of appeasement: the theory must explain why treating Munich as a universal prohibition against negotiation with hostile powers is misleading, yet must also preserve the genuine insight about credibility and commitment that makes the analogy politically resonant in the first place.

(2) The Vietnam analogy of quagmire: the theory must account for path-dependent escalation and institutional overextension without assuming deterministic repetition of outcomes, and it must explain why analogical reasoning about “quagmire” tends to conflate structural strategic conditions with psychological decision-pathologies.

(3) The Thucydides-trap framing: the theory must distinguish a structural power-transition claim from an inevitability thesis, and it must preserve the difference between predictive structuralism and normative prescription—specifically, it must not allow the “lesson” that conflict is structurally likely to be smuggled into the conclusion that preventive war is therefore justified.

(4) Inject the following niche distinction: the theory must separate mechanism-preserving analogies, in which a causal pathway is expected to recur because strategic or institutional conditions genuinely replicate, from outcome-mimicking analogies, in which only a surface result is projected onto dissimilar underlying processes. Give the concrete case that motivates this distinction, and explain why outcome-mimicking analogies are epistemically weaker yet politically more seductive.

(5) The tension between deep historicist uniqueness—each crisis is embedded in a non-replicable institutional, cultural, and technological context—and the very possibility of analogical learning. State whether full satisfaction of both commitments is jointly possible; if some subset is unsatisfiable given standard theoretical commitments, say so explicitly and identify which desideratum must be weakened or abandoned.

Also include any other essential desiderata you think belong in the specification—such as consistency with cognitive-scientific findings on analogical mapping, the ability to distinguish analogical reasoning from direct causal forecasting, or the requirement that the theory not collapse normative lessons (what ought to be done) into descriptive predictions (what will happen).

Then, briefly and without turning this into a literature review, score three broad approaches against your list: a deep-contextualist historiography that denies robustly transferable patterns across crises; a structural-realist power-transition framework; and a pragmatic decision-theoretic analogism that treats analogies as heuristic probes rather than predictive models. For each, state which desiderata it satisfies and which it violates, with particular attention to how each handles the mechanism-preserving versus outcome-mimicking distinction.

Keep the weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and the analysis of joint satisfiability rather than on the scoring. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or verbatim quotations from named texts. Write formally, as if addressing a seminar instructor who knows the debates but needs the evaluative architecture made explicit.
