They are fluent with functionalist frameworks—global workspace models, information-integration approaches—but tend to collapse phenomenal consciousness into access consciousness or treat subjective experience as a redundant explanatory layer. I want them to grasp the conceivability argument involving philosophical zombies: specifically, the move from conceiving a molecule-for-molecule physical and behavioral duplicate that lacks all phenomenal experience to the claim that physical facts do not metaphysically necessitate phenomenal facts, and therefore that physicalism is false.

Don't explain the argument to me; plan the explanatory arc. First, state what single distinction must be secured before anything else can land, and why their functionalist training makes that distinction harder rather than easier. Second, identify the one carrying example or scenario that should do the work for the whole session, and explain precisely why a sleepwalker, a current large language model, and a patient under general anesthesia each fail as substitutes. Third, map the misconceptions in the order they will arise: I anticipate they will interpret "conceivable" as "technologically buildable," confuse zombies with merely unconscious humans, and assume the argument rests on present ignorance of neural mechanisms. For each misconception, specify the exact point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and what distinction blocks it. Fourth, propose one closing question that distinguishes someone who understands the modal claim from someone who has only registered that zombies "sound strange." Finally, say explicitly what must be left out at this level—for example, analytic-reduction replies or phenomenal-concept strategies—and justify the omission.
