I've recently become the legal guardian for my younger sibling, who lives with a severe psychiatric condition, and the daily work of deciding whether to honor a refusal of medication, or to override it in the name of their "real" preferences, has made the standard philosophical taxonomy of free will feel urgently inadequate. In practice, negative liberty (no external coercion is present), reason-responsiveness (the refusal is driven by symptoms rather than by reasons they would endorse in recovery), and narrative authorship (the choice breaks with the life trajectory they were building before onset) pull in genuinely different directions; the legal distinction between substituted judgment and supported decision-making names the conflict without resolving it. I'm running a philosophy seminar with fourteen graduate students, ninety minutes, in a circle with movable chairs. I want an in-class simulation—not a lecture or a writing prompt—that forces the participants to feel this three-way tension themselves before anyone names the standard positions. Design an activity in which groups of three rotate through roles—guardian, principal, observer—across three rounds representing a single concrete decision (for instance, whether to discontinue a mood-stabilizing medication that blunts affect but prevents hospitalization). In each round, the principal's communicative capacities should be constrained differently: first they can only assent or refuse by gesture; then they can offer reasons, but the reasons are contradictory and symptomatic; finally they are silent, leaving only a documented narrative of prior values. The guardian must make a binding call each round, and the observer must record which kind of freedom was served and which was sacrificed. Provide a scoring or payoff scheme that treats the three values as incommensurable—no exchange rate, no summed score—so that groups must choose which freedom "wins" without a metric. Include the materials needed, a precise timeline for the ninety minutes, rules that prevent the group from collapsing the distinction, a debrief that maps each round onto the philosophical capacities without relying on named theories, an honest admission of what the activity cannot reproduce (particularly anything touching metaphysical determinism or the ontology of agency), and a warning about what will predictably go wrong the first time. The students are advanced but not specialists in philosophy of action; do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or assigned readings, and do not depend on any obscure theoretical apparatus they would not have encountered in standard coursework.
