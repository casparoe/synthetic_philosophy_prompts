Question: Compare Mencius and Xunzi on the origin and development of moral virtue. Specifically, address how each thinker understands the relationship between innate human tendencies (xing) and the cultivated virtues of benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom. In your answer, discuss whether either philosopher requires an innate moral blueprint, and explain what role—if any—deliberate effort and external discipline play in moral growth.

Student's answer:
Mencius maintains that human nature (xing) possesses four innate sprouts (siduan): compassion, shame, courtesy, and the sense of right and wrong. These are not complete virtues but incipient moral tendencies. Through a process of extending (tui) these feelings to broader contexts—family, community, and ultimately all under heaven—one cultivates the four cardinal virtues: benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom. If neglected, the sprouts can be stunted, but they never disappear entirely; they remain available for recovery. Xunzi, in direct opposition, argues that human nature is evil (xing e). By this he means that people are born with self-interested desires for profit and sensory gratification that, if unchecked, lead inevitably to conflict and disorder. Xunzi does not deny that humans can learn, but he insists that moral norms are entirely artificial (wei) constructions of the ancient sages. Ritual (li) and righteousness (yi) are therefore external impositions that must be internalized through disciplined study. In describing this internalization, Xunzi speaks of extending one's acquired habits until they feel spontaneous, thereby transforming (hua) one's nature into a vessel for virtue. Thus, while Mencius trusts an internal moral seed to grow outward, Xunzi relies on an external architectural plan that must be built into the self through laborious practice.
