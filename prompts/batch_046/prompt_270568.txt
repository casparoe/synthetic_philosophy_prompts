I am preparing my defense for a doctoral seminar in philosophy of art and ethics next month, a topic that has become personally unavoidable since I began adapting my late grandmother’s unpublished letters into a narrative film. I defend the view that the central moral wrong in dramatizing the dead is not ordinary factual distortion—compressing timelines, merging secondary figures, altering settings—but what I call narrative expropriation: attributing to a dead person psychological motives, emotional responses, or moral self-understandings that are incompatible with their own documented accounts, when no decisive counter-evidence exists. Because the dead cannot correct the record, and because descendants and communities inherit the distortion, this violates a form of posthumous narrative autonomy that is more vulnerable than the interests of living subjects, who retain legal remedies and vocal self-defense. I do not claim filmmakers must be archivists—artistic selection and dramatic structure are inevitable—but I do claim that artistic license should not override a dead figure’s documented self-interpretation without overwhelming justification, and that this obligation does not weaken with historical distance; if anything, the absence of living correctives intensifies it.

The audience will be eight people: three documentary filmmakers professionally committed to artistic freedom as a near-absolute value; two historians of biography deeply skeptical of presentism and anachronistic psychology; one legal scholar who has written on post-mortem privacy rights but considers artistic speech almost sacrosanct; and two moral philosophers with Kantian leanings who will ask about dignity rather than autonomy. They are not hostile, but each constituency is professionally invested in making my claim uncomfortable.

Prepare me for the Q&A. Order the objections by the damage they would actually do in this room, not in the abstract. For each, phrase it precisely as that constituency would put it. Note what I should concede up front, what I must hold at all costs, and where my reply is softest. Identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope they miss. Include the one question I am praying nobody asks. Conclude with a fixed line beginning Verdict: that states, in one sentence, the single reply I must deliver for the proposal to survive the session.
