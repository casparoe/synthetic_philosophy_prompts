In my philosophy of science seminar, two colleagues—Rosa and Julian—became deadlocked over a case involving higher-order evidence and what they eventually termed “partitioned credence.” They were discussing a climate modeller who, using her best physical models, calculates that the probability of a severe regional drought next year is 0.72. She also possesses robust higher-order evidence—years of calibrated self-monitoring showing that when she works past midnight, her extreme-event predictions are systematically inflated by roughly fifteen percentage points. She is indeed working past midnight, so she applies an internal correction and arrives at an agent-level planning credence of 0.57. The twist is that she publishes the 0.72 figure in her official report, reasoning that peer review will supply its own corrective filters, whilst she privately arranges her irrigation and insurance using the 0.57 figure. Julian argued that this is a textbook instance of epistemic akrasia: her all-things-considered belief about the drought, once she has factored in her own fallibility, is 0.57, yet she asserts and partly acts upon 0.72, which she no longer fully endorses. Rosa replied that no akrasia is present because the two figures belong to distinct cognitive compartments—the object-level evidential output versus the agent-level planning credence—so she is not contradicting herself about one and the same proposition but rather distributing beliefs across rational roles. Julian countered that compartmentalisation is only legitimate when governed by a principled boundary, and here the boundary looks ad hoc: she deploys the higher figure where institutional credibility is at stake and the lower one where personal risk is at stake, which smells of motivated reasoning rather than structural separation. Rosa responded that Julian conflates pragmatic reasons for action with epistemic reasons for belief; her planning credence of 0.57 is precisely the rational response to higher-order defeat, whereas her publication of 0.72 respects scientific transparency norms, which do not require a scientist to substitute her private calibration for the unfiltered model output that the community can assess independently. Julian retorted that transparency norms cannot license the assertion of what one takes to be epistemically inferior, and that if she genuinely regards 0.57 as her best estimate, withholding it in publication amounts to a form of strategic omission.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess whether Rosa’s compartmentalisation genuinely avoids epistemic akrasia, or whether Julian is correct to diagnose a hidden contradiction; evaluate Rosa’s appeal to transparency norms against Julian’s charge of strategic omission; and indicate whether either party is neglecting a relevant distinction—perhaps between applying calibration weights to the evidence itself versus applying them to one’s belief about that evidence—that might either dissolve or sharpen the disagreement. Write your response in British English, maintaining a formal and polished register throughout.
