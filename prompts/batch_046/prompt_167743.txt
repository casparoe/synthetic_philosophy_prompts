As a journalist covering the philosophy of logic and its intersections with theoretical science, I have been trying to understand why dialetheism is almost invariably presented alongside paraconsistent logic. The view that some contradictions are literally true—whether in semantic paradoxes like the Liar or, as has occasionally been proposed, in the boundary conditions of physical theories—seems to depend on a formal system that blocks explosion. This dependency strikes me as methodologically circular. If the dialetheist's central claim is metaphysical, namely that reality itself contains contradictory states, then adopting a logic that prevents those states from entailing every proposition looks less like an independent formal discovery and more like a protective stipulation designed to save the metaphysics from immediate triviality. So my question is this: is there any substantive content to dialetheism that survives independently of paraconsistent logic? If one genuinely believed that a particular contradiction was true but retained classical inference rules, the result would be universal triviality; therefore, doesn't the coherence of dialetheism derive almost entirely from the prior decision to revise the logic rather than from any independent argument that contradictions are actually true? And if that is correct, should we treat dialetheism not as a metaphysical thesis about the world, but rather as an interpretive gloss on a particular formal framework—analogous to reading physical curvature into non-Euclidean geometry—rather than as a claim that stands on its own?
