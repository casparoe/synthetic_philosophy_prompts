In the literature on moral revolutions—how norms such as slavery, duelling, and foot-binding come to be regarded as intolerable—there is a well-known but seemingly intractable division. One line of thought, associated with Appiah, holds that honour codes are self-undermining: the same status concerns that sustain a practice eventually generate internal contradictions that dissolve it. A rival line, associated with Williams, treats honour-language as largely epiphenomenal and argues that norms collapse when material interests shift and powerful actors find compliance more costly than defection. Both camps appeal to the same historical episodes—abolition, the decline of duelling, the end of foot-binding—yet interpret them as confirming opposite causal structures.

This disagreement has persisted for decades without convergence. What kind of stall is it? Is there an unacknowledged empirical dispute about the causal efficacy of rhetoric versus interest in specific archives? Is it a conceptual disagreement about what an honour-based explanation must entail—whether honour must be causally efficacious or merely available as a narrative? Or are the two sides addressing different questions: one about the lived experience of moral change, the other about the structural conditions that make change durable?

Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says, distinguish substantive conflict from verbal overlap, and say what would count as progress—whether a formal model, a counterfactual historical case, or a conceptual distinction—given your diagnosis.
