Next month I present to the philosophy-of-death working group—roughly half analytic philosophers of death (mostly deprivation-account sympathizers with a couple Epicurean holdouts), two specialists in classical Chinese philosophy who are sharply suspicious of anachronistic framing, and one phenomenologist of grief—defending a thesis I call “dispositional death” in Zhuangzi. I argue that his response to his wife’s death (the drumming on the tub, the butterfly-dream refusal of mourning, the general indifference to bodily transformation) should not be read as stoic equanimity or mystical dissolution of self, but as the maintenance of full functional and affective continuity alongside the deliberate relinquishment of the narrative identity that makes mortality a harm. I develop this through a distinction I draw between “narrative selfhood” and “processual engagement,” claiming it is implicit in the inner chapters rather than imported from contemporary phenomenology, and I apply it specifically to the problem of whether death can be a bad thing for the one who dies when that “one” is not conceived as a persisting narrative subject. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; frame everything so that the objections target the argument itself, not my familiarity with the literature.
