I cover science and ideas for a regional magazine, and lately I've been obsessed with a problem I hit while drafting a feature on pre-Newtonian mechanics. I kept writing that 17th-century scholars were "investigating gravity," and my editor flagged it as presentist. We fought about whether translating old terms into modern ones is just clear writing or whether it smuggles in theories they never held. I'm auditing a philosphy of history course online and the presentism debates are way more granular than I expected—there's conceptual presentism, normative presentism, teleological framing (treating past work as an imperfect step toward us), and something my prof called epistemic presentism, where you assume past actors had access to modern evidential standards but just failed to use them. This comes up constantly for me: do I call an old idea a "theory," say they "discovered" something, judge their methods by modern peer review? I need a reusable decision procedre I can run in about 20 minutes before filing, with clear gates, because my editor has to sign off and I don't have time to become a historian.

Please act as both a philosopher of history and a veteran science editor who lives on deadlines. Before you build anything, do a quick brainstorm of the four kinds of presentism I mentioned—conceptual, normative, teleological, and epistemic—plus any other form that actually trips up journalists. Then turn that into a flowchart-in-words: fixed order of questions, what counts as a yes/no at each gate, where the procedre stops cold with "don't file this framing," and exactly where it's a judgment call that needs my editor's sign-off rather than a rule. Run it on three cases: describing an alchemist's transmutation work using modern chemistry vocabulary; profiling a beloved 19th-century ecologist who also pushed eugenics, where the procedre should probably tell me to do something I won't like (specifically, don't historicize away the harm by saying "everyone believed that then"); and comparing two 1920s physicists' takes on quantum formalism without framing one as "closer to the modern view."
