I've been trying to defend a somewhat idiosyncratic account of promissory obligation—call it the "architectural tether" view. On this view, promises bind not primarily through social expectation or contract-like reciprocity, but because the act of promising introduces a non-revisable structural constraint into the speaker's practical reasoning space, functioning analogously to how a hard-coded observation operates within a Solomonoff-induction framework: it does not merely update probabilities, it reshapes the hypothesis space available for subsequent deliberation. This, I think, captures why breaking a promise carries a distinctive phenomenological weight—you are not just failing another person, you are violating an internal boundary condition that you have made part of your own cognitive architecture. I am preparing to present this to a small reading group that includes both moral philosophers and people working on formal models of rational commitment, and I want to be ready for what actually undermines it rather than for the objections I have already rehearsed.

So tell me what would count against the architectural tether view—not just the familiar "promises are conventional" objection, but which specific intuitions about duress, self-promises, or revoked commitments it cannot accommodate; which results in decision theory (say, about precommitment strategies collapsing into standard Bayesian updating) or in philosophy of mind (say, about the unity of practical reasoning) it risks contradicting; and whether there is a fatal formal result showing that any "internal restructuring" of this kind is either impossible or redundant. Rank the defeaters from genuinely fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether a committed defender of the view should concede its force or can plausibly dismiss it—and identify the weakest point in the position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook.
