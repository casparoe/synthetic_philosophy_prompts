I retired from a biology lab about three years ago and started sitting in on philosophy seminars, which is how I ended up obsessed with this old strategy-stealing proof they talk about in combinatorial games like Hex. There's this assumption behind it — that if player two could steal player one's strategy, then player one can't actually have a winning strategy to begin with — and philosophers have been arguing for decades about whether that tells us something real about rationality and counterfactual choice, or if its just a formal bookkeeping trick with no philosophical weight. Some people say the sides are talking past each other because one camp treats strategy as an executed plan and the other treats it as an abstract mapping, but I wonder if the stall is deeper: maybe we're smuggling in philosophy-of-action questions about what it means for an agent to possess a strategy they never deploy, and nobody wants to admit thats the real issue.

My working theory — probably half-baked — is that this isn't really a disagreement about mathematics or game theory at all, but about the standards we use for counterfactual explanations, and that both sides are answering different questions under the same name. But I'm not sure. Could there be a hidden formal question here, like whether the argument collapses in continuous-state analogues or in evolutionary game models where strategies mutate in real time rather than being fixed at the start? Or is the disagreement purely verbal, or rooted in starting intuitions that neither side can defend? I want an honest diagnosis — is there a distinction, a theorem, or even a change of question that could break the deadlock, or should we expect it to stall forever? Don't just survey every interpretation; tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is and whether progress is even possible.
