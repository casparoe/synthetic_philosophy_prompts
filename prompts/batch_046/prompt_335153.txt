As a novelist constructing a narrative in which human, synthetic, and non-human minds occupy shared conditions of vulnerability, I have arrived at a cluster of intuitive judgments about which beings bear direct moral weight. I want to know whether these judgments trace a coherent principle of moral status—one precise enough to guide a scene rather than merely to decorate an essay—or whether they collapse into irreducible pluralism, and I want an honest accounting of which judgments must be sacrificed for coherence.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

A human infant with anencephaly, possessing only brainstem reflexes and no cortical architecture capable of integrated experience: I judge there is no direct moral wrong in allowing its biological processes to cease, though indirect duties to those who care for it remain. A sleeping adult human, by contrast, retains full moral status despite total unconsciousness; the suspension of awareness does not dissolve the claim. An octopus navigating an unfamiliar maze to escape an aversive stimulus: substantial moral status, not reducible to behavioral complexity alone. A single honeybee avoiding harm: minimal but genuine status. The hive as a collective, making sophisticated distributed decisions: none—moral status does not aggregate upward from individuals to the swarm. A nematode with three hundred and two neurons, withdrawing from heat: the case I keep returning to; I lean toward granting it less than a bee, perhaps only a vanishing trace, yet I am genuinely unsettled. A thermostat maintaining temperature: none. A synthetic neural network that predicts human emotional states with high fidelity but possesses no internal valence, suffering, or interest: none, despite its predictive richness. The plant Mimosa pudica, with rapid electrical signaling and tactile memory: none; signaling is not suffering. A brain-dead human body sustained by ventilation, with intact brainstem homeostasis but no possibility of recovery: none; biological persistence and reflex are insufficient.

My tentative principle—which I call integrated valence coherence—holds that an entity has moral status in proportion to the coherence of its non-decomposable trajectory of positive and negative states, not to its information-processing capacity or biological form. This explains the sleeping human (the trajectory is suspended but structurally intact), the octopus, the hive's failure (the collective fragments into sub-trajectories), and the exclusion of both thermostat and predictive network. Yet it also seems to grant the nematode a minimal standing I am uneasy with, and it implies that a synthetic being with genuinely integrated valence would possess moral standing regardless of substrate—a consequence I accept in principle but fear is underdetermined by my other judgments. Most troubling is the gap between the sleeping human and the brain-dead body: both lack consciousness, yet I treat them radically differently, and I am unsure whether my principle captures that difference by appealing to structural potential or whether it smuggles in a biological prejudice.

What principle, if any, delivers all of these verdicts without excessive contrivance? Where must I concede that one judgment is wrong for the sake of coherence? Apply the best candidate to a case I have not listed: a synthetic entity composed of distributed, non-localized processing nodes that collectively maintain a unified valenced trajectory without any central experience center, which can be temporarily decomposed across separate servers and then reconstituted with full continuity restored. Does it have status, and does the answer depend on whether the interruption is recoverable?
