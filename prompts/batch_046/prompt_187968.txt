Consider the institutional framing practice observed at official Holocaust memorials and state-sponsored education centers—above all in Germany—where curatorial guidelines and pedagogical materials strictly prohibit comparing the Holocaust to other genocides or to colonial mass violence, treating such comparison as a moral category error that diminishes the event's specific gravity, while memorial institutions for other state-organized atrocities—such as the Kigali Genocide Memorial or Cambodia's Tuol Sleng—routinely invite precisely such comparative framing, encouraging visitors to locate structural parallels with slavery, imperial violence, or other bureaucratic killings. What philosophical commitments about historical uniqueness, moral comparability, and the ontology of modernity does this asymmetry presuppose? Must it rest on a thesis—of the kind advanced in debates like the Historikerstreit and in Zygmunt Bauman's analysis of modern rational bureaucracy—that the Holocaust is not one instance among others but a singular rupture within modern civilization itself, such that comparison inherently normalizes? Or is the prohibition fully explicable by context-specific obligations of German state remembrance without committing to any metaphysics of uniqueness? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes the comparative ban coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and describe what would have to change in these institutions' design and pedagogy if the underlying view—whether a uniqueness claim, a modernity thesis, or a pragmatic politics of memory—were replaced by a framework that treats all large-scale state killing as morally and causally comparable instances of one kind.
