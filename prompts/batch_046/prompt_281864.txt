I am preparing an independent philosophy-of-science essay for a high school research competition, and I have been working through John Stuart Mill's System of Logic (1843), particularly the chapters in Book III where he analyzes causation and distinguishes conditions that involve the presence of an active force from those that involve only an absence. As I understand his position, Mill holds that while a necessary condition involving a present agency can be included straightforwardly in the cause, a mere absence—such as the lack of rainfall—should not be treated as a cause in the same positive sense, unless that absence can be reconstrued as the removal of a counteracting force. He seems to permit negative conditions into the total account of causation only under that specific reconstrual, not as bare omissions.

Here is the difficulty I am trying to resolve. In mechanistic anomaly detection—specifically in fault-tree analysis for redundant engineering systems—practitioners routinely treat an omission, such as a sensor's failure to emit an expected signal, as a causal node that explains a system failure, assigning it weight in the causal graph. This practice appears to treat bare absence as causally productive, which seems to conflict with Mill's stricture unless one reinterprets the omission as the presence of a broken mechanism.

Could you help me understand whether Mill's distinction between positive and negative conditions can be coherently applied to this kind of mechanistic omission-causation? I want to know whether fault-tree analysis implicitly preserves Mill's metaphysics by redefining absence as the presence of dysfunction, or whether it genuinely allows bare omission to function as a cause in a way Mill's framework cannot accommodate without contradiction. If there is a residue—some feature of omission-causation in engineering risk analysis that has no direct counterpart in Mill's logic of conditions—I would like to understand whether that residue reveals a limitation in Mill's account or merely a pragmatic divergence in vocabulary that leaves his core distinction intact.
