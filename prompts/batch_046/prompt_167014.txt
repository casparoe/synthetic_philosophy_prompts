I'm a journalist who covers the history of science and ideas, and I'm currently auditing an upper-level undergraduate seminar on early modern philosophy that treats Hobbes's work in both mathematics and politics. The instructor has asked me to help draft the final examination, which students will complete in ninety minutes, and I want to make sure the essay question cannot be answered by anyone who has merely memorized a standard biography of Hobbes or a textbook summary of Leviathan.

Please design one exam essay question—aimed at students who have read Hobbes's geometric writings, including his dispute with John Wallis over the quadrature of the circle, and his political philosophy in Leviathan—that forces them to work through a specific tension. The tension is between Hobbes's geometric constructivism, according to which genuine knowledge requires the mental or physical construction of its object from first principles and according to which non-constructive or infinitely divisible entities are epistemically suspect fictions, and his political claim that the sovereign is an artificial entity constituted through representation and covenant rather than natural generation. The question should ask students to determine whether Hobbes's suspicion of mathematical fictions undermines his reliance on fiction and artificial representation in defining the commonwealth, or whether his constructivist method actually grounds the legitimacy of the sovereign as a deliberately constructed artifact.

Provide a model answer of about six hundred to seven hundred words that argues through the tension rather than summarizing each side separately, and provide a marking scheme for twenty marks built around five must-hit conceptual moves. For each move, state what it requires, assign marks that sum to twenty, and include a brief note on the typical student error—usually either reducing the relationship to a loose analogy without philosophical pressure, or dismissing Hobbes's geometry as biographically interesting but philosophically irrelevant to his politics. The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
