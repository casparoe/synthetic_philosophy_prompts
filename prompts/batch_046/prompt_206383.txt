I have been trying to hold my judgments about the ethics of belief steady across concrete cases, and I find they pull in different directions. Here are four verdicts I want to preserve if possible, though I suspect they may not all survive together.

First, a marine biologist is testing whether a reef species is photosynthetic. The data sit right at the detection limit; statistical power is low and the effect is genuinely ambiguous. She treats the hypothesis as true for the purpose of designing a larger study and writing a grant, but she does not present it as established. She calls it a working supposition. I think this is permissible—perhaps even professionally required.

Second, a man has strong but not conclusive evidence of his spouse’s infidelity: unexplained absences, a changed routine, nothing definitive. Rather than investigating further, he chooses to interpret the evidence charitably and to believe in her fidelity, because the belief sustains an otherwise healthy marriage and because he thinks constant suspicion would make life with her unlivable. I think this is permissible, though only barely.

Third, a political activist believes, on the basis of one unverified documentary and some social-media threads, that a recent election was systematically stolen. The belief is not compelled by the evidence, but it motivates voter-registration work and binds her to a community. She finds it energizing. I think this is not permissible.

Fourth, a philosopher who has studied the free-will debate for years concludes that determinism is somewhat more probable than libertarian free will. Nevertheless, she continues to regard herself and others as genuinely responsible agents, and she reports that she believes in free will for practical purposes. She does not suppress her theoretical judgment; the two attitudes are compartmentalized. I think this is permissible, but it feels different in kind from the biologist’s working supposition.

What principle, if any, delivers all four verdicts? I can imagine a Clifford-style evidentialist rule that condemns the second and fourth, and a James-style pragmatic permission that might save the second but also risks licensing the third. Is there a principled distinction—perhaps turning on voluntariness, on whether the belief is adopted as an action-guiding policy rather than as a representation of fact, or on whether the practical need is personal versus collective—between the biologist’s supposition, the spouse’s charitable interpretation, the philosopher’s compartmentalization, and the activist’s motivated conviction? Or must I overturn one of my judgments?

Please state the principle, or a small family of competing principles, precisely. For each clause, indicate which case motivates it. Give the verdicts each principle yields for a case I have not listed: specifically, a physician who believes her critically ill patient will recover despite grim statistics, because she thinks her own confidence improves outcomes, even though she privately admits the belief is not well-supported. Finally, be explicit about which of my four verdicts each principle cannot accommodate, and whether saving consistency requires abandoning the biologist’s permission, the spouse’s permission, the activist’s condemnation, or the philosopher’s compartmentalization.
