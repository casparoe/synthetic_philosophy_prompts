I serve on the Acquisition and Ethics Committee of our university library's Special Collections division. We have received an offer to acquire the complete digital archive of the Mercer Consensus Group, a forecasting and decision-analysis collective active from approximately 2014 to 2023 that was well-known within rationalist and effective-altruism forums for running internal prediction markets and structured adversarial reviews. The donation comprises internal memos, calibration ledgers, and forum threads drawn almost entirely from the core membership, but it contains virtually no material from the dissent cohort—members who exited or were expelled following a 2022 methodological split over risk-weighting protocols. The donor, a former senior coordinator, proposes that we catalog the collection as an intact institutional record, noting the absence in the finding aid but treating it as incidental data loss rather than structural omission.

We must recommend whether the library should (a) accept the archive under standard preservation and access protocols; (b) accept only on the condition that we actively construct and integrate a counter-archive of dissenting voices, with dedicated funding for solicitation and preservation; or (c) reject the donation on the grounds that its silences reproduce the very power asymmetries the collective claimed to study.

The underlying philosophical problem concerns archives not as passive storage but as active instruments of epistemic governance—who gets to count as part of the institutional reasoning record. Rather than rehearsing generic critiques, I want the memo to work through three distinctions I think archival ethics rarely handles precisely: first, between negative silence (deliberate suppression of records) and constitutive silence (the inevitable fact that any recording architecture renders certain forms of disagreement—particularly off-platform, private, or post-expulsion deliberation—unarchivable by design); second, whether an archive's value depends on its representativeness of the community's actual belief distribution, or only on the transparency of its selection mechanisms; and third, what I will call the red-team asymmetry—the tendency in adversarial institutions for dissent to be generated in formats (encrypted side-channels, informal debriefs, withdrawn submissions) that lie outside the archival frame not by accident but by institutional logic.

Please draft the committee's formal opinion recommending one course of action, including a concrete implementation timeline if you favor conditional acceptance. The memo should treat the donor's argument with intellectual honesty: he contends that requiring counter-archives sets an impossible standard for every donation, that transparency about gaps satisfies scholarly obligation, and that the internal adversarial protocols themselves are the real epistemic safeguard, not demographic completeness. You should identify the strongest version of this position, then explain specifically why it still does not justify option (a). The document should conclude with an unambiguous recommendation suitable for circulation before Thursday's vote, not with an open-ended reflection.
