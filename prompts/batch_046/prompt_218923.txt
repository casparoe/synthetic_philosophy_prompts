I've been trying to wrap my head around Molinism for a paper I'm writing, and there's one conceptual snag I can't shake. So middle knowledge is supposed to be God's knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom—like, if I were in situation S, I would freely do action A. And it's "middle" because it's logically prior to God's creative decree but posterior to his knowledge of necessary truths. My professor brought up the grounding objection: if these counterfactuals are true before God decides to create anything, and they're not grounded in God's will, what makes them true? I understand the usual replies—maybe they're grounded in the individual essence of the creature, or maybe they're just primitive brute facts—but I'm stuck on how this squares with libertarian freedom.

Specifically, if it's already true (independant of God and independant of my actual choice) that in circumstance C I *would* freely steal the cookie, and God knows this via middle knowledge and then actualizes C, doesn't that make my stealing feel determined? Not causally determined by God, since he doesn't cause the choice, but determined by the counterfactual itself being a fixed feature of reality. If the counterfactual is false, God couldn't actualize that world and get that result, but since it's true and he picks that world, my choice seems locked in by something that isn't me and isn't God either. How do Molinists avoid this turning into a kind of soft determinism? I want an intuitive explanation of where the "freedom" actually lives in this picture, especially regarding the idea that the counterfactual might be grounded in my essence but still allows for libertarian indeterminacy in the actual moment.
