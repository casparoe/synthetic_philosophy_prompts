I'm giving the opening remarks next Tuesday at an interdisciplinary workshop we're running on collective memory and institutional archives — the room is split between philosophers of history and actual archivists/community organizers who run local memory projects, which is exactly the kind of weird mix I like but also means I can't just lecture. The official theme is 'what we owe the past,' yet I'm tired of speeches that only say we must never forget; at the same time I can't just celebrate erasure. I've been working with the tension between Margalit's ethical duty to remember wrongs done to others and Rieff's argument that sometimes the moral work is in letting things fade, but I want to push this through a concept I'm playing with called 'mnemonic debt' — remembering as a kind of unpaid labor the dead extract from the living, which makes forgetting sometimes a form of restitution rather than betrayal. This has to shape what I can actually say: the speech shouldn't be able to end with a call for more monuments or more archives, and it shouldn't endorse wholesale deletion either.

There's also this concrete situation hanging over the workshop — our university archive just recieved a collection of oral histories where the community explicitly asked that certain tapes be destroyed after fifty years, and the staff is genuinely torn about whether honoring that request is a betrayal or a fulfillment. I need the remarks to run about five minutes, spoken in a casual professor-voice, with one concrete image or example that carries the philosophical weight for people who don't spend their days in ethics of memory. Don't just list philosophers; make the idea move. And please, no bibliography or citations — I'm speaking, not writing a paper.
