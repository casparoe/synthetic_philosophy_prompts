Here is an argument I developed for a workshop and now find suspicious because its conclusion undermines Marx without seeming to misrepresent him. (1) Marx defines exploitation as the extraction of surplus value, which requires that labor power be sold as a commodity and produce more value than its reproduction cost. (2) He defines alienation as separation from the product, the production process, one's species-being, and fellow workers. (3) Now imagine a collectively owned, fully automated factory: no labor power is sold for a wage, so by (1) there is no exploitation. (4) Yet the remaining human workers—maintenance technicians, supervisors—are separated from control over design and surplus, and experience the machinery as an autonomous power dominating them, which looks like separation from the process and product. (5) So alienation appears to persist even though exploitation is absent. (6) But Marx claims that abolishing private property and the commodity form of labor power eliminates alienation. (7) In this case private property is abolished and labor is not commodified, yet the separation described in (4) remains. (8) Therefore either Marx is wrong that abolishing the commodity form eliminates alienation, or the separation in (4) is not genuine Marxian alienation—which seems to require arguing that domination by machinery is conceptually distinct from the fourfold alienation Marx describes, but then Marx's critique of technology under capitalism loses its specific force. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Is there a hidden equivocation between the social form of labor and the technical form of production, or does one of the premises carry more weight than it appears? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (6) intact and pay whatever price that requires.
