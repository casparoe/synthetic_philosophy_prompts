I've come round to a kind of pattern-realist stance on the special composition question, and I want to stress-test it properly. I think composite objects exist whenever their parts are bound by dense causal-functional interaction that produces genuinely emergent explanatory patterns, but this comes in degrees rather than being all-or-nothing. So organisms and well-integrated machines are robustly real composites, scattered groupings like 'all the red things in this room' are barely composite at all, and there's no sharp boundary where composition suddenly switches on. This lets me keep tables and chairs without buying the ontological explosion of unrestricted mereology, and it avoids the nihilist's counter-intuitive denial of everyday objects.

But I'm worried I've just built a sliding scale that can be adjusted to dodge any counterexample. What would actually count against this view? I want the defeaters sorted: which ones would be fatal — say, if interaction density turned out to bear no relation to our intuitive judgements about objecthood — which ones would merely be awkward, like having to accept that a loosely coordinated swarm counts as one thing, and which ones it's defenders could fairly dismiss as missing the point. I'd also like an honest assessment of whether this graded approach has already been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that's genuinely a problem for a metaphysical position of this kind. Could results from physics or cognitive science about part-whole relations undermine it, or are those concerns basically orthogonal?
