I've been working through the endurantism/perdurantism debate and keep returning to what looks like a framing problem rather than a substantive ontological disagreement. Both accounts seem able to accommodate the same change-data—an object being bent at one time and straight at another—by distributing properties across either times, via relational or adverbial strategies, or across temporal parts. But consider a precisely engineered biological system designed for symmetric binary fission: for a brief interval the two daughter entities share every molecular part with each other and with the original, then diverge. The endurantist can invoke continuity conditions, best-candidate rules, or branching identities to decide whether one object survived, split, or was replaced. The perdurantist, it seems, must treat the pre-fission temporal parts as belonging to two distinct four-dimensional worms that overlap completely across that slice. This appears to force the perdurantist either to deny that a part can belong to more than one fusion—which undermines the very account of composite persistence—or to accept that two distinct objects can be spatiotemporally coincident in exactly the manner endurantists allow for enduring objects. Given that both frameworks apparently need auxiliary identity conditions to resolve fission, and that perdurantism seems to generate the very overlap problems it was meant to avoid, is there any non-terminological difference remaining? Specifically, if the four-dimensional worm is ultimately just a redescription of an endurantist object-career indexed to times, what empirical or modal work does the temporal-parts ontology actually perform that could not be achieved by indexing properties to times together with standard continuity conditions?
