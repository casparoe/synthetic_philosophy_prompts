I'm on the ethics committe for our city's emergancy preparedness board, and we've got to vote thursday on a proposed "dynamic triage protocol" for surge events. The protocol uses predictive modeling to flag neighborhoods where severe cases are likely to cluster; if capacity gets tight, ambulances get diverted away from those zones to hospitals with open beds, which means some people in the flagged areas won't get ICU care they otherwise might have received. The justification is pure aggregation: saving forty moderate-prognosis patients by not attempting (and failing) to save ten with terrible prognosis, plus preventing system collapse.

One board member—she's been reading Scanlon—says this is exactly the kind of cross-personal aggregation contractualism blocks. She argues you can't treat people as fungible statistical placeholders in an optimization, especially since the diverted patients aren't the ones being saved; they're just probabilities in the model. She also draws a distinction between "we couldn't save you because beds were full elsewhere" and "we actively diverted resources away from you to save others," saying only the first is defensible.

The rest of the board thinks this is seminar-room stuff that doesn't apply to real emergency management. They want me to draft a short written opinion recommending approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, with an actual implementation timeline or revision schedule. The memo needs to take the Scanlon-style objection seriously—not just dismiss it—but also needs to be readable by emergency managers who need a bottom line. In particular, please engage whether contractualism really prohibits aggregation when the harmed and benefited parties are probabilistically determined rather than named individuals, and whether that "active diversion vs. passive scarcity" distinction holds up under pressure. I don't need citations or a reading list; I just need reasoning our board can actually respond to, including an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side.
