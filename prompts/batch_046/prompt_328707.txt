I'm currently working through an advanced metaphysics seminar on the ontology of natural law, and after closely studying Hume's treatments of necessary connection in both the Treatise of Human Nature and the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, I have become preoccupied with a counterfactual historical question. Imagine that David Hume were revived in the present day and thoroughly briefed on the philosophy of science and metaphysics that developed after 1776, but without altering his core epistemological and ontological commitments.

Specifically, consider three anti-Humean positions he might encounter. First, the dispositional essentialist claim that laws of nature derive directly from the real essences of properties such as charge or spin, so that to possess the property just is to be governed by specific lawful regularities. Second, David Armstrong's account, according to which laws consist in contingent relations of nomic necessitation holding between actually instantiated universals. Third, a recently proposed hybrid—call it frame-invariant regularism—which accepts Hume's basic ontology of loose and separate existences and denies primitive modal force, yet argues that lawhood should be assigned only to those regularities that remain stable across every admissible epistemic framing of an observer embedded within the same causal network, thereby attempting to recover objectivity without invoking necessity in re. Given Hume's insistence that all ideas of necessary connection in nature originate in internal impressions of mental determination rather than in external objects, and his corresponding suspicion of occult qualities and multiplied entities, how would he likely assess each of these three views? I would like you to predict, for each position, whether he would regard it as a covert projection of custom onto the world, an unnecessary ontological inflation, or perhaps a tolerable refinement of his own regularism; and further, to identify which of the three he would probably judge the least damaging to empiricism, explaining in conceptual—not bibliographic—terms what his reasoning would be.
