Act as a senior philosopher of science with expertise in metaphysical reduction and its discontents. Then construct a final-exam essay question for an upper-level seminar (75 minutes, target answer length about 700 words) on cranes versus skyhooks and greedy reductionism. Do not ask for summaries or historical surveys; instead, embed the following applied scenario and ask the student to think through it.

Scenario: A social philosopher argues that democratic legitimacy is a skyhook, because it purports to explain why citizens obey laws while secretly presupposing that very obedience. She proposes a replacement crane: an explanation of stable compliance built bottom-up from repeated-interaction game theory, reputation tracking, and institutional feedback loops. Critics reply that this reconstruction is greedy reductionism—it does not explain legitimacy but explains it away, substituting strategic rationality for normative authority and leaving no meaningful residue.

Your exam question should have three parts. Part (a): Clarify the crane/skyhook distinction and explain precisely why being reductionist does not automatically make an explanation greedy. Part (b): Apply both distinctions to the legitimacy case—assess whether the game-theoretic model is genuinely a crane, and whether it is guilty of greedy reductionism or only of ordinary explanatory reduction. Part (c): Identify and defend one specific conceptual move that allows the reconstruction to preserve a non-eliminable normative residue—without invoking any mysterious top-down causal power. If you think no such move succeeds, explain exactly why any attempt collapses back into either greediness or a skyhook.

After the question, provide a model answer of roughly 700 words that separates an excellent answer from a mediocre one by hitting these specific moves: (1) defining cranes as cumulative, bottom-up explanations that build complex phenomena without unexplained explainers, and defining skyhooks as top-down dependencies; (2) distinguishing greedy reductionism (which eliminates or substitutes for the phenomenon being explained) from benign explanatory reduction (which shows how parts realize the whole); (3) analyzing the legitimacy case as a crane that explains compliance stability but risks greediness only if it claims that strategic rationality is identical to normative authority rather than its substrate; (4) proposing the specific move—treating normative justification as a scaffolded functional role that supervenes on the strategic substrate without being reducible to any single strategic state, so that first-person justification remains intact; and (5) critically evaluating whether this move genuinely avoids a skyhook or merely hides one inside the scaffolding.
