Consider the following argument about the boundary between legend and history, using the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus as test cases. A colleague offers this chain, which seems to show that none of the three can count as historically established, yet it extends to events we ordinarily treat as secure. I cannot accept the conclusion, but each premise looks defensible. I want a diagnosis of where the chain breaks.

(1) Call an event E "historically established" only if there exists a chain of evidence linking us to E that includes at least one link interpretable without presupposing the narrative content of any textual source whose primary purpose was non-epistemic—e.g., epic performance, dynastic legitimation, or cultic law.

(2) For the Trojan War, every material trace at Hisarlik is dated and classified through the Homeric narrative framework; stripped of that framework, the destruction layers are indistinguishable from other Anatolian site histories. The literary sources are epic poems composed centuries later for panhellenic cultural consolidation.

(3) For King Arthur, there is no 5th- or 6th-century administrative, epigraphic, or archaeological trace linkable to a specific British war leader without first importing the narrative framework of Geoffrey of Monmouth or the Welsh triads, both composed for political and literary purposes centuries afterward.

(4) For the Exodus, the archaeological record of the Sinai and Transjordan lacks the settlement and material-culture patterns expected from a large migrating population during the Late Bronze Age; traces sometimes cited (certain destruction layers in Canaan) are dated and interpreted through the biblical chronology, and the Pentateuchal account itself is a unified theological-legal document composed for covenantal instruction.

(5) Therefore, in none of these three cases does an independent, non-textually-mediated link exist; so none are historically established.

(6) Apply the same criterion to the crucifixion of Jesus: the canonical Gospels are interdependent texts composed for kerygmatic proclamation; Paul’s letters are pastoral-theological; Josephus and Tacitus offer only brief mentions whose independence from Christian tradition is contested; and any material traces are identified and interpreted through the Gospel narrative. No Roman administrative record of the execution can be read without that narrative framework.

(7) Apply it further to the establishment of the Roman Republic in 509 BCE: Livy, Dionysius, and Plutarch write centuries later and depend on annalistic traditions composed to glorify senatorial families; the archaeological record of the early Forum and Regia is ambiguous and interpreted through these same texts. No non-textual trace bypasses the narrative.

(8) So by (1), we must deny that the crucifixion and the founding of the Republic are historically established.

(9) But that is implausible; both are central to secular and religious historiography.

(10) Therefore, the argument proves too much, and something in (1)–(7) must fail.

Which specific premise or inferential step should be rejected? Explain why that step looked fine—what intuition about evidence and narrative dependency it captures—then explain what three standard positions on the legend-to-history boundary each have to pay in order to reject it. By "standard positions" I mean: (a) the view that history and legend differ only in degree of corroboration, not in kind; (b) the view that archaeological correlation can confirm a narrative even when interpretation is initially guided by it; and (c) the view that methodological naturalism allows us to filter supernatural framing and recover a historical kernel without accepting the full narrative. For each, identify the specific cost—what they must give up about independence, circularity, or transmission—when they deny the step you have singled out.
