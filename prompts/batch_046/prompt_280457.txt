I recently watched a respected colleague suffer sudden professional ruin that many in our field considered disproportionate to any intentional wrong, which has led me to reconsider karma not as folk superstition but as a philosophical theory of moral desert—the view that suffering and flourishing are non-instrumentally fitting to a person's moral quality. Analyze the 2008 collapse of Lehman Brothers and the subsequent fates of its senior leadership, particularly Richard Fuld's retention of substantial wealth and absence of criminal charges contrasted with the loss of pensions among lower-level employees, through the lens of this theory. Identify the relevant moral agents, specify what counts as the desert-tracking outcome in this case, and say whether karma predicts that culpability and material harm should align at the individual level. Then address what the episode reveals about the theory: does the decoupling of moral blame from financial ruin for Fuld, and the inverse suffering of less culpable staff, function as an anomaly, or can the theory accommodate it by appealing to delayed, non-material, or systemically distributed desert? Be precise about where the mapping succeeds, where it forces an answer the theory does not provide, and where the episode shows that karma was never designed to explain institutional collapses of this kind.
