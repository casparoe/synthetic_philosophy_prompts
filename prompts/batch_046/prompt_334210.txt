I run a postgraduate seminar in moral philosophy with fourteen students for a single ninety-minute session on Confucian role ethics. I want an in-class simulation that makes the group feel a specific tension: not the familiar conflict between a father’s duty and a magistrate’s duty, but what I am calling structural role ambiguity — a situation in which the relational field that defines the roles is itself unstable, so that the ethical task is not to choose between pre-fixed obligations but to perform a response that renegotiates what the roles mean in that very context. The students should discover this through action rather than lecture: they should begin by trying to apply their assigned relational positions and only gradually realise that applying them consistently dissolves the situation that makes them intelligible.

Design a runnable activity with assigned nested roles — for example, elder sibling, local steward, disciple to a rival teacher, and family head — facing a staged crisis in which a communal resource has been mismanaged by the very person whose role should have protected it, and no external authority is present to adjudicate. The rules must require participants to speak and decide solely from within their assigned relational positions, with a symbolic payoff scheme (reputation tokens representing harmony and accountability) that rewards both, yet makes it structurally impossible to maximise both without altering what the roles entail in this specific case.
