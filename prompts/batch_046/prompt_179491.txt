I am auditing a graduate seminar on rational choice theory, and after a recent session on the independence axiom I found myself in a sustained argument with a colleague over whether Maurice Allais’s classic counterexample exposes a deep normative defect or merely documents a descriptive failure in how people compute compound probabilities. To sharpen the disagreement, we constructed a non-standard three-stage variant: the agent first chooses between a certain intermediate payoff and a gamble that resolves into one of two second-stage sub-lotteries, each of which then reproduces the original Allais structure—one branch offering a certain high-value outcome and the other a risky alternative—so that the independence axiom could in principle be applied recursively at every node of the tree. My colleague takes what we have been calling the recursive structuralist position: she insists that if preferences still violate independence at the root even after the agent is explicitly taught to compute the tree correctly and to treat intermediate nodes as transparent, then the axiom is normatively unsound for fully rational agents, not just psychologically unrealistic. I lean toward a node-weighting contextualist view: I think the violation persists because agents apply non-linear, reference-dependent probability weighting locally at each node, which means decomposing the compound act into a decision tree analytically misses the phenomenological unity of facing a single choice situation, and that applying independence recursively merely pushes the distortion downward without resolving it at the root.

Write a dialogue between these two interlocutors—the Recursive Structuralist and the Node-Weighting Contextualist—debating whether our three-tier variant strengthens or weakens the case against the independence axiom. Both should be equally sharp and respond directly to each other’s actual points rather than talking past one another. The Structuralist should explain why contextual weighting at nodes is either ad hoc or self-defeating once the tree is fully transparent to the agent, and should address whether treating compound acts as decomposed trees forces rational agents to ignore structurally relevant information about how outcomes are reached. The Contextualist should explain why evaluating acts only by their reduced-form outcome distributions ignores the way reference points are anchored to the choice situation as a whole, and should respond specifically to the charge that her position collapses into an unmotivated rejection of analytical decomposition. Have them converge on a precise statement of their genuine disagreement—whether rational evaluation requires assessing acts by their final payoff distributions alone or by the procedural structure through which those payoffs are generated—and end with each identifying the further case that would force her to revise her view.
