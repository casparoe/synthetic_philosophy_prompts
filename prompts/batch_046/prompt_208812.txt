Consider the cluster of institutional protocols that govern the restoration, display, and market classification of classical and modern sculpture in major museums and auction houses. Conservation departments adhere to a principle of reversibility: any physical intervention on an original substrate—filling a lacuna, reattaching a limb, or stabilizing a crack—must employ materials chemically distinguishable from the original marble or bronze, so that future conservators can remove the addition without damaging the authentic substance. At the same time, these institutions strictly prohibit the reconstruction of missing anatomical parts using modern stone or alloy that mimics the original, even when the result would be visually and materially indistinguishable to viewers and experts alike. Yet these same museums routinely install high-resolution digital projections, augmented-reality overlays, or adjacent plaster casts that present the "complete" work in its hypothetical original state, treating these surrogates as transparent pedagogical aids rather than deceptions. Meanwhile, the art market distinguishes legally and aesthetically between posthumous bronze casts produced from an artist's original molds with estate authorization—sold as original editions—and forgeries produced with stolen molds that yield materially identical objects, criminalizing the latter despite the absence of any perceptible or chemical difference. What philosophical commitments about artistic value, authenticity, and the ontology of artworks does this cluster of practices presuppose?

Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes these rules cohere. Does the prohibition on irreversible material reconstruction presuppose that artistic value is causally or indexically tied to the artist's direct physical manipulation—so that any modern hand introducing original substance violates an essential historical relation—while the permission for digital reconstruction treats the work as a formal type that can be tokened without ontological damage? Or does the distinction between authorized posthumous casts and forgeries depend on a relational theory of authenticity, in which value flows not from material origin but from a chain of institutional authorization? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires—perhaps a normative distinction between "restoration" (reversible, non-deceptive) and "reconstruction" (irreversible, deceptive)—from what it merely accommodates, such as the acceptance of virtual surrogates that do not physically alter the object. Is the practice philosophically consistent: if a digital overlay is permitted as an explicitly marked fiction, why is a physically continuous but materially identical reconstruction forbidden? Would a museum that abandoned reversibility and embraced perfect material reconstruction have to adopt a radically different metaphysics of art—perhaps treating a sculpture as an allographic work like a musical score, instantiable by any token with the correct formal properties—or can the current protocols be sustained under several rival theories of value?
