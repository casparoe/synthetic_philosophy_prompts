I am drafting a seminar contribution on the moral economy of working time, specifically the tension between the Protestant work ethic, the campaign for a 35-hour week, and karoshi—death by overwork in Japan. In a recent discussion, a fellow student dismissed proposals to cap working hours by invoking the proverb “idle hands are the devil’s workshop”. She seemed to mean that reducing labour invites moral corruption, yet another participant insisted the saying only describes unemployment rather than legislated leisure. Before I can reply, I need to know precisely what thesis is being defended and whether it actually threatens the idea that leisure is a right.

Could you unpack the distinct precise claims that this proverb might express—distinguishing empirical predictions about crime or mental health, theological assertions about human purpose, normative arguments about whether leisure is a reward or an entitlement, and economic claims about productivity—indicating for each whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or plainly false? Then specify which of these readings actually support the conclusion that shorter working weeks are harmful or that extreme overwork is morally acceptable, and which are being illicitly smuggled in when the phrase is used to shut down debate about karoshi and labour rights. In particular, consider the counter-claim, associated with the nineteenth-century socialist Paul Lafargue, that idleness should be treated not as a vice but as a positive entitlement—sometimes described as a right to laziness—and say whether the proverb can survive that challenge together with the empirical reality of karoshi.

Please structure your response in the following sections, with approximate lengths:

1. “Restatement of the argumentative context” (about 150 words): identify the specific claim about the 35-hour week or karoshi that the proverb is meant to support.

2. “Menu of precise readings” (about 400 words): lay out the different theses as a numbered list, moving from the trivial to the radical, with brief explanation of each.

3. “Verdicts: platitude, defensible, or false” (about 250 words): classify each reading and give brief reasoning.

4. “Which readings do the argumentative work?” (about 250 words): state clearly which interpretations actually yield an objection to reduced working hours and which do not.

5. “The Lafargue challenge and karoshi” (about 200 words): examine whether the proverb holds up against the idea of leisure as a positive right and against the institutional reality of death by overwork, noting any tensions.
