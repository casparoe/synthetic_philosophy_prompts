My friend and I just spent three hours arguing about Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition—specifically whether treating relations as genuinely real, independent entities forces an infinite regress of linking links, and whether the only stable escape is to accept that the unity of a judgment is primitive rather than decomposable into separable terms plus an added relation. I want to settle our disagreement not with more talk but by putting a seminar through the problem physically, so I need you to design the session.

Design a 90-minute classroom activity for twelve advanced undergraduate or first-year graduate students in metaphysics. The activity should be runnable with only paper, index cards, colored blocks, and string—no screens, no money, no prior lecture. Give them rules and roles that force them to try to build unified "facts" or "propositions" out of discrete tokens (blocks representing terms, clips or strings representing relations), but arrange the mechanics so that every connector token they add to link two terms is itself an unlinked term that demands its own connector. They should feel the regress in their hands: each attempt to finish a structure reproduces the gap.

Build in a collective completion condition—say, every student must submit one stable structure within 45 minutes, and the group only "passes" if all structures hold—so that failure feels shared rather than individual, but do not use real currency. At the point where the tower of connectors becomes visibly absurd, provide a hidden option: a single shared frame or envelope that can hold blocks together without itself being clipped in. Do not name this option in the rules; they should discover it under pressure.

Specify the exact timeline, materials list, role assignments if any, and the debrief structure. The debrief must map specific mechanical moments onto Bradley's moves—the external-relation token, the regress step, the turn to internal relations, and the appeal to a non-composite unity—without resolving the metaphysical dispute for them. Predict exactly what will go wrong the first time you run it: likely, students will treat the shared frame as just another hidden connector and accuse the design of cheating, or they will collapse into treating all relations as internal without seeing the cost. Say how to handle that in the room without settling the ambiguity.

Finally, be frank about which feature of Bradley's actual view this activity cannot reproduce—probably his absolute-idealist conclusion that all relational and pluralistic thinking is ultimately appearance belonging to a single undifferentiated whole—so that I know precisely what I must supply in the discussion that follows. The participants should come away seeing why the problem is not a verbal trick but a structural pressure on anyone who wants both real relations and real unity, and they should only hear Bradley's name and the formal distinction between external and internal relations after they have already hit the wall themselves. Write in American English, keep it formal and polished, and do not ask for a bibliography or citations.
