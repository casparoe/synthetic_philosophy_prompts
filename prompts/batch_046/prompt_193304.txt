After a long argument with a friend over whether AI risk forecasting can be rational in the absence of historical base rates, I want to test Keynes's framework against this modern problem. She defends breaking down forecasts into calibrated sub-credences; I suspect this is the kind of spurious quantification Keynes warned about when he distinguished measurable risk from true uncertainty, particularly since the sample space for transformative AI failures—such as recursive self-improvement leading to misalignment—seems not merely unknown but ill-defined. If Keynes were alive today, how would he evaluate the practice of "credence decomposition" in forecasting tournaments? Would he see it as a legitimate extension of reasoning under ignorance, or as false precision? Apply his idea of the "weight of argument" to a concrete governance case: a lab must decide whether to release a frontier model despite having zero frequency data on catastrophic misalignment, and the board demands either a numerical probability or a rejection of quantification altogether. Would Keynes advise abandoning assigned probabilities in favor of qualitative weights of evidence, and would he treat attempts to "insure" against existential risk through financial markets as a category error—treating an uninsurable uncertainty as if it were a calculable risk? Finally, could he design a decision framework for such governance that avoids both false precision and complete paralysis, and what feature of that framework would most clearly distinguish it from modern Bayesian risk assessment?
