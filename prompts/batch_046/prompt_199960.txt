In a recent philosophy of religion seminar on moral desert, a discussant kept invoking the proverb that as you sow, so shall you reap to defend karma against the charge that it renders human punishment redundant. She treated it as more than a platitude about consequences: she seemed to mean that karma operates as a metaphysically necessary closure on moral accounts, guaranteeing that every intentional wrong is eventually matched by an appropriate suffering regardless of whether any institution administers justice. I want to know precisely what claims that line could be expressing, because different precisifications make her move either profound or incoherent.

Lay out the distinct theses it might encode, presented as a menu rather than a single interpretation. Among them, include at least: (a) a descriptive causal law linking specific intentions to specific future sufferings in the same life or a subsequent one; (b) a normative principle that the cosmos ought to be arranged so that wrongdoers suffer in proportion to their wrongs; (c) a metaphysical identity claim that the wrong and its karmic consequence are not two discrete events but one process viewed from different temporal angles; (d) a probabilistic or statistical tendency, compatible with occasional exceptions and with human punishment filling the gaps; and (e) a claim about the non-transferability of desert, according to which suffering administered by a court or victim can never substitute for the agent’s own karmic consequence.

For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then tell me which readings actually support her specific argumentative use—namely, that karma makes institutional retributive justice unnecessary or even metaphysically inappropriate—without collapsing into the claim that every harmful act literally produces a calculable, one-to-one future harm to the same agent. I am particularly interested in whether any defensible reading can avoid what looks like an overdetermination problem: if both karma and a court assign suffering for the same murder, does the wrongdoer suffer twice for one desert, or does one mode of satisfaction cancel the other? And if cancellation occurs, does that not undermine the claim that karma guarantees justice independently of human institutions? Please address which premises one must reject to escape that tension, and what rejecting them costs for the view that karma is a complete theory of moral desert.
