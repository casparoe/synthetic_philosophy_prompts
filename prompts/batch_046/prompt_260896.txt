The philosophical literature on aesthetic testimony—whether one can acquire aesthetic knowledge that a particular painting, musical passage, or landscape is beautiful chiefly through the report of another—has settled into a familiar cycle. Pessimists maintain that testimony cannot substitute for direct acquaintance because aesthetic judgment requires a first-personal responsive engagement with the object; optimists argue that testimony functions here as in other domains, transmitting warranted belief or even knowledge; and moderates hold that testimony can guide attention or supply background information but never deliver the judgment itself. Despite this apparent taxonomy, the debate has stalled: the same canonical scenarios recur—the museum docent, the trusted friend insisting on a symphony, the expert describing a proof's elegance—without convergence. I suspect the impasse masks a deeper, unacknowledged disagreement about the ontology of aesthetic properties: specifically, whether they are response-dependent in a strongly indexical way, so that the fact that something is beautiful is constitutively tied to a particular subject's experiential state and therefore cannot be the content of another's testimony in the same sense that a color fact might be. Yet I am uncertain whether this is the right diagnosis. It might instead be a verbal dispute over whether knowledge requires acquaintance, or it might be that the two sides are addressing different questions—one about epistemic transmission, the other about the justification of belief—under the single heading of testimony. Please diagnose the stall. Identify whether it is substantive, verbal, or a case of distinct questions being conflated. In doing so, examine what each side actually commits to regarding the docent case and regarding whether aesthetic judgment can be outsourced without loss of its first-personal character. Finally, specify what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction that dissolves the conflict, a formal result about indexical properties in value discourse, or an argument that no resolution is possible because the disagreement rests on bedrock intuitions about experiential authority.
