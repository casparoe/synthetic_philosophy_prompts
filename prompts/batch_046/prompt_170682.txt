I serve as the technical representative on our municipality's Smart Infrastructure Ethics Board, a role I was appointed to largely because I understand distributed systems and failure-mode analysis rather than normative philosophy; by background I am a backend engineer with no formal training in ethics or metaphysics. For our meeting next Thursday we must issue a binding recommendation on Pilot Program 7-B, an autonomous emergency-resource allocator that will be activated during the upcoming regional marathon. The proposal presents two architectures that illustrate exactly the kind of incommensurable values and hard choices my co-chair keeps warning about. Architecture Alpha optimizes continuously across a unified utility curve: it can shift resources away from a small, identifiable demographic cluster if the aggregate survival gain is large enough, treating all inputs as commensurable weights. Architecture Beta embeds a non-derogable constraint that prohibits the system from using protected demographic proxies — even indirectly or probabilistically — in any allocation weight, regardless of the measurable cost in lives saved. There is no common metric between aggregate survival and the non-instrumentalization of protected groups; our charter forbids us from inventing an exchange rate, yet we still have to choose.

The board recently adopted an experimental procedural mechanism — proposed during public comment and now referred to informally as a moral-remainder audit — which requires that whenever we approve an option that sacrifices one incommensurable value for another, the decision memo must formally record the unquantified loss and pair it with a concrete mitigation commitment and timeline. I need you to draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's session. It should recommend a specific course of action — approve Alpha, approve Beta, or approve one with explicit conditions — together with a clear time frame for implementation and review, such as a 60-day pilot with 30-day audit checkpoints. The reasoning must engage the strongest argument on the losing side with genuine intellectual charity: for example, if you recommend Beta, you must take seriously the charge that an absolute constraint is a form of moral negligence that privileges symbolic purity over preventable deaths; if you recommend Alpha, you must take seriously the charge that it collapses moral agents into fungible data points and erases a kind of dignity that cannot be compensated by statistics. Please do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation of specific philosophers; the board needs an actionable institutional document, not a literature review. Write it in a formal tone appropriate for a public record, describing any philosophical concepts in the memo's own language rather than importing technical jargon I do not fully command.
