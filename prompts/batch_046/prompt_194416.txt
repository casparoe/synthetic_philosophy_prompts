Since retiring from experimental neuroscience, I have taken up the philosophy of mind with some enthusiasm, and I have lately been pondering Douglas Hofstadter's account of tangled hierarchies and what he calls the inviolate level—the stratum one can never genuinely exit, as Escher's Drawing Hands so vividly suggest. I should like you to write a poem of exactly sixteen lines, arranged in four quatrains of unrhymed iambic pentameter. The formal constraint is this: within each quatrain, the opening line and the closing line must be identical save for a single word, and that word must shift in grammatical function—say, from direct object to subject, or from the thing observed to the observer. This mirroring ought to enact the very loop Hofstadter describes, in which any attempt to step outside the system merely relocates one inside it at a higher or lower register. The poem should take Escher's hands not as a decorative image but as an argument about the limits of self-observation, and it should resist resolving the tension: there is no final line that breaks the circle. Please write in a contemplative register, using British spelling and punctuation, and avoid any mention of Gödel's incompleteness theorems except as they genuinely illuminate the drawing's logic rather than serving as a borrowed authority.
