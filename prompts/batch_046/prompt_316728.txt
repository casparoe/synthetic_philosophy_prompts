As a retired ecologist who has only recently begun reading philosophy in any systematic way, I have become puzzled by a persistent asymmetry in the institutional practices that compare beef and chicken consumption. Standard life-cycle assessments and policy guidelines meticulously tally greenhouse-gas emissions, feed-conversion ratios, land-use change, and the number of farmed animals directly killed per calorie of nutrition. Yet they treat the wild animals—small mammals, ground-nesting birds, insects, and soil fauna—killed or displaced by mechanized plowing, planting, and harvesting as morally invisible, even though the aggregate suffering and death in crop cultivation may exceed that of pasture-raised beef. At the same time, direct, intentional harm to a domesticated animal is weighted heavily, while structurally identical harm caused indirectly through agricultural machinery is treated as a kind of background externality that falls outside the moral ledger. I want to understand what implicit philosophical commitments make this selective accounting coherent, rather than merely convenient.

Does the practice strictly presuppose a theory of value that grants moral standing only to beings within human care or institutional control, or can it be reconciled with a more expansive sentientist view by appealing to a distinction between direct and indirect causation? Might it instead rest on an epistemic assumption—that wild-animal welfare is too diffuse, too uncertain, or too intractable to be included in policy—rather than on a normative claim about moral status? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of value and moral responsibility that licenses this asymmetry between beef, chicken, and crop-based foods; distinguish what the practice genuinely requires from what merely happens to fit it; and say whether an agricultural accounting framework that genuinely incorporated wild-animal suffering would have to abandon the beef-versus-chicken comparison entirely, or whether it could restructure the comparison on different conceptual premises. I am not looking for a survey of the literature, but for the conceptual anatomy of the practice itself.
