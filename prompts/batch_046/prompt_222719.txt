Having retired from experimental physics, I have recently been working through problems in moral philosophy, and I keep returning to the inefficacy objection in collective-harm contexts—climate mitigation, meat consumption, and voting—where the standard claim is that an individual action makes no discernible difference to the aggregate outcome and therefore lacks moral urgency. The reasoning reminds me of the claim that a single gas molecule’s velocity is irrelevant to the temperature of a container: correct at one scale, yet potentially misleading about how macroscopic properties depend on microscopic distributions. To probe this, I want to contrast the linear-inefficacy view with what I will call a threshold-aggregative framework, on which collective harms are not continuous linear sums but depend on whether the aggregate crosses critical discontinuities, so that an individual contribution is assessed by its membership in the sustaining set rather than by its isolated causal efficacy.

Compare these two frameworks carefully. First, does the statistical-mechanics analogy actually reinforce the inefficacy argument, or does it obscure the distinction between additive properties and emergent, non-additive ones? Second, under conditions of causal overdetermination—where the harm would occur regardless of one person’s choice—does either framework preserve a genuinely non-instrumental reason for individual abstention, or does responsibility dissolve in both? Third, apply the comparison to the difference, if there is one, between voting in a large democratic election and emitting carbon: both appear individually inefficacious, yet our moral intuitions often treat them differently. Which framework, if either, explains that divergence without ad hoc adjustments? I am not looking for a bibliography; I want a disciplined conceptual analysis of where each view succeeds, where it forces an unnatural mapping onto the case, and whether the threshold-aggregative model genuinely rescues individual responsibility or merely relocates the problem to the definition of the critical set.
