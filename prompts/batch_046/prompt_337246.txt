I have spent the last two years in retirement from a career in molecular biology attempting to read philosophy of mind with the same rigor I once applied to experimental design, and I find myself stuck on the paradox of phenomenal judgment within epiphenomenalism. If phenomenal consciousness makes no causal difference, it is unclear how judgments about it could be reliably formed; yet we seem to make such judgments constantly. Rather than asking for a survey of the literature, I want to test consistency against a specific track record. There is a philosopher—whose work I will leave unnamed, since I care about the logic of his rulings rather than his authority—who has addressed this tension through four verdicts on thought experiments. First, in an ordinary case where C-fiber firing causes both a headache and the report "I have a headache," he holds the report true and genuinely about the headache, despite the headache playing no causal role in producing the report. Second, in the case of a phenomenal zombie behaviorally and neurally identical to a conscious human but lacking inner experience, he judges that the zombie's identical utterance is false—there is nothing for it to refer to—even though the causal chain is indistinguishable. Third, in a lesion case where phenomenal experience is eliminated but direct neural stimulation preserves accurate verbal reporting, he rules those reports false, because the relevant phenomenal property is absent. Fourth, in a case where phenomenal experience remains present and normal, but the judgment-forming mechanism is replaced by a silicon module that receives only non-phenomenal neural data yet computes the correct output, he holds the resulting report false, on the grounds that there is no appropriate connection between the judgment and the experience.

Now consider a new case I have constructed: a split-brain patient retains fully normal phenomenal consciousness in the right hemisphere, while the left hemisphere—where linguistic judgment is formed—contains an artificial module. This module receives input from the conscious hemisphere not through ordinary causal energy transfer, but through precisely synchronized oscillation patterns that carry information without constituting physical causation in the standard sense. The module produces the judgment "I am experiencing pain," and there is indeed corresponding pain in the right hemisphere. I want to know what verdict best coheres with the philosopher's four earlier rulings, which of his precedents serves as the closest analog, and whether those precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer. If they do, I ask you to specify the principle he would be adopting with each possible choice—perhaps a principle of causal contiguity versus one of informational correlation without causal mediation—and to propose one further case, maybe involving cross-hemispheric judgment in a split-brain subject with only one conscious hemisphere, that would best distinguish these competing principles from one another. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want only the internal logic of the track record applied to this constructed scenario.
