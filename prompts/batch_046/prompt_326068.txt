You are preparing a single fifty-minute session for a philosophy reading group of undergraduates who have just finished a course on philosophy of mind but have never studied the self-deception literature. Your aim is not to survey theories but to teach the central conceptual puzzle: whether self-deception is even coherent, given that belief aims at truth and deception standardly requires knowing what one hides. Plan the teaching sequence: first, the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and the dependency relations between them; second, the single concrete scenario that should carry the discussion — specify what it must include and what familiar case it should avoid — and why its particular structure illuminates the puzzle; third, the misconceptions the group will likely bring or form, such as the idea that self-deception requires a literally partitioned mind, that it is merely lying to oneself, or that belief's truth-aim makes the phenomenon conceptually impossible, together with the precise point in the sequence where each should be anticipated and defused; fourth, one question you can pose at the end that reveals whether they have understood the conceptual tension rather than merely picked a side; and fifth, what you should explicitly set aside at this level and say so — for example, clinical or evolutionary accounts. Write in British English and maintain a formal register.
