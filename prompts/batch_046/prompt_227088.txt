I am examining karma not in its popularized form but as a precise philosophical theory of moral desert—specifically the Theravada Abhidhammic analysis in which kammic efficacy is indexed to mental-continuity streams (citta-santati) rather than to persisting persons, in which merit and demerit are strictly non-transferable across such streams, and in which the fruition (vipaka) is an impersonal causal regularity rather than an intentional reward or punishment. I also wish to keep in view the Mahayana qualification that this entire accounting apparatus is conventionally operative yet ultimately empty of intrinsic nature (svabhāva-śūnya).

Restate this specific constellation—a diachronic, non-personal, non-transferable system of desert that functions without any substrate of personal identity—using only the vocabulary and distinctions of modern normative ethics and social-choice theory: ex ante versus ex post desert, intergenerational equity, cooperative risk-pooling and moral hazard, the non-identity problem, and the contractarian separation of attributive from substantive responsibility. For each element, indicate whether it translates cleanly, requires distortion (for instance, treating mental-continuity streams as functional analogues of agents or treating vipaka as a distributive outcome), or lacks any counterpart whatsoever—particularly the idea that desert can accumulate within a diachronic process that is not a self-identical subject. Then provide the residue report: does the untranslatable remainder reveal a lacuna in contractarian thinking about moral accounting without agents, or does it suggest that the Abhidhammic framework, once stripped of implicit personalist assumptions, cannot coherently sustain the notion of diachronic bearing? I ask for an honest translation that does not smuggle personal identity or intentional agency back into the source framework; the value of the exercise lies precisely in what fails to carry over.
