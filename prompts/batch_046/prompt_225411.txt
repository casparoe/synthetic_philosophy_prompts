In philosophical discussions of the color phi phenomenon—the visual illusion in which a dot that moves and changes color is subjectively experienced as changing hue at a point in its trajectory before it physically reaches that location—thinkers often distinguish two metaphors for how perceptual experience might be revised. The first, sometimes called an Orwellian revision, treats conscious registration as initially accurate but subsequently rewritten in memory, so that the past is altered to align with a present interpretation and the subject never notices the discrepancy. The second, sometimes called a Stalinesque revision, treats the editing as occurring prior to consciousness itself: the sensory record is censored or reconstructed on its way to awareness, meaning that no unedited original experience ever reaches the subject at all, much as a photograph might be airbrushed before it reaches the archive.

I am trying to determine whether this distinction genuinely illuminates the timing of conscious experience, or whether it dissolves once we examine how predictive and postdictive mechanisms interact in perceptual binding. Could you compare the Orwellian and Stalinesque models specifically as accounts of what happens in the color phi illusion? Please articulate what each implies about the temporal location of the revision—whether it occurs after an initial phenomenal registration or before any registration reaches awareness—about whether either model allows for a raw phenomenal residue that survives editing, and about what each predicts for the reliability and authority of first-person reports about the order and content of one's own perceptions. Additionally, evaluate whether either model can accommodate frameworks in which perception is not a passive recording that gets edited, but an active construction driven by anticipatory signals that are then validated or overridden by incoming sensory data. If predictive processing undermines the assumption that there is a finished percept to be revised, does one of the two metaphors adapt more gracefully than the other, or should we abandon both in favor of a different analogy for how the brain handles temporal inconsistencies in consciousness?
