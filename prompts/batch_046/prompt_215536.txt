My study partner cooked up an argument that seems to show doctors can't reason rationaly. (1) Lewis proved that if P(if A then B) equals P(B|A) then indicative conditionals collapse into material ones. (2) Doctors constantly update on diagnostics using exactly those conditional probabilities—e.g. P(spots|measles) is near 1. (3) So if Lewis is right, doctors are implicitly treating "if measles then spots" as just "no measles or spots". (4) But then a doctor who rules out measles should regard that conditional as true and even informative, which is absurd—clinicans treat it as moot, not confirmed. (5) So either Lewis's result doesn't apply to practical reasoning, or every working doctor is systematicaly incoherent. (6) But the result is a formal theorem; it should apply anywhere probabilities live. Where's the slip? I'd like to keep (2)—that doctors really do use Bayes with conditionals—so I'm hoping the error is in (3) or (4), but I can't name it precisely and I don't want to just deny Lewis outright without seeing the cost.
