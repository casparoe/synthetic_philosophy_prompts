I write as a journalist covering science, institutional design, and the philosophy embedded in them. Over the past year I have been observing how major research funding agencies structure their evaluation pipelines, and I keep encountering the same administrative feature: a rigid bifurcation between what is called the fundamental track and the applied track. The rules are explicit. When a proposal enters the applied stream, reviewers are instructed to judge it exclusively on practical efficacy, feasibility, and direct societal benefit; they are formally barred from assessing whether it advances foundational theory, even when the implementation depends on altering that very theory. In the fundamental stream, the inverse holds—reviewers must not weigh practical outcomes. Researchers who try to submit integrated work, such as a climate-modeling framework whose theoretical architecture is inseparable from its computational deployment, are told to split it into two discrete applications, as if the two levels could not genuinely interact within a single evaluation.

This practice seems to carry a heavy philosophical load. It treats explanatory levels as administratively sealed, which suggests an implicit commitment to something like an inviolate hierarchy: a bottom layer of basic theory and a top layer of application, with no legitimate tangled loops between them. But is that commitment actually necessary, or is the bifurcation merely a bureaucratic convenience that happens to fit one ontology among many? I am trying to understand whether the funding architecture presupposes a specific view of scientific explanation—perhaps one that denies that theory and practice can form a strange loop in which each level simultaneously draws and is drawn by the other, like the hands in Escher's famous image. Does the practice remain coherent if we replace that hierarchical view with a genuinely entangled one, and what concrete changes in review criteria, reviewer training, and institutional incentives would be required to make a single-track system viable? I want an analysis that distinguishes what the current architecture strictly requires philosophically from what it merely accommodates, that tests whether it is compatible with rival views in which levels are not sealed, and that treats the strongest defense of the separation—namely, that cross-track evaluation corrupts the integrity of both kinds of judgment—with genuine seriousness before saying what should replace it.
