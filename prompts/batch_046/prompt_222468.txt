Two philosophers, Riley and Sam, are debating the conceivability argument against physicalism over coffee. (1) Riley says that if we can clearly and coherently conceive a being physically and functionally identical to a conscious human but lacking all phenomenal experience — a philosophical zombie — then physical facts do not metaphysically necessitate phenomenal facts; since there is no explicit contradiction in the scenario, conceivability gives us good reason to think it is metaphysically possible, and physicalism is false. (2) Sam replies that this relies on treating phenomenal concepts as “transparent,” revealing the full intrinsic nature of consciousness; but the phenomenal concept strategy shows they are recognitional or quotational, picking out properties via direct acquaintance rather than structural description. So conceiving a zombie is like conceiving water without H2O — we imagine the absence of the recognitional trigger or acquaintance relation, not the absence of the property itself. (3) Riley counters that even granting recognitional concepts, a zombie is physically identical, which means every recognitional mechanism is present and functioning exactly as in a conscious being; yet if no phenomenal property is instantiated, then the property is not constituted by those mechanisms. Riley adds that phenomenal concepts contain an essential indexical component — an implicit “this experience” — and in the zombie that token-indexical is missing despite complete physical duplication, which directly demonstrates that phenomenal properties are not fixed by physical facts. (4) Sam responds that Riley has smuggled in a “revelation” thesis — that phenomenal concepts reveal the essence of their referents — which the phenomenal concept strategy precisely denies. If the concepts are opaque, then the missing indexical “this” in the zombie is not evidence of a missing property in the world, but only of the absence of a first-person mode of presentation that is part of the concept, not the property. The physicalist can therefore maintain that the property is present in the zombie, presented in a third-person mode, or that the indexical is a feature of cognition, not ontology. Thus Riley’s inference from conceptual absence to metaphysical absence collapses.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing — for instance, whether there is a stable position that accepts the opacity of phenomenal concepts without conceding the zombie’s possibility, or whether Riley’s indexical move genuinely strengthens the argument rather than repeating it. Keep the adjudication neutral; you are reporting the dispute as a bystander and should not signal which side you favor.
