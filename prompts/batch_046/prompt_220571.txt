Consider an institutional protocol observed in some psychiatric treatment centers: clinicians are trained to monitor whether a patient has begun to organize their self-narrative around their diagnostic category—saying, for instance, "I am a schizophrenic" rather than describing specific experiences—and if so, to suspend the categorical label in all clinical notes and session language, replacing it with first-person phenomenological descriptions. At the same time, the center continues to require that same categorical diagnosis for admission, insurance authorization, and risk-assessment forms. What theory of psychiatric classification and personal identity does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does it commit the institution to the looping-effect thesis—that classifications can alter the people they classify—and if so, does it treat that loop as an epistemic distortion, an ontological change, or a moral harm? Can the practice be made consistent with the continued use of categories for administrative and predictive purposes, or does it implicitly presuppose that such uses are philosophically separable from therapeutic interaction? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical framework, distinguish what the protocol strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and take a definite position on whether the practice is coherent or whether its two halves rest on incompatible conceptions of what a psychiatric diagnosis is. Give your reasons directly rather than laying out alternatives for the reader to choose among.
