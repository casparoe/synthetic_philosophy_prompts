In Germany, the extraordinarily dense apparatus of Vergangenheitsbewältigung—state-funded memorials, mandatory Holocaust education, criminalized denial, and ritualized public commemoration—seems to generate a kind of moral surplus or credit line. And in the American South, the prolonged, intensely emotional public debate over Confederate monuments—whether to defend, remove, or recontextualize them—absorbs enormous legislative and cultural energy, while there remains a striking absence of comparable state-sponsored monuments to Reconstruction-era racial terror or to the labor of the enslaved, as though the very intensity of the debate over one form of remembrance substitutes for accountability to a different historical record.
