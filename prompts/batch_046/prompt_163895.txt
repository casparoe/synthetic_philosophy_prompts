The debate between covering-law and narrative accounts of historical explanation has been stalled for decades. Defenders of the covering-law approach maintain that explaining an event requires subsuming it under general laws, even if those laws are statistical or sketch-like, so that the outcome appears expected given antecedent conditions. Narrative theorists reply that historical explanation works by emplotment—selecting a beginning, an ending, and a trajectory that makes an outcome intelligible—and that this selection is not derivable from any law. Both sides now rehearse these moves without movement. I want to introduce a distinction neither camp typically employs: explanatory portability, the idea that a good historical explanation should be transferable across contexts with similar causal structures, versus explanatory entanglement, the claim that historical causes and effects are so interwoven that isolating portable law-like antecedents misrepresents the causal architecture. Given this framing, diagnose the stall: is it a substantive disagreement about whether history permits portable explanations, a verbal dispute over what counts as explanation, or two different inquiries—one into causal structure, one into representational form—pursued under a single heading? Be specific about what each side actually says. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal model of entanglement, a historiographical demonstration that portability fails in practice, or an argument that the portability/entanglement distinction itself collapses?
