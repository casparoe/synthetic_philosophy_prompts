Consider the track record of our Rescue Obligations Review Board, which issues binding judgments on whether a specified agent bears a moral duty to undertake a particular rescue. Our precedents are these.

First, in the Pond Case, an adult walking past a shallow pond sees an unfamiliar child drowning; wading in saves the child but ruins the rescuer’s shoes and exposes her to mild hypothermia. We ruled that rescue is an unqualified obligation; refusal is seriously blameworthy.

Second, in the Distant Famine Case, an individual with disposable income learns that a reputable charity can save one distant stranger’s life for roughly fifty dollars. We ruled that donating is morally required, not merely laudable, for anyone above subsistence; refusal constitutes a serious moral wrong comparable in kind, if not in intensity, to walking past the pond.

Third, in the Kidney Case, a healthy adult is asked to donate one kidney to a stranger, saving that life at the cost of surgery, lifelong reduced renal reserve, and a small but real mortality risk. We ruled that refusal is not blameworthy; donation is heroic and supererogatory, not obligatory.

Fourth, in the Surplus Inventory Case, a corporation holds perishable medical supplies that will be destroyed for tax purposes; donating them to an active disaster zone would save hundreds of lives at negligible net cost. We ruled that the firm is strictly obligated to donate, and its executives are personally culpable for willful destruction.

Fifth, in the Triage Protocol Case, a physician in a mass-casualty event with limited ventilators must allocate them by medical prognosis alone; saving a wealthy benefactor out of gratitude, despite a worse prognosis, violates her duty. We ruled that her obligation is strictly procedural and institutional—personal emotional rescue impulses must be suppressed in favor of the protocol that maximizes lives saved.

We now face a new proposal. A software engineer employed by a private analytics firm discovers a critical, remotely exploitable vulnerability in an open-source water-purification control system deployed in refugee camps. Applying a patch from her laptop would take about thirty minutes and would prevent a contamination event that epidemiological models indicate would likely kill dozens within days. However, the system is maintained by a separate NGO; her employer’s contract does not authorize her access, and an unauthorized remote intervention would expose her to termination, potential civil liability, and criminal computer-access charges in the jurisdiction hosting the server. No institutional reporting or maintenance channel can respond in time. The engineer asks whether she is morally required to apply the patch herself.
