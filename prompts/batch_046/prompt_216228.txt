I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about the nonidentity problem, going by my gut on cases. Classic one: a 14 year old girl has a kid now, the kid has a rough life but is still glad to exist. I feel she hasn't wronged him, since he wouldnt be here otherwise. Then there's this policy case: government builds a coal plant, pollution shifts who meets who over generations, so fifty years later different people exist with slightly worse lives. They wouldnt exist without the plant. I still feel like the government did wrong, even though no particular person was made worse off. Then a weird one: parents use gene editing to make sure thier child is deaf, cause they're Deaf culture and want to share that identity. The kid grows up happy, wouldnt exist with different genes. That feels okay, maybe even good. But then parents just choose to conceive in January instead of July, knowing January conception correlates with a slight bump in some condition. The kid is born with it, lives a decent life, is glad to exist. That feels wrong, but I cant see why its different from the deafness case. One more: a climate bill gives big subsidies that delay childbearing by about a decade, so the people born in 2055 are different from who'd be born otherwise, but they avoid climate harm. I think that's totally fine, maybe required. So is there a principle behind all this? What's the deal with the January conception vs the deaf editing -- both determine identity and yield lives worth living, but one feels like a mistake and the other like a legitimate choice? Give me a principle or a couple candidates that captures the pattern, say which case motivates each clause, be honest about which of my verdicts it has to drop, and tell me what it says about the subway-schedule case I keep thinking about -- where a city changes train times, which changes commute patterns, which changes who has kids, leading to different people with slightly lower wellbeing.
