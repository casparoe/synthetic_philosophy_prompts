I am grading a philosophy exam on moral psychology and normative ethics. The exam asks students to analyze the structure of intuition pumps in ethical reasoning, using the trolley switch case and the bridge-variant (pushing a large person onto the tracks) as central examples.

Please evaluate the following student response to Question 3. Grade it on conceptual accuracy, on the ability to distinguish between explanatory frameworks, and on critical engagement with the methodological status of stylized scenarios.

Question 3 (30 points): In Judith Jarvis Thomson's well-known discussion, the switch case and the bridge case generate divergent intuitive judgments despite identical outcome structures (one dies, five are saved). First, explain why the bridge case might be taken to involve a misleading form of direct bodily agency that activates psychological aversions not necessarily tracking moral relevance. Second, assess whether the distinction between personal and impersonal harm—often invoked to rationalize the divergence—genuinely explains the asymmetry or merely redescribes it in normative vocabulary. Third, identify and defend one reason why ethical thought experiments that rely on highly stylized, low-context scenarios might systematically distort moral judgment.

Student Answer:

In the bridge case, the agent physically pushes a large person onto the tracks, using that person's body to stop the trolley. This direct physical contact activates our aversion to interpersonal violence, which operates as a deep evolutionary heuristic. By contrast, flipping the switch is mediated by machinery, so it feels like operating a system rather than assaulting an individual.

The personal/impersonal distinction, which Thomson introduced specifically to account for this asymmetry, explains the divergence properly rather than merely redescribing it. Personal harm requires direct bodily involvement, and this activates a stronger deontological constraint against using someone as a means. Impersonal harm, being mechanistically mediated, falls under a different, weaker constraint. Therefore, the intuition that pushing is wrong tracks a real normative boundary: we may not use one person's body as an instrument to save others, even when the numbers favor it.

Finally, stylized thought experiments mislead because they lack ecological validity. Real moral decisions occur in rich social contexts with long-term consequences, emotional entanglement, and institutional norms. By stripping these away, trolley-style cases force judgments on the basis of isolated causal structures. This means the intuitions they elicit are calibrated to artificial conditions, so they cannot be trusted to reveal our actual moral commitments.
