In my graduate seminar on collective moral agency, I have been tracking the long historiographical impasse that followed Fritz Fischer's argument that Imperial Germany pursued explicit war aims—above all through the September Program—sufficient to establish unique national responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War. Decades of archival rebuttal and structuralist counter-critique have not resolved the dispute; rather, the debate seems to have bifurcated into two camps that no longer clearly address the same question. One side demands documentary proof of a discrete cabinet decision for preventive war, treating the absence of such a "smoking gun" as exculpatory; the other treats systemic militarism, alliance rigidity, and bureaucratic escalation dynamics as adequate for attributing culpability to the collective, regardless of whether any individual minister intended a general European conflict. I am inclined to think that the stall is not primarily evidentiary but conceptual—specifically, that the disagreement masks a fracture over whether a modern bureaucratic state possesses the kind of intentional structure required to be a proper subject of blame at all, and whether any intentionality it exhibits is original or merely derived from its constituent agents. Is my hypothesis correct? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement—perhaps about the actual distribution of decision-making authority within the Kaiserreich—doing the real work?

More precisely, I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication. Do the Fischer defenders and their critics differ about facts, about what counts as evidence for collective intention, or about the conditions under which an abstract entity like a nation-state can bear derived rather than original intentionality? Are they answering different questions—one about causal overdetermination in multi-actor historical systems, the other about the moral standing required to hold a collective responsible—while using the same vocabulary of "blame"? And given whatever diagnosis you offer, what would actually move the debate? Would progress require a formal account of collective agency that specifies when bureaucratic states inherit intentional properties from their members, a historiographical convention that fixes evidentiary thresholds for collective intent, or is the disagreement ultimately bedrock, anchored in incompatible intuitions about whether attributing moral responsibility to nations is conceptually coherent rather than merely politically dangerous? I want the diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a conceptual distinction, an archival discovery, a model of distributed agency—could genuinely end it, or whether the question itself is ill-posed.
