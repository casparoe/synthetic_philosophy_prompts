Question: Donald Davidson argues that self-deception requires a division of the mind into semi-independent structures. Explain his view and assess whether it succeeds in showing that self-deception is genuinely possible without reducing to mere wishful thinking.

Student answer:
Davidson solves the paradox of self-deception—how one can knowingly cause oneself to hold a false belief—by dividing the mind into semi-independent sub-systems, much like Freud’s structural model of id, ego and superego. One sub-system holds the true belief and suppresses the evidence, while another adopts the false belief. This makes self-deception a real internal deception rather than simple wishful thinking. Davidson’s view aligns with Sartre’s analysis of bad faith, where consciousness splits into a deceiver and a deceived. Because the parts are genuinely separate, there is no direct contradiction. Nevertheless, Davidson succeeds only if beliefs can be fully quarantined with zero cognitive access between parts; since such total encapsulation is psychologically implausible, his account ultimately collapses self-deception back into motivated irrationality rather than genuine deception.
