I am writing as a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun to study philosophy, and I find myself troubled by a question that sits at the intersection of material ontology and aesthetic value. Imagine an oil painting severely damaged by fire, then reconstructed by conservators from fragments mixed with new pigments and canvas so that its present material form is only partly causally continuous with the artist's original work. Now suppose that a second object is produced: an atom-for-atom replica of this reconstructed painting, made with chemically identical materials and techniques, but with no causal link whatsoever to the artist or to the first object. To every possible observer, including experts using spectroscopic analysis, the two are completely indistinguishable. I would like you to write an essay, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, as if composing a formal letter, weighing the controversy over whether the reconstructed original possesses a value that the replica necessarily lacks. You should examine whether that value, if it exists, derives from genetic continuity with the artist, from institutional and historical embeddedness, or from something else entirely, and you should reach a definite conclusion that you justify with reasoning. In particular, address whether the already-broken genetic chain of the reconstructed original undermines the usual argument for privileging originals over copies, and consider whether the fact that all material bodies constantly exchange atoms renders the notion of phenomenal identity philosophically unstable. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the conceptual argument itself.
