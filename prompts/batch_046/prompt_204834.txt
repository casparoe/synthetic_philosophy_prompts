I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and in my reporting on urban policy and public memory I face recurring proposals to rename streets or relocate monuments. I need a reusable decision procedure—something I can run in about fifteen minutes per case, with no philosophy training and no archive access, that I must sign off on alone—to evaluate whether such a proposal is ethically well-founded. Ground it in the philosophy of commemoration: distinguish honorific from archival functions; determine whether retaining a symbol implies endorsement of the figure's actions; and apply one specific, non-obvious conceptual distinction that news coverage usually misses—such as the asymmetry between the harm of sustained public visibility (retention) and the harm of archival displacement (removal), or a test for whether a symbol demands hidden interpretive labor from observers to avoid misreading. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist of tests, with every unavoidable judgment call explicitly flagged as such and not disguised as mechanical. Apply it to three hypothetical cases: (1) a street named after a colonial administrator whose policies caused famine, retained only because local merchants say it aids navigation; (2) a university building honoring a scientist who made a major discovery but also advocated eugenics, with the university proposing contextual signage rather than removal; and (3) a monument to a local labor leader erected not by democratic decision but by a single authoritarian mayor, now proposed for removal by a democratic council. Include one case where the procedure yields an answer I will probably dislike. State clearly what the procedure cannot settle, specify that it must work without bibliographic verification or citation, and keep the steps usable by someone who will be filing on deadline.
