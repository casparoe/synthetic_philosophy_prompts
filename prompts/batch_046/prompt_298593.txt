You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral psychology, social ontology, and normative institutional design. Before producing the final document, think carefully and begin with a brief analytical outline in which you map: (a) the structural distinction between guilt as an action-targeting, reparative, individuated response and shame as a self-targeting, relational, communal response; (b) the concept of affective architecture—the institutional, ritual, and narrative mechanisms through which a culture shapes the prevalence and authority of one moral emotion over another; and (c) the logical conditions under which a hybrid affective regime might be stable, specifically one in which guilt dominates public and legal domains while shame is confined to private, familial, and religious domains.

Your deliverable is a formal joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps: Camp Guilt, which holds that guilt is the superior moral emotion and that a culture can and should institutionalize it; and Camp Shame, which holds that shame is superior and that cultural selection in its favor is both possible and desirable. Draft the statement in American English. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations to specific works or authors. You may invoke general theoretical distinctions, but describe them generically rather than attributing them to named figures.

Structure the statement exactly as follows:

1. Agreed Claims. Number these sequentially. Both camps must sign without reservation. Include agreement on the formal emotional structures of guilt and shame; on the observable fact that different societies have historically embedded one or the other more deeply in law, education, and ritual; on the claim that institutions operate as affective architecture; and on the definition of the hybrid affective regime introduced above.

2. Residual Disagreements. Present these in clearly demarcated subsections. For each disagreement, provide (i) the formulation that Camp Guilt accepts as an accurate statement of its own view, and (ii) the formulation that Camp Shame accepts as an accurate statement of its own view. The disagreements must cover:
   - The moral repair asymmetry: whether guilt’s orientation toward discrete acts creates a more coherent and individually empowering pathway to restoration than shame’s global self-evaluation, or whether shame’s embeddedness in communal judgment produces more durable moral transformation.
   - The cultural selection thesis: whether a culture can deliberately redesign its affective architecture to elevate one emotion without undermining moral coherence, and whether such redesign constitutes authentic collective choice or coercive emotional engineering.
   - The hybrid affective regime: whether the proposed division—public/legal guilt paired with private/domestic shame—is structurally stable, or whether the two emotions are so entangled that suppressing one in any domain erodes the functional integrity of the other everywhere.

3. Cruxes. Identify precise sub-questions such that agreed-upon resolution would move at least one camp. Make these specific rather than generic—for example, whether institutional transitions from shame-based to guilt-based legal frameworks predictably alter rates of voluntary reparation versus social ostracism, holding economic development constant.

4. Agreed Tests and Predictions. For each crux, state what empirical patterns, conceptual arguments, or institutional experiments both sides accept as relevant, recording each camp’s predicted outcome in advance. Be concrete about institutional mechanisms rather than appealing to broad cultural stereotypes.

5. Verbal Disagreement Audit. Conclude with an honest assessment of whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal—whether “moral emotion” is used with different extensional criteria, whether “culture can choose” ambiguously covers collective deliberation versus top-down design, or whether “better” conflates normative adequacy with psychological sustainability.

Constraints: Keep all reasoning in the weeds—avoid broad generalizations about “Western individualism” or “Eastern collectivism.” Focus on the mechanics of emotional institutionalization and the logical relations between repair pathways and emotional targets. Ensure the scenario remains realistic: no exotic technology, no perfect predictors, no demons, no science-fictional devices. The niche framing of affective architecture and the hybrid regime is built into the prompt; work within it rather than reverting to a generic essay format. End with the joint statement itself, clearly separated from your preliminary brainstorming outline.
