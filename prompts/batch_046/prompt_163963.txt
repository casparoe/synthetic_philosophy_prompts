As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past several years studying philosophy of social science rather than returning to the laboratory, I have become absorbed in a problem that feels structurally analogous to debates about emergent properties in condensed matter: can a normative or cultural configuration operate as an autonomous causal variable in macrohistorical explanation, or does it always reduce to underlying material and institutional conditions? I ask you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on this question, using Max Weber’s thesis on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism as the central test case.

Name the first camp the Interventionist Weberians. They hold that Weber’s argument, properly reconstructed, shows that the theological innovations of a worldly calling and predestinarian anxiety functioned as an intervention-variable: holding constant regional markets, legal frameworks, and class structures, populations bearing this normative framework exhibited measurably different patterns of rational capital accounting, disciplined reinvestment, and labor organization, and that altering the theological content in a counterfactual scenario would have altered the economic trajectory. Name the second camp the Materialist Reductionists. They hold that the theological framework was an equilibrium rationalization of emergent mercantile interests and institutional incentives; they maintain that comparable material conditions in other regions produced functionally identical economic behaviors regardless of doctrinal content, so that culture supervenes on material conditions without contributing independent causal force.

Your deliverable should be a formal document that both parties could sign. Structure it in two parts. First, provide a numbered list of agreed claims about the formal structure of Weber’s thesis, about what requires explanation (the emergence of continuous rational bookkeeping and capital reinvestment rather than consumption-oriented accumulation), about why correlation alone is insufficient for causal attribution in macrohistory, and about the present limits of historical data for resolving such questions. Second, provide a disagreements table containing the precise formulation of each camp’s position, written in wording that each side would accept as accurate to its own view; include the ontological disagreement over whether supervenience of values on material conditions is compatible with causal efficacy, and the methodological disagreement over whether counterfactual interventions on cultural variables are coherent at the macro-social scale.

Then identify three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, provide a concrete historical comparison or argument that both sides agree is relevant, together with each side’s explicit prediction about what that evidence demonstrates. I suggest the following cruxes, though you may refine them if the formulation improves neutrality.

Crux One concerns counterfactual stability. Would rational capitalist accounting and reinvestment norms have emerged in Northwestern Europe in the absence of Calvinist and related ascetic doctrines, assuming identical market structures, property rights, and state capacities? Use as the agreed bearing case the comparison between Protestant commercial centers and Catholic merchant communities in Northern Italy during the same broad period, where capital markets and urban institutions were comparably developed but theological frameworks differed. The Interventionist Weberians should predict that the absence of the calling-doctrine and predestinarian anxiety produces measurably lower rates of systematic reinvestment and rational bookkeeping; the Materialist Reductionists should predict convergence in economic organizational form once institutional incentives are held constant, with theological differences explaining only rhetorical framing.

Crux Two concerns the status of elective affinity. Is the observed correlation between ascetic theology and capitalist practice best explained by a direct causal mechanism—normative commitments restructuring economic preferences—or by a selection mechanism in which pre-existing mercantile classes adopted and reshaped religious doctrine to legitimate practices they already pursued? Use as the agreed bearing case the historical trajectory of Methodist and Quaker business communities in eighteenth-century England, where distinct theological contents interacted with rapidly industrializing conditions. Each side should state whether they predict that these distinct theological contents produced measurably different organizational forms, or whether both converged on identical practices dictated by industrialization pressures.

Crux Three concerns the coherence of cultural intervention. Assuming that values supervene on institutional and economic conditions, is it conceptually possible to intervene on a cultural variable without simultaneously intervening on its material base, and does the possibility of such an intervention determine whether culture is causally efficacious? Here both sides should address the logical structure of interventionist causation as applied to macro-social variables, not to individual psychology. The Interventionist Weberians should predict that coherent counterfactuals can be constructed in which theological content varies while institutional structures remain fixed; the Materialist Reductionists should predict that any purported intervention on culture collapses analytically into an intervention on the underlying material conditions, rendering the cultural variable causally redundant.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to highly specialized contemporary theories unknown outside a narrow subfield; keep the conceptual apparatus within reach of someone trained in natural science and general philosophy. The document should read as a serious philosophical text, not a dialogue or an adjudicated verdict. Conclude with a brief section in which each camp states, in wording it accepts, what specific evidence or argument—if discovered—would cause it to revise its core position.
