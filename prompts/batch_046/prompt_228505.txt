As a student in an advanced seminar on aesthetics and the philosophy of emotion, I have been working through the standard formulations of the paradox of tragedy—why audiences actively seek out artworks that reliably elicit sorrow, fear, or despair, and why these experiences are valued rather than avoided. The familiar explanatory landscape includes Aristotelian catharsis, Humean accounts of the conversion of passion into aesthetic pleasure, and more recent simulation- or control-theoretic frameworks that emphasize our awareness of the fictional frame. I do not want a survey of these positions; I want a defended research agenda.

To move beyond repetition, I want to introduce a conceptual distinction that I think is under-theorized. Call it the difference between representational suffering—the negative affect directed at the specific misfortunes of characters or represented events—and presentational suffering—the diffuse negative affect produced by formal properties of the work itself (e.g., dissonant harmonic structures, fragmented narrative temporalities, or visual desolation) that need not correspond to any particular represented loss. The paradox of tragedy is usually discussed as if all the negative affect is representational, but much of what moves us in, say, a slow movement or a bleak landscape seems presentational. I want to know whether resolving the paradox requires explaining both modalities, whether they share a common mechanism, or whether presentational suffering actually dissolves the paradox because there is no represented harm to be enjoyed in the first place.

Assume I am advising a small interdisciplinary group—say, two philosophers and one cognitive scientist—with about eighteen months and no access to large-scale empirical labs. Given that constraint, which of the following open questions, implications, or lines of work should they prioritize, and in what order?

First, the upstream conceptual dependency: is the core paradox really about the compatibility of negative affect with positive valuation, or is it about the conditions under which emotional simulation yields cognitive or social benefits? If the latter, does resolving that functional question make the hedonic puzzle disappear?

Second, the framing problem: to what extent does the enjoyment of representational suffering depend on a stable safety frame that marks the suffering as fictional? How should we think about artworks—particularly immersive or virtual-reality narratives—where the frame is deliberately unstable, and does presentational suffering operate independently of that frame?

Third, the clinical and practical implication: if we develop a precise account of why people seek out and benefit from controlled negative affect in art, what follows for the therapeutic use of narrative or music in grief and trauma processing? Is this line of work conceptually downstream of the framing problem, or can it proceed in parallel?

Fourth, the formal-aesthetic line: can presentational suffering be analyzed purely in terms of perceptual and structural features, or does it always covertly recruit representational content? If it can be isolated, does that suggest the paradox of tragedy was never a single problem but two?

Please do not treat this as a request for a reading list or bibliography. Instead, deliver a prioritization with explicit criteria: which questions are upstream of others, which are tractable given the resource constraint (philosophical analysis plus limited empirical collaboration), which are most neglected, and what kind of argument or preliminary finding over the next year would cause you to reorder the agenda. I am particularly interested in whether the representational/presentational distinction I have injected is a productive research axis or a red herring that should be deprioritized.
