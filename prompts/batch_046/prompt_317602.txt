Write the opening remarks for a three-minute address at an interdisciplinary workshop on mechanistic anomaly detection. The audience is split evenly between engineers who build interpretability tools for neural networks and philosophers of science who study mechanism identity and explanation. The remarks must center on a specific tension: when a detection system identifies an anomalous circuit in one model architecture, and a later analysis reveals a functionally equivalent circuit in a different architecture, have we found the same mechanism recurring, or two distinct mechanisms that merely share an effect? Make this question do real structural work—explain why the answer determines whether anomaly detection tracks stable, cross-system properties or only catalogs local deviations—rather than treating it as decorative philosophy. Do not resolve the debate; present it as the workshop's living problem. Address both halves of the room directly: the engineers should understand why their validation criteria depend on an implicit answer, and the philosophers should see how the engineering practice constrains abstract notions like intervention stability and mechanistic continuity. Do not include citations, named theories, bibliographies, or references that require outside verification; keep all references general.
