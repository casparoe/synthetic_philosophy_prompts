As a doctoral researcher in the philosophy of social science examining the ontology of institutional kinds, I would like you to construct a rigorous argument for the claim that money, race, and gender should not be treated as instances of a single, uniform category of 'social construction', but rather as structurally distinct kinds separated by what I term a revocability asymmetry. Specifically, argue that monetary kinds are thin institutional facts—sustained exclusively by collective intentionality and deferred enforcement mechanisms of the kind Searle describes—and are therefore fully revocable by collective decision without ontological residue. By contrast, contend that racial and gender kinds are thick constructions mapped onto embodied traits, embedded in intergenerational transmission, and reinforced by projected biological essentialism, which renders them sticky or quasi-natural even after explicit social deconstruction. From this, derive the implication that analogies between monetary inequality and racial or gender-based stratification rest on a category mistake: money lacks the body-anchored persistence that generates the distinctive metaphysical and moral features of race and gender. Please distinguish clearly between settled matters (for instance, that money operates through collective acceptance, or that race lacks biological essences in the essentialist sense) and genuinely contested issues among specialists (such as whether gender involves irreducible biological scaffolding, whether Searlean institutional facts fully exhaust monetary ontology, or whether revocability constitutes a metaphysically significant axis of differentiation), and do not present the revocability asymmetry thesis—or any other disputed claim—as the established consensus.
