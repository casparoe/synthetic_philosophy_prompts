You are a professional philosopher specializing in practical rationality, constitutional theory, and the ethics of self-binding. Before analyzing the argument below, spend a few minutes brainstorming the conceptual distinction between first-order and second-order precommitment, and carefully consider how personal agency over time differs from collective democratic agency across generations.

This issue has become personally urgent. Following a recent diagnosis that may eventually impair my judgment, I am drafting an irrevocable medical decision contract—essentially a personal Ulysses contract—that binds my future self to refuse specific treatments even if I later demand them. At the same time, I serve on the charter committee for a community land trust, where we are debating an “irrevocable conservation covenant” that binds all future trustees, including those democratically elected by beneficiaries decades hence. This parallel has led me to construct the following argument, which now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to render democratic constitutionalism impossible whenever self-binding is rational. I would very much like to preserve premises (1) and (5) if I can.

(1) A rational agent S at time t1 should enter a Ulysses contract against action A at t2 only if S can identify, at t1, a specific corruption mechanism that will make S-at-t2 choose A despite S-at-t1’s enduring values.

(2) To prevent S-at-t2 from dissolving the contract, the contract must include conditions for release that S-at-t2 cannot unilaterally control.

(3) But designing those release conditions is itself an exercise of S-at-t1’s judgment; therefore, if S-at-t1 predicts that S-at-t2’s judgment will be corrupted regarding A, she must also predict that S-at-t2’s judgment about whether the release conditions are met will be corrupted.

(4) Consequently, rational self-binding requires “double-locking”: binding not only the primary action but also the meta-conditions for dissolution, so that release requires an external trigger that S-at-t2 cannot influence.

(5) By direct analogy, if a founding democratic generation F predicts that future generation G will corruptly repeal constitutional provision P, then rational constitutional precommitment requires double-locking—entrenching both P and the amendment rules that govern P.

(6) Under double-locking, G can never legitimately alter P, because any attempt by G to change the rules is precisely the kind of reasoning F predicted to be corrupt.

(7) Therefore, if personal Ulysses contracts are rational, constitutional entrenchment permanently disables democratic revision.

(8) Yet democratic legitimacy requires that no generation be permanently disabled from revising its fundamental law.

(9) So either personal Ulysses contracts are not rational—which seems false, given their widespread use in psychiatric advance directives and addiction recovery planning—or democratic constitutionalism is incompatible with rational precommitment.
