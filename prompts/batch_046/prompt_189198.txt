I serve on the scientific-ethics review board of an intergovernmental body that evaluates proposals for large-scale ecological interventions—stratospheric aerosol pilots, gene-drive releases in wild vector populations, synthetic microbiome deployments—where proponents routinely defend action through consequentialist reasoning about expected welfare, risk-weighted outcomes, and net harm reduction. After an extended discussion with a colleague in applied moral philosophy, I became convinced that our board is not merely facing hard empirical predictions, but confronting a specific theoretical failure: consequentialist cluelessness, the condition in which an agent lacks sufficient predictive and evaluative knowledge for utility-maximizing logic to guide choice in any non-arbitrary way. What we need is not another philosophical citation, but a reusable operational framework.

Please construct a decision procedure—structured as an ordered sequence of diagnostic questions with explicit stopping conditions—that my panel, composed of ecologists, risk analysts, and one public-interest advocate and operating under a strict ninety-day review mandate, can apply repeatedly to any intervention proposal. The procedure should translate the philosophical problem into practical tests: first, determine whether the proposal generates first-order cluelessness (absence of predictive models for cascading effects across ecological and temporal scales) or second-order cluelessness (presence of partial models but no justified framework for weighting intergenerational, non-human, or geographically distributed outcomes); second, assess whether the identified epistemic gap is remediable within our institutional window or capacity; third, locate the precise handoff point where technical judgment ends and normative judgment must take over, including criteria for which member of the panel holds sign-off authority at that transition. For each stage, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as a failure, and where the procedure must halt with a negative recommendation regardless of favorable near-term expected utility.

Apply the procedure to two concrete cases: (A) a gene-drive mosquito program targeting malaria reduction in a single watershed, with well-modeled local epidemiology but genuinely opaque evolutionary and ecological spillover dynamics over a fifty-year horizon; and (B) a small-scale solar radiation management trial with a robust twenty-year monitoring protocol but deeply contested valuations of climate-risk reduction versus localized weather disruption and agricultural uncertainty. One of these applications should yield a recommendation that proponents will find unacceptable—specifically, a suspension or denial grounded in satisfied cluelessness conditions despite positive short-term calculations. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do—such as resolving disagreements about which values should dominate when predictive knowledge is absent, substituting for democratic authorization of risk, or eliminating the need for irreducible human judgment at the values handoff—and flag the specific case types where a chair or editor must intervene because the checklist reaches its limit.
