A colleague summarised the debate between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness as follows. Epistemicism holds that predicates like 'bald' have a precise, metaphysically sharp boundary—say, exactly 3,217 hairs—determined not by linguistic usage but by the underlying nature of the property itself; however, our epistemic position is governed by a margin-for-error principle that renders knowledge of this cutoff impossible, so borderline cases are definite truths or falsehoods that we simply fail to know. Supervaluationism, he said, denies that there is any unique correct sharpening: there exist numerous admissible precisifications of 'bald', and a statement is true only if it holds on every admissible sharpening, false only if it fails on all, and otherwise genuinely truth-valueless. He added that supervaluationism thereby sidesteps an 'ignorance' objection—there is no hidden fact we are missing—whereas epistemicism collapses vagueness into ordinary factual ignorance and must explain our reluctance to draw a line near the boundary as a Gricean pragmatic implicature rather than as a semantic phenomenon. He concluded that epistemicism is therefore essentially scepticism about our knowledge of language, while supervaluationism amounts to an anti-realist denial that vague properties exist at all. Is this a fair characterisation? Please separate the parts that accurately capture the epistemicist commitment to sharp but unknown boundaries and the supervaluationist appeal to admissible precisifications from the parts that misrepresent the metaphysical status of the boundary, the explanation of linguistic reluctance, or the respective commitments to realism. In particular, does his claim that epistemicism treats borderline reluctance via Gricean pragmatics actually follow from the view, and does his framing of supervaluationism as anti-realist about properties conflate semantic indeterminacy with metaphysical non-existence?
