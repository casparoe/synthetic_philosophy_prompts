I have come to hold a somewhat unfashionable synthesis of Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence that I want exposed rather than defended. On my reading, Chesterton's fence is not merely a counsel of epistemic humility—an injunction to understand an institution's purpose before tearing it down—but rather generates a defeasible pro tanto reason in favor of preservation that operates even when we cannot reconstruct that purpose. I take this to align with Burke's notion of prescription, but with a sharper formulation: the burden is not simply on the reformer to justify change, but on anyone who would dismantle an institution to show that its continued existence lacks justification through present functional integration, regardless of whether its original rationale remains opaque. This, I think, avoids both the paralysis of absolute traditionalism and the hubris of rationalist reconstruction, yet I want an honest map of its vulnerabilities. What specific observations about institutional decay, what documented cases in which preservation clearly produced harm despite opaque origins, and what arguments—perhaps drawn from evolutionary theory or institutional economics—would show that present functional integration is often just path-dependent lock-in rather than accumulated practical wisdom? I want you to rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, to say which ones a committed defender of this view would be forced to concede and which they could absorb with minor adjustments, and to assess frankly whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability or whether it genuinely risks something.
