I've been thinking about Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities lately, mostly because I work on algorithmic accountability and people keep saying "the system couldn't have done otherwise" like that automatically settles blame. So I went back and read Frankfurt's original thought experiment and a bunch of the standard replies—Fischer, Widerker, all that—and honestly it feels like the debate has been spinning it's wheels since the 60s with zero actual resolution.

My working theory is that it's not really about moral responsibility at all, but about whether "could have done otherwise" gets analyzed through an interventionist causal model—where you need a manipulable pathway—or through a counterfactual-dependence model where you just need a nearby world with a different outcome. The Frankfurt defenders seem to be running one framework and the PAP defenders the other, but neither side makes it explicit, so they're talking past each other about what counts as a real possibility. Is that right, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about decision architecture or brain processes doing the real work? I want a diagnosis—verbal, substantive, or just a clash of intuitions—and an honest answer about whether anything, like a formal result on intervention identifiability or a sharper conceptual distinction, could actually break the deadlock, or if this is just one of those philosophical stalls that never ends.
