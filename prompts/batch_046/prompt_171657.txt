I've been going deep on Hofstadter lately — specifically his claim that the self is more like a pattern or melody that can persist in other brains, not just a ghost locked in one machine. For my philosophy of mind seminar we're actually writing fiction instead of essays this week (professor is weird like that) and I want a story that takes this literally without falling into the usual brain-in-a-vat clichés.

Here's the setup: there's an experimental procedure called cross-cortical resonance mapping — basically, if two neural architectures are isomporphic enough, they can sustain a single first-person loop across both substrates. Not mind-reading, not shared memories, but one continuous 'I' that happens to be running on two brains at once. I want a story about one half of such a pair that's been seperated since adolescence. They suddenly start experiencing the other half's present-tense sensory stream — not as a foreign voice, but as their own perception bleeding through. The protagonist has to navigate not knowing which body is 'theirs,' especially in a scene where they face a mirror and can't locate the first-person perspective in either reflection. The ending should leave it open whether merging back is even possible, or whether the 'I' has already become something that can't be collapsed back into one skull.

Make it philosophical but visceral — I want the reader to feel the disorientation of distributed selfhood, not just understand it conceptually. And please avoid the standard sci-fi tropes about uploaded consciousness; the point here is that there's never been a single container for the self, only the pattern continuing across wetware.
