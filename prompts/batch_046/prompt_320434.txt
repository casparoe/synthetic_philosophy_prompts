I have been trying to extend Hofstadter's Gödelian analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach beyond its usual scope, and I believe I have derived a paradox that threatens the strange-loop account of self-awareness. Gödel's proof depends on arithmetizing syntax so that a formal system can construct a sentence that asserts of itself that it is unprovable; the sentence is true precisely because, were it false, the system would prove it. If self-awareness is structurally analogous—if it consists in a representational system turning back upon itself—then there ought to be a mental analog: a conscious representation whose explicit content is "this very representation lacks phenomenal consciousness." Call this the phenomenal Gödel state. Now, if that content is true, then the representation indeed lacks phenomenal consciousness, which means it is not a conscious state at all, and therefore could not bear that content. If the content is false, then the representation does possess phenomenal consciousness, but then its explicit denial of its own consciousness is false in virtue of the very consciousness required to formulate it. In either case we seem to reach a contradiction that cannot be resolved within a single representational level. Must we conclude that the Gödelian analogy collapses, because intentional self-reference is not syntactic self-reference? Or does the paradox instead show that consciousness requires an infinite regress of meta-representational levels, with no base loop? I am inclined toward the first option, but I want to know precisely where the analogical step breaks down and what a defender of the strange-loop view should say about representations that appear to deny their own phenomenal character.
