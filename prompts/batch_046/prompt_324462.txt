Student answer:
Frege saw that "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative because both names refer to Venus, yet they present Venus differently: one as the evening star and one as the morning star. The reference is the same planet, but the senses differ, so the statement connects two distinct modes of presentation rather than relating Venus to itself. This dissolves the puzzle.

Frege also held that every proper name is really a disguised definite description. So "Hesperus" abbreviates "the brightest body visible in the evening," and "Phosphorus" abbreviates "the brightest body visible in the morning." That is why they can carry different senses.

Now consider arbitrary tags like "Record 734." Because these tags contain no hidden descriptions, they have reference but no sense at all. Consequently, Frege's solution does not apply to them. If a user learns that "Record 734 in Schema A is Record 734 in Schema B," this can never be informative, since without descriptive content there is nothing to differ between the two sides. In fact, for Frege identity is fundamentally a relation between senses: when the senses coincide the statement is trivial, and when they diverge it is informative. But since pure tags lack sense entirely, every identity statement involving them is automatically trivial, and the puzzle never arises in such systems.
