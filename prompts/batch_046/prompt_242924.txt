I am writing a paper for my high school philosophy elective on Arthur Miller's The Crucible, and I want to avoid the standard McCarthyism comparison that every essay seems to make. Instead, I would like you to read the play through the lens of epistemic injustice—specifically the distinction between testimonial injustice, where a speaker receives an unfair credibility deficit or excess, and hermeneutical injustice, where a group lacks the shared interpretive resources to understand its own social experience. I want to apply this framework in a somewhat unconventional direction: rather than treating the girls' accusations as simple credibility excess and the accused women as suffering credibility deficit, I want to examine how the court's procedural rules themselves function as a form of structural hermeneutical marginalization. The defendants are denied not only belief but also the conceptual vocabulary to articulate collective suggestion, gendered coercion, or psychological contagion within a Puritan epistemic culture that has no place for such explanations. At the same time, I want to explore whether Miller dramatizes a kind of preemptive testimonial silencing—particularly in Tituba's forced confession—where certain speakers are never genuinely heard because the framework of credible discourse has already excluded them before they open their mouths.

Please treat the play as a work of art that complicates philosophy, not merely as an illustration. Where does this framework illuminate specific scenes—perhaps Elizabeth Proctor's attempt to explain Abigail's motives, or John Proctor's refusal to sign a false confession that would validate the court's epistemic authority, or the moment when Mary Warren's testimony collapses under collective pressure? Where does the play resist being reduced to an epistemic analysis, perhaps by insisting that the injustice is irreducibly moral, religious, or political? I am especially interested in whether Miller's dramatization of performative certainty—the girls' theatrical displays of possession—reveals something about credibility excess that abstract philosophical argument alone tends to miss. Please respond in American English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since I am working only from the play itself and my class notes.
