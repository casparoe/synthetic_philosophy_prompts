I am designing a 90-minute session for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and science—twelve students, familiar with basic philosophy of science but not specialists in Dennett—where I want participants to experience, rather than merely discuss in the abstract, the central tension of the real-patterns view: that patterns accessible in the intentional stance carry genuine predictive weight and are not mere projections, yet they resist clean reduction to patterns available in the physical or design stance, with cross-stance translation typically producing either explanatory loss or predictive failure. To push this past the standard textbook formulation, I want to build in a somewhat under-discussed complication—what we might call stance recursion in artificial or adaptive systems, where applying the design stance to an AI requires attributing an intentional-stance profile either to the system itself or to its creators, so that the three stances do not form a neat ontological hierarchy but loop back upon one another.

Please design a runnable in-class simulation or structured game—complete with explicit rules, assigned roles, necessary materials, and a non-monetary payoff or scoring scheme—in which students are divided into three explanation teams, each restricted to one stance, and must collectively predict and intervene on the behavior of a complex nested system, for instance a malfunctioning adaptive agent whose internal architecture includes a user-modeling module. The rules should be arranged so that participants discover for themselves that predictions generated within one stance cannot be adopted by another team without either losing counterfactual robustness or failing to anticipate actual behavior, and that the system simultaneously pulls for explanation at all three levels. Include a phased timeline—prediction rounds, intervention rounds, and a final revelation of hidden architecture—plus a debrief that maps specific moments of friction onto the criteria for real patterns: predictive leverage, counterfactual support, and resistance to projective elimination. Also include an explicit, honest note on which feature of stance recursion the activity cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator in closing discussion, specifically whether the loop collapses into a single physical description or genuinely sustains three independent levels of reality.

Finally, please tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—whether conceptual confusion, role imbalance, or a failure to feel the intended friction—and how to correct for it. The design should rely only on concepts a well-prepared model can work with without web access; do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid framing that depends on obscure primary sources or precise textual attributions.
