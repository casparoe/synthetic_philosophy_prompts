Write the opening remarks—about fifteen minutes, spoken aloud—for a workshop called "Deliberation Across Deep Difference," co-hosted by a university philosophy department and a school of theology. The room is deliberately mixed: Rawlsians and other political philosophers who defend public reason; theologians from traditions that reject the idea that revelation must be translated into secular propositions; local policymakers who need practical guidance; and community organizers with no philosophical training.

The philosophical idea must do structural work, not decorative work. The speaker must treat the standard liberal "translation" requirement—citizens should offer reasons that are accessible to all reasonable people regardless of their comprehensive doctrines—as itself a substantive metaphysical claim about what counts as a reason (detachable, propositional, assessable by a shared rational faculty) rather than a neutral procedural rule. The speaker must show the philosophers that defending public reason by appealing to fair cooperation already smuggles in an idealized picture of reason-giving that may not survive real pluralism; show the theologians that asking them to bracket or translate their deepest convictions is not a harmless courtesy but a reshaping of the moral claim itself; and show the non-academic audience, in concrete terms, what is lost—for instance, when a religious objection to a particular affordable-housing zoning plan must be recast as an economic-efficiency argument to enter the public record.

The speech must not resolve the tension with a tidy compromise. Instead, it should propose—tentatively, as the workshop's working hypothesis—that there might be a mode of mutual accessibility that does not depend on translation, something like shared ownership of a concrete problem or a shared exposure to vulnerability, without assuming that such a mode is already available or stable. The speaker must be able to say what needs saying to each part of the room without saying anything false to any part of it.

The tone should be formal, spoken rather than written-to-be-read, and should avoid both sermonizing and lecturing. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or named citations of scholars; do not quote specific texts in quotation marks. Length: approximately 900 words.
