Here is an argument I encountered about whether generational cohorts—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z—are ontologically real kinds rather than marketing labels. (1) A grouping is a real kind only if its members share properties that are non-accidentally explained by a common underlying cause specific to that grouping. (2) Millennials, for instance, share digital-native socialization patterns and delayed property ownership that are causally traced to macro-historical conditions (the spread of broadband and the 2008 crisis) occurring precisely during their formative years. (3) Therefore, generational cohorts satisfy the condition in (1) and count as real kinds. (4) But the same logic applies to anyone who experienced the 2008 crisis between ages 18 and 25, regardless of birth year—this crisis-cohort shares the causal conditions and resulting traits, yet it slices across standard generational boundaries. (5) It applies even more narrowly: people born within the same 30-day window share prenatal hormonal and nutritional environments that non-accidentally shape later psychological traits, so by (1) they too form a real kind. (6) Because we rightly reject 30-day birth windows as real kinds, (1) must be abandoned, and with it the reality of generations. I want to keep (1) intact and preserve the explanatory power of generations, so I resist that conclusion. Which step contains the hidden error—perhaps an equivocation on “common cause” or a modal slide about explanation—and what must I concede if I reject it? Respond in exactly one paragraph, naming the flawed step and stating the cost of fixing it.
