I recently served as a juror in a complex criminal trial in which the court used a structured verdict form: we were required to answer discrete factual questions—whether the defendant possessed the requisite mental state, whether the act occurred as alleged, whether causation was established—before rendering a final verdict on the principal charge. During deliberation, it became apparent that a clear majority endorsed each individual finding, yet different combinations of those endorsements yielded contradictory conclusions about guilt; to preserve unanimity on the final verdict, several jurors adjusted their answers on subsidiary questions, not because they had changed their beliefs about the underlying facts, but because they treated the group’s coherence as a constraint on individual judgment. This institutional design seems to presuppose a particular view of collective rationality—perhaps that a jury is a single agent whose beliefs must satisfy logical closure, or perhaps that it is an aggregative mechanism in which consistency is a strategic achievement rather than a rational requirement. What philosophical theory of judgment aggregation does this practice actually commit to? Does it implicitly endorse premise-based aggregation, conclusion-based aggregation, or some hybrid that avoids the discursive dilemma only by allowing jurors to revise premises for non-epistemic reasons? And if the implicit theory were made explicit, would the practice still be defensible, or would it require restructuring the verdict form so that logical consistency is enforced by procedural rule rather than by private negotiation?
