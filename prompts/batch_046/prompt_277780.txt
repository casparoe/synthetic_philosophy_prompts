I am writing the final paper for an advanced seminar on theories of well-being, and I have become drawn to a position I am tentatively calling tranquilism: the claim that only the absence of craving and the absence of suffering possess intrinsic value, while pleasure, knowledge, achievement, and aesthetic appreciation are valuable solely as instruments for achieving or preserving that absence. On this view, positive mental states are ontologically neutral—being happy is not itself good; only not being unhappy is—so the ideal life is not one of flourishing but of perfect equanimity in which no structural desire remains to generate future suffering. This is stronger than standard negative utilitarianism, which still operates on a single hedonic scale; tranquilism denies that there is any positive side of that scale that counts at all.

I want to stress-test this position rigorously. What would have to be true for tranquilism to be false, and how should the defeaters be ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I am especially interested in whether empirical results—say, from affective forecasting or from neuroscience on reward anticipation that distinguishes craving from satisfaction—could undermine the claim that all positive motivation is covert craving. I also want to know which objections a committed tranquilist should concede as genuinely damaging—perhaps cases where the pursuit of knowledge or art seems valuable even when it introduces no risk of future suffering—and which they can legitimately dismiss by refining the concept of craving. Finally, please assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every apparent counterexample is absorbed by saying the state in question masked a subtle craving, does that insulation protect the view or expose it as empty? I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want an honest map of where the position is genuinely exposed and where it remains defensible.
