Before drafting, take a moment to brainstorm the distinctions among momentary control failure, prior culpable ignorance, and structurally distributed attention, then think carefully about whether outsourcing cognitive labor to an environment can ever be blameworthy in itself.

Write a short research proposal (approximately 1,500 words, suitable for a one-semester graduate seminar) on the absent-minded driver problem, but approached through a framing I will call scaffolded negligence. The case is familiar: a habitual commuter drives a well-known route on autopilot, fails to register an altered traffic signal that has recently changed, and causes harm. Existing treatments usually ask whether he lacked sufficient conscious control at the moment, or whether he was previously negligent in allowing himself to become distracted. I want the proposal to ask instead whether there is a coherent, distinct category of blame that applies when an agent has systematically embedded his attention in external structures—predictable road layouts, routine sequences, the absence of recent change—and then fails to update those structures when they shift. Does this reveal a prior moral decision to treat public space as an extension of private habit, and if so, is the resulting blame stronger, weaker, or structurally different from standard prior negligence?

Your proposal must do the following. First, state the precise question so that it admits a determinate answer: is scaffolded negligence reducible to prior negligence, a more severe variant of it, or an excuse that weakens blame? Second, explain why the standard control-based and prior-negligence accounts leave this specific question open—particularly regarding whether the blame tracks the absence of attention or the mismatch between the reliability the agent demands of the environment and the vigilance he owes to others within it. Third, describe your approach: a conceptual analysis of scaffolded attention paired with a comparative institutional case—such as air-traffic controllers or clinicians relying on automated alert systems—to test whether the excuse structure depends on how attentional labor is socially distributed. Argue why this comparative method is preferable to the obvious alternatives (a purely phenomenological analysis of driver awareness, or an empirical study of accident rates). Fourth, specify the possible conclusions and which considerations—especially about the symmetry between human and technological reliability—would decide among them. Fifth, give an honest assessment of scope risks: for instance, whether the institutional comparison collapses into empirical sociology, or whether analyzing distributed cognition requires a fuller theory than a semester allows.
