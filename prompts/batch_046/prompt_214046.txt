I'm preparing a consistency audit for my independent study presentation on the philosophy of pain, and I'd like to check whether my commitments actually hang together before I present them. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I accept the standard clinical description of pain asymbolia: patients retain normal sensory discrimination—temperature, pressure, localization—and they sincerely report sensations they call "pain," yet they show no withdrawal reflexes, facial grimacing, or verbal expressions of distress, which suggests a selective disruption of the affective-motivational pathway rather than the sensory-discriminative one.

(2) I think that pain is essentially unpleasant: there is no possible situation in which a state is genuinely painful yet phenomenally neutral or even pleasant; the badness is part of what makes it pain.

(3) At the same time, I think this unpleasantness is not a first-order sensory quality like warmth or sharpness, but a second-order evaluative appraisal: we become aware of a bodily damage-representation and then register it as bad-for-us.

(4) I take first-person reports in asymbolia literally: when a patient says "this burns painfully," she is describing a state that genuinely belongs to the pain kind, not misapplying the word to a non-painful sensation.

(5) I also hold that phenomenal consciousness is unitary in this sense: if a subject sincerely reports being aware of a state's qualitative features, none of those features can be present yet hidden from her awareness; there is no "missing" phenomenal layer she fails to notice.

(6) I think the representational core of pain—representing tissue damage in a specific body part—can be intact and correctly reported even when the second-order appraisal mechanism is neurologically blocked.

(7) Finally, I think that even in a counterfactual culture where people experienced damage-representations without any negative evaluation and called them "pleasant warnings," those states would still count as pains, just ones that happen not to be disliked.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious. For each tension, specify what would have to be revised and what that costs. Then tell me which single revision—giving up or modifying one commitment—would clean up the most inconsistencies overall. Be specific about whether my problem is mainly with (2), with (4), or with the combination of (3) and (5).
