Write a sestina (six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi) in which the six rotating end-words follow the classic permutation and are: equation, orbit, fold, shadow, predict, and stretch. The poem should take as its subject deterministic chaos—not the popular “butterfly effect” as a loose metaphor for contingency, but the precise conceptual tension that a fully deterministic dynamical law (an equation containing no stochastic term) can generate orbits with such sensitive dependence on initial conditions that long-term prediction becomes structurally impossible, leaving only a shadow of what might have been calculated. Use the formal rotation of the end-words to perform the idea: each time a word returns in a new position it should feel like a state being iteratively folded and stretched, with the equation remaining invariant even as the orbit diverges beyond foresight. The envoi must gather all six words together without resolving whether this unpredictability is an epistemic limit or an ontological feature of the dynamics; leave the tension open. The poem should be technically accurate enough that a reader familiar with strange attractors recognises the mechanics of stretching and folding rather than a generic chaos cliché, yet it should remain fully intelligible as verse to someone with no mathematics. Do not include citations, references to named texts, bibliographic suggestions, or lists of further reading; the poem itself should carry the entire argument. Use British spelling and punctuation conventions.
