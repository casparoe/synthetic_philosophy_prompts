I was arguing with my flatmate about just war stuff after watching some documentary on private military firms, and we got onto this thing that I can't find a proper name for. So, moral equality of combatants says soldiers on both sides have the same rights to kill and be killed regardless of whether their cause is just — that part I mostly get. But then I started noticing that people who accept this perfectly well for uniformed state soldiers suddenly change their tune the moment you keep all the structural features the same — organised command, wearing insignia, following the laws of war, direct participation in hostilities — but swap the state employement for a corporate contract, or put the pilot in a container in Nevada operating a drone. They treat these people as plain murderers or criminals rather than combatants with reciprocal rights, and they don't seem to think this needs any argument; it's like the equality was never really about the moral structure of combat, but about a kind of uniform-based membership card.

I've started calling it "badge-contingent equality" in my notes, but I doubt that's a real term anyone uses. Is there an established concept for this specific failure mode, where the moral equality of combatants gets treated as a priviledge of state affiliation rather than a feature of organised hostilities? How exactly is it different from the standard revisionist view that unjust combatants are wrong to fight? And please reply in British English — I'm trying to get used to the spelling conventions.
