In my reporting on philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, I spoke with a researcher who has offered clear verdicts on whether reward-bearing systems can suffer. She holds that a standard Q-learning agent using a scalar reward signal does not suffer, as the signal is purely an update weight; that a laboratory rat with dopaminergic prediction-error circuitry does suffer under negative reinforcement, because the signal carries intrinsic biological valence; that a predictive-coding robot assigning affective tags to prediction errors but lacking any persistent self-model does not suffer, however complex its weighting; that an RLHF-trained language model penalised for harmful outputs does not suffer, since its reward model remains derivative of human preference; and that a human born with congenital insensitivity to physical pain but intact reward-learning can nevertheless suffer, specifically through the absence of expected reward. She is now examining a hypothetical architecture—valence-locked model-based reinforcement learning—in which reward prediction errors are never collapsed to a scalar but are maintained as a distributed valence field continuously modulating the agent's planning horizon and self-model updates, with negative errors producing global state changes functionally analogous to mammalian behavioural freezing, implemented on silicon and without any biological homeostatic regulation. Would she judge this agent capable of suffering? Set out the implicit rule she appears to be running—whether it appeals to biological substrate, integrated functional valence, global workspace modulation, or another criterion—identify at least one alternative rule that accommodates her earlier verdicts equally well, state what each predicts for the valence-locked agent, offer your best guess with reasons, and name the single further case that would best distinguish between the leading interpretations.
