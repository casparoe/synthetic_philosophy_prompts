Here is an argument I sketched for a workshop on higher-order consciousness and now find suspicious: it seems to show that higher-order theory eliminates unconscious mental states altogether, which cannot be right, yet each step looks defensible.

(1) On the standard higher-order view, a mental state is phenomenally conscious if and only if it is the actual target of an occurrent, non-inferential higher-order representation (HOR).

(2) If a mental state S is unconscious, then by (1) there is no actual HOR of S.

(3) Now consider causally efficacious unconscious states—subliminal priming effects, or type-II blindsight, in which subjects guide reaching or choice behavior toward stimuli they sincerely deny perceiving. For the cognitive system to adapt downstream processing to S, there must be some representational state in the architecture that registers S’s occurrence; otherwise S would be causally inert with respect to behavior.

(4) Any representational state that registers S’s occurrence just is an HOR of S, even if it is embedded in a subpersonal predictive-processing loop rather than in a personal-level judgment.

(5) So every causally efficacious unconscious state is actually targeted by an HOR.

(6) By (1), every such state is conscious.

(7) Therefore there are no causally efficacious unconscious mental states; all states that do real cognitive work are conscious.

(8) But this flatly contradicts established findings in cognitive psychology and neuropsychology.

So either higher-order theory is false, or one of the premises fails. I am inclined to preserve (1) and reject (4), arguing that subpersonal registration is not the same kind of mental representation that a HOT requires. But then I worry I am merely stipulating that unconscious states lack HORs, without explaining how the system tracks them. If I reject (3) instead, I seem committed to saying that unconscious states influence behavior with no internal tracking at all, which sounds mysterious. Which step do you take to be weakest, and what does abandoning it cost the higher-order theorist? In particular, does rejecting (4) force a sharp personal-level versus subpersonal divide that makes higher-order theory incompatible with predictive-processing models of cognition?
