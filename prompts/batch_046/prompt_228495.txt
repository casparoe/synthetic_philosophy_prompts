Question: Compare church-state relations in England, Germany, France and the US, focusing on established churches, church taxes, and secularism. Is the German Kirchensteuer compatible with genuine secularism?

Student answer:
England retains an established church, yet unlike Germany it does not levy a direct church tax; the Church of England relies on endowments and voluntary donations, although local councils still collect small parish maintenance fees that act much like a state-collected religious levy. In Germany, registered Catholics and Protestants pay Kirchensteuer through the state tax office, which clearly shows Germany is not secular, because a secular state ought never to gather funds on behalf of religious bodies. France reached full secularism with the 1905 law, which separated church and state and also prohibited all religious symbols in public schools and ended every kind of state subsidy to religion. The US First Amendment erects an absolute wall of separation, meaning religious influence is entirely absent from public life and churches can never receive indirect state support such as tax exemptions. So France and the US are genuinely secular, England is semi-secular despite establishment, and Germany fails the test because of the Kirchensteuer.
