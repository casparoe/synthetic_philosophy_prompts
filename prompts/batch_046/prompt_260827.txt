At a philosophy of biology workshop, two participants—Leah and Marcus—debated whether origin essentialism applies cleanly to in vitro fertilization. Leah argued that if you are essentially tied to the particular sperm and egg that formed you, then selecting one embryo over another in IVF means parents literally determine which metaphysically necessary person exists; she took this as a reductio, proposing instead that personal origin should be defined by the causal pathway within a compatible biological range, not by the exact genetic token. Marcus replied that this collapses origin essentialism into psychological continuity: if mutations or alternative embryos within the same parents can yield the same you, then identity is no longer anchored to a specific material origin. He insisted that selecting embryo A over B actualizes one potential person rather than another, and that this is a metaphysical fact, not a moral problem—though he granted that origin essentialism need not be token-specific down to every base pair, only to the particular embryo or zygote that actually initiated the causal chain. Leah countered that Marcus’s “particular embryo” criterion is arbitrary once multiple embryos are available simultaneously, and that without a principled boundary between “same origin with variation” and “different origin,” his view cannot explain why a naturally conceived person with a genetic mutation is still the same person, whereas an IVF-selected sibling-embryo is a different one. Marcus answered that mutation occurs within an ongoing causal chain, whereas selecting B breaks that chain entirely, replacing it with a numerically distinct one.
