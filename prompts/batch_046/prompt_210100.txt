Here are my verdicts on commemorative ethics, and I want the principle—if one exists—that captures them, stated precisely, with the clause that each case motivates and an honest statement of which verdict must go if simplicity requires it.

Removing a statue of a colonial administrator and melting it into a public fountain feels right: the material form is transformed rather than erased. Renaming a street after one of his victims, but leaving the old name on the signs struck through in black, feels right: the defaced signifier is preserved as evidence, not honor. Keeping the statue and name intact with only a corrective plaque feels wrong: the additive note does not dislodge the honorific frame. Replacing the statue and renaming the street, then installing a kiosk that lets visitors vote on whether to restore either, feels wrong in a different way: the moral decision is outsourced to transient majorities. Replacing the statue with an abstract form whose dimensions encode, in fixed geometric ratios, the census-scaled magnitude of displacement attributed to the figure—while leaving the original pedestal empty and inscribed with the crossed-out name—feels right: quantification honors the harmed without installing a substitute hero.

The hard pair is the second and third: both preserve the harmful name in view, yet I approve one and reject the other. The distinction seems to turn on whether the original signifier is framed as honor or as forensic record, but I am unsure that frame survives scrutiny, especially since the fourth case makes the frame explicitly procedural and I still reject it.

What principle delivers verdicts one, two, and five while excluding three and four? Does it require me to reject four on the ground that democratic delegation violates a present duty of moral judgment, or can it treat procedural openness as a separate vice? Apply it also to a case I have not listed: a city retains the street name but replaces every sign with one listing, in identical font and alphabetical order, both the original honoree and a figure from the harmed community. Must I approve or reject?

Take a definite position: defend one unified principle that saves the maximum number of verdicts and name the single one I should overturn, or argue that two of my verdicts are genuinely incommensurable and specify which pair. Be explicit about where your principle misfires and what it predicts for the alphabetical-sign case.
