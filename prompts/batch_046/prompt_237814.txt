A colleague has built an argument about political dirty hands that seems to dissolve the very concept. She lays out these steps: (1) For dirty hands to be a real phenomenon—not merely excused wrongdoing—there must be acts that are both politically necessary and genuinely wrong, where "genuinely wrong" means the prohibiting norm is not canceled but only overridden. (2) If a norm is not canceled, then it continues to apply fully to the agent; violating it always produces unavoidable moral residue—accountability, a duty of acknowledgment, or persistent guilt—no matter how weighty the countervailing reasons. (3) Therefore, any dirty-hands act leaves the agent with an uncancelable moral stain. (4) But if the stain is unavoidable, then the agent was never released from the norm; she was only given overwhelming reason to breach it. (5) In that case, the situation is not a distinctive political dilemma but an ordinary moral tragedy within a single normative framework, which means there is no special category of "dirty hands" separate from tragic conflict. (6) So we must either deny that norms can persist without cancellation (giving up moral residue) or deny that dirty hands form a distinct category (giving up the phenomenon). She insists she wants to keep (1) and (3). Where does the argument slip—between (2) and (3), between (4) and (5), or in some other move? If you reject one step, explain why it looked plausible and what rejecting it costs, especially whether it forces a dual-system view of political and private morality.
