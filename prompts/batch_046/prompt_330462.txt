I've been retired from the lab for about three years now—used to run a molecular biology group, nothing too fancy—and lately I've been hanging out with philosophy grad students at the university pub. One idea that's grabbed me is program equilibrium, which people keep bringing up when they talk about logical omniscience in game theory. So here's the position I've ended up with, and I want to know what's genuinely wrong with it, not just what's out of fashion.

My view is that program equilibrium is the right framework for rational interaction because it treats logical omniscience as a category mistake rather than just an unrealistic idealization. In standard epistemic models, agents are required to know all logical consequences of their beliefs, which no real system can do. Program equilibrium replaces that with actual algorithms running with bounded resources—the equilibrium conditions apply to the outputs of those algorithms, not to some ghostly belief state. So it's not merely "relax omniscience a bit"; it's rebuilding rationality conditions around computation itself.

What would actually count against this? I want an honest map of exposure: which formal results, which intuitive countercases, which coordination failures would be decisive, and which would just be awkward? If bounded algorithms systematically fail to reach coordination where omniscient agents succeed, is that a fatal blow, or can defenders always say the algorithm was just poorly designed? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem? Please seperate what's settled in this area from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on it, and don't present a disputed interpretation as the consensus. I want to know where the bodies are buried, not just the standard objections people raise in intro courses.
