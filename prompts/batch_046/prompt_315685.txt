I have recently been appointed trustee of a small family foundation that has already committed £400,000 to a community arts centre now clearly failing to meet its welfare goals; redirecting the remaining endowment to mental-health outreach would almost certainly produce greater benefit, yet several board members argue that abandoning the centre betrays the original donors and undermines the foundation's standing as a reliable partner. This has led me to examine whether honouring sunk costs can ever be rationally required rather than merely a cognitive error. Please give me your actual credence, on a scale from 0 to 1, that honouring sunk costs is rationally defensible in at least one structurally distinct class of cases — namely, where the expenditure occurred within a cooperative scheme involving partial commitments, and where abandonment would damage the agent's diachronic credibility in analogous future interactions, even when forward-looking welfare maximisation clearly favours withdrawal. Decompose this into separate terms: first, the probability that a purely forward-looking expected-utility framework is normatively inadequate for institutional or fiduciary decisions of this kind; second, the probability that credibility-based or integrity-based reasons can genuinely outweigh welfare gains in such schemes; third, the probability that the cooperative-scheme framing correctly distinguishes rational loyalty from the standard fallacy; and fourth, the probability that the specific case I face actually belongs to that defensible class rather than to the fallacious one. For each term provide a rough number and a one-line justification, then combine them into your overall credence. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, and what kind of empirical observation or conceptual argument would most shift your view. Please avoid citing particular authors or texts; I am interested in the structure of the reasoning, not in bibliographic verification.
