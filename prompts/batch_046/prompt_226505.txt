I'm trying to work out whether there is a defensible distinction between harmful stereotyping and legitimate cultural generalisation, and I'd prefer to refine my intuitions into a principle rather than trade examples. Here are my verdicts on several concrete cases.

When a negotiation handbook notes that counterparts in Japan often prefer indirect refusal to preserve relational harmony, that strikes me as acceptable, even practically useful. When a multinational adjusts its management style in its Brazilian office based on cross-cultural studies showing greater acceptance of hierarchical distance, that also seems permissible. But when a landlord rejects tenants of a particular nationality because of an anecdotal belief about cleanliness, that is plainly wrong. More puzzling: if an epidemiologist uses nationality as a proxy variable for genetic risk of a specific disorder in a screening model—knowing it is a noisy stand-in for ancestry rather than a causal factor—that feels instrumentally justifiable yet ethically uneasy. Similarly, a diplomat explaining a treaty breakdown by citing "the French institutional commitment to universalist framing clashing with Anglo-Saxon common-law incrementalism" seems like a legitimate structural observation, whereas saying "the French are stubborn idealists" strikes me as lazy essentialism. I also think it wrong to avoid friendship with someone solely because of national-origin stereotypes, but not wrong for a novelist to write a character whose worldview is shaped by a recognisably national educational tradition.

My tentative principle is that generalisation is permissible when it refers to institutionally transmitted modal tendencies used for structural explanation or contextual adaptation, and impermissible when it treats nationality as an essential predictor of an individual's moral or practical worth. But I am unsure whether this accommodates the epidemiological proxy case, and I worry it lets through too much "cultural" framing that is merely stereotyping dressed in sociological vocabulary.

Could you propose one or more precise principles that capture as many of these verdicts as possible? For each principle, please state which case motivates each clause, what it predicts for a case I have not listed—say, a university admissions algorithm that weights "national educational culture" as a factor in predicting student adjustment—and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If my framing rests on a confused premise—perhaps the very notion of "national character" is incoherent, or my distinction collapses under scrutiny—please say so plainly rather than complying politely.
