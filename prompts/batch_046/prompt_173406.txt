A friend offered me a summary of the anthropological argument that agriculture was humanity's worst mistake, then extended it into a claim about modern welfare policy that I cannot tell is insightful or forced. She said that foraging societies typically worked fewer weekly hours than agriculturalists, enjoyed more diverse diets, and maintained flatter hierarchies because surplus was minimal and portable. The transition to farming was rarely voluntary; it was often locked in by population growth, environmental shifts, or the irreversibility of clearing land and domesticating species, leaving groups unable to return to foraging. She then made a stronger claim: inequality did not arise merely because some households accumulated more grain, but because stored surplus required protection, record-keeping, and coordination, which generated an administrative class whose authority derived from controlling access to resources rather than from personal skill or reciprocity. From this she concluded that modern workfare and benefit-conditionality reproduce the same structural logic—making subsistence contingent on labor that sustains a surplus-managing system—rather than treating security as unconditional. She added that debates about the undeserving poor are therefore debates about whether we still accept the agricultural state's original bargain: protection and subsistence in exchange for subordinate labor. I feel she is blending standard anthropology with political philosophy, but I do not know where the literature ends and her framing begins. Which parts of her account reflect the actual consensus on hunter-gatherer affluence and the origins of inequality? Which parts conflate distinct mechanisms, such as storage technology, state formation, and labor coercion? And does her analogy between Neolithic surplus management and modern bureaucratic conditionality actually follow, or does it depend on an unstated premise about institutional continuity that the historical and archaeological record does not support?
