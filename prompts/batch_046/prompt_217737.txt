As a journalist covering the long arc between philosophical logic and contemporary computing, I have been revisiting Charles Dodgson’s 1895 dialogue “What the Tortoise Said to Achilles,” published in Mind, in which Achilles can never compel the Tortoise to accept a deductive step without first invoking a further rule that licenses that step, generating an infinite regress of justification. The puzzle is not about mental slowness but about grounding: every attempt to justify deduction seems to presuppose more deduction, leaving the rules themselves apparently unmoored.

This brings me to a development Dodgson could not have witnessed: the rise of formal proof assistants—systems like Lean or Coq—in which inference rules are hard-coded into a small, trusted kernel, and human mathematicians construct arguments that the machine verifies mechanically. In some mathematical communities, this mechanized checkability is now treated as a gold standard of certainty, not because each step feels intuitively obvious to a human reader, but because it conforms to an explicit, immutable formal grammar. A subtle philosophical move accompanies this practice: reasoning is split into an object-level, where the proof lives, and a meta-level, where trust is placed in the kernel’s design rather than in any discursive justification of its rules.

If Dodgson were alive today and confronted with this architecture—particularly with the growing tendency to regard machine verification as having settled questions of deductive legitimacy—how do you think he would have responded? Would he have seen automated verification as Achilles finally catching the Tortoise by fixing the rules in silicon, or would he have insisted that the Tortoise now simply asks why we should trust the kernel? More specifically, I am interested in whether he would have treated the separation between object-level proof and meta-level kernel-trust as a genuine philosophical resolution of his regress, or merely as a practical relocation of it; and whether he might have drawn a distinction between the psychological comfort of outsourcing inference to a machine and the logical question of whether that outsourcing answers the demand for justification he originally dramatized.
