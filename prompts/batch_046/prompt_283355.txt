Write a poem of exactly fourteen lines, divided into two seven-line stanzas with a sharp volta between them, taking up Daniel Dennett's idea that the self is not an inner substance but a center of narrative gravity—an abstract point around which experiences, memories, and social attributions orbit and cohere without any fixed essence at the core. Use a rhyme scheme in which the end-words of lines 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 13 are grammatical variants of a single root (for example: tell, told, tale, telling, retold, untold, teller), so that the sonic architecture itself performs the philosophical point: the self is not one voice but a family of narrative inflections pulling toward an empty center. The first stanza should establish the intuitive Cartesian picture of an inner speaker who owns its thoughts; the volta should turn on the recognition that if this speaker is only gravitational, then relocating the entire archive—journals, testimony, recorded speech—to another body or a distributed system relocates the self without contradiction. The second stanza should press this further by imagining a collective of interlocking narratives that might generate its own center of gravity, and it should leave the tension unresolved rather than resolving it into either individualism or collective dissolution. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind recognizes Dennett's anti-essentialist move, and formally tight enough that the rhyme-family does real structural work rather than mere ornament.
