I have been working through the logic of choice without preference, particularly in the case of Buridan's ass, and I am struck by how differently the problem gets framed depending on whether one treats rationality as purely cognitive or as involving something like volitional appetite. On one side stands Buridan's own resolution: when reason finds two options perfectly equal, the will can still intervene and choose arbitrarily, which preserves freedom but seems to leave rationality itself incomplete or silent at the decisive moment. On the other side stands the contemporary rational-choice framework, which treats perfect indifference as an artifact of an underspecified preference ordering—resolved by introducing infinitesimal perturbations, lexicographic rules, or arbitrary tie-breaking mechanisms—thereby dissolving the paradox but at the cost of never allowing reason to be genuinely mute. I am trying to assess whether there is a coherent middle ground that takes the medieval notion of appetitus seriously: not as a fully formed preference, but as a directed orientation—attention, bodily posture, contextual salience—that introduces asymmetry without either rational determination or pure arbitrary volition. Could you compare Buridan's will-based resolution with the modern tie-breaking resolution, specifically with respect to whether the modern framing actually addresses Buridan's core concern that rationality alone cannot move the agent, or whether it simply redefines rationality to exclude the possibility of perfect indifference? And please evaluate whether this intermediate idea of an appetitive orientation adds anything philosophically, or whether it merely smuggles in the tie-breaker under a different name. I am especially interested in whether either framework can accommodate the possibility that an agent might rationally choose not to choose—that is, to remain in the state of indifference—without that being treated as a failure of agency rather than an exercise of it.
