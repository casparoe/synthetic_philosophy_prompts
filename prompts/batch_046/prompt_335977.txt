As a novelist, I am preoccupied with the tension between arranged contingency and experienced authorship: my characters act as though they originate their choices, yet every circumstance has been set by me. This has led me to take seriously Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument—that true moral responsibility is impossible, since being ultimately responsible for one’s actions requires being ultimately responsible for one’s nature, which in turn demands being causa sui, a condition no finite being can satisfy. Before I allow this conviction to govern the moral architecture of my next book, I want a precise map of its exposure. Not the familiar compatibilist reply that responsibility requires only appropriate responsiveness to reasons, but the deeper vulnerabilities: which intuitions—perhaps about narrative repair, retroactive authorship, or the coherence of volitional revision—would genuinely threaten it? What formal results, if any, demonstrate that circular self-causation in character formation is conceptually vicious rather than benign? Would empirical findings about neuroplastic agency undermine the argument, or would its defenders dismiss those as addressing only proximate, not ultimate, causation? Among these potential defeaters, distinguish those that proponents of the Basic Argument would themselves concede as damaging—say, a proof that self-formation can be non-viciously circular—from those they would reject as missing the point about ultimate origination. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, say whether the view has been so qualified and insulated that nothing remains genuinely at risk, and whether such insulation should be regarded, from a novelist’s perspective, as philosophical rigor or as the closure of a narrative that no longer permits genuine dramatic choice.
