Design a runnable 90-minute workshop for twelve graduate students in philosophy or religious studies that makes them feel the logical—not merely psychological—force of Jain anekāntavāda and its syādvāda (sevenfold modal predication), without letting it collapse into generic perspectivism. The room should have movable chairs, paper, and a projector; participants need no prior Jain expertise, but the facilitator must understand the distinction between dravyāstika and paryāyāstika standpoints and the four nikṣepas (substance-placing, mode-placing, name-placing, and present-state-placing). The concrete case must be ethically and ontologically dense: propose that a museum board must decide whether to deaccession and repatriate a twelfth-century ritual object to a diaspora community, so that material permanence, temporal change, linguistic designation, legal ownership, and religious efficacy genuinely intersect rather than serving as cosmetic labels.

The deliverable should have three parts, presented with no preface or commentary. First, the staged activity: specify the exact sequence of rounds, how participants are divided (if at all), what each may write or say in each round, the hidden constraints revealed at specific times, and the precise moment when the group is forced to draft a single unqualified consensus statement and then to watch it collapse. Second, the debrief: map each round onto a specific structural feature of anekāntavāda (the nikṣepa standpoints, the syāt qualifier as an ontological rather than epistemic marker, the impossibility of simultaneous unqualified affirmation and negation), predict the two or three predictable failures (e.g., participants treating standpoints as subjective opinions rather than objective aspects of the object; the facilitator resolving the tension prematurely), and state exactly how to correct each in real time. Be explicit about which feature of Jain logic the workshop cannot reproduce—most likely the metaphysical claim that the object literally bears contradictory properties in different respects—and state what the facilitator must supply verbally to cover that gap. Third, include the niche conceptual twist: require participants in the final round to classify their own prior claims according to which nikṣepa they used, then to rewrite one earlier sentence using all seven saptabhaṅgī modes (syād asti, syād nāsti, etc.), so they experience the qualifier not as hedging but as a logical form demanded by the object’s manifoldness.

Begin with the complete activity rules, staging, and debrief outline; follow with brief reasoning explaining why these mechanics cause participants to construct the problem themselves before the idea is named.
