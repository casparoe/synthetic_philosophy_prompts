As a novelist constructing a narrative that moves between the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward, I am struck by how the historiographical dispute over whether these events are properly classed as natural disasters or political crimes has remained frozen for decades. Sen's entitlement analysis frames Bengal as a distributive failure despite sufficient aggregate food; critics extend or resist this framing for the other two cases, citing ecological shocks, climatic variability, and administrative breakdown. Yet both camps cite the same mortality figures and draw on overlapping state archives without converging. Is the disagreement substantive—rooted in incompatible causal models—or verbal, hinging on whether 'political crime' requires intentional agency rather than systemic negligence? Or are the disputants actually pursuing different inquiries, with one asking about proximate entitlement collapse and the other about long-run structural vulnerability? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest assessment of whether a conceptual distinction—perhaps between counterfactual robustness under overdetermination and direct allocative failure—could resolve the stall, or whether the evidentiary opacity inherent in colonial and authoritarian record-keeping makes any resolution illusory.
