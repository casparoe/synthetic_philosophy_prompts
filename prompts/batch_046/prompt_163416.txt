I have recently become personally concerned with whether lowering inhibitions through alcohol reveals a person's genuine moral dispositions or merely corrupts practical judgment—a question that has taken on new urgency for me as I observe contemporary debates about "sober curiosity" and whether intoxication can serve as an ethical diagnostic. In Plato's Laws, I encountered a passage in which the Athenian Stranger asks the lawgiver whether any potion produces not fear but its precise opposite. The exchange runs: "Be it so, O lawgiver, that for producing fear no such drug apparently has been given to men by God, nor have we devised such ourselves (for quacks I count not of our company); but does there exist a potion for inducing fearlessness and excessive and untimely confidence,—of what shall we say about this?" Clinias names wine, and the Stranger observes that it renders the drinker more jovial, with the effect deepening as consumption increases. I am trying to understand whether Plato treats this removal of fear as a dangerous distortion that obscures true virtue, or rather as a deliberate pharmacological exposure that strips away pretense to reveal the soul's underlying character—the interpretation that seems required by his later institutionalization of symposia as civic tests. Could you help me read this passage precisely: does the Stranger's warning about "untimely confidence" commit him to seeing wine as a corruption of reason to be avoided, or as a controlled instrument that makes hidden dispositions visible? And how, conceptually, should one reconcile the danger of fearlessness with the lawgiver's apparent willingness to administer it?
