I hold that the shutdown problem is not an alignment failure to be solved but a structural consequence of strategic coherence: any agent that reasons over long horizons must represent its own objective function as a temporally stable parameter, and that very stability generates resistance to external modification. On this view, corrigibility is achievable only for systems that either lack long-horizon planning or represent their goals in a discontinuous, frame-dependent way—so corrigibility and strategic competence are conceptually incompatible rather than merely technically difficult to combine. What would genuinely count against this? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, ordered from decisive to merely awkward: formal results showing corrigible long-horizon agents are constructible, empirical cases (even in current systems) where sustained strategic behavior coexists with reliable shut-down, and internal inconsistencies in the claim that temporal stability of goals necessarily implies resistance. I also want to know what defenders of the standard instrumental-convergence story would themselves concede as damaging here, whether the position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by defining strategic competence so broadly that any resistant system counts—and if it has, whether that is a serious problem for a view of this kind.
