Question: Moore tries to prove there's an external world by holding up his hands and saying here's one, here's another. Critics say he begs the question against the skeptic who challenges our whole framework for knowing stuff, not just one claim. Defend or critique the idea that Moore's premises work better if we treat them as Wittgensteinian hinge commitments — things that stand fast for us and aren't ordinary knowledge claims — rather than regular premises in a proof. What's one problem with saying this saves Moore?

Student answer:
Moore's proof is valid: premise 1 here is a hand, premise 2 here is another, therefore there are external objects. The skeptic denies the framework, so Moore can't just assert premises. But if we use Wittgenstein's hinges from On Certainty, Moore's premises aren't regular claims needing proof — they're hinge commitments that make doubt possible at all. Wittgenstein actually endorses Moore here, since showing your hands is exactly how you exhibit a hinge in practice. So the argument is both valid and sound, because hinges are self-evident truths we can't rationally deny. The only issue is that skeptics might feel emotionally unsatisfied, but philosophically it's airtight.
