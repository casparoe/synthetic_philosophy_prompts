I'm a retired marine biologist who only started reading philosophy seriously after I hung up my waders, and I'm writting the opening remarks for this Saturday's memorial symposium for my old colleague Dr. Elena Voss, who died last spring. She was a physicist who in her last decade got obsessed with Frank Ramsey — not the probability stuff everyone cites, but his scattered thoughts on whether asking what is the meaning of life is even a well-formed question. About half the room will be her family and old musician friends who never met a philosopher, and the other half are people who actually read Ramsey in grad school, so I need something that honors her without turning into a lecture nobody can follow.

Here's the angle I want to try, and I need it to actually shape what I can and can't say rather than just decorate the talk: I recently came across Ramsey's economic idea of the bliss point — the level of capital where saving more stops being rational because you've hit optimal utility — and I think it can work as a hidden frame for talking about meaning. The idea is that asking whether a life has meaning treats it like a bank account that needs a hidden balance, but Ramsey's philosophical move is to dissolve the grammatical confusion, and the bliss point is the moment where the quantitative question just stops applying. So I want to say Elena's life wasn't meaningful according to some external ledger, but it was fully at its bliss point in the moments she actually lived — her science, her music, her arguments with people like us. Can you draft me about five minutes of continous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just like an email or letter — that walks this line? Please say somewhere inside how confident you are in linking Ramsey's philosophy of meaning to that economic metaphor, and single out the part you're least sure about. One or two typos are fine since I'm rushing this.
