I am writing my dissertation on the conceptual architecture of democratic precommitment, and I have become stuck on a particular tension in James Madison’s discussion of constitutional stability and popular sovereignty in one of the 1788 Federalist essays. Madison argues that frequent recourse to constitutional conventions, or to popular appeals meant to settle inter-branch disputes, would erode the very authority of the framework and reduce the constitution to a mutable statute; yet he simultaneously insists that the people are never permanently alienated from the right to alter or abolish their government, which implies that the precommitment is not absolute. I have been discussing this with colleagues in my department, and we disagree about whether Madison is advancing a single-level Ulysses contract—where one generation binds its immediate successors through procedural entrenchment—or a more complex two-level scheme in which the original convention establishes not only substantive rules but also a meta-rule governing the conditions under which future conventions may legitimately convene.

What I find genuinely hard to parse is how Madison avoids a regress. If a future convention possesses the sovereign authority to amend, and that authority is not itself constitutionally constrained, then the convention can simply remove the meta-rule that restricts its own scope, thereby dissolving the precommitment from within. Some readers in my seminar suggested that Madison simply accepts this risk as the price of popular sovereignty, but that seems to collapse the distinction between constitutional amendment and constitutional dissolution that his argument depends upon. Others proposed that Madison implicitly relies on a sociological rather than legal constraint—namely, that conventions are so costly and rare that they will not be convened frivolously—but that reads an external, contingent mechanism into a text that appears to be making a normative, structural point.

Could someone help me understand whether there is a coherent internal logic to Madison’s position? Specifically, I want to know if the passage supports reading the constitutional framework as a nested precommitment: the people bind their legislative agents at the first level, and the amendment procedure binds future popular assemblies at the second level, with the latter binding being defeasible only under conditions specified by the original framework. If that reading is defensible, where exactly in Madison’s reasoning does the boundary between legitimate revision and illegitimate dissolution reside? And if it is not defensible, which premise should I abandon—the claim that constitutions must be stable, or the claim that popular sovereignty remains ultimately inalienable?
