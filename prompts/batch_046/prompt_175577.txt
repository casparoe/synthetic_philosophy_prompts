Having recently retired from experimental physics, I have been working through puzzles in decision theory, particularly the parable in which a man meets Death in Damascus, is told he will be sought in that city, flees to Aleppo, and finds Death waiting there. I keep noticing a structural feature in this story and in related constructions—such as versions where the agent introduces a random device to choose between Damascus and Aleppo, yet the outcome remains correlated with Death’s location. The feature is this: the agent’s choice, or the output of the mechanism producing it, does not merely predict the state of the world; rather, the world-state seems to be defined as whatever corresponds to that specific output, so that counterfactual reasoning about “what I would have done” collapses into reasoning about which world-state was already fixed. This is not an ordinary empirical correlation, nor is it physical backward causation, nor is it the standard predictor-agent setup of Newcomb’s problem, because the link is built into the framing conditions of the scenario itself rather than into an external predictor. Does this configuration have an established name—perhaps in the literature on evidential reasoning, self-locating belief, or decision-theoretic paradoxes? If not, I would like a precise term and definition that distinguishes it from evidential correlation, self-fulfilling prophecy, causal dependency, and standard Newcomb-style problems, together with an assessment of whether it marks a genuine paradox or merely an artifact of how the scenario is specified.
