I'm currently working through the classical source-critical tradition—Herodotus's willingness to record hearsay with explicit attribution and cross-cultural comparison, and Thucydides's stricter rejection of myth and secondhand report in favor of direct eyewitness testimony subjected to rigorous cross-examination and suspicion of bias and embellishment. To test how far these categories travel, I want to apply them not to an ancient text but to a modern archival controversy: the 1980s denialist challenge to Anne Frank's diary, in which critics associated with Holocaust denial pointed to alleged ballpoint-pen annotations—an anachronism, since ballpoints were not widely available until after 1951—as proof of postwar fabrication, while a Dutch state forensic investigation in the mid-1980s subsequently examined the original notebooks, the rewritten drafts, and Otto Frank's edited publication, confirming that the ink and paper were consistent with the 1942–1944 period and that the handwriting matched known samples of Anne's.

Here is the conceptual framing I'm trying to sharpen: a distinction between what I will call material autopsy—the physical provenance, material consistency, and forensic audit of the artifact itself—and narrative testimony—the evaluation of the account's internal coherence, its corroboration with external records such as the annex's occupancy logs, and the legitimacy of editorial mediation. The denialists seemed to collapse these into one standard, treating any editorial change or modern annotation as corruption of the eyewitness chain, while defenders treated forensic material verification as sufficient to settle narrative questions about authenticity. Analyze the episode through both Herodotean and Thucydidean lenses, but be disciplined about the mapping. For each thinker: does material autopsy correspond to direct observation, or does it fall outside his framework because he had no concept of forensic science? How should each view treat Otto Frank's posthumous editing—does Herodotus's attribution of transmitted sources make editorial selection legitimate, or does Thucydides's demand for unmediated eyewitness testimony render any editorial hand a fatal corruption? Where does the theory explain the denialists' move, perhaps as a misapplied Thucydidean suspicion of bias, and where does it miss the controversy entirely because the dispute turned on technological anachronism rather than on rhetorical embellishment? And what does the episode reveal about whether ancient source criticism can be extended to modern personal archives without generating category errors? Be explicit about where the analogy is forced, where it is silent, and whether any other conceptual model does better at explaining why the forensic resolution satisfied some observers and not others.
