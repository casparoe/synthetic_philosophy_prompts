Here's an argument my study group constructed linking the denial of the Cartesian theater to an intolerable moral conclusion, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) The Cartesian theater model posits a single functional locus in which disparate cognitive streams are integrated into a unified phenomenal display.
(2) Dennett's multiple drafts model denies any such privileged locus; there are only parallel, competing narrative fragments, and what counts as the conscious experience depends on which draft is accessed by a subsequent probe or report.
(3) If there is no locus of integration, then in the absence of a probing mechanism that elicits a specific draft, there is no determinate fact about what a subject is experiencing at time t.
(4) Moral concern for suffering tracks the occurrence of genuine experienced suffering, not merely the disposition to produce a report.
(5) But on the multiple drafts view, an unprobed painful stimulus produces only a distributed neural pattern that might yield different reports under different probes; without being probed, it does not constitute a determinate suffering-state.
(6) Therefore, if the multiple drafts model is correct, we lack reason to be morally concerned about unreported suffering in non-verbal animals, pre-linguistic infants, or unresponsive patients.
(7) That is clearly false: moral status should not depend on the presence of a reporting mechanism.
(8) So we must reject (2) and retain a version of the Cartesian theater.

We want to keep (2) and reject (7), but we need to know which intermediate step fails. Is the slide from (3) to (5) illegitimate—perhaps because distributed patterns can still ground determinate suffering without being probed—or does the error lie in (4)'s assumption that moral concern requires a fully determinate phenomenal fact? Identify the weakest link, explain why it initially seemed plausible, and say what abandoning it costs for the coherence of the multiple drafts account.
