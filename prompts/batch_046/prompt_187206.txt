I have been considering whether Hobsbawm's analysis of invented traditions and Anderson's account of imagined communities apply only to modern nations or to collective identity as such. If these frameworks establish that large-scale shared belonging requires print-capitalism and deliberately constructed rituals, then it seems that any widespread political identity prior to industrial modernity—say, Romanitas in late antiquity or a Frankish communal consciousness in the Carolingian era—must be either a retrospective projection by historians or a purely local kinship network lacking collective self-awareness. How, then, should we classify the origin narratives and ritualized legal practices documented in pre-literate societies that appear to presuppose a shared identity beyond face-to-face interaction, yet lack both bureaucratic state apparatus and mass literacy? Must we dissolve such cases into either "nation" or "tribe," or is there a coherent intermediate conceptual space that the Hobsbawm-Anderson framework fails to capture?
