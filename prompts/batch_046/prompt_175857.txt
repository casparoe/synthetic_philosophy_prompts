After being presented with an investment instrument whose payout doubles with each successive period of failure but whose probability of failure also halves—so that the classical expected value diverges while the premium requested equals my total liquid savings—I find the St. Petersburg paradox has become personally urgent rather than abstract. I want to close a seminar discussion with a Petrarchan sonnet: an octave with rhyme scheme ABBAABBA, a volta at line 9, and a sestet (CDECDE or CDCDCD) that resolves into a final couplet. The octave should build the ensemble-averaging argument precisely: the fair coin, the exponentially decaying probabilities, the exponentially growing stakes, the infinite sum that refuses closure. The volta should turn on the recognition that a human biography is not an ensemble but a single time-path—what one might call a time-averaged trajectory—so the infinite expectation, though mathematically correct, never touches one actual life. The sestet should hold both conclusions in suspension without collapsing them: the mathematics demands infinite willingness to pay, yet the lived constraint of a single existence refuses it. The closing couplet should rhyme the concept of the infinite against the concept of the singular, leaving the tension named but unresolved. Please let the form carry the philosophy: the octave’s enclosed rhymes should feel like accumulation without escape, and the volta’s structural break should mirror the shift from statistical population to individual biography. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of probability or decision theory recognizes both the classical paradox and the time-averaging critique, but it should read first as one person’s poem about an impossible choice, not as a versified lecture. No citations, no references to specific texts, and no resolution that pretends the paradox is solved—only the tension, left open in the rhyme.
