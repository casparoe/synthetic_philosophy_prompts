Two study partners, K and L, were arguing about whether Darwin’s reasoning about natural selection and Turing’s reasoning in the imitation game share the same logical structure—sometimes described as a competence-without-comprehension inversion.

K began: Darwin explains biological design without attributing comprehension to nature. His analogy to artificial breeding is just that—an analogy. Once cumulative variation with selection can produce functional complexity, the breeder’s understanding was never part of the explanation. So Darwin’s inversion is purely eliminative: he removes a mental prerequisite that was never conceptually necessary.

L replied: That ignores the directional asymmetry. In artificial selection, the breeder’s intentions determine which traits are preserved; the competence lies in the selector, not the selected. Darwin transfers the outcome—adaptation—to nature but removes the selector’s mind entirely. Turing does the opposite: he transfers mental attribution—intelligence—to a machine but removes any internal access to understanding. One eliminates the agent from the process; the other adds an attribution despite a missing mechanism. They are not parallel inversions but inverse directions.

K countered: But suppose a mechanized breeder—an algorithm that optimizes phenotypes without understanding—replaced the human breeder. The result would be identical. That shows comprehension was never conceptually necessary to artificial selection either. So Darwin and Turing are making the same move: both demonstrate that a functional result (design, intelligent behavior) can be sustained by a process lacking the mental properties traditionally assumed essential. The only difference is which mental property gets eliminated.

L then said: If that is right, then Darwin hasn’t merely eliminated comprehension from nature; he has shown that comprehension wasn’t doing explanatory work in breeding either. But that undermines the very analogy that made natural selection intelligible—artificial selection derives its evidential force precisely from our familiarity with intentional selectors. Strip that away, and Darwin’s argument collapses into the bare claim that complex order can arise from random variation, which was exactly what his critics found implausible.

K closed: The critics’ implausibility is a psychological reaction, not a logical flaw. Darwin’s point is that the mechanism works regardless of whether anyone understands it. Turing’s point is that intelligent behavior can be produced regardless of internal comprehension. Both replace mentalistic prerequisites with functional stability.
