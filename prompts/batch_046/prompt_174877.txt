I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about why debates over the demarcation of pseudohistory—specifically Fomenko’s New Chronology and the ancient-astronaut narrative—seem permanently stalled despite widespread agreement on the underlying descriptive claims. We both accept that Fomenko uses statistical analyses of textual parallels to collapse ancient and medieval chronologies into a compressed timeline, and that ancient-astronaut proponents treat technological anachronisms in archaeological contexts as evidence for extraterrestrial engineering; we also agree that mainstream historiography rejects both on grounds of consilience with material culture and independent dating methods. Yet after hours of discussion we had not moved an inch. My friend insists the disagreement is purely verbal: historians and pseudohistorians are simply operating with different implicit rules for the word evidence—one group demands integration across independent sources, the other permits ad hoc immunization against counter-evidence—so no single epistemic standard is actually being violated, only different language games are being played. I offered a competing hypothesis. I suggested that beneath the rhetoric lies a substantive split over what I want to call chronological incommensurability avoidance—the principle that a responsible historical framework must not arbitrarily reconcile two independent chronological systems without external mediation. Mainstream practice respects this constraint; Fomenko systematically violates it but conceals the violation behind formal statistical apparatus, while ancient-astronaut theories violate it by treating explanatory gaps as proof of alien presence. At the same time, I worry we may be answering different questions under a single heading: my friend seems to be asking what social or disciplinary function the label pseudohistory performs, while I am asking what epistemic feature makes a chronological claim historically responsible. I therefore want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication of Fomenko or von Däniken. Is the stalemate verbal, substantive about standards of evidence, hidden behind a dispute over which anomalies count as legitimate data, or rooted in bedrock intuitions about explanatory responsibility that neither side can argue for? Are we genuinely addressing one question or two distinct ones—one sociological, one epistemological—disguised by shared vocabulary? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a precise conceptual distinction, a formal criterion for permissible chronological integration, a reframing of the question itself, or is the impasse inevitable and why?
