I am preparing the interviewer script for a recorded postgraduate seminar in philosophy of economics. My guest is an invented but philosophically serious interlocutor—Dr. Elena Voss—who defends a reconstructed version of the Milgrom-Stokey no-trade result applied to epistemic states rather than to financial assets. Her core claim is that, in a prediction market with genuinely common priors and common knowledge of rationality, sustained price divergence can indicate only one of two things: either hidden private information, or a failure of full epistemic rationality. To prevent her view from collapsing into a trivial scepticism about disagreement, she introduces a niche amendment she calls “transparent cognitive liquidity constraints”: traders may rationally diverge in their revealed probabilities if they share common knowledge of one another’s bounded reasoning budgets, and if those budgets are themselves priced into the market mechanism rather than treated as hidden variables. She insists that this preserves the normative bite of the no-trade result without reducing every persistent disagreement to irrationality.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her merely to restate this position. I want at least one question on each of the following: (a) whether her reinterpretation of common priors as epistemic rather than merely doxastic commits her to an implausible form of logical omniscience; (b) how her “transparent cognitive liquidity constraints” differ in kind from standard behavioural-finance explanations of market anomalies, and whether the transparency conditions she requires could ever be satisfied in actual market design; (c) what her view implies for the epistemology of democratic deliberation if we model citizens as traders in a policy-prediction market with shared reasoning budgets; and (d) whether the normative force of the no-trade theorem survives if we relax the assumption that rationality is common knowledge rather than merely common belief.

For each question, include a brief follow-up keyed specifically to the answer she is most likely to give—namely, the reply that appeals either to the distinction between epistemic and instrumental rationality, or to the claim that transparency is an idealisation rather than an empirical prediction.
