Two philosophers, M and K, are arguing over whether normative reasons can exist without any reasoners—what K calls "free-floating rationales." M defends agent-dependence: a reason to φ is always a reason for some agent to φ, so if every conscious being vanished, reasons would vanish with them, leaving only bare causal processes. K defends objectivity: reasons are constituted by value-facts or functional norms that do not require recognition. Their exchange proceeds as follows.

(1) M offers the extinction case: if all sentient life disappeared tomorrow, there would still be decay, radiation, and gravity, but no reasons. The fact that a rock falls is not a reason for anything; it is just physics. Therefore reasons require reasoners.

(2) K replies with the evolutionary precursor case: long before conscious agents existed, there was a normative fact that certain bacteria had reason to avoid thermal vents—not because anyone told them, but because the damage to replication constituted an objective failure condition. The reason existed; only the recognition was missing.

(3) M counters that K conflates explanatory reasons ("the reason the bacteria moved was heat damage") with normative reasons ("the bacteria had reason to move"). The first needs no mind; the second does, because normativity is essentially guidance for an agent. Without an agent to guide, there is no norm, only damage.

(4) K introduces a structural analogy: mathematical truths do not need mathematicians to be true. Similarly, value-facts—that suffering is bad, that flourishing is good—do not need valuers. A reason is just the normative upshot of such a value-fact, and it persists as long as the value-fact does, regardless of whether anyone registers it.

(5) M responds that this collapses the distinction between "it is good that X happens" and "there is a reason for X to happen." Even if suffering is bad in a mind-independent sense, for it to generate a reason—something that genuinely counts in favor of action—there must be an action, hence an actor. A free-floating reason with no possible agent is like a debt with no debtor: grammatically puzzling.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the five moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify your rating. Then say whether either party is missing a distinction or middle position—perhaps between explanatory and normative reasons, or between value-facts and action-guiding considerations—that could dissolve the stalemate without reducing reasons either to mere psychology or to ungrounded objective demands.
