I am a high school senior enrolled in an advanced philosophy-of-language seminar, and for my capstone project I have been assigned to act as a junior field linguist documenting a hypothetical isolated language—call it K—across a twelve-week simulated ethnography with no bilingual informants available. Each week I receive behavioral transcripts—gestures, assent and dissent patterns, and contextual usage notes—and I must update a provisional translation manual; my instructor expects me to treat Quine’s thesis on radical translation not as a skeptical dead end but as an operational framework I apply repeatedly. I need you to convert that thesis into a concrete, reusable decision procedure—essentially an ordered checklist or flowchart rendered in prose—that I can execute in about twenty minutes for each new lexical entry, relying solely on observable behavioral dispositions rather than on any hidden mental state I might imagine.

The procedure should begin with data collection and then move through ordered stages that test analytic hypotheses, apply a principle of charity constrained by ontological parsimony, and explicitly flag every node where judgment is irreducible rather than algorithmic. Because I will use this under weekly deadlines, please build in explicit hard-case handling: for competing ontological mappings—such as when an ostensive cue could support a concrete-object reading, an undetached-part reading, or a temporal-stage reading—and for evidential or modal particles that lack direct perceptual correlates, especially in restricted contexts like dream reports. Then apply the full procedure to three worked cases: first, a basic ostensive entry where the manual initially seems straightforward; second, a case where the steps force me to adopt an ontologically strange translation—rendering a recurring term as referring to momentary time-slices rather than enduring entities—despite my reluctance; and third, a boundary case involving a ritual evidential marker used only in dream-reporting contexts, where behavioral evidence is sparse and the procedure must either suspend judgment or commit to an interpretation I will find uncomfortable. In each application, number the steps executed, identify the judgment-call nodes, and state the manual’s provisional entry.

I discussed an early draft with classmates and we could not agree on whether charity should override ontological simplicity when interpretive stakes are low; please preserve that tension as an explicit branching point rather than resolving it by fiat. The deliverable must also be honest about its own limits: tell me what the procedure cannot settle—whether it can ever determine a uniquely correct ontology, for example—where it will systematically misfire if I treat it as more authoritative than a heuristic, and which conditions of data scarcity lie entirely outside its scope. Please keep the format practical enough that a student without formal logic training can run it under time pressure, but do not simplify away the conceptual distinctions that actually do the work.
