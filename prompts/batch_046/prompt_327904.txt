I serve on a municipal planning ethics review board that convenes twice monthly to evaluate development and budget proposals in which genuinely different kinds of goods—historical preservation, ecological integrity, affordable housing, public health—come into direct conflict, and we are required to produce documented recommendations within about twenty minutes per case for review by elected officials who have no philosophical training. Because proponents have begun to invoke the idea that certain values are "incommensurable" in order to claim that no rational comparison is possible, I need you to construct a reusable decision procedure, presented entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, that my colleagues and I can run repeatedly to determine whether a given conflict involves values that are genuinely incommensurable in the philosophical sense—where comparison is blocked by the structure of the values themselves rather than by ignorance, difficulty, or strategic refusal—or whether the values can be rationally related through weighing, trumping, or the more subtle relation of being "on a par," in which options are comparable without being ranked as equal, better, or worse. The procedure should proceed in strict order: first, ask whether the competing values can be subsumed under a single covering value that permits direct comparison, and specify what evidence would be sufficient to establish this; second, ask whether one value trumps the other regardless of magnitude within the relevant domain; third, ask whether the values belong to domains that make cross-domain comparison conceptually inappropriate rather than merely difficult; and fourth, ask whether they stand in a relation of parity that permits rational choice without aggregation into a common scale. At each stage you must state what counts as a sufficient answer, what documentation the board should require from proponents, and precisely where the procedure stops and transfers authority to democratic judgment rather than philosophical analysis, being explicit about which steps are genuinely procedural and which are judgment calls disguised in procedural language. Then apply the procedure to two concrete proposals: the demolition of a nineteenth-century mill district to build climate-resilient affordable housing, where preservationists claim that aesthetic and historical value is incommensurable with housing need; and the allocation of a fixed municipal health budget between expanding palliative care for elderly residents and funding a pediatric gene-therapy trial, where advocates on both sides claim the values are incomparable. You must include at least one application in which the procedure yields an answer that one constituency will find unwelcome—perhaps that the values are in fact commensurable and must be weighed directly against one another, or conversely that they are genuinely on a par and therefore the decision must be made without any rational resolution that favors one side. Lead with the bottom line in your opening paragraph: summarize in continuous prose what the procedure actually delivers, what it explicitly cannot settle, and its specific failure modes when small groups invoke incommensurability strategically to block any decision. Write the entire response as a formal letter or memo, using only continuous paragraphs, with no structural formatting of any kind.
