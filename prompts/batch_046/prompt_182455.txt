In my graduate seminar on Sartre, a debate has stalled that I find genuinely frustrating. The question is whether adopting a stable, long-term self-conception—for example, identifying oneself as “someone who values intellectual honesty above social harmony”—necessarily constitutes bad faith. One side argues that any such identification fixes my essence and denies my radical freedom to become otherwise at any moment; they invoke what they call the “transparency condition,” according to which an authentic self-ascription must be accompanied by a live, simultaneous awareness that I could revoke it in the very next instant. The other side replies that Sartre’s notion of an authentic project allows me to freely commit to a durable orientation without self-deception, provided I do not treat that orientation as a brute given on a par with my height or my past. Both sides agree on the core texts discussing facticity and transcendence, and both treat radical freedom as non-negotiable. Yet after a full semester of discussion, nobody has moved an inch.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really an exegetical dispute about Sartre at all, but a hidden normative disagreement about whether personal continuity is a precondition for moral agency or an obstacle to it. Is that correct, or is it instead a verbal dispute about the word “project” versus “essence”? Could there be an empirical question—perhaps from developmental psychology—about how people actually maintain self-conceptions over time that is secretly doing the work? Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says, not by surveying interpretations, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a phenomenological example, a formal model of commitment, or an honest argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
