Within the predictive-processing and free-energy-principle literature, several conceptual tensions have emerged that go beyond straightforward empirical testing: whether the framework’s account of action—via active inference and epistemic foraging—genuinely distinguishes biological agency from optimal control theory, or merely redescribes exploration bonuses in information-theoretic language; whether precision-weighted prediction errors commit the view to a representationalist ontology that conflicts with enactivist interpretations of Markov blankets and self-organizing dynamics; whether the free energy principle’s universal scope—applying to any system that maintains its own existence—renders it unfalsifiable and therefore metaphysical rather than scientific; and whether the dissolution of action into “self-evidencing” dynamics undermines normative accounts of autonomy, or whether a reconstructed, normative conception of agency can be recovered from within the framework.

Assume you are advising a small philosophy-of-science research group that can pursue only two of these lines over the next three years, with no empirical laboratory work—only conceptual analysis, formal modeling, and critical engagement with existing theoretical literature. Which two should they prioritize, and in what order? Defend the ranking by specifying: (a) which questions are upstream of the others, in the sense that settling them would reframe or dissolve the remaining debates rather than merely adding to them; (b) which are tractable for philosophers without direct access to neurophysiological or clinical data; (c) what specific conceptual result or theoretical move over the next few years would cause you to revise the priority; and (d) which lines you are deprioritizing for this particular group, with an explanation of why they matter less given these aims and constraints—not merely that they remain unresolved.

Your deliverable should be a defended agenda with explicit ordering criteria, not a survey of the field and not a list of readings or citations. Avoid bibliographic recommendations.
