I'm working through an argument about Leibniz's moral philosophy that seems to dissolve the distinction between sin and virtue by pushing his metaphysical commitments too far. The steps run as follows:

(1) For Leibniz, moral truths are necessary truths of reason: they depend on the rational order that God, as perfect intellect, necessarily recognizes and wills.
(2) God actualizes only that world which maximizes overall perfection; any world containing a genuinely wrong action—understood as an action that violates rational moral order—would be less perfect than an otherwise identical world lacking it.
(3) Because every substance contains a complete concept that fixes all its predicates, and because all substances are interconnected by the principle of sufficient reason, removing one wrong action from the actual world would alter the complete concepts of every other substance and thus produce an entirely different world.
(4) Therefore, the comparison in (2) is impossible: there is no accessible possible world that is "the actual world minus this sin" with everything else held constant.
(5) Consequently, for any action that actually occurs, there is no available world in which that specific action is absent but the same overall perfection is maintained.
(6) So every actual action is included in the best possible world not accidentally, but as a necessary component of the optimal arrangement—meaning no actual action can be genuinely wrong.
(7) This yields the conclusion that moral blame is always mistaken, since blame presupposes that the agent could have performed an action that was both available within the best world and morally superior; but if every actual choice is fixed by the optimal whole, no such alternative was genuinely available.
(8) The result seems absurd—Leibniz plainly wants to preserve both divine justice and human accountability for sin—yet each premise sounds like something he should accept.

Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked defensible given Leibniz's commitments to rational moral order, complete concepts, and universal connection, and then lay out what the two main interpretive responses—one that saves contingency by restricting the scope of necessary moral truths to general principles rather than particular choices, and one that saves blame by treating wrong actions as locally defective but globally necessary—each have to concede or reconstruct in order to avoid the conclusion.
