Rather than opening with a lecture on the concept, please design a classroom simulation—specifying roles, round-by-round rules, materials, and a point-based payoff scheme that requires no real money—through which participants must solve a hidden classification problem by generating variants and selecting among them purely on output success, followed by a round in which one participant mechanically executes the winning strategy without being permitted to interpret it. Crucially, build in a hidden correlation—an irrelevant feature that correlates with success, analogous to what evolutionary biologists call a spandrel—so that the group discovers that the most effective rule succeeds for reasons no one understands and that no deliberate reasoner proposed. The rules should be arranged so that the participants encounter the inversion of uncomprehending competence before the facilitator names it.

Please include a precise timeline, a debrief that maps each stage onto Darwinian natural selection and Turing-machine execution respectively, and an honest account of which feature of the philosophical idea—such as the syntactic formality of computation or the deep temporal scale of evolutionary adaptation—the activity necessarily fails to reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Also tell me what is likely to go wrong the first time the simulation is run and how to handle it.
