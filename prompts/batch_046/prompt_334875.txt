I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy, but recently I have been trying to think through whether normative reasons can exist independently of anyone who holds them — what I understand is sometimes discussed under the heading of free-floating rationales. I tried to formalize my intuition into an argument, and now I am stuck because the conclusion seems to spread normativity across the physical world in a way that feels absurd. Here is the chain, with each step numbered: (1) Every reason must have a bearer — there must be some entity for whom the reason counts. (2) If a bearer has no mental architecture at all — no beliefs, no desires, no representational states — then attributing a reason to it is like invoking an exception handler for a function that was never called; there is no internal structure for the reason to engage with. (3) So every genuine reason is ultimately anchored in the mental life of some reasoner. (4) But consider the direct reason not to cause unnecessary suffering: this applies to an animal that suffers, not merely derivatively through a human observer's preferences. (5) If that reason were anchored in human reasoners, then the animal's suffering would matter only because we care, which seems wrong — the suffering itself seems to generate the normative weight. (6) Therefore, that reason binds anyone who can affect the suffering regardless of their own mental states, meaning it is not anchored in any particular reasoner's psychology. (7) Given (3), the only way to avoid contradiction is to treat this as a free-floating rationale — a normative fact that exists independently of all minds. (8) But if rationales can float free of reasoners entirely, then they apply wherever their conditions are met, with no restriction to agents. So there is a reason for this glacier not to melt, for that differential equation to preserve its symmetry, and for a black hole not to evaporate, since each outcome is structurally 'better' in the relevant sense. (9) That is clearly absurd; normativity is not a causal force scattered through physics. (10) So the argument proves too much: either I deny that suffering carries direct weight, or I accept cosmic normativity. I want to keep (4) and (6) if possible, and I would prefer not to give up (1). Where does the argument actually fail? Please identify the weakest step, explain why it initially looked plausible, and describe what each major alternative — rejecting that step versus accepting the absurd conclusion — costs conceptually. Please write your response in American English.
