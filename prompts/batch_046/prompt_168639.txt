I recently received the following summary of heterophenomenology from a philosopher of science colleague who is preparing a syllabus on consciousness studies. She writes: Heterophenomenology treats all first-person reports about experience as texts to be interpreted through the intentional stance, not as direct windows onto phenomenology. Because Dennett rejects any Cartesian theater in which experience comes together, the method entails that there is no further fact—no intrinsic phenomenal property—beyond what is captured by a subject's sincere verbal dispositions. Consequently, heterophenomenology dissolves the hard problem: if a system can be interpreted as sincerely reporting its experiences, then it is conscious, and neuroscience need only explain the mechanisms generating those reports, not any accompanying inner glow. This means first-person data are not privileged evidence but merely one more behavioral stream, and the method is therefore fully compatible with a robust phenomenal realism about artificial systems that produce analogous reports. Moreover, since interpretation involves a Quinean radical translation of phenomenal vocabulary, there is no uniquely correct reading of any report, so consciousness attribution is always underdetermined and conventional. I am inclined to think she has blurred the line between methodological neutrality about phenomenal properties and an outright metaphysical denial of them, and that she has smuggled in both a functionalist criterion for artificial consciousness and an indeterminacy thesis that Dennett's framework does not obviously license. But I want a committed assessment, not an inventory of readings. Is this summary fair? Identify precisely which claims are accurate to Dennett's position, which conflate heterophenomenology with eliminativism, functionalism, or Quinean semantic indeterminacy, and whether her inferences about artificial consciousness and interpretive underdetermination actually follow. Take a definite stance on whether the summary is systematically misleading, defend that verdict, and say what a corrected account of heterophenomenology's methodological commitments would actually require.
