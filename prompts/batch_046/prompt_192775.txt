I am trying to locate the weak point in an argument about trans-generational collective guilt that uses Jaspers’s fourfold distinction—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—without reducing it to metaphor or symbolic atonement. The argument runs as follows. (1) If a state is a diachronic institution with legal and bureaucratic continuity, then the obligations and liabilities it acquires can survive the complete replacement of its membership, just as sovereign debt survives a change of government. (2) Jaspers assigns political guilt to citizens qua members of the state for actions performed in their name, regardless of personal involvement; citizenship is transmitted across generations by law and socialization, so present citizens occupy the same institutional role vis-à-vis past actions as the citizens who were present. (3) Therefore, political guilt is inherited in exactly the same structural sense that treaty obligations are inherited: the collective subject that incurred it persists. (4) But guilt is either culpable blame or it is not; if it is culpable blame, then descendants are being blamed for acts they could not have prevented, violating moral individualism; if it is not culpable blame, then “political guilt” is just a name for distributive liability, and Jaspers’s normative demand for collective reckoning loses its distinctive moral force. (5) The same problem arises for metaphysical guilt: if this guilt attaches to anyone who failed to prevent evil, and historical distance does not eliminate the counterfactual possibility that a present agent could have intervened—since the past is modally fixed but the failure is evaluated from a timeless moral standpoint—then every living person bears metaphysical guilt for every past atrocity they did not stop. (6) This renders metaphysical guilt either universal and therefore morally empty, or arbitrarily bounded by temporal proximity, which makes the boundary ad hoc. (7) So we face a dilemma: either we accept genuinely trans-generational, non-metaphorical collective guilt and abandon the principle that moral responsibility requires personal agency, or we reject such guilt and thereby undermine Jaspers’s entire apparatus for post-conflict reckoning, since both political and metaphysical guilt depend on collective subjects persisting across generations. (8) Because moral individualism is non-negotiable, the framework is self-undermining.

Which step should I reject? I want to know whether the flaw is in the analogy between sovereign debt and guilt (steps 1–3), in the false dichotomy between culpability and liability (step 4), in the modal slide that makes metaphysical guilt universal (steps 5–6), or in the framing of the dilemma itself (steps 7–8). For each option, explain why that step looked initially plausible, what giving it up costs for someone who wants to preserve Jaspers’s distinctions, and which standard responses—corporate-personhood theories, four-dimensionalist accounts of collective agents, or a strict separation of liability from blame—actually solve the problem rather than rename it. If you think the argument is valid and the real cost is abandoning one of the premises, say which premise is least expensive to surrender and what that surrender implies for collective responsibility across generations.
