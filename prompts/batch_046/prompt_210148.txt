So in our strategic-studies seminar there's this argument about Munich, Vietnam, and the Thucydides trap that just won't die. One camp says analogies are indispensible because structural dynamics — commitment problems, misperception spirals, rising-power anxiety — actually recur, and if you ignore Munich in '38 you miss the mechanism that produces appeasement. The other camp says analogies are just narrative isomorphism: you can always map Vietnam quagmire dynamics or Peloponnesian patterns onto a new crisis if you ignore the instituional details, so they have zero predictive content and only serve rhetorical purposes. We've been stuck on this for years — same papers, same rebuttals, nobody conceding anything. My hunch is that the stall isn't about missing data but about hidden standards of evidence: the analogists are doing process-tracing (looking for similar causal mechanisms) while the skeptics are demanding covering-law generalizations or statistical base-rates, and neither side admits which standard they're using. But maybe its worse — maybe they're answering different questions, one about explanation and one about prediction, under the same word 'analogy'. What's the actual structure of the disagreement here? Is it verbal, substantive, or bedrock intuition about what history is for? And if it's substantive, what kind of distinction or result — formal, empirical, conceptual — would actually move the needle instead of just generating another round of examples?
