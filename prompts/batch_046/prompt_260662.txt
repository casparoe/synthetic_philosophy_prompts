I have been trying to work out my own view on what makes a condition count as a disease, and I want to know whether my judgments form a coherent pattern or whether I must abandon some of them. Here are my verdicts. Normal aging-related cognitive slowing is not a disease; it is simply a life stage. Type 1 diabetes is clearly a disease. Severe congenital deafness in a community of fluent signers is not a disease. The identical deafness in a non-signing community, where it produces profound isolation and dependency, is also not a disease, though the harm is far greater. Pregnancy, despite its physiological stress, risk, and deviation from a non-pregnant baseline, is not a disease. A persistent state of severely low motivation that prevents all employment is a disease when it reflects a specific neurological dysfunction, but not when it reflects a reasoned, values-based rejection of available work. I feel genuine ambivalence about obesity in an environment of caloric abundance: part of me treats it as a metabolic disease, part of me as a predictable environmental adaptation. I feel the same ambivalence about opioid addiction, whether it began with a post-surgical prescription or with recreational use; in both instances I lean toward calling it a disease, but less confidently in the second.

There is one pair of verdicts I find especially difficult to reconcile. I think that dyscalculia — a specific, severe impairment in numerical cognition — remains a disease even if the affected person lives in a society with virtually no quantitative demands. But I do not think that an equally specific, equally severe inability to recognize faces is a disease if the person lives in a small, stable community where every individual is identified by voice and context. The two conditions seem structurally parallel — both are localized cognitive deficits — yet my judgments diverge.

I would also like to see any proposed principle tested against three cases I have not listed. First, a hypothetical state I will call adaptive melancholia: a persistent, painful low mood that functions exclusively to motivate necessary political resistance in an unjust society, with no other impairment and no response to standard treatment. Second, a genetic variant that renders a person unable to digest synthetic additives found only in modern processed foods, causing chronic inflammation that disappears entirely when the additives are removed. Third, a congenital state of permanently elevated empathic responsiveness that produces debilitating vicarious suffering but no other functional impairment.

Is there a single principle — naturalist, normativist, or hybrid — that delivers all of my verdicts without contrivance? If not, which should I overturn? Please state the best available principle precisely, identifying the specific case that motivates each clause, giving explicit verdicts on adaptive melancholia, synthetic-additive intolerance, and elevated empathy, and saying honestly which of my listed judgments it cannot accommodate.
