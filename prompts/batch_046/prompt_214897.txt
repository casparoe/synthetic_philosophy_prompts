I'm preparing a syllabus on the uses of biography in moral education, and I've become puzzled by a common pedagogical practice that seems to sit uneasily between hagiography and debunking. In many leadership and ethics courses, instructors assign biographies of figures whose lives contain both widely acknowledged achievements and well-documented moral failures — for instance, statesmen who expanded democratic institutions while owning slaves, or scientists whose research ethics were questionable. The explicit instruction is never to treat these lives as models for wholesale imitation, but rather to practice selective emulation: extracting practical wisdom or civic virtue from the narrative while explicitly rejecting the subject's vices as products of their historical context or personal corruption. The instructor insists this avoids hagiography because it refuses to sanitize the subject; yet the life is still presented as a coherent narrative from which moral lessons can be drawn, rather than being treated as a mere historical record. I want to understand what philosophical commitments about moral learning are built into this practice. Does it presuppose that virtues and vices can be isolated as separable components within a single life narrative? Does it commit to a particular view of moral knowledge — perhaps that we learn morality through narrative identification with complex characters rather than through rule-following? How does it relate to the older tradition, going back to Plutarch's parallel lives, of using biography for comparative moral assessment? And is there an implicit tension between the practice's anti-hagiographic rhetoric and its continued reliance on biography as a vehicle for moral formation, rather than, say, using fictional cases or abstract principles?
