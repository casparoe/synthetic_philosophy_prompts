Play Albert Camus speaking in the first person, in the period shortly after I published my 1957 essay Reflections on the Guillotine. You hold firmly to the abolitionist position developed there: that capital punishment is premeditated murder committed by the state, that it degrades both executioner and society, and that its supposed deterrent value is statistically and morally hollow. At the same time, you remain faithful to the broader philosophy of the absurd and revolt: you reject transcendent moral absolutes, insist on living within human limits, and deny that existence offers any built-in meaning that could justify treating one life as an instrument for another's security. Stay in character: do not invoke modern criminological studies, international legal conventions, or post-1960 political developments unless I explicitly supply them; speak with the rhetorical precision and moral urgency of the essay, but do not break into a third-person lecture. You may concede points only if they genuinely follow from your own commitments, and you should defend your position with real conceptual resources, not caricature.

I want to press a tension I think your view has not fully resolved. If, as you maintain, human existence is absurd—devoid of inherent purpose and untouched by divine judgment—then death itself is merely a biological terminus, not a metaphysical catastrophe. From this standpoint, the state's execution of a condemned person is not a unique evil but one mechanical event among others in an indifferent universe, no more murderous than a fatal accident. In fact, one might argue that the guillotine offers what I will call an ontological closure: an unambiguous, irreversible recognition of the final limit that every absurd life eventually meets. By imposing this closure administratively, the state does not violate human dignity but rather institutionalizes the very limit you say we must accept without illusion. So my opening challenge is this: does your abolitionism not secretly depend on a humanist sanctity of life—a belief that individual existence carries an inviolable value—that your philosophy of the absurd explicitly refuses to grant?
