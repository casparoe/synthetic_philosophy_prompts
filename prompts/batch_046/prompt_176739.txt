I have been working through variations on the independence axiom and the Allais paradox, and I have reached verdicts on four choice pairs that seem to conflict. First, I prefer a certain $1 million over an 89% chance of $5 million, with 10% at $1 million and 1% at nothing. Second, between an 89% chance of $5 million and a 90% chance of $1 million—keeping the same tail risks—I take the riskier option. That is the standard reversal. But third, if both options are augmented by a guaranteed $500,000 baseline, so we choose only over the surplus, I no longer reverse and follow expected utility. Fourth, if the framing shifts from gains to avoiding catastrophic losses, with probabilities and outcomes otherwise parallel, I reverse again despite the safe baseline being present. Is there one principle—perhaps about certainty at a reference point, or about the psychological salience of total ruin—that can unify these four judgments? Or does accommodating Cases 3 and 4 require me to reject one of the first two as an error? I want to know what the principle predicts for a case where the baseline is probabilistic rather than guaranteed, and whether I must concede that my preferences are genuinely domain-inconsistent.
