In Plato's Laws, Book I (around 649a–650b), the Athenian Stranger proposes that a legislator should observe citizens at symposia—while they are under the influence of wine—to learn their true characters. He compares this practice to testing the strength of a body through gymnastic exercise or the mettle of a horse through riding, suggesting that wine, by dissolving fear and loosening inhibition, operates not as mere indulgence but as a kind of revealing trial. The Stranger seems to think that if citizens can be trained to remain orderly and moderate even while drinking, the city can be confident in their underlying virtue; yet he also warns that unsupervised drinking exposes hidden viciousness.

I'm trying to understand a tension that runs through this argument. On one reading, Plato treats intoxication as a form of controlled epistemic exposure: the legislator deliberately engineers conditions of vulnerability in order to gather moral knowledge that sober, guarded interaction conceals. On another reading, he is recommending a kind of moral stress-test, where the point is not merely observation but active habituation—learning to maintain rational governance precisely when the conditions for failure are maximized.

What I want help with is this: does the passage treat the drinker's diminished rational capacity as a temporary suspension of moral agency (so that actions under wine are evaluated differently—perhaps as allowing vice to surface rather than doing it), or does it hold the drinker fully accountable, using the symposium as a rigorous examination of character? And if Plato genuinely believes that wine reveals a pre-existing, stable soul-structure, how does he avoid the objection that he is conflating situational behavior—shaped by the pharmacological and social environment of the symposium—with enduring moral traits? In other words, is there a coherent distinction in the text between what intoxication reveals and what it creates?

I'd also like to understand whether this framework bears on modern debates about voluntary intoxication and responsibility: if a person chooses to enter a state where they are likely to act badly, does that choice itself carry a special moral weight that differs from accidentally finding oneself in a dangerous situation? But I mainly want to stay close to the passage and not drift into general ethics. Could you walk me through the logic of the Stranger's argument, point out where modern readers are most likely to misread it by importing contemporary assumptions about addiction or autonomy, and suggest how the comparison to gymnastic testing holds up or breaks down?
