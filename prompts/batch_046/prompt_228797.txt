I recently learned that a pocket watch my grandfather left me was looted from a prisoner of war during the Second World War. Before he died, he made me promise never to sell or part with it, but to pass it to my own child. The descendants of the original owner have now traced it and asked for its return. I want to honour my word, yet I also feel compelled to rectify an old injustice. When I talk to friends, they frame it as a clash between loyalty and fairness, but I am troubled by a deeper question: what is it, exactly, that makes a promise binding? Is the force rooted in the harm breaking it would cause to those who relied on it, in the voluntary act of placing oneself under obligation, or in the social expectation that promises ought to be kept? And does that binding force survive when the circumstances that made the promise seem appropriate—my grandfather's belief that the watch was rightfully his—have turned out to be radically false?
