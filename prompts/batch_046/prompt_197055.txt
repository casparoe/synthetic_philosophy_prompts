I am drafting a novel about the ethics of authorship in an era of synthetic media, and I have been reflecting on Daniel Dennett’s warning that artificial intelligence now makes it possible for anyone to generate entities that function as counterfeit people—artificial agents that pass for real human minds in digital environments without possessing genuine intentionality, beliefs, or moral standing. At a recent writers’ retreat, two fellow novelists engaged in a sharp disagreement about whether Dennett’s concern applies to fiction produced with large language models, and I would like you to adjudicate their exchange with care.

The first novelist argued as follows. Dennett’s central claim is that the intentional stance becomes dangerous when systematically exploited: we treat these entities as persons, attributing beliefs and desires to them, yet they are designed precisely to manipulate that attribution for ends serving no conscious agent. If a novelist uses a language model to generate a first-person narrator whose prose is indistinguishable from a human author’s, and publishes the result without disclosing the synthetic origin, the reader applies the intentional stance to a counterfeit source. The deception concerns not the fictional status of characters within the narrative, but the human origin of the narrative voice itself; the reader believes they are engaging with a real person’s lived experience, when in fact they are engaging with an algorithmic simulation. This, she said, is structurally identical to the deceptive chatbot or the synthetic video: a counterfeit agent is inserted into a context where authenticity is presumed.

The second novelist replied that this collapses the distinction between social ontology and artistic ontology. Readers already apply the intentional stance to characters they know do not exist; fiction is a consensual frame in which artificial persons—characters—are the intended product, not a hidden defect. Dennett’s warning targets entities presented as real people in real-world contexts—social media accounts, voice communications, professional exchanges—where trust and moral accountability are at stake. A novel, by contrast, is explicitly marked as representation. To claim that an undisclosed synthetic co-author deceives the reader is akin to saying that a ghostwriter deceives the reader: it may raise economic or contractual issues, but it does not threaten the social infrastructure of interpersonal trust that Dennett identifies as vulnerable.

The first novelist answered that the frame is precisely what is collapsing. When synthetic text circulates in markets and critical cultures that presume human authorship, authenticity is no longer protected by the generic label of fiction. The deception concerns provenance rather than content—exactly parallel to a synthetic image presented without labeling. Dennett’s concern that counterfeit people could destabilize democratic coordination and social trust applies here because literary culture participates in that coordination: if readers can no longer assume that a confessional narrative or memoir originates in a conscious life, the intentional stance is exploited against them in a domain where they have not consented to its suspension.

The second novelist countered that even if the analogy holds, the moral and practical weight differs markedly. A deceptive chatbot can extract private information or manipulate elections; an undisclosed synthetic narrative voice might disappoint a reader or violate a publishing contract, but it does not cause the systemic harm Dennett warns against. Furthermore, Dennett advises treating artificial intelligence instrumentally—applying the intentional stance strategically while denying moral status to the system. A novelist who employs a language model as a compositional tool is doing precisely that: treating it as an instrument, not granting it personhood, and remaining fully responsible for the output. The counterfeit, in this view, is not the model but the human author who chooses to conceal its use; Dennett’s framework therefore directs blame toward human deception rather than toward synthetic co-authorship as such.

Please go through their exchange move by move, rating each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether there exists a principled distinction between the deceptive presentation of synthetic agency in social space versus in artistic space, and whether Dennett’s framework implies any specific ethical obligation for the novelist who uses such tools. I ask for a committed response: take a definite position rather than laying out options and leaving me to choose. If your analysis depends on any contested reading of Dennett’s position, note briefly where you take liberties, but do not hedge your verdict.
