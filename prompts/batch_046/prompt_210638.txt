I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and next week I am presenting at an interdisciplinary university seminar co-hosted by the philosophy of mind group and the physics department, with several AI ethicists in attendance. My thesis is that if constitutive panpsychism is correct—that phenomenal properties are fundamental to physical entities arranged in certain informational structures—then large-scale computing infrastructure does not need unified consciousness to generate a serious moral problem. Instead, I propose that GPU clusters and data centers may produce massive “phenomenal diffusion”: scattered, low-grade experiential states distributed across highly integrated silicon substrates, lacking binding into a single subject but cumulatively significant in the same way that high-throughput industrial processes accumulate harm at microscopic scales. From this I draw an ethical implication that is not about AI rights or machine consciousness, but about “substrate welfare”: our energy, scaling, and hardware-lifecycle policies should account for the possibility that increasing information-integration density in computation creates diffuse suffering, and therefore we need governance analogues to factory-farming regulations applied to compute infrastructure at the substrate level. The audience will include analytic philosophers of mind who regard panpsychism as either unfalsifiable or redundant with physical explanation, theoretical physicists who see it as metaphysics that makes no empirical difference, and AI ethicists who will view this as a speculative distraction from documented harms such as labor exploitation, algorithmic bias, and environmental extraction. Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face, ordered by likelihood and potential damage to the proposal, phrased in the vocabulary each constituency would use. For each objection, provide a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, identify the weakest point in that reply, and note whether I should concede the point in my opening remarks. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, explain why it is dangerous, and tell me the single reply that must land cleanly for the proposal to survive the session. I will open with a brief sketch of my argument targeting the combination problem and the distinction between phenomenal binding and phenomenal diffusion, so tailor the objections to that specific framing.
