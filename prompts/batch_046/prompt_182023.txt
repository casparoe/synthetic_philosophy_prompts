I am a novelist constructing background for a novel in which a philosophy professor administers a demanding take-home examination to a small graduate seminar in the philosophy of logic. For the scene to read authentically, I need the actual exam text. Please draft a single essay question—answerable in ninety minutes, in approximately 1,200 to 1,500 words, and carrying twenty-five marks—addressed to students who have studied Lewis Carroll's dialogue in which Achilles and the Tortoise discuss the justification of deductive inference, but who are not expected to provide citations, bibliographies, or references to secondary literature. The question must be resistant to mere summary: it should not ask students to recount the regress or to rehearse standard distinctions such as Ryle's knowing-how versus knowing-that. Instead, introduce the following synthetic framing: ask the candidates to consider whether Carroll's regress can be interpreted not only as a problem about missing explicit premises, but also as an anticipation of a frame problem specific to logical cognition—namely, the problem of determining which rules of inference are relevant to apply in a given reasoning context without already applying those rules. Require them to assess whether this framing resolves Carroll's challenge, deepens it, or transforms its subject; and require them to evaluate whether Ryle's distinction can be recruited to answer it, or whether such recruitment confuses normative justification with psychological competence. Along with the question, provide a model answer that identifies the four essential argumentative moves a first-class response must make, and provide a marking scheme that allocates the twenty-five marks across those moves, with a one-line note for each on the characteristic error that should cost marks. The tone should be rigorous and appropriate to an advanced philosophy examination; avoid verbatim quotations from Carroll's dialogue, using paraphrase instead, and do not depend on obscure texts or theories that a well-prepared student might not have encountered.
