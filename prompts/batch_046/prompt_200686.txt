I am preparing an interview for my science-and-ideas column with a historian of philosophy who has recently advanced a revisionist interpretation of Émilie du Châtelet's account of happiness. Rather than reading her treatise on the subject as a conventional Enlightenment synthesis of Lockean sensation and Wolffian rationalism, the scholar argues that du Châtelet's doctrine of necessary illusions—particularly regarding romantic attachment and the pursuit of glory through study—anticipates a structural feature of contemporary predictive-processing frameworks: that affective stability depends on suppressing high-precision prediction errors about social comparison and bodily vulnerability. She further contends that this makes du Châtelet's moral psychology closer to a proto-virtue-theoretic framework than to hedonism, since these illusions are not merely instrumental pleasures but constitutive conditions for the exercise of intellectual virtue.

I want eight to ten interview questions that avoid asking her to restate the view from scratch. Please include at least one question on how the predictive-processing parallel avoids reducing du Châtelet's normative claims to descriptive psychology; one on whether her claim that study produces moral happiness survives the objection that modern labor structures and automation make sustained intellectual work structurally inaccessible to most people; and one on what her view implies for the ethics of maintaining illusions in an age of algorithmic transparency and data-driven self-knowledge. For each, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—if she appeals to the distinction between physical and moral happiness, press her on whether that distinction holds when predictive models treat both as error-minimization strategies. The sequence should press where the literature presses: anticipate standard objections about anachronism, about whether the necessary-illusion category risks absorbing every counterexample and rendering the view unfalsifiable, and about whether the framework can distinguish healthy self-deception from pathological denial. End with a question asking what concrete finding—whether from cognitive science, clinical psychology, or the historical record—would genuinely weaken her interpretation rather than merely complicating it, and whether she regards her view as making any empirical risk at all.
