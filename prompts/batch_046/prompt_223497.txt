I've been working through some Kantian texts with a study partner who argues that the standard account of moral worth collapses into an implausible conclusion if we take the right-reasons condition with full rigor. She presents the following chain: (1) An action has moral worth only if performed from duty, where duty requires respecting the moral law for its own sake, independent of any expected outcome. (2) If an agent expects their action to yield good consequences, then any psychological state that represents those consequences provides a derivative reason—one that depends on the value of the result rather than on the authority of the law itself. (3) Any rational agent whose action actually produces good consequences must have expected, with at least some non-negligible credence, that those consequences might occur; otherwise the alignment between action and outcome is purely accidental. (4) Therefore, whenever an action genuinely improves the world, the agent necessarily harbors at least one derivative reason. (5) Here she introduces what she calls the contamination thesis: if a derivative reason is present anywhere in the agent's practical reasoning, it taints the entire motivational structure, so the action cannot be said to be done purely from duty. (6) So we face a dilemma: either the good consequences were genuinely unexpected, in which case they are accidental and do not reflect well on the agent's practical reasoning, or they were expected, in which case contamination destroys moral worth. (7) Hence, no action that actually makes the world better can possess moral worth.

This strikes me as suspicious, but I can't locate the precise error. Is the flaw in step (3), by assuming that rational action requires explicit expectation of the specific good results that happen to materialize? Or is it in step (5), where she moves from the presence of a derivative reason to its motivational dominance? I would especially like to know what a careful Kantian would say about the distinction between being aware that an action will have good effects and being moved by those effects, and whether preserving moral worth requires rejecting the contamination thesis or instead weakening the independence condition in (1). If I have to choose, I am inclined to keep (1) and reject (6), but I want to understand exactly what that choice costs—whether it makes moral worth too common, or whether it requires us to treat agents as opaque even to themselves about their own expectations.
