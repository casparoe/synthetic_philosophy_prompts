As a novelist, my native vocabulary concerns plot architecture, genre contracts, dramatic irony, and the suspension of disbelief rather than formal epistemology. I would like you to restate Goodman's problem of induction and the specific paradox of grue—that observed regularities can be projected onto the future through arbitrarily gerrymandered predicates, undermining any privileged claim for the green-emerald projection over its green-before-time-t-and-blue-thereafter rival—entirely within that narrative framework. Be precise in your correspondences: let the observed emeralds stand in for some established plot pattern or genre convention; let the predicate grue map onto a narrative category that behaves like one convention up to a point and then violates it; and let the inductive leap correspond to the reader's expectation of narrative resolution. Once the translation is complete, provide a residue report. Which elements of the philosophical argument—particularly the symmetry between competing projections and the claim that no observation can adjudicate between them—survive intact? Which elements must be distorted to fit the logic of storytelling, where emotional coherence and causal plausibility usually constrain what an audience will accept? Is there any feature of Goodman's challenge that has no counterpart in narrative craft whatsoever? Finally, tell me whether the residue reveals a limitation in using stories as a model for inference, or whether it suggests that the original paradox depends on a kind of logical freedom that narrative, bound by the demands of coherence and affect, never actually exercises.
