I work as a senior backend engineer on a federated data platform, and our architecture review board regularly receives proposals to align schema terms—such as customer, consent, or transaction—across regional nodes that operate under different regulatory environments and serve different user populations. After encountering the Twin Earth scenario in a discussion about semantic externalism, I realized we have no structured method for deciding whether a shared term denotes the same concept in both nodes or whether its reference is fixed partly by an external environment that differs too radically for safe merging. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a philosophical essay, that engineers with no formal philosophy background can run in about thirty minutes before signing off on an alignment. The procedure should take seriously the core claim of semantic externalism: that the meaning or reference of a term depends in part on the world outside any internal documentation or mental representation, and should present an ordered sequence of practical tests with clear passing conditions, explicit stop points that reject the merge, and marked hand-over points where judgment must be deferred to a legal or domain expert because the issue is one of values rather than structural fact. It should also flag the specific categories of case it is not designed to settle.

Please apply the checklist to three scenarios: aligning the term customer between an EU node governed by GDPR and a US node governed by contract law; aligning consent between a clinical-research node and a marketing-automation node; and one deliberately constructed case where the checklist returns approve merge but the practical consequence is clearly wrong—explain precisely which feature of the externalist framework the procedure failed to capture, why no purely procedural amendment can fully close the gap, and what this reveals about the limits of operationalizing semantic externalism in engineering practice. Finally, state explicitly what kinds of alignment proposals fall entirely outside this procedure's scope and where it is most likely to misfire even when applied correctly.
