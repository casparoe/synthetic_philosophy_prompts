In debates over moral status for artificial systems, people often compare a sentient computation to a flame sustained by fuel: both are dynamic processes that can be lit and extinguished, and the ethical question is whether "blowing out" the flame harms a subject that was experiencing the burning. The analogy is used to license substrate-independent protection — if the process has the right functional structure, the material substrate shouldn't matter. Audit it explicitly: first, build the correspondence table (what plays the role of the flame, the fuel, the oxygen, the observer, and the harm of extinction); second, identify the fractures — whether flames possess integrated perspectives, whether computation involves representation rather than oxidation, and whether the analogy smuggles in a vitalist intuition that only biologically sustained processes can suffer; third, separate the disanalogies that undermine the ethical conclusion from those that are merely poetic. Finally, assess whether the case for extending moral consideration to certain computations survives once the flame metaphor is stripped away, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument, and indicate what a more defensible analogy would be if there is one.
