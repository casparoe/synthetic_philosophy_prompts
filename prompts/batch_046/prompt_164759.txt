Construct a decision-tree taxonomy for macrohistorical theories of long-term change, aimed at philosophy-of-history graduate students. Do not organize it by author; instead, build it from four binary commitment-nodes that split the logical space, then locate the named thinkers inside it.

The four nodes, in branching order, are:

1. Directionality across epochs: Does the framework treat long-run change as possessing an overall directional arrow (development, maturation, inevitable decline) that persists across cycles, or does it treat direction as purely local and illusory?
2. Mechanism of recurrence: Is the driver of repetition primarily endogenous to the unit (institutional decay, loss of group solidarity, cultural aging) or primarily exogenous (environmental shock, invasion, resource collapse)?
3. Scale of the bounded unit: Does the cycle apply to the lifespan of a state or dynasty, to a civilization or culture-spirit, or to a trans-civilizational world-system?
4. Epistemic function: Is the framework presented mainly as a predictive or descriptive law of history, or mainly as a normative, interventionist diagnostic (telling actors how to avoid or hasten decline)?

Start with node 1 at the root and order the remaining nodes so that dead branches are minimized. Every internal node must be phrased as a concrete claim a student could accept or reject without knowing which thinker it maps to. At each leaf, place one of the four named positions—Polybius’s anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun’s asabiyyah cycle, Spengler’s morphological culture-history, or Toynbee’s challenge-and-response model—or mark it “unoccupied.” For every unoccupied combination (there must be at least two), supply one sentence diagnosing why it is empty: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Because the four thinkers do not cover all sixteen combinations, you must explicitly flag any pair of named thinkers that the tree cannot genuinely separate on these four nodes, and briefly explain what further distinction would be needed to split them. Keep the instrument free of bibliographies, reading lists, citations, and verbatim quotations. Build the logical space first, then map the thinkers onto it.
