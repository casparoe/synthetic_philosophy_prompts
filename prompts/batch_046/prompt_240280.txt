I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I have been reading informally about modal logic and the metaphysics of identity. Last week, over dinner with two friends—Leo, who reads philosophy of language as a hobby, and Mira, who works in data architecture—the conversation turned to whether I could genuinely have become a musician instead of an engineer. Leo defended what he called transworld identity: I am literally the same individual in every possible world in which I exist, much like a database record that retains its primary key even when its field values change across different query contexts. Mira argued for counterpart theory: in worlds where I am a musician, there exists someone remarkably similar to me, but it is not numerically identical to me—more like a commit on a separate git branch that shares history but has a different hash.

Leo pressed that Mira's view makes the ordinary claim "I could have been a musician" misleading, because it is not me who is the musician but a counterpart. Mira replied that counterpart theory actually preserves that intuition without requiring me to instantiate contradictory properties; different counterparts bear different properties, so the law of non-contradiction remains intact. Leo then asked why, if there is no literal identity across worlds, I should care specifically about my musician-counterpart rather than any arbitrarily similar individual—if caring tracks only resemblance and causal continuity, then my concern seems arbitrarily selective. Mira answered that practical concern operates like system restoration: we value a rebuilt node not because it is the same running process but because it inherits state and functional continuity from the original, which is enough to ground rational preference without requiring metaphysical identity. Leo countered that this concedes too much, reducing "I could have been otherwise" to a claim about similarity metrics rather than a fact about me.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: analyse Leo's database analogy and his charge that counterpart theory falsifies ordinary modal claims; analyse Mira's git-branch analogy and her appeal to non-contradiction; then evaluate Leo's concern about arbitrary selection and Mira's restoration analogy. In particular, say whether either analogy genuinely illuminates the distinction or merely imports technical vocabulary that obscures it, and indicate whether there is a missing middle position—perhaps one that permits literal identity across worlds but denies that an individual can bear genuinely contradictory properties in the same respect—or whether Leo and Mira have exhausted the logical space. Do not signal which side you favour overall, but do say precisely where each argument succeeds or fails.
