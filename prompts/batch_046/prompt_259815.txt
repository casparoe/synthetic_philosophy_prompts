I serve as the philosophical advisor to an interdisciplinary board that approves predictive risk models used to allocate emergency preservation funds to submerged archaeological sites threatened by climate-driven erosion. Our reviewers—conservators and marine engineers with no formal philosophy training—must decide whether a proposed model's singular probability estimates are sound, yet they repeatedly encounter what is known as the reference class problem: every prediction about a particular site depends on the class into which it is sorted, and no non-circular formal rule uniquely determines the correct class. I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist of diagnostic questions with explicit stopping rules—that these reviewers can run in approximately ninety minutes per proposal, knowing that the board chair must personally sign off whenever the procedure reaches a judgment-flag rather than a technical result. The procedure should preserve the core philosophical insight that widening or narrowing the reference class is not a correctable error but a structural feature that transforms the prediction itself, while dropping formal apparatus that would confuse non-specialists.

Construct the procedure by specifying, in sequence: the questions reviewers must ask about how the model selects and justifies its reference class; what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage; where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation (approve with conditions, reject, or escalate); and where it explicitly marks a decision as value-laden or pragmatic rather than technically determined. Then apply it to three cases: first, a model predicting collapse within ten years for a specific submerged settlement using the broad class "all submerged wooden structures in temperate estuaries"; second, a model using the narrower class "structures with comparable sediment chemistry and tidal range" that admits only two prior analogs exist; and third, a model that assigns the site to multiple overlapping classes, yields a range of probabilities, and recommends intervention at the highest-risk endpoint—a recommendation my board is politically inclined to accept for this high-profile site, but which the procedure should resist or flag. For each case, report the procedure's output, explain any divergence from intuitive preference, and identify the closest analog among the three. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot resolve—where the choice of reference class necessarily remains a matter of judgment rather than technique—and limit your response to 400 words.
