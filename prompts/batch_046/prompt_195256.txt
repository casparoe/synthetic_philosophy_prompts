okay so i just started volunteering with this local library board and there's this voting rule thats been bugging me. for normal decisions — buying books, opening hours, whatever — everyone gets one vote, including people who just joined. but if the issue is about structural repairs, asbestos, or any health code stuff, only board members with an engineering or epidemiology background get to vote. everyone else is literally told to stay out of the room. the chair calls it "epistemic division of labor," which sounds fancy but i think its just elitism dressed up. anyway, my question is: what theory of democratic legitimacy does this actually presuppose? does it assume that voting is only about expressing preferences, so it's okay to silence people when the question is factual? or does it think political equality is just moral equality, which doesn't apply to technical knowledge? and heres the part that feels inconsistent: we also vote on which books to buy for the science section, and nobody asks whether you have a physics degree. so is the practice really committed to one view, or is it just ad hoc? i want to know what philosophical commitments about authority, equality, and the purpose of democracy this rule needs to be consistent, and what it would have to change if we actually believed that knowledge differences don't justify excluding people from collective decisions.
