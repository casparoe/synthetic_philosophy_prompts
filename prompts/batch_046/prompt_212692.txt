A university curriculum committee has been applying Alfred North Whitehead’s philosophy of education—specifically his account of the rhythm of learning, the value of what he calls “useless” knowledge, and the danger of isolating technical precision from exploratory wonder—to review proposals for new degree tracks. Their record so far is as follows. They approved a course in pure number theory that explicitly forbids applications and assessment beyond proof construction; they rejected a certificate program that teaches only immediately marketable skills with no theoretical framework; they approved, with conditions, an interdisciplinary first-year seminar that moves students from open-ended inquiry into disciplined method but deliberately stops short of synthetic generalization; they rejected a semester-long module consisting entirely of repetitive technical drills with no initial exploratory or framing phase; and they approved a doctoral specialization that demands deep precision in a narrow field, but only for students who have already completed a broad liberal-arts foundation that included both romance and generalization.

Now they face a proposal for a two-year intensive program: Year One is entirely unstructured exploration across disciplines, with no grades, no required methods, and no synthesis—pure romance, sustained for twelve months. Year Two is entirely high-stakes technical specialization in a single domain, with no reference back to the broader questions raised in Year One—pure precision, sustained for twelve months. The two years are administratively linked but pedagogically separated; there is no transitional course and no integrative capstone.

Predict the committee’s verdict if they wish to remain consistent with themselves. Identify the closest analog among their five precedents, and then lay out at least two distinct rules that fit their track record equally well— for instance, a rule about the internal structure of any single course versus a rule about the overall arc of an educational life; or a rule about the temporal adjacency of stages versus a rule about their logical ordering across an institution. Say what each rule predicts about the two-year proposal, give your best-guess verdict with the reason for preferring that rule, and state honestly whether the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case and, if so, what principle the committee would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, specify the further case—different from any above—that would best discriminate between the competing rules. I am less interested in the bare answer than in the mapping of rules to verdicts and in the precise point where the committee’s implicit philosophy becomes visible.
