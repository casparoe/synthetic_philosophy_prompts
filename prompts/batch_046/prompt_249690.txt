I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind, and I have been asked to design a single ninety-minute graduate seminar for first-year MA students who have read Ryle’s original chapter on knowing how but have not yet encountered the intellectualist reply—the view that skilled performance is ultimately underwritten by propositional knowledge. The students arrive with two entrenched preconceptions: from popular-science writing, they treat knowing how as merely knowing that plus muscle memory or repetition; and from a superficial reading of Ryle, they believe he denies that skilled agents possess any mental representations, awareness, or propositional thought whatsoever during action. They also have an introductory background in cognitive science, so they are prone to conflate Ryle’s logical distinction with the declarative-versus-procedural memory taxonomy used in psychology.

I need a teaching plan—not the explanation itself—for presenting the knowing-how / knowing-that distinction to this specific audience. Structure your response as follows:

First, the dependency order: specify the exact sequence in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that Ryle’s regress argument is received as a claim about logical priority rather than as an eliminativist denial of mental content. Indicate what must be firmly in place before the carrying example is introduced, what the example itself must establish, and what synthesis must come afterward to prevent the students from collapsing the distinction back into a psychological taxonomy.

Second, the carrying example: identify the one concrete scenario that should carry the entire session. I am inclined to use the expert diagnosis of a mechanical watch fault—knowing how to feel and hear an escapement irregularity versus knowing that the service manual specifies 0.5 mm of end-shake—but I will accept an alternative if you justify it. State precisely what properties this example must possess and what it must lack; then explicitly explain why the standard bicycle-riding example should not serve as the primary vehicle here, given this audience’s prior misconceptions.

Third, the misconception map: list the predictable misconceptions the students will bring or form during the session, including the “muscle-memory reduction,” the behaviorist misreading of Ryle, and the conflation with cognitive-science memory categories. For each misconception, specify exactly where in the sequence—before the example, during its exposition, or in the post-example synthesis—it should be anticipated and defused.

Fourth, the check for understanding: provide one precise question I can pose at the end of the session that will reveal whether the students have grasped the logical-dependency claim rather than merely memorized Ryle’s slogans or reverted to the intellectualist reduction.

Fifth, the boundary: state clearly what I should openly refuse to explain or develop at this level—whether that means withholding the full intellectualist counter-argument, avoiding neuropsychological case studies such as amnesic patients, or setting aside debates about embodied cognition and predictive processing—and justify each omission in terms of the session’s dependency structure, not in terms of difficulty alone.

The plan should be addressed to me as the seminar leader, should avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should not depend on verifiable attributions of specific texts beyond Ryle’s well-known discussion.
