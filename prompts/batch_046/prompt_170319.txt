I've been reading about Malthus for a history of science course and I'm stuck on whether being 'right but early' is actually a legitimate rescue or just a cop-out. So here's an argument my roommate made that seems to prove way too much about falsifcation in general:

(1) Malthus predicted that population growth, being geometric, would eventually outstrip agricultural growth, which is arithmetic. (2) For nearly two centuries, technologial innovation and demographic transition have kept global per-capita food production rising, not falling. (3) So by standard Popperian criteria, Malthus's specific predicition has been falsified. (4) But defenders say Malthus wasn't wrong, just early — the resource ceiling hasn't been hit yet, but eventually it will be. (5) If 'not yet' is always available as a defense, then any long-run prediction that hasn't come true can be rescued the same way. (6) Therefore no long-run theory can ever be genuinely falsified; falsification only applies to short-run predictions.

My roommate says (6) is absurd — obviously some long-run theories (like phlogiston or the luminiferous ether) are dead and not coming back. So one of the steps must be wrong. But I can't tell if the problem is that Malthus is actually falsified now, or that the rescue strategy is illegitimate, or that there's a hidden distinction between 'rescue by delay' and 'rescue by ad hoc modification'. Can you walk me through which premise to reject, what rejecting it costs, and specifically how the Malthus case differs from genuinely dead theories? I'm particularly confused about whether 'right but early' requires some independent argument that the underlying mechanism hasn't changed, or if that's just special pleading.
