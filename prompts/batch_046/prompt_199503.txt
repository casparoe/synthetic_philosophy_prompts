Next week I defend, in a philosophy-of-mind and metaphysics joint seminar, the claim that conceivability tracks metaphysical possibility only intra-modally: imagining a phenomenal state without its usual physical substrate, or a physical system with an alien phenomenal profile, is not merely epistemically risky but structurally impossible, because mental imagery extends analogically from experienced exemplars rather than constructing logical possibilities de novo. The audience includes analytic metaphysicians committed to two-dimensional modal rationalism, cognitive scientists who study mental imagery and analogical reasoning, and graduate students skeptical of armchair limits on imagination. They know the standard zombie and inverted-spectrum literature well but will resist any thesis that gives imagination built-in structural boundaries rather than contingent failures. Prepare me for the objections in the order they will come: phrase each as that specific interlocutor would phrase it, distinguish the objections that have real force from those that are rhetorical, tell me what I should concede up front, identify the weakest point in my analogical-extension argument that I must defend rather than hope they miss, and flag the one objection I am hoping nobody raises—namely, that my view collapses conceivability into psychological feasibility and thereby abandons metaphysics for cognitive science.
