I have been working through Keynes's scattered remarks on what he called the art of life itself—particularly his 1930 essay on economic possibilities, in which he distinguishes absolute from relative needs and predicts that within a century people might work fifteen hours a week and devote the remainder to living well. A colleague who studies the history of economic thought has developed a consistent interpretive framework for applying that distinction to modern institutional design, and I am trying to determine what principle she is actually running rather than merely listing her conclusions.

She holds that an unconditional basic income serves the Keynesian good life if and only if it covers absolute needs without being so generous as to eliminate all social pressure toward productive activity; on this basis she approves Alaska's Permanent Fund Dividend, which she regards as too small to remove the incentive to work, and rejects Spain's means-tested minimum vital income, which she criticizes as bureaucratic and tied to job-search conditions that violate the unconditional character of true economic security. She approves the four-day work-week experiments in Iceland and Belgium, seeing them as reducing compulsory labor while preserving its social structure, but she rejects fully automated post-work communes that abolish market exchange entirely, arguing that Keynes wanted to preserve the dignity of voluntary contribution rather than eliminate productive engagement altogether. She approves state-funded public libraries and municipal parks because they cultivate the non-pecuniary arts that Keynes valued, yet she rejects state subsidies for luxury wellness retreats on the ground that they cater to relative needs and status competition rather than to genuine flourishing.

Given this track record, I want to know how she would rule on three new institutional proposals, but I care more about identifying the competing rules that could generate her earlier verdicts and seeing where they diverge. First, a policy that taxes inheritances above a modest threshold and uses the revenue to fund universal sabbaticals—every worker receives one paid year off every seven years. Second, a municipal ordinance capping advertising expenditure for luxury goods in all public spaces. Third, and this is the case I find most ambiguous: a proposal for what its advocates call cognitive basic income, under which citizens receive not cash but guaranteed, state-funded access to advanced education, artistic training, and philosophical counseling, financed by taxing routine cognitive labor that artificial intelligence can now perform. Would she see this as extending Keynes's vision—liberating people for higher pursuits—or as violating it by removing a domain of meaningful human contribution?

I ask you to lay out at least three plausible rules consistent with her earlier judgments—perhaps one centered on the boundary between absolute and relative needs, one on the social psychology of contribution and the danger of unstructured idleness, and one on the proper division between collective goods the state should provide and personal consumption choices it should not direct. For each rule, say what it predicts about the three proposals, with particular attention to whether it treats the elimination of cognitive labor differently from the reduction of manual labor. Then give your best guess at which rule she actually follows, with the reasoning for preferring it, and identify the single further case—an institution or policy not yet mentioned—that would best distinguish between the leading candidates. I am especially interested in whether her framework contains a principled distinction between types of labor or whether that distinction collapses into nostalgia once automation reaches the cognitive domain.
