Me and a couple friends in an art-philosophy group have been circling the same seperation-of-work-from-artist argument for months and it's driving me nuts. The debate is whether immoral art can be genuinely good art: side A says yes, aesthetic value is autonomous, knowing the painter was a thief doesn't change the brushwork; side B says no, art is an expressive act so moral corruption in the maker is like a false premise in an argument — it undermines the whole thing. We've used the same examples (a brilliant novel with a racist narrative voice, a sympthony composed for a propaganda regime, a sculpture made with stolen bones) and nobody moves. My working theory is that we're actually running three debates under one label: one about whether 'good' means aesthetically good or all-things-considered, one about whether art is more like a tool or more like speech, and a hidden one about whether an audience's knowledge of the artist's sins can literally change the work's aesthetic properties — not just our reaction, but the properties themselves. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden empirical claim about audience harm or artistic intent doing all the work? I want to know what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, substantive, different questions — and whether anything (a distinction, a thought experiment, changing the framing) could actually break the stall, or if we should just accept it's bedrock intuition.
