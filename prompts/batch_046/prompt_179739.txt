I have been working through Zhuangzi’s remarks on death and transformation—his suggestion that dying is comparable to waking from a deep sleep, and that what we call the self is not a fixed substance but a temporary configuration of qi—while also examining a recent municipal ordinance that permits alkaline hydrolysis followed by mandatory incorporation of the remains into an unmarked, rotating communal soil system used for urban agriculture. The policy explicitly prohibits individual headstones, memorial plaques, or any permanent geographic marker, and it requires that the processed soil be distributed across changing agricultural plots so that no enduring spatial link to the deceased survives. I am trying to understand what philosophical anthropology and ontology this institutional design quietly presupposes.

Does the practice assume that personal identity is strictly coextensive with biological life, treating the dead body as mere recyclable waste, or does it instead presuppose a form of distributed continuity in which the deceased is literally transformed into part of the living community? How does it conceptualize the boundary between person and environment—does it depend on a reductive materialism, or could it be interpreted as institutionalizing a Zhuangzian view that death is not annihilation but a change of form? Furthermore, if we took seriously Zhuangzi’s further claim that the distinction between self and other, living and dead, is ultimately illusory, what elements of the current ordinance would have to change, and which parts could remain intact? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of death, identity, and moral obligation toward remains that makes this policy coherent; distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates; and say whether the same underlying commitments could license the opposite institutional arrangement—say, a legal mandate for permanent, individually marked monuments—without internal contradiction.
