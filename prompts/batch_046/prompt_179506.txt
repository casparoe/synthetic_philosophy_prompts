I'm studying the metaphysics of parts and wholes — specifically the debate over when scattered parts genuinely compose a single object, often called the special composition question. Across several authors, I've noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I can't place, and I'm trying to determine whether it has an established label and, if not, how exactly to delimit it.

Consider two cases. First, a theorist proposes that parts compose a whole only when they are linked by a continuous causal process. They are then presented with a cosmic coincidence: atoms distributed across light-years happen to replicate the exact relational-causal structure of a functioning brain. Rather than accepting that this scattered collection is an object, the theorist says the causal links are not integrated — but integration here seems to amount to nothing more than the absence of scattering, which was precisely the puzzling feature of the case. Second, another theorist holds that functional unity is sufficient for composition. Confronted with a working heart whose chambers sit in separate buildings connected only by wireless signals, they reply that the parts fail to compose because they lack spatial co-location, even though co-location was never part of the original functional criterion and is not necessary for functional unity.

The common structure is this. A compositional criterion is introduced as an objective, mind-independent condition. A boundary case is engineered so that the criterion is technically satisfied (or violated) in a way that conflicts with ordinary judgments about existence. Instead of revising the criterion or biting the bullet, the defender introduces an implicit parameter — integration, co-location, naturalness — that precisely tracks our ordinary abilities to track and individuate objects, while presenting that parameter as an innocent refinement rather than an intuition-saving patch.

This is not plain ad hocness, since each added clause is individually defensible. It is also not mere vagueness, because the boundary is being deliberately relocated rather than left indeterminate. Nor is it straightforward projectivism about objects, because the defender continues to present the criterion as describing mind-independent composition conditions.

Does this pattern have a precise name in the literature — perhaps as a specific failure mode of compositional theories? How exactly does it differ from saving the appearances, from ontological extravagance objections, and from the problem of the many? If no established term captures it, can you coin one, define its necessary and sufficient conditions, specify its boundary cases, and explain what distinguishes it from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis adjustments?
