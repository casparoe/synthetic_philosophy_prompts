I'm running an upper-level seminar on metaethics and philosophy of religion—about fourteen undergraduates, one seventy-five-minute session, no prior reading assigned. I want a structured in-class simulation that makes the students feel the structural pressure of the Euthyphro dilemma rather than merely diagramming it. The activity should be staged in three movements: first, small groups of Legislators must construct a minimal moral code for a fictional society facing a resource crisis, using only collective deliberation and feeling that their reasons are self-standing; second, they learn that a Sovereign role can nullify any rule with a simple veto, forcing them to experience the authority horn; third, an Epistemic Auditor—who possesses but is contractually forbidden from disclosing the Sovereign's private reasoning for each command—enters, so the Legislators must decide whether moral knowledge or obligation requires access to that hidden justification, or whether they can evaluate commands independently without it. Only after they have made actual binding choices under these constraints should the facilitator name the Euthyphro structure.

Design the game with explicit roles (Legislators in subgroups of four, one Sovereign, one Epistemic Auditor), rules of deliberation with turn-taking, and a payoff mechanism that needs no real money—perhaps a cohesion-point system for the fictional society that collapses if the Legislators reject too many sovereign commands, but also collapses if they accept commands that violate their independently constructed rules. Include a debrief that maps each stage onto the independence and arbitrariness problems, anticipates predictable failures (for instance, students treating the Auditor's silence as mere theatrical flair rather than an epistemic constraint, or Legislators refusing to engage the Sovereign's veto to avoid feeling the second horn), and is frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce: specifically, the metaphysical asymmetry of a being with ontological priority over the moral order, which the activity necessarily flattens into a peer-like role-play. Also note which feature must be supplied by the facilitator—namely, the claim that in the theological version the authority is not arbitrary precisely because it is identical with necessary being, something the procedural rules of the game can gesture at but never instantiate.

The idea should emerge from the rules and incentives, not from a concluding lecture: arrange the sequence so that participants discover for themselves that accepting hidden authoritative reasoning without evaluation makes their prior moral reasoning redundant, while rejecting it risks treating authority as dispensable. Make the opening instructions minimal—just enough to start constructing rules—so that the shift from independent deliberation to constrained obedience feels like a genuine disruption they experience rather than a premise they are told to accept.
