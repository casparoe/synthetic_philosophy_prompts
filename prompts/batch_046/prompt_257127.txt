I'm planning a single fifty-minute first-year philosophy seminar. The students have just worked through Hume's bundle theory and Locke's memory criterion, and they have settled into a false dichotomy: either there is a substantive self or the self is an illusion we ought to dismiss. I want to teach Dennett's notion of the self as a centre of narrative gravity — not as a substance, not as an eliminable fiction, but as an abstractum with genuine causal-explanatory weight. Plan the session's explanatory arc.

First, set out the dependency order: which idea must be secured before Dennett's claim can even make sense to them, given that they currently treat "fictional" as synonymous with "merely unreal"? Second, choose one concrete carrying example that is not the standard illustration of an author writing an autobiography. The example should involve a being or system that lacks a single biological brain yet still generates a unified narrative locus; specify exactly what this case must possess and what it must lack in order to show that the locus is not an entity inside the system but a pattern that the system produces. Third, map the misconceptions they will form in sequence — likely that narrative gravity is only a metaphor, that it requires an internal storyteller, that it collapses back into Locke's memory chain, and that it cannot ground moral responsibility. For each misconception, indicate precisely where in the arc it should be headed off and how to absorb the objection without derailing the session. Fourth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level — for instance, the full physics analogy, the evolutionary account, or the debate with Galen Strawson — and say so outright. Finally, design one check question I can ask at the end that distinguishes whether they have grasped that a centre of narrative gravity is an abstract object with real explanatory work, rather than merely repeating that "the self is a story". Write the plan in British English.
