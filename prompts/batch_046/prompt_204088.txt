For a graduate seminar in cognitive aesthetics (10 participants, 120 minutes), design a structured, turn-based simulation that lets participants experience Hofstadter's claim that creativity is best understood as knob-twiddling within a parameterized conceptual space rather than ex nihilo invention. Rather than using music or language, construct the activity around a collective living concept—a working definition of distributive fairness—represented by six explicit, named parameters (for example: strict proportionality, need-weighted adjustment, procedural transparency, reversibility under role-reversal, scale-invariance, and emotional salience). Provide precise rules: one participant per turn adjusts exactly one parameter by one discrete step; the group then votes, without discussion, on whether the revised concept remains recognizably the same theme or has slipped into a different idea; and assign hidden individual incentives, such as private scorecards rewarding convergence on one's own pre-committed position, so that coordination failures arise organically. Specify the timeline, any assigned roles (facilitator, recorder, dissent-holder), and the stakes or point system. Include a debrief that maps specific moments—successful maintenance of identity, abrupt rejection, gradual drift—onto parts of the knob-twiddling model, and state clearly which feature of that model (for instance, the largely unconscious, automatic nature of parameter adjustment) the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Be explicit about what participants should come away seeing: that small, lawful variations preserve conceptual identity while cumulative shifts dissolve it, and that recognizing a theme is a judgment about parameter proximity rather than membership in a fixed category. Finally, note one predictable failure mode of the activity (e.g., participants treating parameters as independent levers rather than interdependent dimensions) and how to handle it.
