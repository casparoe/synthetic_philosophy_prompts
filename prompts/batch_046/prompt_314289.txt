I'm running an 80-minute graduate seminar on moral psychology for about fourteen students who have already worked through Strawson's distinction between participant reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, and the direct moral address embedded in ordinary interpersonal relationships—and the objective attitude, which treats a person as an object of social management, cure, or policy. I want a runnable in-class simulation or structured role-play that makes them feel, in real time, why shifting to the objective attitude is not merely a change in private judgment but a transformation in the kind of relationship one stands in with another person. Rather than a standard one-on-one blame scenario, construct a case involving a small intentional community—say, a research cooperative or communal household—that must decide collectively how to respond to a long-time member, call them J, who has recently begun repeatedly violating shared norms because of a developing neurological condition.

The scenario should include a constructed twist that is intelligible but not a standard textbook formulation: the group must choose between maintaining what we might call an institutional reactive posture, in which the collective continues to address J with shared moral expectations as a practice rather than as summed individual grievances, and adopting an institutional objective posture, in which J is treated purely as a coordination problem to be managed or contained. Provide explicit rules, role cards for at least four hidden roles—such as a direct victim of J's latest violation, a close friend who knows J's remorse is genuine but intermittent, an elder responsible for maintaining communal norms, and a neutral member with no personal stake—along with a timeline that fits inside the eighty minutes. Use stakes that are social and relational rather than monetary: for instance, seating arrangements that place J inside or outside the deliberative circle, speaking protocols that require direct second-person address versus third-person reporting about J, or collective votes that determine whether J retains full membership rights. Make sure the hidden information creates an asymmetry that forces participants to discover for themselves whether explanation and excuse should dissolve the reactive stance.

Finally, include a debrief that maps specific moments from the deliberation onto parts of Strawson's framework—particularly the contrast between viewing someone as a fellow participant and viewing them through the objective lens—plus an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to handle it. Be explicit about which feature of genuine reactive attitudes—most likely their deep emotional involuntariness and long-term embedding in a particular historical relationship—this staged exercise cannot reproduce, and note exactly what the facilitator must supply verbally or structurally to compensate for that absence.
