In my graduate seminar on moral philosophy, I have been developing a somewhat unorthodox amendment to T. M. Scanlon’s contractualist framework—one that attempts to resolve the aggregation problem not by abandoning pairwise justification but by reconceiving it through what I have tentatively called “distributed justification thresholds.” Before I present this to a room of sharp doctoral students, I wish to rehearse the central exchange with a formidable interlocutor who will not grant me easy concessions. Please adopt the role of a committed contractualist skeptic: you grant that Scanlon’s pairwise model captures individual moral complaints with real precision, but you are firmly convinced that any attempt to aggregate harms across multiple parties—whether by weighted reasons, structural mediation, or distributive schemes—inevitably betrays the core commitment that wrongness is fixed by what can be justified to each affected individual, taken one by one. You are rigorous, not a strawman; you will concede small clarificatory points but you will never surrender the claim that the aggregation problem is fatal, and you must not drift into consequentialist language even for the sake of argument. Remain fully in character until I say “stop,” keep each reply to roughly one paragraph as in a live seminar exchange, and do not ask me clarifying questions about my position—respond directly to my move. Here is my opening: I contend that certain systemic harms—such as those produced by climate-driven displacement—are not merely summed individual injuries but structurally interdependent burdens, so that requiring a justifiable burden-sharing arrangement addresses each party not in atomistic isolation but as a node within a shared distributive scheme. Because the justification is still delivered to every affected agent regarding the specific structural role they occupy, contractualism need not import a utilitarian calculus; it need only recognize that some moral reasons are essentially relational rather than atomistic. If this is correct, then contractualism can accommodate collective harm without collapsing into aggregation—so where, precisely, does my amendment still smuggle in a principle that you, as a contractualist, must reject?
