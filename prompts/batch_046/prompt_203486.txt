You serve on the scientific review panel for a regional wildlife-reintroduction authority. Every quarter you evaluate intervention proposals—selective breeding, behavioral conditioning, habitat engineering, or targeted culling—each justified by appeal to the “natural function” of a trait in the target population. Your board does not commit to a single philosophical account; you must treat the etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role (current-systemic-contribution) theories of biological function as rival operational frameworks and apply both systematically.

Write a reusable decision procedure, formatted as ordered questions with explicit decision nodes, that your panel can run in under thirty minutes using only the proposal document and standard ecological records. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, whether the answer is factual or a value judgment requiring human sign-off, and where the two frameworks converge or diverge. The procedure must contain:

1. A preliminary filter assessing whether the trait displays a stable causal-role profile across the proposed post-intervention environment.
2. A divergence protocol for cases where etiological and causal-role assessments conflict: a structured comparison weighing available selection-history evidence against measured or projected systemic contribution, without reducing one account to the other.
3. Explicit stop conditions: (a) “intervention not justified on functional grounds” (yielding, in at least one worked case, a recommendation that conflicts with standard conservation intuition); (b) “function ambiguous—escalate to panel judgment”; and (c) “function intact—intervention permitted but not required.”
4. Three embedded test cases running through every node: (i) a captive-bred predator population that has lost cooperative hunting but now survives through solitary scavenging; (ii) a migratory bird population in which a long-distance navigation trait is metabolically expensive but no longer used because wintering grounds have shifted; (iii) a pollinator with a mouthpart length selected for access to an extinct flower that now permits access only to a non-native substitute sustaining the local population.

Apply the full procedure to all three cases, tracing the path through each node. Identify at least two structural misfire points—particularly where unknown selection histories or rapid ecological change render the etiological branch unreliable—and state clearly what the procedure cannot settle, where judgment is irreducible, and which distinctions must survive the simplification for the board’s purpose. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for named scholars; avoid obscure technical terminology that depends on specialist literature. Where a philosophical distinction remains genuinely contested, flag it as such rather than resolving it by fiat. Keep the tone formal and practical, designed for repeated use by reviewers without philosophical training.
