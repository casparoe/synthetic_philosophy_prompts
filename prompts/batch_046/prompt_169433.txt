I am working through Scanlon's contractualism and its aggregation problem, but I think I have pushed the view into a contradiction. Here is my reasoning. (1) Contractualism holds that an act is wrong if it can be justified only by a principle that at least one person could reasonably reject. (2) In a rescue case where we can save either five people or one person, but not both, the principle "save the five" allows the one to die; the one can reject it, but each of the five can reject the principle "save the one." (3) Because contractualism weighs complaints, and there are five rejecters on one side versus only one on the other, the principle saving the one is more rejectable; so contractualism directs us to save the five, which is just aggregation by head count. (4) Now apply this to a factory-safety rule that imposes a negligible risk on each of 10,000 workers in order to eliminate a certain fatal risk to one nearby child. By the same weighing, 10,000 rejecters outweigh one, so contractualism must forbid the rule, even though the total burden is trivial and the benefit is saving a life. (5) Thus contractualism aggregates complaints to justify saving the larger group, yet also aggregates them to block small burdens spread across many people; the two results are structurally identical but morally opposite. (6) This suggests that contractualism either collapses into utilitarian head-counting or is internally inconsistent. Which step—2, 3, or 5—contains the hidden mistake, and what does correcting it cost Scanlon's claim that his view avoids aggregation altogether?
