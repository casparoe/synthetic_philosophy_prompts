In my philosophy-of-physics seminar, a recurring comparison treats the three main quantum interpretations as programming paradigms: Everettian mechanics is described as object-oriented (every branch of the wavefunction is a real object instantiated in a multiversal structure), Copenhagen as procedural (the formalism is a recipe for predictions with no commitment to an underlying ontology), and Bohmian mechanics as low-level assembly (a deterministic hidden-variable engine running beneath the quantum syntax). The analogy is pressed to support the claim that picking among them is largely an aesthetic or pragmatic decision—like choosing a language—rather than an empirical one, since all three compile the same observable predictions. I want to know how far the comparison actually carries. Please lay out the explicit correspondence table: for each interpretation, what plays the role of the syntax, the runtime environment, the debugging strategy, and the ontological overhead. Then identify the points where the programming-domain features do not map onto physics—especially regarding empirical underdetermination, the absence of a compiler or user, and the status of probability. Finally, assess whether the conclusion that interpretation-choice is non-empirical survives once these disanalogies are exposed, and suggest whether a different analogy (e.g., coordinate systems, statistical mechanics, or model selection in data science) would do the argumentative work more honestly.
