I'm trying to figure out what my gut says about when uttering 'X is wrong' actually commits me to disapproving X, especially once you stick it inside weird sentences. Plain 'Hiding a prognosis from a patient is wrong' — yeah, I'm condemning it. 'If hiding a prognosis is wrong, then doctors shouldn't do it' — I can say that hypothetcally without actually condemning it; it feels like I'm just tracing a rule. But 'Either hiding a prognosis is wrong, or honesty is overrated' — that feels different, like I'm forced to take the first horn and thus condemn hiding it, even though it's buried in a disjuncion. Meanwhile 'It's not true that hiding a prognosis is wrong' sounds like I'm endorsing the hiding, not just being agnotic. 'My sister thinks hiding it is wrong, but I disagree' is totally cohearent. But 'Hiding a prognosis is wrong, though I don't disapprove of it' sounds like a Moore-paradox contradiction. There's also this argument form: 'If hiding is wrong then so is lying about it; hiding is wrong; therefore lying is wrong.' In the middle of that I do feel committed to the condemnation. So is there one principle behind all this? Maybe that I only express the attitude in bare assertions and in premises of arguments I accept, but not in conditionals or embedded reports? But then why does the disjunction drag it in? And I'm still going back and forth on this nested case: 'If either hiding is wrong or I'm irrational, and I'm not irrational, then hiding is wrong.' Would saying that commit me? I want either a principle that saves all my verdicts, or an honest call on which one I should drop, plus a prediction for that nested case.
