Hey — I'm putting together a seminar on American exeptionalism and ran into a weird conceptual knot. Two colleauges (not me, I'm just eavesdropping) got into it over whether exceptionalism is mainly a historical thesis, a normative claim, or an ideology. Here is the back-and-forth:

(1) A said it's a descriptve historical thesis: the US never had feudalism or a mass socialist party, so its political development started from genuinely different instituional conditions — that's just comparing starting points, not celebrating them.

(2) B replied that picking those absences already smuggles in progress: you're treating feudalism and socialism as the "normal" path and American deviation as special, which is Whig history running in reverse.

(3) A countered that describing what's missing isn't teleological — it's like saying this plant has no thorns, not that it's destined for greatness.

(4) B said the "no thorns" line still assumes thorns are the baseline, and framing absence as distinctiveness is exactly how normative exceptionalism hides inside descriptve language.

(5) A then floated this idea of "negative exceptionalism" — that America's identity is built more on what it never developed (no aristocracy, no state church) than on positive achievements — and argued that makes it an ideological construct rather than a real historical pattern.

(6) B said if exceptionalism is purely negative it's unfalsifiable, which makes it bad history but maybe perfect ideology: a rhetorical void you can fill with whatever you need.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each from clearly wrong to clearly right, justify briefly, and say who's closer on the thesis/norm/ideology distinction. Also, are they both missing something about how these three layers might actually work together rather than competing?
