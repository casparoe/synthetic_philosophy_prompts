I am giving the opening remarks—about five minutes—at an interdisciplinary workshop on collective belief formation. The audience is split evenly: formal modelers of cascade dynamics on one side, and science-studies scholars and policy staff who use “herding” only informally on the other. I want the remarks to welcome both groups without condescension, and I want the philosophical idea to do real structural work—shaping what I can and cannot claim about rationality and error—rather than serving as decoration.

Use this conceptual frame. In standard information-cascade models, rational agents suppress private signals once observed predecessors diverge, yielding an equilibrium in which public belief is uniform and wrong despite widespread private doubt. Add a specific twist—call it “reflexive cascade pressure”—in which each agent knows her own action will be observed by the very predecessors she is watching, and this second-order visibility changes the rational weight she assigns to her private signal. The cascade then stabilizes at a point where private dissent is actually stronger than in the first-order model, yet public unanimity is more rigid, because agents rationally fear being read as irrational dissenters rather than as cautious updaters.

Write the remarks so that this distinction structures the welcome: avoid claiming herding is merely irrational or that experts are immune, and close by posing the workshop’s guiding question—under what institutional conditions does reflexive cascade pressure become epistemically productive rather than corrosive? Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Begin with the speech itself, with no preface or commentary.
