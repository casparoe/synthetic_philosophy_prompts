I'm trying to understand why the AI existantial risk argument doesn't just apply to everything. Here's the version that bugs me:

(1) If there's a nonzero chance of extinction from misalligned AI, and future value is huge, then the expected loss dominates any benefit.
(2) But there's also a nonzero chance of extinction from a bioengineered pandemic, a fusion reactor meltdown, or an asteroid we deflect wrong.
(3) So the same expected-value logic should make us shut down all of those fields immediately.
(4) Nobody serious proposes that, not even the people who push AI risk.
(5) So either they're being selectivly paranoid, or there's a hidden premise that AI risk is different in kind.
(6) The usual answer is that AI is "agentic" risk—it's actively optimizing against us—while the others are just accidents.
(7) But agentic vs accidental seems like a distinction about intentions, not outcomes; from a counterfactual-casual view, a pandemic that evolves to maximize spread behaves exactly like an agent.
(8) So it looks like the argument either overgeneralizes to all tech, or it sneaks in "AI is special" without justification.

Which step should I reject? Is there a real difference between agentic and accidental extinction risks that saves the argument, or is the whole expected-value framing the problem? I feel like something slips between (6) and (7) but I can't name it.
