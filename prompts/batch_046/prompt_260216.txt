I'm auditing my own commitments on distributive justice for a philosophy seminar I'm taking, and I'd like a rigorous check on whether the package holds together rather than a general defense of luck egalitarianism. Here is where I've landed: (1) I accept the core thesis that justice requires neutralizing brute-luck disadvantages while respecting option-luck differences. (2) I draw the boundary not by actual choice but by normative control—an outcome counts as option luck only if the agent faced a genuinely open alternative that she could have taken without bearing costs that are disproportionate relative to the stakes. (3) I reject the expensive-tastes objection in its usual form: if a preference is socially cultivated but the agent retains the capacity to revise it, the resulting welfare deficit is still option luck. (4) At the same time, I maintain that macro-level structures—say, gendered labor-market norms—produce outcomes that are brute luck for individuals inside them, because the structural parameters themselves were never within any single agent's control, even when those individuals exercise local agency. (5) Finally, I think compensation should be bounded by what I call a responsibility-sensitive ceiling: full equalization of brute-luck deficits is unjust not merely for pragmatic reasons but in principle, because it obliterates the moral distinction between responsible and irresponsible paths and eliminates the informational function that unequal outcomes serve in a cooperative scheme.

The tension I'm most worried about is between (2)-(3) and (4). If gendered norms make resistance disproportionately costly, then by my own control criterion the resulting disadvantages seem to collapse into brute luck—so (4) is consistent with (2), but that seems to undercut (3)'s insistence that cultivated preferences stay on the option-luck side. Is there a principled way to assess control at the structural level without letting every locally costly choice become brute luck? A second worry is between (1) and (5): if justice demands full neutralization of brute luck, what principled work is the ceiling doing? Is it an internal constraint on the luck-egalitarian view or an external, perhaps liberal, addition? There may also be an apparent conflict between (3) and (4) that dissolves once we distinguish individual-level revision capacity from structural-level parameter control, but I can't tell whether that's the right move or just a way of hiding the problem.

Please treat this as an audit, not a lecture: rank the tensions by severity, specify for each exactly which commitment must be abandoned or modified and what that costs me conceptually, and clearly separate what is broadly settled in the literature (for instance, that the brute/option distinction is central to the view) from what is genuinely contested (for instance, whether structural luck can be accommodated without collapsing the distinction). I don't need references or a reading list—just a precise diagnosis of whether my package is consistent and, if not, the cheapest repair.
