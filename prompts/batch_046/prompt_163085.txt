I chair the scientific-advisory panel for a federal environmental-health agency. We review roughly twelve observational studies each quarter that claim a specific chemical or physical exposure caused a specific biological harm, and we must decide whether the evidence justifies a regulatory warning, restriction, or intervention. The stakes are high—false positives trigger costly remediation and public alarm, false negatives leave populations unprotected—while my panel has thirty minutes per study, no specialist philosophical training, and every recommendation must be signed off by a non-technical administrator. I want a reusable decision procedure, expressed as an ordered sequence of practical tests with clear stop points, derived from al-Ghazālī’s occasionalist critique of necessary causation: the view that nature contains no intrinsic necessary connections, that observed regularities are contingent habits sustained by direct divine creation of each event, and that miracles demonstrate these habits can be broken without logical contradiction. Translate this metaphysics into an audit protocol a reviewer can run without jargon: distinguish claims that depend on hidden assumptions of intrinsic causal power from claims that rest only on robust habitual conjunction; identify the empirical analogues of “miracle” cases—where the putative cause is present but the effect is absent, or the effect occurs without the cause; grade how severely the contingency of the link undermines action; and flag exactly where statistical reasoning ends and value judgment begins. The deliverable must include: (a) five to seven ordered questions with criteria for sufficient answers; (b) explicit stop points yielding “no regulatory action” and explicit hand-off points where the decision must go to human judgment because it involves ethical trade-offs rather than factual audit; (c) worked applications to three hypothetical studies—one linking a common pesticide to a rare neurological disorder in forty agricultural workers, one linking high-temperature workplace exposure to kidney failure in a cohort where dehydration is widespread, and one in which a strong statistical association between a food additive and a metabolic disorder is present but the procedure returns a recommendation against issuing a warning because the causal claim smuggles in an unstated mechanistic necessity that the data do not support; (d) an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do, such as establish metaphysical certainty, replace statistical inference, or resolve distributive-justice questions; and (e) a brief meta-section identifying which steps in your reasoning are most vulnerable to failure and what kind of counter-argument or empirical finding would cause you to revise the procedure’s design. Write in formal, polished prose, avoid bibliographic references, citations, or reading lists, do not quote any real text verbatim, and ensure the procedure is usable by someone with no background in medieval philosophy.
