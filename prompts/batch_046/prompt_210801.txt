I have been turning over an argument about pain asymbolia that now strikes me as suspicious, because if it succeeds it seems to show that no mental state can be intrinsically bad. Here is the chain:

(1) In pain asymbolia, subjects retain intact nociceptive processing and can discriminate location and intensity, yet report that the experience is not unpleasant and that they do not suffer.

(2) If pain is intrinsically bad solely in virtue of its sensory character—its particular qualitative feel—then anyone who instantiates that sensory character should have something bad happen to them.

(3) Pain asymbolics instantiate the sensory character without anything bad happening; hence the sensory character alone is not sufficient for badness.

(4) Suppose instead that pain is bad in virtue of an additional affective or evaluative component—call it the “disvalue marker”—that accompanies the sensory character in ordinary cases but is absent in asymbolia.

(5) If a mental state is intrinsically bad, then every token of that state-type must be bad; otherwise the badness is only contingent on accompanying features, not intrinsic to the state itself.

(6) In asymbolia, the state-type “pain with sensory character S” occurs without the disvalue marker and is not bad; therefore that state-type is not intrinsically bad.

(7) The same reasoning applies to any proposed intrinsic-badness account: identify the feature F that makes pain bad, then observe that asymbolics either have pain without F (if F is affective) or have F together with the sensory character yet still do not suffer (if F is sensory); either way, F fails to guarantee badness across all tokens.

(8) Hence no feature of pain is such that its presence makes the experience necessarily bad; pain is never intrinsically bad, only instrumentally bad (e.g., because it usually causes avoidance behavior).

(9) But if this method empties the concept of intrinsic badness for pain, it empties it for every mental state—including extreme anguish or terror—since we can always imagine a dissociation that removes the affective response while leaving the putative bad-making feature intact.

(10) That conclusion is absurd, so one of the steps must fail.

Where exactly does the argument slip? I am inclined to protect (5) and (6), but I want to know which premise or inference is the weak point, why it looked compelling, and what repairing it costs for representationalist, imperativist, and evaluativist theories of pain’s badness—especially given that the badness might depend on a conjunction or structural relation between sensory and affective elements rather than on any single isolable feature.
