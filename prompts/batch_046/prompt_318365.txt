Please write an essay on whether moral luck genuinely undermines the coherence of moral desert. Rather than rehearsing the familiar synchronic cases, direct your analysis towards what I term diachronic moral luck: situations in which an agent's moral standing is altered not by any change in their mental states or conduct at the relevant time, but by the later unfolding of events that recast prior intentions or character traits in a new evaluative light. You might consider, for instance, the whistle-blower whose cautious dissent is praised only after a subsequent catastrophe demonstrates its prescience, or the researcher whose negligent oversight is condemned only when a later replication crisis reveals its damage. Weigh the argument that such retroactive reassessment is a rational refinement of our moral framework against the argument that it betrays an unacceptable instability in our practices of attribution. Reach, and rigorously defend, a clear conclusion on whether diachronic moral luck constitutes a distinctive threat to desert-based morality, separate in kind from the standard synchronic difficulties raised by Nagel and Williams.
