I have recently taken over the conservation of my late grandmother’s ceramic studio, where several vessels were rebuilt using both surviving original clay fragments and modern synthetic binders that chemically integrate with the old material at the molecular level, and this practical responsibility has made me need a rigorous philosophical framework rather than an intuitive answer about whether the restored object is still her work. Before I assess any existing account, write the full desiderata for an adequate theory of material constitution in cases of gradual artifact replacement and functional transformation. Specify the phenomena it must explain—survival through discontinuous repair, identity across change in kind-use, and the relation between an enduring vessel and its successive material phases—the distinctions it must preserve (e.g., between being constituted by matter and being strictly identical to that matter, between artifact and artwork, between temporal parts and the enduring whole), and the constraints from metaphysics of artifacts or materials science it must respect. For each requirement, state the motivating case or consideration, flag any pair you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable—especially any tension between continuity conditions and essentialist intuitions about original material—and identify the single desideratum that eliminates the most popular theories. Then briefly note how the standard constitution view, a strict three-dimensionalist identity theory, and a process-ontological alternative fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria.
