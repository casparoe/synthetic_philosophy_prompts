I’m a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas sitting betwen biology and anthropology, and at a panel afterparty last week two researchers got stuck in a loop about Dawkins-style memes. Maya, who works on learning models, said treating culture as Darwinian replication is a category error: genes have high-fidelity copying and random mutation, but people deliberately alter ideas—tweak a recipe, edit a story—to match their goals. Calling that 'selection' just puts biological lipstick on human agency. Leo, an evolutionary anthroplogist, pushed back that fidelity only needs to be good enough for diffrential retention, not perfect. He brought up fishing-net designs across river basins: the knot structure stays, mesh size adapts to local fish, bad versions disappear, good ones spread.

Maya said that’s intentional design with feedback, not random mutation, and if you allow directed mutation then every human invention counts as 'evolution,' which makes the concept empty. Then she introduced 'memetic scaffolding'—her idea that what actually replicates isn’t the idea itself but the social structure that makes people want to copy and modify things in the first place. Leo said that’s just niche construction, the phenotype expressing the memotype, and it complicates the analogy without breaking it. They turned to me and asked who was right about what, and whether they’re both glossing over a bigger issue. I honestly don’t know. Can you adjudicate point by point—who’s right about fidelity, about directed mutation, about scaffolding versus niche construction—and say if there’s a deeper problem they’re missing about whether culture needs a Darwinian frame at all?
