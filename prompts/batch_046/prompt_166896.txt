After a lengthy argument with a friend about whether “Western values” denotes a coherent moral-political category or functions primarily as a movable boundary marker in geopolitical rhetoric, I want to map the logical space of positions systematically rather than by the labels people happen to wear. Please construct a decision-tree taxonomy—rooted in questions a participant could answer without already knowing the philosophical nomenclature—that sorts positions on three interlocked issues: whether the category is coherent at all; if it is coherent, whether that coherence is genealogical (derived from specific historical traditions that are jointly necessary) or normative (universalizable in principle but historically institutionalized most fully in the West); and whether the phrase operates in argument as a descriptive cluster, a prescriptive benchmark, or what I will call an “epistemic shield” (deflecting internal critique by framing dissent as un-Western).

Structure the tree so that each internal node is a single claim—phrased as a question a respondent could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to—that genuinely divides defenders. Each leaf should carry a descriptive name (or be marked “unnamed”) and exactly one sentence stating its most awkward commitment: the consequence its holders typically resist acknowledging. Build the tree from the logical combinations of answers first, then locate actual positions within that space. For any combination that remains unoccupied, briefly diagnose whether the vacancy is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. The tree must include and clearly separate at least the following: (i) the genealogical essentialist, for whom Judeo-Christian, Enlightenment, and Roman-legal lineages are jointly necessary conditions; (ii) the institutional cluster theorist, who treats liberal democracy, secular public reason, and individual autonomy as a family-resemblance set with no essential core but with a contested center; (iii) the normative universalist, who holds that the values are globally valid and the West happens to have institutionalized them most thoroughly; (iv) the rhetorical-debunking view, which holds the phrase is irreducibly strategic and collapses under analytical pressure; and (v) a view that accepts coherence but finds the cluster internally unstable—specifically, that a robust commitment to individual autonomy undermines collective self-determination in ways that make the category self-defeating. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and explain why the diagnostic questions fail to split them.

Keep the instrument suitable for a philosophy of politics seminar: do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not invoke obscure named theories that a well-read non-specialist could not be expected to recognize; and if you introduce a specific concept in a node, define it within that node rather than assuming prior knowledge. The point is to sort people by what they actually believe about the phrase’s coherence, content, and rhetorical function, not by which political camp they identify with.
