I'm working through notes for an essay on symmetric choice — specifically, whether Buridan's ass exposes a hidden structural condition in rational-choice theory rather than merely a failure of will. The framing is this: decision-theoretic formalisms (Savage-style, Jeffrey-style, or more recent causal decision theories) all require that a rational agent be able to identify the option it selects, not necessarily by superior value but by some feature that distinguishes the act of selecting it. In a case of perfect symmetry — two options with identical payoffs, identical causal profiles, and mirror-image spatial positions, with no internal randomizer available — the agent cannot form the distinguishing belief that this is the option it is now selecting without breaking the symmetry arbitrarily. The result isn't psychological paralysis but normative: the architecture of rational choice lacks the conditions for execution. I contrast this with physical symmetry breaking, where no justifiability constraint applies, and I argue that adding an exogenous tie-breaker (a coin flip, a timestamp hash) doesn't solve the problem but relocates it — it shows that rationality alone is incomplete. Third, is the conclusion — that perfect symmetry produces a normative deadlock rather than a practical one — substantive, or is it just a rebranding of the familiar indifference problem?
