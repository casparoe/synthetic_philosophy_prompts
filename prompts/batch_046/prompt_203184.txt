Please consider the following argument, which my study group constructed to show that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible, though I fear it proves far more—namely, that practical rationality has no determinate content whenever choices are hard. I should like to know which step fails, and at what philosophical price.

(1) A situation counts as a genuine moral dilemma only if an agent faces two or more mutually exclusive actions, each all-things-considered morally required, with no permissible omission.
(2) If an action is all-things-considered required, the agent has a decisive practical reason to perform it.
(3) Decisive practical reasons are comparable: given incompatible actions supported by reasons R and R', either R outweighs R', R' outweighs R, or they are exactly balanced.
(4) In the first two cases one action is required and the other forbidden; in the third, rational comparison yields no directive, so neither action can be all-things-considered required.
(5) Hence no situation satisfies (1): apparent dilemmas reduce to resolvable conflicts of prima facie duties or to rational indifference.
(6) Consider now a symmetric case: a physician with one dose of medicine and two patients whose needs and claims are identical in every morally relevant respect. By (3) the reasons are exactly balanced.
(7) By (4) neither saving patient A nor saving patient B is required; yet allowing both to perish is clearly impermissible.
(8) This seems contradictory: if saving A is not required, saving B is not required, and saving neither is forbidden, then there is no permissible course, violating the principle that ought implies can.
(9) To resolve the contradiction we must reject (4)'s claim that balance eliminates requirement: instead, both saving A and saving B are equally required, and the agent must choose one while bearing unavoidable moral loss for the other. This just is a genuine dilemma.
(10) But if (9) holds, then decisive reasons need not uniquely direct action; an agent can be required to do A and required to do B despite their incompatibility.
(11) Consequently (2) collapses: possessing a decisive reason to do A no longer entails that B is forbidden, for decisive reasons may conflict without resolution.
(12) If decisive reasons can conflict without resolution, then all-things-considered obligations become unstable—there is no single correct answer that practical rationality delivers.
(13) Thus we face a disjunction: either we deny genuine dilemmas and accept that symmetric conflicts impose no specific obligations (making it puzzling why letting both die is wrong), or we affirm them and dissolve the determinacy of practical reason altogether.

Which premise should be abandoned? I am particularly reluctant to surrender (1) or (9), since I wish to preserve both the coherence of moral requirements and the intuition that unavoidable moral loss is real. Please reply in British English, with careful reasoning and without offering bibliographies, citations, or lists of further reading.
