A university research-ethics board has published verdicts—always valid or invalid, with brief reasoning—on six informed-consent cases for clinical studies.

Case 1: A healthy adult consents to a low-risk vaccine study after being told there is “a small chance of temporary side effects,” without numerical probabilities. The board ruled valid, noting that qualitative risk framing is permissible for interventions of this magnitude.

Case 2: The same volunteer is told the study involves Drug A, but the compound administered is Drug B, which shares a trade name yet operates through a different mechanism. The board ruled invalid, holding that the identity of the intervention itself had been misrepresented.

Case 3: An unemployed participant is offered £400 for a study that otherwise pays nothing; they state plainly that they would not enrol without the payment. The board ruled valid, stating that economic inducement does not amount to coercion provided reasonable alternatives remain open.

Case 4: A patient with early-stage Alzheimer’s disease (MMSE 24) consents to a high-risk surgical trial. The board ruled invalid, observing that the risk profile exceeded the patient’s assessed decision-making capacity.

Case 5: A participant consents after the principal investigator, who is also their direct employer, suggests that enrolment would “reflect well on their commitment.” The board ruled invalid, identifying an undue influence arising specifically from institutional authority.

Case 6: A 17-year-old with a formal psychological assessment confirming adult-equivalent decision-making capacity consents to a minimal-risk observational study. The board ruled valid.

Now consider Case 7. A 21-year-old with moderate learning difficulties (assessed IQ approximately 65, able to understand concrete explanations but not abstract statistical reasoning) consents to a moderate-risk gene-therapy trial. The researcher explains all physical risks in plain, concrete language, yet describes the 25 per cent chance of permanent disability only as “unlikely to happen,” omitting the figure entirely. The participant requires £3,000—offered as study compensation—to avoid imminent eviction; they are not in debt to the researcher, who holds no authority over them, though a support worker (unaffiliated with the study) encouraged them to “think about the money.”

Predict the board’s verdict on Case 7. Identify at least two plausible governing principles that fit Cases 1–6 equally well yet diverge on Case 7, state what each predicts here, and give your best guess of the actual ruling with your reasons. Then specify the single further case that would best distinguish between these competing principles.

If you were recommending a ruling rather than predicting one, state which decision best coheres with the board’s own precedents, identify the closest analogues, and say honestly what principle the board would be adopting either way.
