I've been thinking on my own about how to judge whether I should really believe something when the arguments feel convincing up close but look suspicious from a distance—what people seem to call the inside view versus the outside view—and about how to practice real epistemic modesty rather than just feeling humble. I don't have academic training, but I've developed four mental strategies for contested moral or political questions, and I'd like you to rank them in tiers—S, A, B, C, or D—according to how effectively they correct for overconfidence rather than merely performing it. The first is the double-entry move: I separately list my best inside-view reasoning and the outside-view base rate for people like me being right about this kind of claim, then try to hold both figures in mind without letting one erase the other. The second is the reverse wager: I ask what betting odds would genuinely make me nervous to take either side, not the odds I would comfortably announce. The third is the ancestor's shadow: I imagine holding my belief two centuries ago with the prevailing intuitions of that era, and I check whether it would still feel self-evident or whether I'm mainly riding a historical wave. The fourth is the silent partner: I construct an imaginary opponent equally intelligent, honest, and informed who holds the opposite view, then attempt to explain why they are wrong without using phrases like "obviously" or "any reasonable person." Please assign each strategy to a tier and, in one or two sentences per item, say exactly why it lands there—specifically whether it pulls belief toward the truth, risks self-deception, or is mostly theater. I'm particularly interested in whether any of these four are actively worse than doing nothing, and if so, which one and why.
