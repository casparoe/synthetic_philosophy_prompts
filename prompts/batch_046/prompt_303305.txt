I'm designing the capstone session for my graduate seminar on metaphysics and the philosophy of mathematics, and I'd like an in-class simulation that forces students to inhabit the tension between actual and potential infinity rather than merely describing it. We are twelve students, ninety minutes, no technology beyond index cards and a whiteboard. The session should center on Hilbert's hotel, but I want to push past the standard "shift everyone up" parable: I want participants to discover that managing an infinite occupancy requires commitments about definability and constructibility that a finitist can reject without contradiction.

Design a structured activity with explicit roles, rules, and a debrief. Divide the group into Hotel Managers, who accept that a completed infinite set of rooms exists and can be rearranged as a totality, and Finitist Auditors, who accept only potential infinity—any assignment must be executable step-by-step for each particular guest, with no appeal to "the total mapping" as a completed object. Use index cards numbered 1, 2, 3, ... to represent rooms. Round One should reproduce the classic Hilbert scenario: full occupancy, one new arrival, Managers solve by shifting. Round Two should escalate: an infinite bus arrives with passengers labeled 1, 2, 3, ..., and Managers must assign each to a unique vacant room using only an explicit rule (no hand-waving). Then reveal a "predicative constraint"—every assignment for passenger n must be computable from n alone, without quantifying over the entire infinite set of prior assignments or the totality of rooms. Managers must now defend whether their rule survives, or whether they have smuggled in an actual infinity disguised as an algorithm. Round Three should present the Auditors with the Cantorian pairing intuition—that every infinite subset can be put in bijection with the naturals—and ask them to block the Managers' defense without simply denying arithmetic. The Managers should feel the pull of Cantorian reasoning, but the Auditors should be able to point out that "there exists a bijection" is not the same as "we have exhibited the assignment for each n."

The debrief should map each round onto specific philosophical positions: Hilbert's formalist acceptance of actual infinity as a regulative idea, Cantor's realist commitment to completed infinite sets, and finitist or predicativist objections—along constructivist lines—that such commitments outstrip what can be stepwise constructed. Include an honest assessment of what the card-game format cannot reproduce, such as the precise logical strength distinguishing countable from uncountable infinity in full ZFC, and tell me how to supply that missing rigor in discussion without undermining the experiential point. Also warn me what will predictably go wrong: likely, the Managers will invoke "common-sense" infinity and the Auditors will slip into mere denialism unless I frame their role at the outset as demanding constructive transparency rather than rejecting arithmetic itself.
