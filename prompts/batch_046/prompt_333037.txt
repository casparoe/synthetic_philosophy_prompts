I am drafting material for a postgraduate seminar on embedded agency and distributed cognition, and I would like you to help me work through a consistency problem concerning a particular review panel's implicit criteria. The panel — comprising philosophers of mind, cognitive scientists, and one systems biologist — has issued five published rulings on whether a given system exhibits embedded agency, understood here as the exercise of capacities that depend essentially on relations between an entity and its environment or constituent tools, where those capacities are not reducible to the properties of the isolated parts considered separately.

Their track record is as follows. First, they ruled that a human cyclist navigating complex urban traffic together with her well-fitted bicycle counts as an embedded agent: the navigational and balancing capacities in question belong to the coupled system, though neither the cyclist alone nor the bicycle alone possesses them. Second, they denied embedded agency to a chess grandmaster consulting a printed opening manual, describing the manual as an external storage device rather than a functionally integrated component of her reasoning process. Third, they affirmed that a termite colony constructing ventilation architecture through purely local chemical signalling counts as an embedded agent, despite the absence of any central controller and despite no individual termite possessing the relevant architectural knowledge. Fourth, they ruled that a human patient fitted with an adaptive deep-brain stimulator — which continuously modulates neural activity in response to real-time biofeedback loops — forms an embedded agent with the device, precisely because her emotional-regulation capacities are now constituted by the coupled system rather than by her neural tissue in isolation. Fifth, they denied embedded agency to an automated factory assembly line comprising both human workers and robotic conveyors arranged in a functional production sequence; they argued that, although the line is causally integrated, it remains a mere aggregation of separate agents and mechanisms lacking the unified normative responsiveness to its environment that characterises genuine embedded agency.

The panel must now deliver a ruling on a synthetic microbiological grid: engineered bacteria arranged in a lattice, communicating exclusively via quorum sensing, collectively altering chemical-output patterns in direct response to temperature gradients, with individual bacteria being fully replaceable without disrupting the collective behaviour, and with no central genome, controller, or persistent individual identity directing the response. Some members argue this is clearly analogous to the termite colony; others suggest it resembles the factory line, or perhaps the chess-manual case in that each bacterium is merely executing a local rule.

Please adopt the role of an expert philosopher of mind specialising in theories of distributed and embedded cognition. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming at least two distinct theoretical rules that could consistently explain all five prior rulings. Then predict what the panel should decide about the microbiological grid if they wish to remain consistent with themselves, identifying the closest precedent among the five and being explicit about whether your recommendation requires adopting a new principle. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, state clearly what principle each possible choice would commit them to, and specify one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the competing rules.
