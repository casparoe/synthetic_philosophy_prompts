The following reasoning about democratic binding seems valid, yet its conclusion—that constitutional democracy collapses into an impossible infinite hierarchy—strikes me as absurd.

(1) When an individual knows her future preferences will be systematically distorted—by craving, fatigue, or affective forecasting error—she may rationally adopt a Ulysses contract that restricts her later choice set.

(2) A democratic polity can be modeled as an intertemporal collective agent with a social preference ordering that aggregates preferences across generations.

(3) If future generations' preferences are likely to be distorted by temporary conditions—populist surges, security panics, resource shocks—the present generation ought to entrench constitutional provisions, such as rights protections or fiscal rules, that constrain those future choices.

(4) But the entrenchment mechanism itself—supermajority thresholds, judicial review architectures, amendment procedures—was designed by past generations who were themselves subject to temporary distortions.

(5) So if (1) licenses binding future agents, it also licenses binding the mechanism that binds them; we ought to have precommitted the amendment rules against being overturned by future distortions, then precommitted the rules governing those meta-rules, and so on.

(6) This generates an infinite regress of meta-entrenchment clauses that no constitutional architecture can implement.

(7) Therefore either democratic constitutionalism is rationally impossible, or the analogy between individual and collective precommitment in (2)-(3) collapses.

(8) I want to preserve both (1) and the normative pull of (3)—that some cross-generational precommitment is legitimate—without accepting (7).
