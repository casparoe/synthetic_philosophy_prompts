I am writing a dissertation on institutional decision-making under bounded rationality, and I have noticed a recurring pattern in regulatory and organizational case studies that I cannot place precisely within the existing taxonomy. Decision-makers begin with a vaguely specified aspiration level, search among available alternatives until one clears that level, and then halt—behavior that, taken alone, is classic satisficing. What interests me is the specific move that follows: when an auditor or external critic demonstrates that a clearly superior alternative existed within the same search space, the decision-makers do not defend their stopping point by appealing to cognitive limits, time constraints, or search costs. Instead, they retroactively sharpen the aspiration level—adding conditions that the chosen option uniquely satisfies and that the overlooked option fails—while insisting that this sharper criterion was operative from the start. The revision is not merely private self-deception; it functions as a public justification that renders the original search path normatively irreversible, because any further search is now framed as unnecessary by definition. I have examined the literature on adaptive aspiration levels in Cyert and March, on rationalization in cognitive dissonance theory, and on post-decisional justification in philosophy of action, but none of these capture the exact coupling of bounded search with the retroactive reconstruction of the stopping rule itself. Is there an established concept—perhaps in organizational sociology, philosophy of science, or the theory of practical reason—that describes precisely this two-stage structure? If not, how ought one distinguish it from ordinary satisficing on one side, and from strategic misrepresentation or motivated reasoning on the other? I am less concerned with the psychological mechanisms of self-deception than with the logical form of the justification: the way the boundary of "good enough" is redrawn ex post to coincide exactly with the option actually reached, and whether this introduces a distinct failure mode for bounded rationality that Simon's framework does not already cover.
