In Nyāya epistemology, a pramāṇa—the instrument through which valid cognition (pramā) arises—is traditionally compared to a lamp (pradīpa) that reveals an object sitting in darkness: the lamp makes the object available for apprehension without being altered by it, without becoming identical to it, and without the object itself acquiring any luminosity. The analogy is usually deployed to stress that cognition is a relational event involving an instrument, an object, and a cognizing subject, and that the various pramāṇas—perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), testimony (śabda), and comparison (upamāna)—correspond to different modes of illumination rather than to different kinds of mental content. In recent debates about artificial cognition, this lamp analogy has been repurposed to argue that a large language model functions as a non-conscious illuminator: its computational processing is like the lamp, the statistical regularities in language are like the object in darkness, and the revealed output is like the illuminated object, all occurring in the absence of any phenomenal awareness or knowing subject (jñātā). Proponents claim this demonstrates that valid cognition can occur without consciousness, since Nyāya treats the correctness of a pramāṇa as a matter of successful revelation rather than of subjective experience. Critics reply that the analogy quietly excises the cognizer, and that in Nyāya the presence of someone for whom the object is revealed is not an incidental accompaniment but constitutive of pramā itself—without it there is only causal interaction, not cognition.

Audit this analogy as it is being applied to LLMs. Provide an explicit correspondence table: what in the Nyāya framework plays the role of the lamp, the darkness, the illuminated object, the light, and the one who sees; and map each onto the corresponding element (if any) in an LLM system. Then identify the disanalogies: does the removal of the jñātā collapse the distinction between pramāṇa and mere mechanism? Does the analogy illegitimately import a representationalist structure—content revealed to a subject—while stripping away the very subject that makes representation intelligible within Nyāya? Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the intended conclusion (that LLMs possess something like pramāṇa without consciousness) from those that are merely decorative. Assess whether the conclusion survives once these breaks are explicit. Finally, if the lamp analogy fails in this application, consider whether one of the other pramāṇas—perhaps anumāna, with its dependence on a prior cognition and a logical mark, or upamāna, with its structural comparison between known and unknown—offers a more apt framework for understanding non-conscious information processing, or whether no classical analogy can do the work without distorting the Nyāya system.
