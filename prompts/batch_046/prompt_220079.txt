Having retired from a career in physical chemistry, I have lately taken up philosophy of language with the same mixture of fascination and exasperation that I once reserved for recalcitrant crystallography. I am particularly struck by Putnam’s Twin Earth argument for semantic externalism, yet I find its usual presentation—dry thought-experiment followed by a tidy distinction between narrow and wide content—unsatisfyingly tidy. I should like you to compose a satirical government travel advisory, written in the deadpan, bureaucratic register of a Foreign Office briefing, for British scientists planning fieldwork on Twin Earth. The advisory must treat semantic externalism not as a philosophical thesis but as an established law of nature: warn travellers that the term water denotes XYZ rather than H₂O; note that mental states remain internally identical despite the shift in reference; and advise that customs officials now require visitors to declare both their physical luggage and the narrow content of their intentions upon arrival. The satire should quietly expose the familiar tensions—that if meaning lies outside the head, self-knowledge becomes oddly elusive, and that the boundary between psychological description and chemical analysis begins to blur—without ever stating them as explicit objections. To keep the exercise conceptually sharp, please inject one specific, somewhat niche complication: model the interaction between the visitor’s Earth-formed linguistic expectations and the Twin-Earth community as a feedback loop that gradually alters the traveller’s own psychological taxonomy, so that upon return she cannot reliably determine whether her thoughts are directed at H₂O or XYZ. The piece should run to approximately 600 words, be composed in British English, and stand entirely on its own conceptual feet; I have no need of bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
