I've been working through Dennett's framework of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—with a study group, and we keep returning to the constellation analogy. The claim, as it is usually deployed in these discussions, is that beliefs and desires stand to neural dynamics roughly as Orion stands to its constituent stars: not physically fundamental, not carved at the joints of nature, but genuinely predictive and projectible from the right perspective. The analogy is used to defend a middle-ground position—mental states are real patterns in precisely the sense that constellations are real patterns—which licenses treating the intentional stance as more than a mere instrumental fiction without committing us either to dualism or to an eliminativist reduction of folk psychology to physics. How far does this mapping actually hold?

I would like an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the individual stars, the spatial and gravitational relations among them, the observer's line of sight, the cultural history of the figure, and the predictive utility of recognizing the shape? Then the disanalogies: where does the mapping break, and which breaks matter for the intended anti-reductionist conclusion? In particular, does the constellation analogy quietly import features—such as the arbitrary cultural origin of the figure, or the absence of causal powers residing in the constellation itself—that undermine the claim that intentional states do genuine explanatory and causal work? Please separate the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from those that carry argumentative weight. Finally, does the conclusion that mental states are real but not physically basic survive once these breaks are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the entire burden? If the constellation comparison collapses under scrutiny, what would be a more robust analogy for real patterns in the intentional domain—one that preserves predictive utility without smuggling in the wrong kind of observer-dependence?
