Right, I've been stuck on this argument my tutor threw at me and I can't see the exit. (1) Leibniz principle says if two things share every property they're the same thing. (2) Black's spheres are exactly alike except one is left of the other. (3) But being-left-of isn't an intrinsic feature of either sphere, it's just a relation between them. (4) So they share every intrinsic property and stand in symetric relations, meaning they're genuinely indescernible. (5) Hence they should be identical, which is absurd, so PII is false.

But here's where it runs away with me. (6) The same logic applies to any pair where only relations differ. (7) Take two electrons in a helium atom: same charge, spin, mass, and their positions are just relational coordinates in the wavefunction. (8) If spatial separation doesn't count as an intrinsic distinguisher, then these electrons are indescernible too. (9) So by the Black-spheres reasoning they ought to be one electron, but quantum mechanics clearly allows two. (10) To block this I need either to say relations do distinguish them after all — which saves quantum mechanics but brings back Black's spheres as identical — or I need to accept primitive thisness (haecceities) for electrons but not spheres, which feels totally ad hoc. (11) So the argument that kills PII for spheres seems to either collapse quantum mechanics or force me into haecceitism I don't want.

I feel like there's a slide between (3) and (4), or maybe between (8) and (9), but I can't name it. Which step do you actually reject, and what does giving it up cost? I'd especially like to keep (2)-(4) intact if possible, and I'm suspicious of any answer that just says 'quantum mechanics is different' without saying why.
