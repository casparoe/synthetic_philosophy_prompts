I am presenting a job-market paper at a philosophy-of-logic and AI-ethics workshop whose audience consists mainly of modal logicians and computer scientists specializing in epistemic logic. My thesis is that logical omniscience should not be eliminated by weakening the knowledge operator—replacing it with belief, awareness, or impossible-worlds structures—but should instead be retained as a normative ideal for artificial agents, provided the closure rules are restricted to deductions computable in polynomial time. I argue that if we distinguish sharply between an agent’s epistemic state (the propositions it knows) and its computational access (the resources available to derive them), omniscience becomes a feature of the idealization rather than a descriptive failure; for engineered systems, I claim this distinction is constitutive, because an AI should be said to “know” a logical consequence only when that consequence is reachable within its bounded inference architecture.

The audience is thoroughly versed in Hintikka-style semantics, standard real-agent objections, and recent non-omniscient epistemic logics for multi-agent systems. They are skeptical of normative idealizations in applied settings and tend to treat resource bounds as implementation-layer constraints that have no place in the logic of knowledge itself. Several have published on awareness-based resolutions and view complexity-theoretic restrictions as arbitrary truncations of logical closure.

Prepare me for the objections I will actually face: state them in the vocabulary this audience uses—e.g., charges of conflating epistemic logic with computational complexity theory, of smuggling an implicit awareness parameter back into the semantics, or of confusing the logic of knowledge with the engineering of inference engines. For each, indicate which have genuine force and should be conceded up front, which I must hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the single reply that must land for the proposal to survive, and include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—specifically, the challenge that polynomial-time closure is not “logical” closure at all but merely a pragmatically motivated cutoff that abandons the very concept of deduction. Use the argument sketch above as the target; do not request a bibliography or further reading list.
