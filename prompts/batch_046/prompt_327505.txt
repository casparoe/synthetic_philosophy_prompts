I've been preparing for a graduate seminar on determinism and scientific worldviews, and I keep running into what seems like an unacknowledged tension in Einstein's position that I can't square. On one hand, he insisted that human beings possess no free will in any meaningful sense—he described our actions as fully determined by causal forces that lie beyond conscious control. On the other hand, he rejected the statistical interpretation of quantum mechanics with his well-known remark that God does not play dice, which sounds like a commitment to strict physical determinism. What's puzzling is that he also described himself as holding Spinoza's conception of God: not a personal deity who answers prayers, but the rational order of nature itself, expressed through immutable law. So it seems his denial of free will and his rejection of quantum randomness both stem from the same source—namely, that if nature (or God) operates through necessary rational laws, then there is simply no causal gap in which an uncaused human will could operate. In other words, wasn't Einstein's determinism fundamentally theological, grounded in a pantheistic conviction that everything, including human action, unfolds according to the rational necessity of divine nature? And if that's right, doesn't his view collapse into a strange contradiction: if God is identical with the totality of nature, and human beings are part of that totality, then saying humans lack free will is really just saying that God lacks free will, since our actions are nothing other than God acting deterministically through us? I'd like to understand whether there's a coherent way to read Einstein here without reducing his physics to theology, or whether the standard interpretations miss that his determinism was always meant as a religious rather than scientific claim.
