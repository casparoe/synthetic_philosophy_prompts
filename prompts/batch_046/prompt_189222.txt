I am writing a novel in which a character undergoes corpus callosotomy and subsequently exhibits the divided hemispheric awareness documented in neuropsychological literature, yet continues to narrate her existence in an unbroken first-person singular, with no phenomenological sense of co-authorship or internal rivalry. From this I have adopted the view that split-brain patients possess one consciousness that is informationally partitioned but not ontologically divided—specifically, that the unity of consciousness is constituted not by integrated access to information but by singular narrative authorship: there is one autobiographical subject precisely so long as one continuous grammatical “I” governs the life-story, regardless of which hemisphere initiates a given action. Tell me what would count decisively against this position—not merely the standard demonstrations of hemispheric independence, but which neuropsychological findings, which first-person phenomenological reports, and which philosophical results about diachronic personal identity it genuinely risks. Which of these defeaters would a defender of singular narrative authorship concede as damaging, and which could be dismissed by insisting that narrative unity is a higher-order achievement that survives hemispheric dissociation? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and assess whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that insulation is a virtue for a concept meant to ground literary character, or a vice.
