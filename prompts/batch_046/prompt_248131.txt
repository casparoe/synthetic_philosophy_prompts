Consider the widespread institutional and discursive practice of grouping Norwegian corporate board gender quotas, Indian constitutional reservation mandates for Scheduled Castes and Tribes, and American university admissions preferences under the single category of "quotas" or "affirmative action," and then evaluating all three with a shared vocabulary of merit, fairness, discrimination, and equality of opportunity. The Norwegian law requires roughly forty percent representation of each gender on public limited company boards without any reference to historical guilt or compensatory justice; the Indian system is anchored in constitutional provisions allowing special measures for backward classes and operates through hard percentage mandates in education and public employment as explicit redress for caste-based exclusion; American university practice, shaped by legal restrictions on explicit racial quotas, has largely migrated to a "diversity" rationale that treats race-conscious selection as an educational good rather than as rectification of past harm. What theory of distributive justice, or of the moral status of group membership, does this conflation presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical framework that makes it coherent to discuss all three with the same normative concepts, distinguish what that framework strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether the shared vocabulary actually depends on a single unified theory or is instead philosophically underdetermined—perhaps smuggling together representational parity, historical rectification, and pedagogical benefit without noticing their incompatibility. Then describe what would have to change in policy design and public justification if we abandoned the unified category and treated each system according to its own distinct rationale.
