my friend keeps saying that watching a tragedy is basically like riding a rollercoaster or walking through a haunted house: you pay to enter a container you know is safe, you feel the fear or sadness for real, then you walk out and its over. he uses this to dissolve the paradox of tragedy -- there's no contradiction in loving Oedipus Rex or a requiem because the negative feeling is just a thrill in costume, safely framed. im not convinced the container works that cleanly. can you lay out the mapping precisely -- whats the safety harness, whats the ticket booth, whats the post-ride relief? then show where it fractures: in rollercoasters the negative affect is designed to resolve in pleasure, but tragic sadness often lingers for days and we seem to value that residue; also people watch Schindlers List when they're already down, not just for kicks. does the analogy smuggle in the premise that all negative affect in art is instrumental to a pleasure payoff, and does that survive once you notice we sometimes seek out sorrow itself? if the rollercoaster picture breaks, is there a better analogy -- maybe tragedy as a mourning ritual, or an emotional vaccine, or something closer to exercise for empathy -- and which one actually handles the lingering effect? i want the correspondence table, the disanalogies that matter versus the ones that dont, and whether his conclusion -- no paradox, just safe thrills -- holds up or collapses.
