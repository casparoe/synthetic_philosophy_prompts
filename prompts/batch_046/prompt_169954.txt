I have been drafting a novel set in the psychiatric wards of New York in the early 1970s, and my research into the 1973 American Psychiatric Association vote to remove homosexuality from the DSM-II has left me with a conceptual puzzle I should like to examine more rigorously. The episode is well documented: the shift from pathologising same-sex orientation to recasting it, in DSM-III, only within a category of ego-dystonic homosexuality, the compromise brokered by Robert Spitzer, and the simultaneous institutional claim that this was a scientific correction rather than a moral or political concession. I should like you to analyse this reversal through the lens of the naturalism versus normativism debate about health and disease — not merely to classify the two sides, but to treat the vote itself as a case study in conceptual taxonomy. What does a strict biostatistical naturalist have to say about whether the APA discovered a fact about human functioning or merely revised a normative judgment about acceptable deviation? How does the normativist account for the persistence of that ego-dystonic category — is it an admission that dysfunction is inseparable from the patient’s own values, or does it expose a hidden naturalist commitment to statistical rarity? I want the analysis to run in both directions: show where the theory illuminates the actors’ reasoning and where it fails to capture the institutional dynamics — the pressure from activists, the internal professional anxiety about scientific credibility, the strategic ambiguity of Spitzer’s wording — and say explicitly where the framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the conceptual mapping alone, and the answer should be accessible to a reader who knows the historical outline but has not specialised in philosophy of medicine.
