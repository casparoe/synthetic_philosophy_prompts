I'm writing a long-form feature on the bioethical frontier that some researchers call the hedonistic imperative—the proposal that biotechnology should be directed not merely at curing disease but at eliminating suffering itself, and perhaps at re-engineering the affective architecture of conscious beings. In reporting this, I've developed a set of gut-level verdicts on concrete cases, and I want to see whether they point to a coherent principle or merely to a cluster of inconsistencies I haven't faced.

Here are my judgments. Case one: offering consenting adults a reversible neural implant that suppresses nociceptive signaling entirely, while preserving cognition and the capacity to opt out, strikes me as not merely permissible but arguably obligatory—if we can remove suffering without damaging agency, withholding it seems negligent. Case two: mandating prenatal genetic editing that eliminates the neurobiological substrates for grief, existential dread, and deep attachment strikes me as wrong in a way I can't fully justify; my resistance isn't that these states are pleasant, but that preemptively excising them seems to violate something about the integrity of human emotional range. Case three: a pharmaceutical compound that flattens affective range so that peak joy becomes indistinguishable from mild contentment is acceptable, in my view, only in terminal palliative care; deploying it broadly feels like a category mistake, though I can't tell whether the mistake is moral, aesthetic, or metaphysical. Case four: using viral gene drives to re-engineer wild predators into herbivores and to suppress fear-responses in prey strikes me as impermissible, yet I suspect my resistance may rest on an unexamined bias toward "natural" suffering rather than on a principled distinction from clinical pain relief. Case five: a technology that doesn't suppress suffering but transforms one's cognitive relation to it—allowing pain to be registered as neutral information—strikes me as preferable to elimination, which suggests my objection isn't to the absence of suffering per se but to the manner of its absence, specifically to what I might call the loss of "evaluative range," the capacity for experience to carry contrasting valence.

Is there a single principle that delivers all five verdicts, or must I choose between a simpler account that overturns at least one judgment and a more complex one that saves them all? Does the principle turn on consent, on reversibility, on a distinction between somatic and existential suffering, on the preservation of evaluative range, or on something else entirely? And what should I conclude about the scenario I keep returning to: a global, non-coercive but universal program that gradually lowers baseline affect to mild contentment for all sentient beings, achieving a "zero-suffering equilibrium" without anyone's explicit consent but with universal and irreversible benefit?
