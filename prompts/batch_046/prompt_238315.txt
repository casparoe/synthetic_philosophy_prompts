Exam Question:
Consider the Nyāya theory of pramāṇas (valid means of cognition), focusing on upamāna (comparison or analogy). Some philosophers argue that upamāna can be fully reduced either to anumāna (inference) operating on perceived similarities, or to pratyakṣa (perception) mediated by linguistic convention. Classical Naiyāyikas, by contrast, treat upamāna as an autonomous pramāṇa.

Write an analytical essay addressing:
(a) The standard Nyāya account of how upamāna produces valid cognition, with attention to sādṛśya (similarity) and the cognition of a word-object relation (e.g., recognizing a gavaya as the referent of that term after being told it resembles a cow).
(b) The specific feature that defenders claim distinguishes upamāna from anumāna—particularly regarding the absence of a liṅga (probans) and vyāpti (invariable concomitance)—and whether this defense succeeds.
(c) One cross-traditional or modern objection: either that upamāna collapses into perceptual judgment mediated by convention, or that it presupposes an unreduced notion of sāmānya (universal) that Nyāya struggles to justify without circularity.

Student Answer:
Upamāna in Nyāya is the cognition of a relation between a word and its object that arises through similarity (sādṛśya). For example, if a person who has never seen a gavaya (a wild bovine) is told that it resembles a domestic cow, and she subsequently encounters one in the forest, she recognizes the creature and thereby grasps the application of the term "gavaya." This cognition is not an instance of anumāna, because there is no liṅga (probans) or vyāpti (invariable concomitance) linking the perceived properties to the conclusion; instead, one apprehends the resemblance directly, guided only by prior testimony.

The result is a distinct epistemic achievement: knowing that this particular object is the referent of that word. Because direct sensory contact with the object is present, one might think upamāna collapses into pratyakṣa mediated by language. But Nyāya resists this, since perception alone yields cognition of the object’s properties, not of its linguistic designation. Thus upamāna occupies a middle ground, combining testimony and perception without being reducible to either.

That said, the autonomy of upamāna depends on accepting Nyāya realism about universals (sāmānya). If recognizing similarity requires prior cognition of a shared universal—such as "cowness"—then upamāna presupposes a theory of universals that is itself controversial. Buddhist epistemologists reject such universals as metaphysically redundant, and some modern critics argue that Nyāya’s account of universal perception is circular: we perceive the universal only because we already classify instances together, yet this classification is precisely what upamāna is supposed to explain. Even so, this circularity concerns the metaphysical groundwork rather than the epistemic structure of upamāna itself, so it does not show that upamāna is reducible to anumāna. Consequently, upamāna remains an irreducible pramāṇa, though its defense requires a prior commitment to Nyāya ontology.
