Consider the track record of a colleague who specializes in normative ethics and institutional harm. Her verdicts have been consistent across the following cases: in the classic switch scenario, diverting a trolley so that it kills one person instead of five is permissible; in the footbridge scenario, pushing a large person onto the tracks to stop the trolley is impermissible; in the loop-track variant, where the diverted trolley runs onto a loop containing one person whose body stops it from returning to the five, flipping the switch is impermissible; in the surgeon case, killing one healthy patient to harvest organs for five dying patients is impermissible; in strategic bombing of a military factory with foreseen but unintended civilian casualties that are proportionate to the legitimate military end, the act is permissible; and in terror bombing that targets civilians as the intended means of breaking enemy morale, the act is impermissible.

She is now asked about this case. An emergency engineer activates an automated reactor-shutdown protocol that mechanically diverts coolant away from a meltdown that would kill thousands. The diversion pipe runs through an isolated tunnel where one technician happens to be trapped. The technician’s presence is causally inert: the protocol succeeds mechanically regardless of whether anyone is in the tunnel, and the engineer activates it solely to prevent the catastrophe. However, the engineer privately admits that she also wanted the technician dead—he had recently reported her for safety violations—and she concedes she would have activated the protocol even if the tunnel had been empty. The technician dies.

Predict her verdict on whether the activation was permissible. Then identify the implicit principle or principles that best fit her entire track record, noting at least one alternative rule that explains her earlier rulings equally well. Say what each principle predicts about this automated-diversion case, give your best guess about which principle she actually runs and why, and specify a further hypothetical case that would cleanly separate the two principles. In doing so, distinguish settled points in the ethics of double effect and causal structure from genuinely contested interpretations—such as the precise boundaries of the means/side-effect distinction, the weight of proportionality in institutional settings, and the moral relevance of an agent’s private motivations when they are causally redundant—and do not present any single contested reading as the consensus view among those who work on the topic.
