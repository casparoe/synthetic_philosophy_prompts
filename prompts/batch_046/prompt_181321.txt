I have been working through my intuitions about belief revision under what I will call structurally ambiguous evidence—situations in which not only the hypotheses but also the evidential weights, the reliability of sources, and even the partition of possibilities are themselves uncertain. I want to know whether a single extension of Jeffrey conditionalization can capture my verdicts, or whether I am forcing coherence onto incompatible intuitions.

Case 1: I observe a faint spectral anomaly and initially distribute credibility 40/60 between instrumental noise and a genuine physical effect. I then learn that the instrument’s false-positive rate is near 30%, but I do not know whether that rate applies uniformly or is elevated specifically for anomalies of this kind. My verdict is that I should revise my beliefs, yet treating the 30% figure as a standard likelihood ratio in Jeffrey’s rule feels wrong; instead, I want to redistribute my prior over sub-partitions of the noise hypothesis according to how the rate applies locally.

Case 2: A domain expert reports a precise posterior over a set of competing models. I have independent, imprecise beliefs about whether she is affected by a known cognitive bias, but I do not know the bias’s prevalence or its directional effect in this domain. My verdict is that I should not apply a fixed discount factor to her report; rather, I should expand my state space to include possible bias-states, condition on the report within each state, and then average over the bias-state space using my second-order beliefs—something like Jeffrey conditioning with an uncertain evidential mapping.

Case 3: I receive two reports that conflict. I can assign each a reliability score, but the conflict itself seems informative about the correlation structure between the sources, perhaps indicating a hidden shared dependency. My verdict is that updating should revise not only the hypotheses but also my beliefs about that correlation structure, which in turn feeds back into the weights assigned to the reports.

Case 4: I learn that one hypothesis in my original partition is not merely unlikely but conceptually incoherent given other commitments I hold. My verdict is that standard Jeffrey conditioning, which presupposes a fixed partition, should not apply; instead, I should reconstruct the partition, redistribute prior mass over the remaining coherent cells, and then condition—rather than leaving the incoherent cell with zero weight inside the old frame.

Is there a coherent formal principle—perhaps Jeffrey conditioning over a space of partitions, or conditioning with an uncertain evidential kernel—that delivers all four verdicts? Please state it precisely, identify which clause addresses which case, predict its verdict on a scenario I have not mentioned (for instance, updating on a prediction-market price that aggregates both informed and noise traders), and be honest about whether the principle requires a genuinely new formalism or collapses into a simpler Bayesian rule once properly formulated. If one of my verdicts must be abandoned to preserve coherence, say which one and why.
