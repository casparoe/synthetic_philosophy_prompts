In my philosophy of science seminar we have been discussing structural realism, and I noticed a puzzling divergence in how different physics subfields present their results institutionally. In high-energy physics, major experimental collaborations publish data using what they call “model-independent” parameterizations: they report correlation functions, decay branching ratios, and cross-sections, and their official data releases and press protocols deliberately avoid attributing causal properties or ontological status to whatever produced the signals. The institutional norm is to present the mathematical structure of the observations while remaining officially agnostic about underlying entities. By contrast, in condensed-matter physics, researchers routinely speak of quasiparticles—phonons, holes, anyons—as entities with genuine causal powers, and patent filings for new materials treat microscopic mechanisms as real targets of engineering, with no comparable institutional reluctance about ontology.

What philosophy of scientific knowledge does that divergence presuppose? Does the high-energy publication norm strictly require structural realism—the view that we can know only relational structures and must suspend judgment about unobservable entities—or is it compatible with rival positions, such as entity realism combined with a merely pragmatic policy of caution? Reconstruct the implicit epistemological and ontological commitments embedded in the high-energy practice, and distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then say whether the two subfield norms are philosophically consistent with one another, or whether they quietly rely on incompatible theories of what scientific knowledge amounts to. Finally, if a funding agency mandated that all physics publications include explicit ontological commitments about the entities studied, what would have to change in current high-energy practice, and would structural realism remain a defensible live option?
