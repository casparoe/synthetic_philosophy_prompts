Examination question:

In Confucian role ethics, moral obligations are said to arise from one's position within a network of relationships rather than from abstract universal rules. Consider the following case. Dr. Lin is a deputy minister in a national health agency. She learns that her adult son, employed at a private biotechnology firm, has made an unauthorized alteration to a clinical-trial dataset. The alteration is minor, creates no patient risk, but violates mandatory reporting protocols. Her family urges her to suppress the discovery; her professional role demands recusal and disclosure to regulators. Analyze this conflict through the lens of Confucian role ethics. Specifically: (1) Are filial piety and bureaucratic loyalty genuinely in conflict, or do they share a common root? (2) Can the concept of empathic reciprocity — extending one's feelings from close relations outward — resolve the tension without reducing either obligation to instrumental calculation? (3) Does the principle of graded love, by which obligations diminish with relational distance, determine which duty takes precedence here?

Student answer:

Confucian ethics is best understood as a form of virtue ethics centered on individual character cultivation through ritual practice. In this case, Dr. Lin occupies two roles — mother and minister — that generate obligations of filial piety and bureaucratic loyalty. Both obligations are expressions of humaneness, so they share a common root and are not fundamentally opposed, only in practical tension.

First, the classical teaching that the family is the root of the state implies that family obligations have lexical priority over state obligations. Therefore, Dr. Lin should protect her son, though she ought to do so with sincerity rather than through outright deception.

Second, empathic reciprocity resolves the remaining tension. This concept requires putting oneself in another's position and applying the same judgment universally. If Dr. Lin were her son, she would want protection; if she were her superior, she would want disclosure. Because reciprocity demands a single standard applied to all, she must report the violation, since transparency is what she would demand from anyone in her position. This avoids treating ethics as mere cost-benefit reasoning.

Third, graded love confirms the resolution. Because obligations diminish with relational distance, Dr. Lin's son is closer to her than the abstract public, so filial piety should prevail in principle. At the same time, her bureaucratic role creates a specific duty. The appropriate balance is to fulfill filial piety privately by advising her son to self-report, while maintaining bureaucratic loyalty by refraining from active obstruction of the investigation. This integrates both roles without reducing either to an instrumental means.
