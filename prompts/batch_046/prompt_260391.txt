I teach a 90-minute advanced metaethics seminar with twelve students seated in a circle; we are working through the metaphysics of normativity and the problem of reasons without reasoners. I want an in-class activity — a structured deliberation or role-play with explicit rules, assigned roles, and a non-monetary scoring mechanism — that makes students feel the force of free-floating rationales: the idea that normative reasons can bind agents even when no mind authored them, no social practice instituted them, and no recognition can ground them. To avoid a generic "objective values" discussion, frame the exercise around orphaned obligations: students must collectively resolve a resource-allocation problem using principles that the rules present as discovered rather than invented, with no originator available to authorize them. The design should ensure that students run into the problem through the incentives — for instance, coordinating on an outcome they individually find unmotivated because the governing reason is not theirs — before anyone names the concept. Include a debrief that maps specific behavioral moments onto the metaphysical structure (e.g., the moment a student cites a rule she cannot attribute to any person or practice), state plainly what feature of free-floating rationalism the activity necessarily fails to reproduce (the absence of any causal or intentional bridge between reason and agent), and explain how you will supply that gap in the closing discussion. Please commit to one specific architecture rather than surveying options, and defend why that architecture captures the distinctive "free-floating" character of reasons rather than merely hidden or unknown objective facts. Finally, predict exactly what will go wrong the first time — likely a collapse into ordinary interpersonal moral disagreement — and specify the in-the-moment correction that restores the frame without breaking it.
