I am convening an adversarial collaboration between two camps in anthropic reasoning: proponents of the Self-Sampling Assumption, who maintain that rational credence should be distributed uniformly over an observer’s actual reference class; and proponents of the Self-Indication Assumption, together with critics of SSA who press the presumptuous philosopher objection, who hold that the total number of observers in a possibility should enter the prior multiplicatively and that treating oneself as randomly drawn from actual observers can yield unwarranted cosmological confidence. Draft the joint statement they should produce. Structure it as follows: first, a numbered list of precise, non-vacuous claims about observer selection, reference-class boundaries, and the logic of indexical updating that both sides genuinely accept; second, a table of the exact residual disagreements, with each position rendered in neutral wording that the opposing camp would recognize as fair and that avoids attributing hidden motives; third, the cruxes—those sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side—specifically (a) whether the presumptuous philosopher reveals a genuine formal reductio of SSA or only a misapplication of reference-class boundaries, (b) whether deliberative symmetry requires a predictor and an observer to apply identical indexical reasoning, and (c) whether a constructed scenario we may call the Mirror Deliberation Case distinguishes the two assumptions in a way not captured by standard urn or multiverse examples. In that case, a predictor knows she will instantiate exactly one of two deliberative architectures, A or B, each programmed to output a report on the prior probability of architecture A; she receives such a report but possesses no indexical information identifying which architecture produced it. Both sides should agree that this case bears on crux (b), record in advance what SSA and SIA respectively predict about her rational belief update, and state whether they anticipate convergence or divergence. For each crux, include one concrete canonical test—such as the two-urn problem with unknown total populations, or the comparative observation of a large versus small cosmological universe—stating what logical or empirical result both sides agree is relevant and what each camp predicts. The document should be formal, should contain no bibliographic references, citations, or appeals to authority, and should not direct readers to external literature; its purpose is to determine whether the disagreement is ultimately formal, pragmatic, or rooted in an unstated normative commitment about deliberation itself.
