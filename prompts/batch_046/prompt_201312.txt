After a long and rather exhausting argument last night with a friend who works in museum conservation, I remain unsettled about whether a statue and its clay are one thing or two, and I want you to write a formal dialogue that pushes the problem into genuinely unfamiliar territory rather than replaying the standard Ship of Theseus. The interlocutors—call them Althea and Bion—should be equally competent. Althea holds a strict material-essentialist view: the statue just is that specific continuous body of clay, so if conservators eventually replace every molecule, what remains is at best an authorized replica, regardless of visual fidelity. Bion holds a hylomorphic-institutional view: the statue is essentially its form together with its authorized provenance-chain, so gradual material replacement is unproblematic as long as institutional continuity and shape are preserved. Now inject this complication: the statue’s form is partly constituted by an external digital twin—a high-resolution photogrammetric mesh tied to a persistent museum URI—that did not exist at the work’s creation and that can now be used to regenerate destroyed physical parts. Althea should argue that this digital constituent undermines Bion’s position, because if form can be outsourced to a non-physical registry, then the clay was never essential, but neither was the original artistic act itself. Bion should reply that the twin is only a record, not a constituent, but then must face Althea’s counter that in contemporary practice the registry causally governs which interventions are authorized, so it now functions as part of the identity conditions. They should respond to each other’s actual moves, avoid talking past one another, and end without a cheap resolution. Keep the exchange rigorous but natural, do not ask for bibliographies or citations, and do not rely on niche theories that require specialist knowledge beyond what is laid out here.
