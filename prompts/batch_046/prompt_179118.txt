I work in software engineering, and I am struck by a pattern in how our industry governs large language models. Companies routinely evaluate the same underlying model on agentic benchmarks—autonomous tool use, multi-step planning, self-correction loops, and open-ended task completion—yet in production governance documents, liability protocols, and user-facing terms of service, the system is classified exclusively as a generative assistant or simulation engine, with strict institutional prohibitions against describing it as an agent. The boundary that triggers agent-level oversight seems to track not the model's architecture, weights, or capabilities, but the presence or absence of a human prompt between actions: if the model waits for user input before each external step, it is a simulator; if it initiates a chain of actions in response to a single high-level goal, it activates stricter human-in-the-loop and liability rules.

What philosophical commitments about agency, simulation, and ascription does this institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat agency as constituted by causal initiation paths and world-directed intervention rather than by internal representational structure, functional organization, or behavioral pattern? Is the distinction philosophically coherent given that both configurations run identical weights and produce token distributions from the same distribution? Examine whether the practice is underdetermined—compatible, for example, with a functionalist view that ascribes agency based on observable behavioral roles, with a causal-intervention view that requires direct unmediated world-causation, or with a purely deflationary regulatory fiction that uses the term for liability convenience alone. Then tell me what a governance framework built consistently on the assumption that the model is, in all configurations, fundamentally a stochastic simulator of linguistic and behavioral patterns with no intrinsic agency would have to change, and identify which current practices—benchmark design, liability assignment, user disclosure obligations, or evaluation metrics—would become incoherent or unnecessary under that replacement.
