I'm working through some ideas for a philosophy of science workshop on ontology identification, and I keep running into the same flat framing: either you accept Quine's criterion and live with whatever exists according to our best theory, or you retreat into some form of epistemic humility. But I'm convinced there's more logical space here, especially once we inject a distinction that doesn't usually appear in the standard textbooks—namely, between what we might call structural archival silence, where the loss of identifying information is itself a lawlike feature of the system being studied, and accidental archival silence, where the loss is merely contingent and could in principle be repaired. I'd like you to write a taxonomy, rendered entirely as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables, that sorts positions on ontology identification under conditions of permanently inaccessible information. The tree should be built from diagnostic questions rather than from philosopher-labels: each branch should ask whether, in a concrete case—say, a theoretical entity whose intermediate decay traces are systematically erased by the very detectors that confirm its endpoint products, or a historical population whose census records were destroyed by a policy that itself reflects the social reality being studied—the inaccessibility undermines ontological commitment, invites what I'm calling a shadow-catalogue entry defined negatively by the gap it leaves, or leaves the ontology unchanged. You must include at least four leaves: a Quinean-style realist position that holds ontology fixed regardless of epistemic access; an epistemic-suspension view that brackets commitment precisely where identification fails; the shadow-catalogue view that expands ontology to include entities structurally defined by the absence of accessible identifying properties; and a pragmatic-deflationary view that treats ontology as bookkeeping and therefore revises it freely when records vanish. For each combination of answers that doesn't map onto one of these four, note briefly whether it seems unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate—perhaps because two positions agree on every concrete case yet differ only in rhetorical framing—and explain briefly why the separation fails. The audience is graduate students who know the basics of ontological commitment but haven't memorized a taxonomy of this specific problem, so phrase every node as a question they could answer without already knowing which philosopher defends which answer.
