Construct a thought experiment—no more than one paragraph, with no exotic technology or supernatural elements—about a small community that preserves an apparently irrational ritual (call it the fence) regulating access to a scarce but non-vital resource. The ritual’s origin is undocumented, and archives show that past abolitions coincided with measurable social harm, yet the causal pathway is entirely opaque; only the correlation is transparent. Engineer the case so that three positions yield distinct verdicts: (1) a strict Burkean conservative, invoking the fence principle and the authority of inherited practice, refuses abolition because the function remains unknown; (2) a rationalist reformer, accepting the correlation but requiring an intelligible causal mechanism before preservation, demands immediate abolition; and (3) a weak-fence interpreter—who treats the principle as purely epistemic, mandating understanding before change but not preservation for its own sake—reaches a verdict different from both, despite sharing the conservative’s skepticism of rationalist planning. After presenting the scenario, show the reasoning for each position, identify the single structural feature (the opacity of causation versus the transparency of correlation) that isolates the weak-fence view from the strict Burkean view, explain how removing that feature collapses the distinction back into a standard conservative-versus-reformer dispute, and note honestly how the rationalist might attack the conservative’s reliance on opaque correlation without conceding the case. Keep the setting realistic and the stakes ordinary—something a normal decision-maker could face.
