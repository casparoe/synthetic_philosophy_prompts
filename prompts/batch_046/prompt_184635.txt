For my advanced philosophy seminar I have been asked to write out my working profile on the self and personal identity so that others can press on it, and I want to see whether my commitments actually cohere or whether I am holding incompatible pieces without noticing. Here is where I stand. I accept Dennett's proposal that the self is a center of narrative gravity, by which I mean it is not a metaphysical substance or an inner ego but an abstract functional point that organizes experience into an autobiographical story. I think first-person authority is best understood as narrative coherence rather than introspective access to a hidden essence: we are authoritative about ourselves because we author the story, not because we peer inward at a soul. I also believe there is a real, non-illusory phenomenal difference between my present conscious experience and that of a perfect behavioral duplicate, and I explain this not by positing a hidden ego but by what I call indexical anchoring—the idea that the narrative is structurally tied to this particular biological stream of consciousness, so the story always carries a non-transferable point of view even though the anchor itself is not a substance. I hold that if someone's autobiographical memory were completely erased and replaced with fabricated but internally coherent memories, the result would be literally a different self, not merely the original person deceived. At the same time I reject both Cartesian immaterial egos and demanding psychological-continuity theories that require actual memory links across every interval; I think narrative continuity alone is sufficient for personal identity, and temporary gaps or disruptions in the narrative do not by themselves break identity, provided the overall arc can eventually be resumed. Finally, I believe moral responsibility attaches to this narrative continuity rather than to any deeper metaphysical bedrock, yet I also maintain that we can be genuinely responsible for actions that currently fall outside our narrative awareness, so long as they can eventually be woven into our story. I have tried to avoid the standard textbook positions, but I worry that some of these views pull against one another in ways that are not obvious. Please examine this set of commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to conflict. For each real tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would be that restores consistency without forcing me to abandon my broader outlook. Then tell me which single change to my view would clean up the most tensions at once. I ask that you write entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a long letter or email, because that is the format our group requires.
