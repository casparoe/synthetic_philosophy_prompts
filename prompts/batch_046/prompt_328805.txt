I'm drafting a consistency check on my position regarding American exceptionalism—treated simultaneously as historical thesis, normative claim, and ideology—before I finalize an essay. Here are my commitments:

(1) Historically, American exceptionalism is false. US state-building follows settler-colonial patterns comparable to Canada, Australia, and Argentina; there is no unique teleology, only contingency, violence, and institutional borrowing.

(2) Normatively, I defend a restricted exceptionalism: the Declaration's universalist language establishes what I call creedal instability, wherein exclusion becomes conceptually unstable because the founding framework is explicitly non-ethnic. This does not depend on American virtue but on the structural gap between universalist premise and particular practice, which fuels reform movements with unusual conceptual leverage.

(3) I reject exceptionalism as ideology—the claim that America is uniquely virtuous or exempt from international norms and class analysis—as dangerous propaganda that licenses imperial overreach and suppresses solidarity.

(4) The normative claim in (2) is compatible with severe internal criticism, because the standard is immanent: one measures US practice against its own universalist premises rather than comparing nations.

(5) Given (1), the normative claim cannot depend on essential American uniqueness. Any polity that entrenches comparable universalist commitments (e.g., post-war constitutional settlements elsewhere) could exhibit analogous dynamics. Thus American exceptionalism is contingent at best.

(6) Nevertheless, I maintain that the US exhibits unusually rapid and broad-scope legitimation crises—civil rights, labor, gender—relative to peer societies, which suggests the particular institutional embedding of these principles produces effects that are not fully generalizable.

My concern is that (6) smuggles historical uniqueness back in after (1) and (5) deny it. Is (6) merely an empirical observation consistent with (1), or does it implicitly resurrect the exceptionalist thesis? Second, does (2) collapse into (3) once we acknowledge that the creedal framing has itself served ideological functions? Third, there seems to be tension between (4) and (5): if the normative standard is not uniquely American, why should an American prioritize reforming US practice rather than promoting universalist reform globally?

Please audit this package: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, rank them by severity, indicate the least costly revision for each, and flag any tension that dissolves on closer inspection. Do not treat all conflicts as equally serious; I want to know which single revision would clean up the most problems if I had to choose one.
