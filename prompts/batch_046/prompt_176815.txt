As a retired research physicist who has recently begun formal study in philosophy of mind and logic, I am assisting in the design of a final examination for an upper-level seminar on computation, cognition, and mathematical knowledge. I require a single essay question, together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme, on Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments—specifically the Lucas–Penrose line—framed so that it rewards analytical reconstruction over memorized summary.

The question must center on the following technically precise hypothetical, which I have formulated to test structural understanding rather than label recognition. Assume that human mathematical cognition is not instantiated by one formal system F but is better represented as a dynamically coupled ensemble E = {F_i} of context-sensitive subsystems. Each F_i is locally consistent, effectively axiomatized, and accessible to explicit reflection, but the subsystems are not globally integrated into a single axiomatic framework: commitments endorsed within one cognitive context need not be jointly consistent with those endorsed in another, and the subject does not necessarily detect cross-context contradictions because of cognitive compartmentalization. Nevertheless, for any F_i that the subject understands, she can construct the corresponding Gödel sentence G(F_i) and recognize its truth.

Please draft an essay question suitable for a 90-minute examination that asks students to evaluate whether this ensemble model defeats the Lucas–Penrose conclusion that human mathematical cognition is non-algorithmic. The question should require explicit treatment of four issues: (a) whether the anti-mechanist argument can be reformulated to apply subsystem-by-subsystem, and whether such a reformulation yields a unified conclusion about E; (b) whether Benacerraf-style objections concerning the consistency of the human reasoner are dissolved, relocated, or intensified when consistency is distributed across an ensemble; (c) whether the demonstrated capacity to recognize Gödel sentences for arbitrarily many F_i establishes a genuinely non-computable ability, or merely demonstrates that no single Turing machine can capture the entire ensemble; and (d) what implications this carries for Penrose’s further claim that non-computability, if real, must be anchored in a specific physical mechanism (for example, quantum-gravitational objective reduction).

In addition, provide:
1. The essay question itself, clearly worded and self-contained.
2. A model answer of approximately 650–750 words. The answer should exhibit the specific philosophical moves that separate deep understanding from surface familiarity, and should not read as a generic summary of Lucas and Penrose.
3. A marking scheme totaling 25 marks, organized around at least five must-hit analytical points. For each point, specify the marks available, the distinction between an A-level execution and a B-level execution, and a one-line characterization of the typical error that costs marks.

The difficulty should reside in the crux: students must determine whether distributing consistency across subsystems saves mechanism by denying the existence of a single target formal system, or whether it inadvertently strengthens the anti-mechanist case by multiplying the Gödel sentences that human cognition can grasp. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, specific editions, or citations; the task should be completable by a well-prepared student without access to external sources.
