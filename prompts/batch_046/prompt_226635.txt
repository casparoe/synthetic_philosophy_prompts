You sit on a university ethics committee that has adopted an adversarial review protocol for evaluating high-stakes automated decision systems. Under this protocol, a vendor presents the safety case for a proposed algorithm—here, one that screens financial-aid appeals—followed by a critique from an advocate representing affected students, with a panel of non-technical faculty members judging the exchange. In the latest hearing, the vendor's representative made no false claims: every statistic was correctly computed for the subsystem cited, every technical definition was accurate within its domain, and every risk assessment followed established internal protocols. However, the metric for "error" measured only classification mistakes on standardized forms, excluding non-standard documentation; the failure-mode analysis treated component errors as statistically independent despite known interaction effects; and the overall narrative assembled these correct fragments into a systematically misleading portrait of low risk. The advocate raised concrete, intuitive concerns—students being silently routed to denial, appeals evaluated without meaningful human oversight—but could not match the granular technical framing. The panel, finding the vendor's argument rigorous and the advocate's response anecdotal, voted to approve. You feel the debate failed not because of dishonesty but because the danger was distributed across many individually defensible choices of framing and definition, leaving the non-expert judges with no clear falsehood to reject. Can you help me understand the precise structure of this failure? Specifically, is there a principled reason why adversarial debate with a non-expert judge might be systematically vulnerable to risks that are embedded in the architecture of technically correct but selectively constructed argumentation, and does this point to a deeper limit in using debate as a safeguard for systems whose reasoning can be hidden inside the gaps between verifiable claims?
