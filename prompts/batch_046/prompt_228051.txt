I am preparing the opening remarks for a single-day community workshop on aging, memory, and moral life, held in the public library of the small town where I grew up. The audience will be about forty people, roughly split between local seniors who come mainly for companionship and have no formal philosophical training, and a smaller group of graduate students together with one retired professor of Buddhist philosophy who will notice any misstatement of anatta or dependent origination. I have no academic background myself; I have been thinking about these matters on my own for about a year, and I have become stuck on a particular tension that I want to shape what I say rather than merely decorate it. The standard account I keep encountering is that Buddhism denies a permanent soul, but the version that actually troubles me is more specific: if there is no enduring self that persists through time—only a stream of dependently arisen moments—then the conviction that I am the very same being who caused harm thirty years ago and now bears an obligation to repair it seems to rest on a kind of fiction. Yet the urgency of that repair does not feel like a fiction to me. I want the remarks—roughly fifteen minutes of continuous spoken prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—to navigate this tension without resolving it into platitudes. They must be intelligible and respectful to the seniors, who will not know technical terms, and they must avoid anything the scholars would regard as a naive reification of selfhood; but they should also not sound like a lecture. The philosophical point needs to do real structural work: it should determine what kinds of comfort, acknowledgment, or orientation I can offer about facing one's past, and what I must withhold because it would contradict the idea I am taking seriously. Please write these remarks. In addition, please say what specific argument or discovery about personal identity across time would cause you to abandon the claim that anatta genuinely threatens the coherence of long-term moral repair—indicate where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Present your answer in continuous prose only, as in a letter, with no formatting devices.
