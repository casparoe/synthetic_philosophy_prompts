I'm working through a problem at the intersection of ontology and epistemology for a seminar on identification across epistemic gaps, and I've constructed an argument that seems to force an unacceptable dilemma, but I can't locate the precise misstep. Here it is, laid out step by step. (1) If two entities are genuinely distinct, there must be at least one property that differentiates them. (2) Any differentiating property that is in principle inaccessible to every possible observer cannot serve as a criterion for ontological identification; ontology should only register distinctions we are in a position to track, even if only indirectly. Call this the Accessible Identification Constraint. (3) Therefore, if two entities share every property that is even in principle accessible, they are not ontologically distinct—call this the Profile-Identity Thesis. (4) Now consider two reconstructed historical agents, call them Agent A and Agent B, separated only by a single ambiguous cuneiform tablet that could refer to either; every archaeological, linguistic, and material property accessible to us is identical across both reconstructions. (5) By (3), Agent A and Agent B are not two entities but one, despite the speculative narrative that treats them separately. (6) Apply the same reasoning diachronically: my present mental state and my mental state from five minutes ago share no directly accessible properties—the past state is now epistemically inaccessible—so by (3) they do not belong to the same persisting subject. (7) To avoid the absurd conclusion that I do not persist over time, we must reject (3) for personal identity, allowing identification to be secured by causal-historical links that are themselves partly or wholly inaccessible. (8) But consistency then requires allowing the same kind of inaccessible link for Agent A and Agent B—say, an unrecoverable genealogical chain—so (3) should also be rejected there, meaning they could indeed be distinct. (9) Yet if we systematically allow ontology to depend on inaccessible differentiae whenever it suits us, there is no principled barrier to multiplying entities behind every epistemic veil; every ambiguous record could shelter an infinite family of indistinguishable beings. (10) So we face a forced choice: either we accept the Profile-Identity Thesis and surrender diachronic personal identity, or we reject it and open ontology to unrestrained, unverifiable multiplication. Both horns seem intolerable. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? If I want to preserve both personal persistence and some constraint on historical ontology, is there a principled middle ground, or do I have to pay one of these prices?
