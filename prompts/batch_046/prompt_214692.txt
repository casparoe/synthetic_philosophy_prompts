In our philosophy of mind seminar, two classmates—Jordan and Riley—got into a sustained disagreement about Molyneux's problem, and since I was sitting right there I heard the entire exchange without feeling that either side was obviously wrong. Jordan began by framing the problem as a direct test of whether spatial concepts are modality-independent: if a person born blind, who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere solely through touch, suddenly gains sight and cannot immediately identify them visually, that failure suggests there is no shared, pre-experiential framework linking tactile and visual space. Riley pushed back by arguing that this framing ignores what we now understand about cross-modal plasticity—namely that sensory-substitution devices can eventually train the brain to interpret tactile input as spatial layout, but only after a period of calibration. Therefore, Riley said, the blind man's immediate failure would not prove the absence of innate spatial structures; it would only show that cross-modal mapping requires learned calibration, a result compatible with either empiricist or rationalist accounts. Jordan replied that if calibration is genuinely all that is needed, then the classic empiricist answer to Molyneux—that the subject merely needs time to associate visual ideas with tactile ones—fully dissolves the puzzle, leaving no philosophical work for innate ideas to do. Riley countered that this misses a deeper Kantian point: even learned calibration presupposes some unified concept of space that makes the association intelligible in the first place, so reducing the problem to pure association is circular rather than explanatory. Jordan then objected that invoking an unobservable, a priori conceptual layer is unnecessary if we can explain calibration through neural remapping mechanisms, and that adding such a layer violates parsimony. Riley concluded by noting that parsimony governs ontology only cautiously, and that Jordan was conflating the neurological mechanism of learning with the philosophical conditions that make cross-modal learning possible at all.
