i'm trying to think through what a theory of collective guilt that stretches across generations actually needs to pull off, not just for one moment but for a nation that keeps going after the original perpetrators are dead. jasper talked about criminal, political, moral and metaphysical guilt, but i want the full spec if we're asking whether a nation can be guilty in 2025 for something done in 1945.

give me the requirements list -- the kinds of cases it has to handle (citizens born after the fact, immigrants who join later, descendants who actively reject the legacy, the state itself as a legal person), the distinctions it can't collaspe (like between guilt and shame, or between inheriting responsibility vs inheriting a psychological burden, or between diachronic collective agency and just a sum of current people), the historical or institutional results it can't flat-out contradict, and why each one is a real constraint and not just a prejudice of one camp. also tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the usual suspects -- maybe the ones that treat nations like big individuals, or the ones that reduce everything to present-day distributive blame.

i'd especially like the list to include something about 'moral inheritance' versus 'causal inheritance': the theory should be able to say whether a child of perpetrators inherits guilt in the same way they inherit genes, or whether there's a normative bridge that has to be built separately. and if some subset of these desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so -- that'd be a result in itself.

a table is fine. keep it focused on the criteria, not on scoring every philosopher who ever wrote about it.
