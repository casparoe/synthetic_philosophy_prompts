I am a novelist presently drafting a multi-layered narrative in which a protagonist's decisive present action is explained, within the story's internal logic, not by any depicted past event but by a "deep" historical fact that the text never shows—only its explanatory shadow falls across the present scene. A philosopher friend has invited me to lead a single session at her interdisciplinary salon, attended mainly by fellow writers and one analytic philosopher, on metaphysical grounding and fundamentality; none of the writers have encountered the technical literature, though they understand narrative structure, the difference between what a story depicts and what it presupposes, and the sense that some plot elements depend for their meaning on others. I want to teach them not what every specialist says about grounding, but how to think with the idea—specifically, how explanatory dependence differs from causation, temporal sequence, and narrative importance—using my own novel's ontology as the carrying case rather than the usual examples from physics.

Plan the session's explanatory arc: which preliminary distinction must be secured before the term "grounding" can be introduced without collapsing into causation or authorial intention; what concrete case, drawn from this narrative-ontology situation, should carry the entire explanation, and precisely what that example must contain (the unrepresented backstory fact as the putative ground) and must lack (any direct causal chain shown in the plot, any appeal to my mental state as author) in order to function. Then map the misconceptions the audience will predictably form in the order they will arise—likely that fundamentality means narrative significance, that grounding operates backward in time like memory, that an unrepresented fact is ontologically "thinner" than a depicted one—and specify the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted. End with one diagnostic question I can ask to determine whether they have grasped grounding as an objective explanatory-dependence relation rather than as a literary device, and state clearly which technical subdivisions—such as the monism versus pluralism dispute about whether there is one fundamental level, or debates concerning negative grounding—should be deliberately left out at this level and why.
