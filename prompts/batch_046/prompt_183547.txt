Consider the institutional pattern in many labor-law jurisdictions: courts criminalize the physical confinement of workers as forced labor, yet routinely enforce employment contracts that impose large, debt-backed financial penalties—functionally binding workers to employers for years—while treating the worker's formal right to resign as proof that liberty remains intact. Meanwhile, the same legal systems vary sharply by jurisdiction in whether they enforce non-compete clauses that restrict post-employment movement. What conception of liberty—negative, positive, or republican—does this bifurcated treatment presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that distinguishes permissible debt-enforced retention from impermissible physical retention, ask whether that theory is consistent with the differential treatment of non-competes, and describe what labor-contract doctrine would have to change if it were rebuilt on an explicitly republican account of non-domination rather than on formal freedom of exit.
