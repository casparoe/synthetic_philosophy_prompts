I serve as a grader for an advanced graduate seminar in practical reason, and I have recently encountered these issues outside the classroom: after completing my doctoral work, I am deciding between an academic appointment and a senior role in public-policy design, and the two paths strike me as genuinely on a par rather than as options separated by a calculable margin. I am now assessing a take-home examination I assigned on incommensurable values and hard choice. Please grade the student answer below.

Question: Ruth Chang argues that hard choices are not merely close calls, in which a small improvement to one option would make it clearly superior, but cases of parity, in which two alternatives stand in a relation that is neither equality nor incomparability. First, explain parity and show why the small-improvement model fails to capture it. Second, contrast Chang's account with Bernard Williams's claim that incommensurable values lack a common measure and therefore leave practical reasoning without guidance. Third, evaluate whether Chang's voluntarist thesis—that an agent can generate reasons by committing to one option—genuinely resolves Williams's worry that reason runs out, or whether it merely relocates the gap.

Student answer: Ruth Chang distinguishes hard choices from close calls by rejecting the small-improvement model: if options were merely close, then a slight advantage to one would render it clearly better, but in true parity no such improvement alters the rational standing of either option. Parity is a relation in which options are comparable—there is no failure of comparison—yet neither is better than the other, and they are not equal in value either. This differs from Bernard Williams's notion of incommensurability, in which values possess no shared scale, so practical reasoning cannot rank them and effectively stops. Joseph Raz extends Williams's position by treating incommensurability as necessarily entailing incomparability, which would make rational choice impossible in such cases.

Chang rejects this Raz-Williams line. She maintains that parity does not block rational choice; rather, the agent resolves the hard choice by committing to one option, and this commitment creates reasons that did not exist beforehand. Once I commit to philosophy rather than policy design, my commitment supplies the missing justification, so reason does not run out—it is constructed through the act of choice. Therefore, Chang's voluntarism successfully overcomes Williams's skepticism: where Williams sees an arbitrary gap that reason cannot bridge, Chang identifies the precise point at which reason is actively produced.
