You chair the rapid-review ethics panel at a tertiary trauma and palliative-care center, where you must sign binding approvals or rejections on proposed clinical protocols within ninety minutes, often with incomplete nursing notes and without access to the attending physician’s full mental-state report. Your panel receives frequent submissions involving foreseen but unintended harm—high-dose opioid infusions that suppress respiration, damage-control amputations performed to prevent systemic sepsis, and withdrawal of life-sustaining machinery under resource constraints—and the residents routinely conflate institutional protocol aims with individual clinician intentions, applying the doctrine of double effect inconsistently. Write a reusable, ordered decision procedure—formatted as a numbered checklist with explicit decision nodes—that a non-philosopher panelist can apply to determine whether a proposed intervention is permissible under a strict, institution-level reading of the doctrine of double effect. The procedure must include: (1) a formal-aim extraction step that distinguishes the protocol’s documented endpoint from the clinician’s reported intention, with instructions for conflicts; (2) a proportionality assessment using quantifiable clinical parameters that explicitly marks where value comparison exceeds algorithmic resolution; (3) a means-end filter tailored to resource-allocation scenarios, distinguishing withdrawal because a treatment is futile for the patient from withdrawal that is physically necessary to reallocate the device; and (4) an identification node for cases where the bad effect is causally necessary for the good effect to occur even though it is not the psychological aim. Then apply the procedure to three submissions: Case A, a palliative protocol calibrating an opioid infusion to eliminate consciousness with expected respiratory arrest within hours, where arrest is not listed as the order-set endpoint; Case B, a trauma protocol mandating limb amputation to prevent sepsis, where surgical notes confirm the limb is non-viable but family members allege the procedure was performed to free ICU capacity; and Case C, a triage protocol that removes a patient with minimal survival probability from ECMO to allocate it to a higher-probability patient, executed by the same clinical team and physically requiring the first removal. For each case, state the procedure’s verdict, identify the specific node requiring non-eliminable judgment, and include one case where the procedure yields a permissibility judgment that conflicts with common clinical intuition. Finally, delineate precisely what the procedure cannot adjudicate—such as distributive justice across the hospital census, long-term institutional reputation risk, or the moral psychology of individual nurses—labeling each boundary clearly as OUT OF SCOPE, and note the conditions under which the panel must escalate rather than decide.
