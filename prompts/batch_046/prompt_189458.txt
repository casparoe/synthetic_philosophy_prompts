I'm putting together a small poetry programme for my study group on the French Revolution -- we've spent the term in a bit of an arguement about whether 1793 was driven by class struggle, ideology, or sheer contingency, and whether revolutions really do devour their own children or if that's just a neat historiographical fiction we use to tidy up the mess. I want a villanelle, 19 lines exactly, with two refrains that keep turning like the debate itself: one about the guillotine feeding on the child it made, and one about no law writing the blade, only the turn.

The form should do the structural work -- the refrains circling back, and in the final couplet sitting side by side without reconciling, so you feel class-struggle explanation and contingency passing each other without ever touching. It shouldn't be a sermon about Robespierre specifically, but it should take seriously the idea that revolutions consume their makers not because some historical law demands it, but because the machinery of virtue has no brake: the same committees that defend popular sovereignty end up manufacturing the enemy inside the people. Someone who knows the historiography should recognise the tension; someone who doesn't should still feel the procedural trap closing. Keep it accurate enough that a specialist wouldn't wince, but don't quote anyone verbatim -- just let the rhyme scheme carry the argument.
