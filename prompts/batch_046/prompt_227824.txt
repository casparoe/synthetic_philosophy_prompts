Elena pushed back, saying this misses a crucial distinction in Pascal's thought between justice that is merely established and justice that ought to be established; she claimed that Pascal treats true justice as hidden from fallen human reason but not therefore nonexistent, and that legitimate force is legitimate only when it serves as a temporary, humbling instrument oriented toward that hidden divine order rather than as its source. Marcus countered that Elena is smuggling in modern liberal assumptions Pascal would reject; for Pascal, recognizing our profound ignorance about true justice should precisely humble the powerful and restrict them to using force only to preserve civil peace, which creates the conditions for people to seek salvation. Elena answered that moderation without a publicly accessible criterion is still arbitrary, and that Pascal's own emphasis on the irreducible plurality of national customs undermines any claim that a single hidden standard can be legitimately enforced by one state apparatus.

In particular, address whether Pascal's view allows for any genuinely non-arbitrary distinction between legitimate and illegitimate force; whether the hidden-justice interpretation is textually defensible without relying on obscure secondary literature; and what both Marcus and Elena are missing about how Pascal's theological commitments interact with his political skepticism. Work through the arguments exactly as they stand.
