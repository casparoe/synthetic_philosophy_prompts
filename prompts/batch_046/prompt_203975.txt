I'm designing a conceptual map for a seminar on theory confirmation in fundamental physics, specifically for frameworks—such as multiverse scenarios derived from string landscape considerations or eternal inflation models—where direct empirical discrimination may be permanently unavailable. I want a taxonomy structured as a decision tree that sorts positions not by their familiar labels but by answers to a sequence of concrete conceptual commitments. The root node is the acceptance of the following claim: there exist well-defined theoretical frameworks in physics that may never yield unique, direct empirical predictions distinguishable from all competing frameworks.

From that root, the first branch must ask whether the absence of competing theories that reproduce the same low-energy phenomenology—call this the uniqueness condition—can itself confer any epistemic weight on a framework, or whether it remains evidentially inert. The second branch should ask whether the necessity of anthropic or selection-effect reasoning to account for observed parameter values undermines whatever weight uniqueness might provide, or whether such reasoning can be integrated without loss. The third branch should distinguish whether mathematical productivity—specifically, the derivation within the same formalism of previously unconnected results—counts as an independent form of non-empirical support, or whether it is merely an aesthetic virtue with no evidential import. The fourth branch should address whether statistically analyzable indirect signatures, even if they are not decisive on their own, can bridge the gap between non-empirical uniqueness and empirical science, or whether they are too weak to matter.

Each leaf should carry a name if it corresponds to an existing named position (for example, a form of structural realism, a skeptical instrumentalism, or a particular philosophy-of-science stance), or be labeled unnamed if the combination of answers appears unexplored. For each unnamed or sparsely occupied combination, include one sentence diagnosing why it might be unoccupied—whether incoherent, unattractive, or merely neglected. Crucially, flag any pair of leaves that your tree genuinely cannot separate: positions that would answer every branch question identically despite being conceptually distinct.

The tree should contain between six and eight leaves. Do not ask respondents to identify positions by name; every node must be phrased as a substantive claim or case judgment that someone could answer without prior knowledge of the taxonomy's labels. Avoid referencing specific bibliographies, author lists, or citation demands. The purpose is to diagnose where someone's actual commitments fall within the logical space of non-empirical confirmation, not to test familiarity with the literature. Additionally, inject one specific conceptual distinction of your own—whether a refined version of predictive dilution, a particular reading of explanatory breadth, or another niche framing—that helps separate two branches that standard classifications tend to conflate.
