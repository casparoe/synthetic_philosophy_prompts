After a prolonged and somewhat frustrating discussion with a close friend about whether rational agents can ever have a reason to engineer their own confusion, I find myself trying to pin down a conceptual distinction that neither of us could name precisely. The recurring pattern we kept circling is this: an agent foresees a future decision point at which possessing the full history of her prior actions or current position would lead her to choose suboptimally. Rather than relying on sheer willpower, she restructures the environment—by destroying records, relying on a cognitive limitation she knows she will suffer, or using a randomizing mechanism—so that at the critical moment she genuinely lacks the information that would derail her. One concrete instance is an experimental researcher who, anticipating that she will be unable to resist peeking at preliminary results, physically designs the study protocol so that she cannot access intermediate data. Another is a deliberate variant of the absent-minded driver scenario: the driver does not merely suffer from poor memory, but actively chooses, before departure, not to install any exit markers, precisely because she knows that seeing them at an ambiguous point would trigger the wrong choice.

What I want to know is whether this specific configuration—deliberately constructing one's own future informational blindness as a rational planning strategy—carries an established name in decision theory or the philosophy of action. It is clearly adjacent to precommitment, yet precommitment typically binds one's future actions rather than binding one's future epistemic access. It is also distinct from the standard absent-minded driver problem, in which the ignorance is an exogenous cognitive constraint, whereas here it is endogenously designed. Is there a recognized term that captures exactly this combination of foresight and self-induced epistemic limitation? If not, can it be defined in a way that distinguishes it from strategic ignorance in game theory, where information is hidden between agents rather than within one? I would also like to know where its conceptual boundaries lie: for example, must the agent genuinely forget the information, or is it sufficient that she renders it permanently inaccessible? And does the strategy still count if the agent retains only a probabilistic belief about her position, provided that belief is too noisy to guide action?
