Consider the proposal that long-term constitutional entrenchment clauses—specifically, provisions that lock climate-adaptation funding priorities in place for half a century and can only be overturned by a three-fourths legislative supermajority—are best understood as collective Ulysses contracts rather than as ordinary democratic rules. What are the most important implications of this framing for democratic legitimacy, intergenerational fairness, and the rationality of binding future citizens to priorities that may become technologically or ecologically obsolete? I ask for a committed answer: take a definite position on whether this Ulysses-contract interpretation ultimately strengthens or undermines the normative case for such mandates, defend that view with concrete reasoning, and avoid merely listing counterarguments without resolving them.
