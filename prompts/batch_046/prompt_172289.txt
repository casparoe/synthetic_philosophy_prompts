I'm curating an oral-history archive for a deindustrialized town whose main factory closed in 1998. Our interviews with former line workers, plant managers, and municipal regulators yield three incompatible accounts of the closure: global market pressure, a strategic misstep by headquarters, and an unavoidable environmental-compliance measure. The grant requires a single "reference narrative" that records how the event actually happened, on the theory that future researchers need a stable baseline rather than an unresolved tangle. To meet this without simply imposing my own synthesis, I have designed a weighting scheme that scores each testimony by its overlap with primary documents—pay stubs, internal memos, council minutes—so that the final account is reconstructed probabilistically from corroborated fragments rather than chosen by editorial judgment. A co-curator argues that this misses the point entirely: whether I use intuition or an algorithm, I am still assuming there is one true causal chain hidden behind the sources, and any narrative that assembles fragments into coherence imposes a teleological order that was not present in the events themselves. She says the grant's demand for a "reference narrative" is therefore not merely hard to satisfy but conceptually incoherent, because historical reality does not come pre-structured for extraction. I am unsure whether she is right that reconstruction and construction are indistinguishable here, or whether quantitative weighting genuinely changes the epistemic status of the result. Is there a defensible middle ground between "it happened exactly like this" and "all narratives are equally constructed," or does my weighting scheme just disguise the same old mistake in numerical form? I should add: if I am actually conflating an administrative problem—grant compliance—with a philosophical problem about the nature of historical knowledge, please say so plainly rather than treating them as the same issue. I would rather be corrected on that confusion than receive an answer that politely papers over it.
