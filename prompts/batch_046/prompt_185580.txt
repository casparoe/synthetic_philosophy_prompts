My friend and I just finished a long, circular argument about whether dreams are experiences. She kept defending the Malcolm-style line: that to dream is not to undergo any conscious experience during sleep, and that dream reports are essentially confabulated narratives constructed upon waking rather than retrospective descriptions of an inner show. I kept pushing a Dennett-style line: that dream reports do track real mental events, but that the received view of dreams as fully formed cinematic experiences playing out in a Cartesian theater during REM is the real mistake; on my view the experience is either fragmented, non-narrative, or assembled at the boundary of waking, yet it remains genuinely experiential rather than purely fabricated. We realized we keep agreeing on the data—people sincerely report dreams, REM correlates with reportability, direct introspection of sleep-state consciousness is unavailable—while disagreeing about what conceptual framework should govern those data.

Could you draft a joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the Malcolm camp and the Dennett camp on whether dreams are experiences? I want the document to be something both sides could sign without either feeling misrepresented.

Please use the following structure:

1. Agreed claims: Numbered statements about the phenomena, the limits of third-person access, the unreliability of naive introspection about sleep, and what is not at stake in the dispute (for example, that people talk about dreams, or that REM sleep is neurologically distinctive). Be precise enough that they are not vacuous.

2. Disagreements table: For each remaining point of divergence, provide (a) the Malcolm-camp formulation in wording they would accept, (b) the Dennett-camp formulation in wording they would accept, and (c) the exact crux—the sub-question such that settling it in one direction would move at least one camp.

Specifically, include rows on:
- The ontological status of the mental events, if any, occurring during REM.
- The authority and temporal location of dream reports: do they describe sleep-state occurrences, or do they describe events at or after awakening?
- The role of narrative coherence: is the story-structure of the report an artifact of reconstruction, or is it present (perhaps in non-verbal form) during sleep?
- The significance of lucid-dream signaling studies: do real-time reports from within REM undermine the claim that no experience occurs during sleep, or can they be accommodated as exceptional boundary cases?

3. Cruxes and predictions: Identify the two or three deepest cruxes. For each, state what each camp predicts about the outcome of a hypothetical decisive study—say, a protocol that interrupts REM at varying latencies and compares immediate non-verbal indicators with subsequent narrative reports, or a neurofeedback paradigm that allows graded signaling without full awakening. Each side's prediction should be recorded in advance, with no hedging.

4. Verbal vs. substantive: Conclude by assessing whether any portion of the dispute is purely verbal—if so, say exactly which portion and why—without dissolving the parts that remain genuinely substantive.

Write in American English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask me to supply references. Keep it formal and polished, as if submitting to a philosophy journal's adversarial-collaboration section.
