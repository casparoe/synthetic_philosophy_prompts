I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I have been organizing a monthly lunch seminar for colleagues in my engineering group—mostly people with computer-science training who can follow rigorous conceptual arguments but have never taken a philosophy course. Next month I want to tackle the debate over whether societies have a moral duty to preserve collective memory of past wrongs, and whether there are conditions under which collective forgetting is not only permissible but ethically required—a discussion I understand is associated with thinkers such as Margalit and Rieff, though I have not studied their texts closely. Rather than asking for a summary of positions, I want a teaching plan that lets me explain the underlying conceptual architecture well enough that the team can apply it to problems in our own domain, particularly to data-retention policies, archive design, and the decision to remove or preserve harmful digital records.

Please plan the explanation for a fifty-minute session with this audience. First, specify the exact order in which the conceptual components must be introduced: which distinction—perhaps between individual psychological remembrance and collective institutional remembrance, or between retaining an event in a living social practice versus maintaining its public accessibility—must be in place before the normative question about duty can even be formulated, and why that dependency is structural rather than optional. Second, identify the single carrying example or case that should anchor the entire session: a concrete scenario with enough emotional and structural weight to illustrate the tension, together with a precise statement of what features it must possess and what simplifying features it must deliberately lack—should it involve a technological archive, a monument, or an oral tradition, and why? Third, map the misconceptions this particular audience is likely to import, given that they habitually think in terms of storage, retrieval, fidelity, and system design—such as treating collective memory like a database with perfect or reversible erasure, or conflating the deletion of a record with the disappearance of a harm; for each misconception, indicate the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off before it takes root. Fourth, propose one diagnostic question I can ask at the end to determine whether they have grasped the normative structure of the debate—distinguishing remembrance as an obligation from remembrance as a preference—rather than merely formed an opinion about archives. Fifth, state clearly which elements of the broader philosophical literature—perhaps finer distinctions in the philosophy of history, specific historical applications, or technical debates about the metaphysics of groups—should be openly set aside at this level, and how I should acknowledge that omission without making the framework feel arbitrary. The plan should avoid untranslated technical jargon, should not depend on citations or texts the audience could not verify, and should not collapse into a generic exhortation to “never forget”; but I want the framing precise enough that an engineer can see why the question is not merely about data storage.
