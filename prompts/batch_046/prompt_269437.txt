I am working through Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" and am stuck on a specific tension rather than the familiar relativist thesis. Montaigne describes the Brazilian cannibals' practice of killing enemies in battle and then roasting and eating them, emphasizing that they do not torture prisoners while they are still alive. He contrasts this with European religious wars, in which living captives are tortured and burned for heresy, and asks which party is truly barbarous. He thus seems to invoke a universal standard—cruelty inflicted on living bodies—while simultaneously insisting that each culture ought to be judged by its own internal norms.

My question is whether these two moves can be reconciled without contradiction. Specifically, does Montaigne implicitly distinguish between practices that are constitutive of a culture's social identity and practices that represent an instrumental deviation from that identity? If cannibalism is embedded in the cannibals' honor code, whereas religious torture departs from Christian charity, then comparing the two by the suffering they cause to living bodies might not violate relativism, because the comparison targets only the latter kind of deviation. Yet I am unsure whether the text actually supports this reading, or whether Montaigne simply applies a hidden universal metric without acknowledging it. Could you help me see which interpretation the passage sustains, and whether there is a more precise conceptual distinction—perhaps between evaluative frameworks internal to a practice and those external to it—that clarifies the argument?
