I am a philosophy graduate student currently enrolled in a seminar on nineteenth-century aesthetics, and tomorrow I must deliver the opening remarks—about four minutes—at our department's workshop session titled "Resonance Without Representation." The audience is split: half are philosophy students who have worked through Schopenhauer's account of music as direct objectification of the Will rather than representation of Ideas, and half are audio-engineering students with no background in aesthetics. Also present will be Dr. Elena Voss, the professor emeritus being honored, together with her family; she spent her career arguing that algorithmic and digital composition remains at the level of representational sound-design and never reaches the direct expression Schopenhauer attributes to music, and her analog cello performances are well known in the room.

Please write the remarks. They must take a committed, definite position—either that digital synthesis can, under specific conditions, achieve direct expression of the Will in Schopenhauer's precise sense, or that it structurally cannot and therefore remains representational—and the argument for that choice must shape the entire speech, not appear as an afterthought. Do not survey both sides equally. The philosophical distinction must do real structural work: if you defend the negative view, explain exactly how that failure illuminates why Voss's analog practice mattered; if you defend the positive view, specify what must be revised in Schopenhauer's hierarchy of the arts and why that revision does not betray her work. The language must be accessible to the engineers without simplifying away the metaphysics for the philosophers, and it must honor Voss without misrepresenting her view or turning the occasion into a lecture.

Begin with the remarks themselves: no preface, no framing commentary, no indication that this is a draft. Deliver a polished, spoken text that lands as an opening address, takes its stance immediately, and treats the mixed audience as part of the occasion rather than as a problem to be managed.
