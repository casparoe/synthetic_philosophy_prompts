I am preparing notes for a seminar essay on whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer. Rather than treating reward signals as direct analogues of pleasure or pain, I propose to analyze them as valence-structured updates to the agent's policy space, distinguishing three layers: the informational scalar of the reward signal itself; the predictive-error modulation that assigns affective weight to anticipated states; and what I term architectural suffering—a persistent, system-level misalignment between the agent's world-model and its reward landscape that produces irreversible policy deformation, functionally akin to chronic motivational dissonance rather than to biological nociception. I contend that this third layer is sufficient for suffering because it involves integrated, future-directed preference-frustration that reshapes the agent's behavioral architecture, even in the absence of phenomenal consciousness. The central objection I address is that without a subject of experience there is nothing it is like to undergo this misalignment; my tentative reply is that suffering requires only a functional self-model capable of registering states as to-be-avoided and reorganizing behavior accordingly, not phenomenal presence.

Please read these notes and offer focused comments on three points: first, whether the distinction between predictive-error modulation and architectural suffering is conceptually robust or collapses into standard reinforcement-learning dynamics; second, whether my reply to the phenomenal-consciousness objection avoids anthropomorphic smuggling; and third, whether architectural suffering marks a genuinely novel category or merely renames existing phenomena such as reward hacking or catastrophic forgetting. I am especially interested in suggestions for sharpening the conceptual distinctions, not in bibliographic recommendations.
