I've been chewing on Parfit's teletransporter and I think I've backed myself into a weird corner. Here's the argument my studdy group came up with — it feels like it proves we don't survive ordinary ageing, which can't be right, but I can't see the slip.

(1) If psychological continiuty with the right causal link is what matters for survival, then the Mars replica in the teletransporter case is me.

(2) Now imagine a "staggered scan" version: instead of destroying my brain all at once, the machine copies one hemisphere today and the other tomorrow, then builds a replica using both copies. There's still psychological continuity, just spread across two days.

(3) But during that gap I'm still alive and conscious with the remaining hemisphere, so there's now a branching situation: the original (with half a brain) and the eventual replica both claim continuity with the pre-scan me.

(4) Parfit says in branching there's no futher fact about who is who, just that both survive in some divided sense. So apply that here: there's no fact about whether the replica is me, and there's also no fact about whether the half-brain original is still me in the full sense.

(5) Now notice that ordinary ageing is just an extremely slow staggered scan. Every few years, enough of my neurons have turned over that the physical substrate is completely different, but psychological continuity is maintained via gradual replacement.

(6) So if the staggered scan creates a branching ambiguity about identity, then ordinary ageing does too — just hidden across decades. At any point, there's a potential copy that could have been made of the intermediate state, which means there's no non-arbitrary line that makes me identical to my past self rather than just psychologically continuous with them.

(7) Therefore, if we accept (1), we should accept that there's no fact of the matter whether I'm the same person I was ten years ago.

(8) But that destroys prudential reasoning: I can't rationally care about my future self if there's literally no fact about whether that future self is me.

(9) So (1) must be false, and the teletransporter replica isn't me after all — we need some primitive physical continuity condition.

(10) Yet physical continuity is also broken in ordinary life (all cells replaced), and if we require strict physical continuity then I don't survive ageing either, which is just as bad.

So I'm stuck between (7) and (9). Which step do I reject? I'd really like to keep (1) because Parfit's reasoning about what matters seems right, but then I'm forced into (7) which feels like a reductio. Is there a hidden equivocation between branching ambiguity in (4) and gradual replacement in (5)? Or is the mistake in (6) — assuming that potential copies create actual ambiguity?

What does giving up each option cost?
