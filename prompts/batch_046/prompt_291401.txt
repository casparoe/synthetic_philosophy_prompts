I'm currently taking a course on medieval philosophy of action, and we've been working through Buridan's treatment of the ass paradox and his claim that the will can rationally determine itself when reason presents two goods as exactly equal. I want to apply this framework to a problem that only arises with modern computational architectures, but I need to inject a specific configuration that doesn't map neatly onto either Buridan's rational will or contemporary stochastic models.

Consider a resource-bounded autonomous planning system that faces two action paths with precisely identical expected utility. Because computing that equality itself consumes resources and delays execution, the state-space shifts during deliberation: some options expire, others become available. The system breaks the tie not by invoking a hidden preference or injecting random noise, but by applying a meta-rule that selects whichever option minimizes the computational cost of having made the choice—including caching effects, so that sometimes it picks the path evaluated first and sometimes the opposite, depending on architecture. The result is choice without preference, yet the determination comes neither from an arbitrary act of will (as Buridan might permit for rational agents) nor from subcortical randomness (as modern neuroeconomics often posits for human tie-breaking).

Given Buridan's commitment to rational self-determination and his rejection of brute determinism in voluntary action, how would he likely diagnose this case? Would he treat the meta-rule as a disguised rational preference—perhaps for cognitive economy—or as a failure of true voluntariness because the determination originates in the system's architecture rather than in the will? Furthermore, if he were confronted with contemporary neuroscientific evidence that human brains often resolve similar ties through low-level noise rather than through deliberative rational determination—evidence he could not have anticipated—do you think he would restrict his thesis that the will can always rationally determine itself between equals to a narrower domain of genuinely moral choice, or would he instead try to reinterpret that neural noise as an instrument of the will's hidden rational ordering? I am less interested in a historical survey than in a precise philosophical diagnosis: what exactly distinguishes this computational case from Buridan's ass, from modern heuristic models, and from pure determinism, and where should the boundary of "voluntary" choice be drawn if rational determination can be outsourced to architecture or to biology.
