I was arguing with a friend last week about heterophenomenology — Dennett's idea that in consciousness science we treat first-person reports like texts about experience, not like direct peeks into a private inner theater. I think the method extends naturally to language models: when an LLM reports "I'm unsure about this answer," we should interpret that heterophenomenologically, as expressing its functional self-model rather than as proof or disproof that there's "something it's like" to run it. My friend says I'm stretching Dennett past breaking point, but I want to be honest about what should change my mind — I think its the right stance, but maybe I'm wrong.

What evidence or arguments would actually damage this extended view? Specifically, would it matter if mechanistic interpertability showed the circuits generating self-reports are totally decoupled from the computation producing the answer? Or if human subjects' reports turn out to be causally coupled to behavior in ways synthetic reports never are? Has the position already been qualified into unfalsifiability — so that any counterevidence just gets recast as "a different kind of self-model"? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, plus an honest assessment of whether defenders would concede any of them, and whether insulating the view like that is a problem for a methodological stance in science.
