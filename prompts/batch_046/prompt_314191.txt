Here is a consistency check I need before presenting my position in a discussion group. I work as a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, but I have developed a set of views on rational belief revision that I apply both to debugging distributed systems and to everyday reasoning. My commitments are as follows. First, I endorse a broadly Bayesian framework: one should revise credence only in response to new evidence, not simply because another agent holds a contrary belief. Second, in engineering practice, if I have direct observational access to a system state—say, I have personally read the logs and identified a specific failure mode—I do not think I should reduce my confidence in that observation merely because a colleague interprets the same data differently; my first-hand epistemic access ought to insulate the belief from peer disagreement. Third, I nevertheless hold that in complex distributed environments, disagreement among multiple independent reviewers is a valuable fault-detection signal: if several qualified peers independently reject my root-cause analysis, I should treat that disagreement as evidence of my own error. Fourth, I take the dogmatism paradox to be solved by restricting the evidential force of disagreement to situations in which one lacks direct access to the primary evidence; once first-hand observation is present, disagreement becomes epistemically inert. Fifth, I also believe that true independence among reasoners is practically impossible, since shared training, tools, and conceptual frameworks always introduce hidden dependencies that undermine the idea of genuinely independent verification. Sixth, despite recognizing this universal dependency, I still operate in practice as if my direct observations carry authoritative weight. Please examine these commitments: identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which conflicts are merely apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, rank the tensions by severity, and specify the single least costly revision that would restore coherence. I am particularly interested in whether my appeal to direct observational access in engineering contexts is compatible with my admission that independence never fully holds.
