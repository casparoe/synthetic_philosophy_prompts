I'm a retired wildlife epidemiologist — used to run cohort studies on badger populations — and about three years ago I started sitting in on philosphy courses at the local college, mostly to keep my brain from turning to mush. Lately I've been stuck on the self-sampling versus self-indication problem, especially the presumptuous philosopher objection, and I want to pitch a one-semester independent study to the philosophy department (they let retirees do a directed project if it's rigorous). But I want to inject something from my old field: in epidemiology, a referance class isn't a metaphysical thing, it's a sampling frame you fix before you observe. So here's the proposal I need written up.

The precise question: Is the presumptuous philosopher's charge — that self-indication forces you to expect membership in the largest possible observer class — actually a disguised complaint about post-hoc adjustment of your sampling frame, and can we neutralize it by treating reference-class selection as a methodological step governed by the same rules as cohort inclusion criteria? In other words, does framing self-indication as a claim about study design rather than about personal identity let us preserve the intuitive weight of self-sampling without falling into hubris?

Why it's open: Philosophers usually treat the reference-class problem as being about which beings are "you," and they debate doomsday-style predictions without asking whether the feeling of presumptuousness comes from violating a statistical norm scientists already accept — namely, that you can't redefine your study population after you've seen where you landed. The existing answers don't settle whether the objection survives once we separate the sampling frame (chosen prior to observation) from the study population (defined after).

Approach and deliverable: I'd do a conceptual mapping, not data collection. I'd align the logical structure of self-indication with epidemiological cohort-selection rules — left-truncation, selection bias, inclusion/exclusion criteria — then construct a decision procedure, almost a checklist, for when anthropic reasoning is methodologically valid versus when it's post-hoc rationalization. The result should be a clear failure condition: if the reference class can be arbitrarily expanded after the observer notices their position, the procedure flags an invalid inference. I'd apply it to the classic presumptuous-philosopher scenario and to one case where the analogy breaks — probably where consciousness or first-person identity messes up the sampling-frame analogy.

Scope: One semester, roughly 5,000 to 6,000 words. I'm not trying to solve all of anthropic reasoning; I'm testing whether a scientific sampling lens dissolves one specific objection. I expect failure might come exactly at the point where observer identity stops being like cohort membership, and I want the proposal to say honestly that documenting that breakdown counts as success, not defeat.
