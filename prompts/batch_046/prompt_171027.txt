I am writing an independent study paper for my high school philosophy course on whether introspection can be trusted, and I need help interpreting my teacher's consistent rulings. She has judged four student reports: she accepted that a student genuinely felt pre-presentation anxiety despite showing no physical symptoms; she rejected that a student studied out of fascination, since logs showed study began only after a parental threat; she accepted that a moral dilemma felt genuinely disturbing, even though the student laughed while describing it; and she rejected a claim of having no implicit racial bias, observing that introspection does not reach sub-personal associations.

Now consider a new case: a student reports a sudden "flash of insight" while solving a problem—describing a vivid, all-at-once visual appearance of the answer—though reaction-time records show gradual improvement across trials, and cognitive-science literature treats such moments as reconstructed narratives rather than direct experiences. The student insists the feeling was unmistakable.

Take a committed position: state the two most coherent rules that could govern her four verdicts, predict what each rule says about this new report, identify whether case 1, 2, 3, or 4 is the closest analog and defend that choice, and give your best guess at her actual verdict with explicit reasons. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, specify the principle she would be adopting with each option, and propose one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the rules. Do not present a neutral menu; defend one interpretation.
