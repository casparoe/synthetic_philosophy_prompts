I am writing a philosophy paper for my advanced seminar on aesthetics and emotion, and I have been tracking my tutor's verdicts on whether emotional responses to fictional representations are "cognitively appropriate" — meaning rational, not self-deceptive, and not merely simulated without warrant. Here are her rulings across seven cases.

First, she held that a viewer who weeps at the death of a well-known tragic hero, knowing the actor lives and the script is invented, responds appropriately; the emotion is directed at the represented suffering, not at a real-world object.

Second, she held that a reader who feels sustained anxiety throughout a horror novel, despite knowing the monster is invented, also responds appropriately, though the anxiety lacks the action-oriented urgency of real-world fear.

Third, she held that an audience member in an immersive theater piece who feels genuine embarrassment when an actor addresses them by name and mocks a real personal trait — all within a performance the audience member knows is staged — responds inappropriately; the emotional target has crossed from represented content to the actual self.

Fourth, she held that a historian who reads a meticulously researched novel about a real famine and feels sorrow specifically for an invented child victim inserted by the author for pathos — and who continues to feel that specific sorrow after learning the child never existed — responds inappropriately; the particular emotional object is anchored to a false particular within an otherwise accurate representation.

Fifth, she held that a child who plays with plastic action figures and expresses concern when one is "wounded" in the game, while knowing the figure is plastic, responds appropriately; this is structurally analogous to adult engagement with literature, operating within an explicit pretense.

Sixth, she held that a subject who reads a documentary-style narrative about "Alex," later learns that Alex is a composite of several real people with no single corresponding individual, and continues to feel sympathy for Alex's represented suffering, responds appropriately; the emotional object is the represented suffering, not the metaphysical particularity of the bearer.

Seventh, she held that a viewer who watches a documentary-style film using undisclosed fabricated footage of an event that never occurred, feels outrage at the depicted injustice, and then retains a residual outrage specifically at the depicted event after learning of the fabrication — rather than redirecting all outrage to the filmmakers — responds inappropriately.

Next week we will discuss an eighth case. A viewer participates in an interactive narrative generated in real time by a language model. The viewer knows the system produces text, images, and plot events algorithmically, partly in response to the viewer's own biometric feedback, but does not know which events were pre-scripted by a human author and which were generated responsively to maximize emotional engagement. When a beloved character dies — an event the system reveals was generated dynamically in direct response to the viewer's rising heart rate and prior emotional attachments, with no human author having planned it — the viewer feels profound grief. The viewer knows the character never existed, that no human authored this specific death, and that the algorithm produced it specifically to exploit the viewer's emotional state.

Predict my tutor's verdict on this eighth case: does she call the grief appropriate or inappropriate? Then identify the implicit rule she has been running across all eight cases — and at least one alternative rule that fits the first seven verdicts equally well. For each rule, say what it predicts about case eight. Give your best guess for which rule she actually holds, with reasons. Finally, tell me what a ninth case would need to be in order to distinguish conclusively between your leading rule and its strongest rival.
