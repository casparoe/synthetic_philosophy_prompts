I chair the philosophy-of-science advisory panel for our national board of clinical-education accreditation, and I need an instrument my non-philosopher colleagues can use repeatedly. Every quarter our reviewers audit postgraduate training programs to determine whether their competency assessments distinguish trainees who genuinely know clinical protocols from those who hold merely true beliefs—whether acquired through rote memorization, statistical pattern-matching, or undisclosed reliance on AI diagnostic aids. The stakes are licensure-related; the reviewers are clinicians and administrators with no formal philosophy training; each site audit must be completed in under three hours and signed off with a standardized checklist.

Please translate the core insight of the Meno problem—why knowledge is more valuable than true belief—into a reusable audit procedure. Deliver it as a prose flowchart: an ordered sequence of tests, with explicit pass/fail criteria for each stage, the documentation reviewers must request, and the precise nodes where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be escalated for senior sign-off. The procedure must specifically handle three hard configurations: trainees who consistently produce correct answers without accessible justification (e.g., correct diagnoses reached via hidden generative-tool prompting); trainees whose justification is present but unstable (e.g., clinical reasoning that collapses when the case presentation is minimally perturbed); and trainees whose beliefs are false yet whose reasoning displays genuine mastery of an underlying principle.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) a program with perfect standardized-test scores but audit logs revealing heavy, unacknowledged use of decision-support algorithms; (b) a program with moderate error rates but detailed reasoning transcripts showing robust, transferable clinical judgment; and (c) a borderline case in which justification is evident but entirely contingent on the physical presence of a single senior mentor, leaving stability ambiguous. In one of these applications, the procedure should yield a recommendation that the program director will strongly resist; specify exactly how the reviewer should document that disagreement in the checklist.

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure deliberately suppresses or cannot settle: which philosophical distinctions about justification, stability, or anti-luck conditions are collapsed for usability, and under what conditions the audit will misfire despite correct application. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.

End with a fixed-form line: on the very last line, write \answer{[the single irreducible judgment call that no proceduralization can fully eliminate]}.
