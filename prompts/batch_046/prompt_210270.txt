For an independent philosophy project in my final year of high school, I am trying to determine exactly what a philosophically adequate theory of inductive reasoning must accomplish, particularly in light of the no-free-lunch results in statistical learning theory, which seem to establish that no single learning strategy can dominate every other strategy across all possible data distributions. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or author citations; I want the full conceptual specification—the phenomena it must cover, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must preserve, the formal results it must not violate, and the reasoning that makes each of these a genuine requirement rather than the prejudice of one camp.

Please lay out the desiderata in list form, with the motivation behind each entry. The theory must explain why inductive generalization is treated as rational in domains such as clinical medicine or gravitational physics, yet as unwarranted in astrology or conspiracy theorizing, without simply assuming that science succeeds by definition. It must maintain a strict distinction between having some inductive warrant at all and having a specific degree of warrant, since in practice we rank generalizations by confidence and do not treat them as equally secure. It must be fully consistent with the no-free-lunch result that a universal, domain-independent learning algorithm is impossible, which means it cannot reconstruct induction as one globally valid inference rule applied uniformly everywhere. At the same time, it must account for the fact that human reasoners begin with strong, domain-specific prior assumptions—treating certain possibilities as live and others as negligible—without deriving those priors themselves from earlier inductive steps, which would be circular. The theory should also avoid collapsing normative justification into mere evolutionary reliability or pragmatic success, because that would change the subject away from the question of why we are entitled to expect the future to resemble the past. In addition, please consider whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so, what that impossibility reveals about the structure of the problem itself.

Then identify which single requirement does the most damage to the popular existing approaches—for instance, to a naive universal Bayesian account, to a pure pragmatic or evolutionary story, or to a straightforward falsificationist model—and briefly score those main contenders against your list. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases, however, rather than on the scoring.

The standard of adequacy I have in mind is a theory good enough to guide actual scientific methodology and ordinary reasoning about future events, not one that dissolves the problem by redefining induction away. A table organizing the requirements and the brief assessments is acceptable if it clarifies the structure.
