I've been re-reading Hofstadter's treatment of tangled hierarchies and the "inviolate level" — the expectation that any strange loop, like Escher's Drawing Hands, must bottom out in some untouched ground, even when each level seems to generate the other. It strikes me that Christopher Nolan's Inception stages almost exactly this architecture with its nested dreams, but I'm unsure whether the film actually achieves a true strange loop or whether it quietly preserves an inviolate foundation and just disguises it with ambiguity.

Read Inception through this lens. Where does the narrative set up an expectation that some layer is the untouched ground — perhaps through the mechanics of totems and "kicks," through Cobb's emotional need for a definitive bottom level, or through the visual grammar that distinguishes dream architecture from "real" physics — and where does it systematically undermine that expectation? Is Cobb's totem an attempt to impose an inviolate distinction between dream and reality from within the loop itself, analogous to trying to designate one hand in Drawing Hands as the prior drawer? And does the ambiguous ending, with the spinning top, represent a genuine tangled hierarchy in which no level is prior, or is it a refusal to collapse the loop that leaves the inviolate level intact but hidden?

I'd like a reading that takes both the film's narrative structure and the formal distinction seriously: don't reduce Inception to a mere illustration of strange loops, but say where the film's emotional logic — Cobb's guilt over Mal, the intrusion of the past into every constructed level, the question of whether waking matters if the experience is fully real — adds something that the abstract concept misses. Point to specific sequences (the hotel corridor, the limbo of unstructured space, the final airport scene) and say whether they illuminate or distort Hofstadter's idea.
