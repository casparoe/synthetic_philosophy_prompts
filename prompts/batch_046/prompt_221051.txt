I'm currently enrolled in a seminar on the philosophy of historical judgment, and we've been debating whether it is conceptually coherent—and not morally obscene—to rank mass atrocities by severity. Some classmates insist that placing events like the Holocaust, the Holodomor, the Rwandan genocide, and the transatlantic slave system on a single axis of moral badness is not only epistemically reckless but performatively disrespectful; others reply that refusing all comparison collapses into a particularism that hides structural patterns of state violence. To move beyond slogans, I want to ask about a more restricted claim: my credence concerns whether an ordinal evaluation is possible and non-obscene if we restrict ourselves to what I'll call morphological comparison—mapping shared structural features such as bureaucratic intentionality, scale of destruction, and systematic erasure of victim identity—rather than axiological ranking, which would place them on one metric of total moral badness. Please state your credence, between 0 and 1, that such morphological comparison can yield a defensible ordinal judgment without being obscene.

Decompose your reasoning into at least four terms with rough probabilities: (a) that moral values across disparate historical contexts are sufficiently commensurable to support cross-case evaluation at all; (b) that the obscenity objection targets mainly the implied equivalence of axiological ranking rather than morphological comparison itself; (c) that structural features can be isolated and weighed without smuggling in a totalizing moral calculus; and (d) one further factor you think belongs in the derivation, with brief justification. Show how they combine into your final number. Then tell me which term you are least confident in, and what kind of evidence—whether from the psychology of comparative judgment, the phenomenology of survivor testimony, or conceptual analysis of incommensurability—would most shift that term and therefore your overall credence.
