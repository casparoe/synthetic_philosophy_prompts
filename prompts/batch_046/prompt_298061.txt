I cover science and policy debates, and I repeatedly encounter advocates framing regulatory demands—on climate engineering, AI governance, or public-health mandates—as expressions of “the will of the people.” I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in conceptual analysis of populism and democratic legitimacy rather than in polling data, that I can run in under fifteen minutes per claim to determine whether such an invocation is substantively democratic or merely rhetorical. Structure it as an ordered set of diagnostic tests with clear stop conditions: first, referential specificity—who counts as “the people” and whether dissenters are excluded retroactively (a move I will call phantom constituency); second, procedural provenance—whether the claim derives from an articulated collective judgment or is imputed by the speaker; third, normative bindingness—whether the invoked will actually bears on the technical policy question at hand. For each test, specify what answer allows the procedure to continue, what triggers an immediate “rhetorical—do not treat as democratic mandate” verdict, and where the question is one of values that must be handed off to human judgment rather than settled by the checklist. Flag structural blind spots, especially conflicts between diachronic and synchronic popular will. Apply the procedure to three cases: (i) an environmental coalition invoking “the people” to block a federally approved carbon-capture trial; (ii) a government minister using the same phrase to justify accelerating deployment of an experimental climate technology against expert warnings—this should be the case where the procedure returns a verdict I will dislike; and (iii) an argument that interprets non-participation in a referendum as silent assent. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure is structurally incapable of resolving, particularly when democratic legitimacy and scientific authority pull in opposite directions. The deliverable must require no legal expertise, no proprietary data, and no citation of specific theorists by name; it should be defensible to an editor on conceptual grounds alone.
