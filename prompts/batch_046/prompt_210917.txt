I'm currently taking an upper-division seminar on Philosophy of History and Environmental Humanities, and I serve as the undergraduate representative on our university's Interdisciplinary Curriculum Review Board. We meet next Thursday to vote on a proposed mandatory first-year module titled "Actors Without Intention: Climate, Pathogens, and Causal Agency in Modern History." The proposal, submitted jointly by History and Earth Sciences faculty, frames the 1815 Tambora eruption and the 1918 influenza pandemic not as background conditions but as primary historical agents that "determined" specific political outcomes—comparing famine-governance responses in post-eruption Switzerland versus Ireland during the 1816 Year Without a Summer, and arguing that labor-movement collapse in 1918 was driven more by viral dynamics than by concurrent wartime policy choices. The module draws on recent environmental-humanities arguments about non-human agency, but our board's charter requires that approved core curricula preserve a clear distinction between causal explanation and moral attribution, without endorsing any particular metaphysics of agency. Several members, including a political scientist who studies institutional choice, object that the module risks a form of environmental determinism by systematically sidelining human decision-making; others, including a historian of medicine, defend it as a necessary corrective to anthropocentric bias in standard narratives. Please draft the formal written opinion I should circulate before Thursday's meeting: it should recommend a concrete course of action—approve with specific mandatory revisions, approve with a monitoring condition, or reject—along with a defined implementation timeline, and it should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side rather than simply restating the charter's language.
