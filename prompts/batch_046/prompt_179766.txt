A friend and I just spent hours going in circles about moral luck, and I want a disciplined, conceptually precise resolution rather than another back-and-forth. Write a formal essay on the following controversy: suppose two agents are in perfect epistemic symmetry—identical beliefs, reasoning capacities, intentions, and awareness of risks—but they face circumstantially different environments that produce opposite outcomes (one acts without harm, the other causes structurally identical harm through no further fault of her own). Does this outcome-variation justify any difference in moral desert? In weighing the issue, examine the tension between the control principle, which ties desert to what lies within an agent’s power, and the evidential view, which treats outcomes as merely diagnostic of will-quality rather than constitutive of it. Also consider whether constitutive luck—the moral temperament and capacities one happens to possess—already undermines the symmetry argument at its root. Reach and defend a definite conclusion: either that epistemic symmetry combined with outcome-variation genuinely erodes desert and forces a revision of our reactive attitudes, or that desert is indexed solely to the quality of will manifested in action, rendering outcome-variation morally inert even in these structurally parallel cases. Do not end non-committally; justify your verdict with conceptual analysis rather than with empirical case-studies alone.
