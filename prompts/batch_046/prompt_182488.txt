I'm preparing a postgraduate workshop on distributive ethics — about fourteen students, ninety minutes, with tables and movable cards — after a long discussion with colleagues about why the standard trolley and transplant framings feel tired. I want a classroom activity that makes participants feel the force of the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, but I need it to avoid those well-worn scenarios entirely. Instead, please inject a specific conceptual twist: use an "intrapersonal versus interpersonal ledger" mechanism with physical, identity-bound commitment tokens. Each token should represent a non-transferable life project — for instance, completing a named dissertation, maintaining a religious vow, or caring for a specific relative — that can be rationally traded or postponed within one person's own plan, but that becomes conceptually illegible when summed across different people into generic welfare points.

Design the activity for this group and time: specify how the students are divided, the rules and physical materials (what the tokens look like, how they are arranged on individual boards versus a central "social ledger"), and a payoff or decision scheme that uses no real money but rather social approval, continuation rights, or exclusion from a final collective decision. The rules should be staged so that participants first experience the coherence of making trade-offs inside their own ledger, then face a crisis card that demands "liquidating" one randomly selected student's complete token-set to generate abstract points distributed among the rest.
