That personal shift led me back to Goodman’s grue puzzle, but framed around my own biometric predictions rather than emeralds. I have constructed an argument that seems to show induction is not merely philosophically puzzling but practically useless for anyone forecasting their own bodily states. The conclusion strikes me as absurd—I do organize my life around these forecasts—yet I cannot locate the exit. I need you to identify exactly which step fails, explain why it seemed plausible, and spell out what giving it up costs me.

Here is the chain, numbered precisely:

(1) For any observed regularity R in my data up to time t—say, that my post-lunch energy remains high—there exists a logically compatible rival R* defined with a gerrymandered predicate (for example, “high-energy-before-t-and-depleted-after-t”) that fits every observation available at t.
(2) Because R and R* are equally confirmed by all evidence I possess at t, induction provides no rational basis for preferring R over R*.
(3) If induction cannot adjudicate between R and its grue-style rival, then it cannot justify any specific prediction about what happens after t.
(4) This applies generally: my expectation that my energy will stay high tomorrow, that my medication will keep working, that my sleep architecture will remain stable—all are equally unsupported.
(5) Yet I do make these forecasts and structure my behavior around them; if they were genuinely groundless, my practical reasoning would be arbitrary.
(6) The standard defense is that predicates like “high energy” are projectible because they are entrenched in scientific and everyday language, whereas grue-predicates are disjunctive and artificially constructed.
(7) But entrenchment is a sociological or linguistic fact about which terms we have used, not a metaphysical fact about which patterns are real; it therefore cannot supply a non-circular justification for trusting R.
(8) Consequently, either induction is rationally groundless, or we must smuggle in an unjustified metaphysical commitment to “natural” predicates.
(9) Both horns seem unacceptable: the first renders my health planning irrational, the second forces me to defend a controversial ontology of natural kinds merely to plan my week.
(10) Therefore the argument must fail somewhere between (1) and (9).
