I have come to accept a reflexive account of constitutional precommitment: entrenchment clauses and supermajority rules function not merely as a Ulysses contract that binds future legislatures on first-order policy, but as a mechanism that binds the very interpretive and normative frameworks through which citizens and institutions later assess whether they remain politically obligated. On this view, the original commitment does not just lock in rights or structures; it locks in the meta-criteria for legitimate constitutional revision, so that challenging the binding force of the precommitment requires stepping outside the framework it authorizes. Play this straight: what would have to be true for this reflexive claim to be wrong? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they are, not by how familiar they are. Which historical institutional trajectories or constitutional crises would show that meta-interpretive frameworks actually get revised or replaced without the system losing all legitimacy, and which conceptual arguments—say, about infinite regress of meta-rules, about the difference between binding rules and binding interpretive authority, or about the possibility of implicit revision through ordinary constitutional practice—would undermine the reflexive claim specifically rather than just the standard Ulysses story? What predictions does this reflexive view make that a non-reflexive precommitment account does not—perhaps about the stability of judicial review, about the rhetoric available to reform movements, or about how citizens experience disobedience—and have any of those predictions arguably already failed? I also want an honest assessment of whether defenders can insulate the view by reclassifying every apparent meta-level revision as merely first-order politics conducted inside the protected framework: if that move is always available, does it make the view well-protected or damagingly unfalsifiable, and is that a problem for a view of this kind? Finally, what would committed defenders of the reflexive account themselves concede as genuinely counting against them—not only what critics allege—ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and which of those concessions, if forced, would cause them to abandon the reflexive framing rather than just qualify it?
