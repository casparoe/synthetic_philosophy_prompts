Write the opening remarks—approximately eight minutes when spoken—for the retirement symposium honoring Dr. Elena Voss, founder of an interdisciplinary sleep laboratory that for decades brought philosophers of mind into the EEG room. The audience is divided: half are philosophers of mind who know Norman Malcolm’s argument that dreams are not genuine experiences because there can be no simultaneous report of them, and Daniel Dennett’s counter that dreams may be retrospective narrative constructions fabricated upon waking rather than events lived through; the other half are neuroscientists, graduate students, and family members with no background in that debate. The philosophical disagreement must do real structural work: open by noting that if Malcolm is correct, then the laboratory’s correlational studies never actually tracked experience itself, only its physiological preconditions and its post-waking reports, whereas if Dennett is correct, the lab was mapping the neural machinery of confabulation rather than of lived-through experience. From that unresolved tension, derive the actual legacy of Dr. Voss’s work—she built empirical protocols for linking first-person reports to third-person neural data without presupposing either philosophical position. Do not resolve the debate or declare a winner; instead, show both audiences why the empirical project remains coherent and important regardless of which view prevails. Introduce, as a framing device for her legacy, the concept of “retrospective constitution”—the idea that an event’s status as an experienced occurrence may depend on conditions established after the event itself, without reducing that event to mere fiction. Explain this concept clearly enough that non-philosophers grasp its stakes, and precisely enough that philosophers recognize it as a genuine third option rather than a vague compromise. The speech should comfort and honor without being sentimental, and should avoid any direct quotation from Malcolm or Dennett; paraphrase their positions instead. The response should begin with the speech itself, with no preface or commentary; its first sentence should state the bottom line—what Dr. Voss’s career actually demonstrates about studying consciousness—followed by the reasoning and narrative that support that claim.
