I'm grading a take-home essay for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar. The assignment asked students to apply distinctions in the self-deception literature to a specific case, without merely summarizing canonical positions.

Question: Self-deception is often analyzed through either "partition" theories, which posit some form of internal division allowing one part of the mind to know a truth that another part hides, or "deflationary" theories, which reject literal internal division and instead explain the phenomenon through motivated reasoning, bias in belief-formation, and strategic avoidance of evidence. Now consider a hybrid framework—call it "functional partitioning"—that denies any homuncular division of agents but maintains that cognitive subprocesses can be motivationally shielded from one another, so that evidence available to one reasoning pathway is systematically unavailable to another, not through suppression but through structural isolation in processing.

Consider this case: A mathematician becomes convinced her proof of a lemma is correct, despite initial peer objections that point to a subtle gap in her reasoning. Over time, she genuinely believes the lemma is sound. She also systematically avoids seminar contexts where she might be required to defend the specific steps that originally troubled her, and when she thinks about the proof, she directs her attention to the elegant structure of the conclusion rather than the contested intermediate steps. She is not consciously lying to herself; she reports her belief sincerely.

Using the distinction between partition theories, deflationary theories, and the hybrid "functional partitioning" view, analyze whether this is a case of self-deception. In particular: (1) Would a pure deflationist say she lacks self-deception because she lacks hidden knowledge? (2) Would a partition theorist require evidence of an actively deceptive "part" of her mind? (3) How does functional partitioning change the analysis, particularly regarding her avoidance behavior and her directed attention?
