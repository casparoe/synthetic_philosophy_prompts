For my graduate seminar in practical philosophy—ten students, seventy-five minutes, in a room arranged with two facing tables—I want a structured simulation that makes participants feel, before they can name it, the ambiguity between treating an everyday convention as a moral obligation and treating it as a mere coordination device that outsiders have no reason to observe. The setting should be a fictional municipality, call it Veridia, with three local practices: a queueing protocol that is purely spatial but enforced through silent exclusion; a gratuity norm that functions as a hidden wage subsidy and is calibrated differently for residents and strangers; and a pedestrian-crossing rule that is locally efficient but globally dangerous for anyone who does not share the residents’ tacit risk calibration. Divide the group into four Residents and six Visitors. Give the Residents full normative briefings—including a secret disagreement among them about whether the gratuity rule is morally binding or merely conventional—while giving the Visitors only descriptive instructions, with no indication of which practices carry moral weight. Use a token economy: each Visitor begins with ten comfort-tokens and must navigate three decision points (joining a queue, completing a transaction, crossing a simulated street), losing tokens to Resident sanctions or gaining them through Resident approval, but never being told whether the sanction expresses moral blame or social inconvenience. After thirty minutes of interaction, halt the simulation and have the Visitors rank their felt obligations from strongest to weakest, then reveal the Residents’ hidden disagreements.

The debrief should map each decision point onto a different philosophical account—say, a Humean convention theory, a practice-internal authority view, and a universalist moral argument—while being frank that the activity cannot reproduce the actual phenomenology of long-term socialization, which the facilitator must supply. I want an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time: likely, Visitors will over-moralize the queueing rule and under-moralize the gratuity rule, and I need guidance on how to correct that asymmetry in the moment without breaking the simulation. Please include a timeline, the exact rules for token exchange, the hidden briefing for Residents, and a note on which feature of the philosophical problem—specifically, the distinction between normative expectations that generate genuine obligations and those that generate only internal reasons—the staged interaction necessarily simplifies. The rules, incentives, and hidden roles should be arranged so that participants run into the problem themselves before it is named; no lecture up front, and the group should not hear the framing question about visitors and local conventions until after they have experienced the token losses and gains.
