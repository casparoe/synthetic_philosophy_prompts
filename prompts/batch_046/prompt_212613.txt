I was discussing with a colleague who works in neuropsychology the well-known results from patients who have undergone complete corpus callosotomy. In particular, we considered cases in which the left hemisphere can verbally identify a stimulus presented to the right visual field, while the right hemisphere—controlling the left hand—selects a different object appropriate to a stimulus shown only to the left visual field, apparently without access to the left hemisphere's verbal report. My colleague argued that this demonstrates nothing more than a division of information-processing pathways within a single, unified conscious subject. I objected that if each hemisphere can generate its own behavioral response, construct its own post-hoc rationalization, and remain systematically ignorant of the mental contents available to the other, then we have precisely the conditions we would ordinarily take to distinguish two separate minds.

My confusion, I think, concerns whether phenomenal unity is a primitive metaphysical feature of consciousness or merely a functional integration that can be decomposed. I have read that philosophers distinguish access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness, but I am unsure how that distinction bears on these empirical cases. So I want to ask: given that split-brain patients can exhibit genuinely independent volitional control and separate narrative construction in each hemisphere, doesn't this empirically show that phenomenal unity is not necessary for personal identity over time? Or does the persistence of shared subcortical arousal, emotional integration, and a single continuous autobiographical narrative—mostly available to verbal report—indicate that a deeper unity remains intact, with the behavioral dissociation merely masking it? I suspect I am treating behavioral independence as sufficient for phenomenal separation, but I would like you to identify exactly where that inference goes wrong and to clarify what these cases actually tell us about the structure of conscious unity.
