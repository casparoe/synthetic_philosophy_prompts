I have constructed the following argument for my graduate seminar on social ontology and find it suspicious because it seems to show that Lewis's account of convention cannot accommodate gradual normative drift without collapsing. I would prefer to preserve both the idea that conventions genuinely evolve and that Lewis's coordination-problem component is doing real explanatory work, but I suspect I must pay a price somewhere.

(1) For Lewis, a convention is a regularity R sustained in a population by common knowledge that each agent prefers to conform to R in situations of type P, on the condition that others do so.

(2) This requires that agents share an ability to identify which situations fall under P; otherwise the conditional preference has no determinate object.

(3) The boundaries of any practical coordination problem are vague. Agents extend R to borderline cases through analogical judgment rather than through explicit collective renegotiation, producing a kind of domain slippage.

(4) Once such an extension is visible, common knowledge updates: agents come to know that others treat the borderline case as falling under P.

(5) By induction, R gradually covers a domain P* whose members share no non-trivial feature with the original P except that R applies to them.

(6) At P*, the sustaining common knowledge concerns the regularity R itself, not a shared preference to solve the original problem P, since P is no longer instantiated.

(7) Therefore either (a) the practice at P* is not a Lewisian convention, lacking the right kind of preference-based common knowledge, or (b) the original common knowledge was never specifically about P but about "whatever cases R governs"—rendering the coordination-problem component redundant.

(8) If (a), then genuinely evolving norms are never conventions, which is implausible. If (b), Lewis's theory collapses into a bare regularity-plus-common-knowledge account.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Identify the precise step or inference, explain why it initially looks sound, and then price the alternatives: what must we surrender if we reject that step, and can we do so without abandoning either gradual norm evolution or the explanatory specificity of Lewis's coordination framework? I am inclined to save the evolutionary claim over the redundancy claim, but I want to see the full bill.
