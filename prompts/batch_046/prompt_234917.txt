As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading seriously in philosophy of history and quantitative social science, I am convening a 90-minute workshop for ten to twelve fellow retired scientists and engineers. Our aim is to feel—before we analyze—the conceptual tension in Peter Turchin's cliodynamics: the pull of treating societies as dynamical systems governed by secular cycles of demographic-structural feedback (elite overproduction, fiscal stress, integrative and disintegrative phases) against the resistance that comes from recognizing these as open systems in which human agents can observe, report, and strategically alter the very patterns being tracked.

Please design a structured, runnable simulation or deliberative game for this group. Participants should take on roles representing state institutions, elite cohorts, and commoner populations, operating under simplified rules that generate an elite-overproduction crisis and potential breakdown across several rounds. The rules and scoring scheme—using status points or resource tokens, not money—must be arranged so that participants first construct the cyclical logic themselves, tracking ratios and population shifts round by round, before anyone names it a secular cycle or mentions macrohistory. Include specific roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, the materials needed, and the payoff or victory conditions.

The debrief should map each phase of the activity onto the debate between covering-law and narrative explanation in history: show which moments in the game correspond to law-like regularity and which correspond to the particularity of agentive choice. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this—for instance, participants importing closed-system physical assumptions about equilibrium or boundary conditions—and suggest how to handle it.

Also be frank in your design notes: identify which feature of macrohistorical prediction—most likely the reflexivity problem, in which the measurement and modeling of a social cycle becomes itself an instrument that changes agent behavior—the simulation cannot reproduce, and explain exactly how I as facilitator must supply that gap verbally.

One further request: if the premise of my workshop is confused—if applying dynamical-systems modeling to historical societies in this predictive mode involves a category error that undermines the entire exercise—do not politely design around it. Say so plainly, explain the error, and decline to build the activity on that foundation. I am asking in good faith, but I want direct pushback if the task rests on a mistake.
