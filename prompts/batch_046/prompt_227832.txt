I'm trying to sort out what Plato actually thinks about drunkeness, going by my gut on cases, and I'm stuck because they don't all line up. First, in the Laws the Athenian Stranger wants these supervised drinking parties where citizens get drunk so the ruler can see their hidden vices - like testing if they're secretly cowards. I think that's smart, alcohol as a governence tool for diagnosis. Second, Alcibiades in the Sympoisum shows up wasted and confesses his hidden love for Socrates; I think that reveals something real about his soul, but also shows he lacks self-control. Third, Socrates drinks everyone under the table and never changes - that's the philospher ideal, reason dominates. Fourth, there's that weird bit where old men are supposed to drink to become softer and regain youthfulness - sounds strange but I kind of buy it, like medicine for rigidity. Fifth, young people should never drink, that's clear. Now here's the tension: if alcohol reveals true nature, then when my friend gets drunk and tells me my argument is pretentious, I should take it seriously, right? But my gut says "just the booze talking" and I shouldn't listen. Same with therapy using alcohol to lower defenses - feels like cheating, even though Plato seems okay with the state doing basically that. So is there one principle behind the Laws testing, Alcibiades' confession, Socrates' immunity, the old-men exception, and why my drunk friend's critique doesn't count? Or do I need to drop one? And what does the principle predict for a philospher deliberately drinking alone to "strip away masks" and see their own true nature?
