I am delivering a seminar paper next Thursday to a metaethics workshop on evolutionary debunking arguments. The thesis I defend is restricted rather than global: evolutionary explanations undermine only those moral realist commitments that treat cross-cultural normative invariants as epistemically accessible without mediation by local normative equilibrium, and only if one grants what I term the modal isolation condition—that a belief is debunked precisely when its content is modally isolated from the causal pathway that generated it. I contend that standard debunking arguments meet this condition for strong realist invariants but fail it for constructivist norms that are causally entangled with their own evolutionary production, yielding a targeted antirealist conclusion rather than wholesale moral scepticism.

The audience comprises four non-naturalist realists who regard genealogical arguments as categorically irrelevant to justification, two evolutionary ethicists convinced that debunking is self-defeating or empirically overreaching, one philosopher of science alert to ad hoc restrictions in inference to the best explanation, and a Kantian who maintains that categorical practical reasons lie outside the domain of causal explanation altogether. They know the literature around Street and Joyce well, they are unsympathetic to middle-ground positions, and they will press me on whether the modal isolation condition is anything more than a relabelled reliability filter.

For each, indicate what I ought to concede immediately, where I must stand firm, how to reply within two minutes, and where that reply is softest. Identify the single reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive the session, and include the one question I am praying nobody asks—most likely whether the modal isolation condition collapses into a generic epistemic reliability condition, or whether documented cases of cross-cultural moral convergence falsify the local-equilibrium claim.
