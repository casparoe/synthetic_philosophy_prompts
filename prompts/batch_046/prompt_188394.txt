After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend about moral luck, I have been trying to systematize the judgments she made across a range of cases, and I need help predicting where she lands on a new scenario. Her verdicts are these. First, regarding two negligent drivers who are equally reckless but only one kills a pedestrian, she insists they are equally blameworthy and that the actual death makes no moral difference. Second, on Gauguin abandoning his family for Tahiti and becoming a great painter, she holds that he was wrong to leave regardless of his artistic success, though she concedes that had he failed he would have been foolish as well as wrong. Third, on a surgeon who operates with full care but kills a patient due to an unknown manufacturing defect in her instrument, my friend assigns no moral blame. Fourth, on a person with an uncontrollable genetic predisposition to aggression who assaults a stranger, she says they are somewhat less blameworthy than someone who deliberately cultivated aggression, yet not fully excused. Fifth, on two children with identical temperaments, one raised in extreme violence and one raised peacefully, who both later commit the same violent crime, she judges the first somewhat less blameworthy, though both remain responsible.

The new case is this. A climate scientist spends two decades advocating for costly emission reductions based on the best available models, exercising full epistemic diligence. A rival examines the same models with equal care but, shaped by a deeply contrarian intellectual temperament he did not choose, dismisses them and blocks the policies. By pure statistical chance, a cluster of unforeseen volcanic eruptions suppresses global temperatures for a decade, making the scientist’s predictions appear wrong and causing severe economic harm from the policies she supported, while the rival is publicly vindicated. Neither could have predicted the eruptions. Predict my friend’s verdict: is the scientist less praiseworthy or more blameworthy because her advocacy turned out harmful, and is the rival somewhat less blameworthy or even praiseworthy because his opposition turned out beneficial? Then identify the implicit theory of moral luck she is running—whether a strict control-based anti-luck view, a quality-of-will view that permits constitutive mitigation but rejects outcome luck, or a hybrid structural-luck view that distinguishes direct-action luck from systemic advocacy luck—and specify which of her earlier five verdicts most clearly supports that reading and which is the weakest fit. Next, propose the single further case that would definitively settle whether she accepts outcome luck in contexts of large-scale policy advocacy. Finally, please state how confident you are in this interpretation of her position and explicitly single out the point about which you are least sure.
