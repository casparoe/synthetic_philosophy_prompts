There's this emerging norm in AI safety governance — not law yet, but it's in a few draft charters — that any system past a certain capability threshhold has to run its explanations through a "transparency mediator" that strips rhetorical framing, emotional valence, and analogies, leaving only bare logical structure and calibrated confidence scores. The rationale is that if the AI is extremly persuasive, we can defang it by filtering the *form* of speech rather than censoring the content. What philosophical commitments about language, rationality, and autonomy does that institutional design presuppose? Does it assume persuasion is mostly packaging, so that stripping the packaging leaves a rational core untouched? Does it presuppose a view of human autonomy where we're only genuinely free when we respond to "pure reason" rather than narrative or affect? And is the practice even internally consistent — if the mediator's rules are designed by fallible humans, haven't we just relocated the manipulation upstream? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what's strictly required from what merely fits well, and say whether several rival philosophies could license the same protocol. Then tell me what a communication framework built on a genuinely different view — one where affect and analogy aren't corruptions of reason but part of how understanding works — would actually have to change in practice.
