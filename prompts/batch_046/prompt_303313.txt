I run a 90-minute graduate seminar on decision theory for 12 students with no phones or web access. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the specific pull of Pascal's mugging, not through a generic small-probability/large-reward story but through a structured allocation mechanism: each student receives 100 abstract "credence tokens" that they must distribute across five nested modal scenarios of increasing implausibility and exponentially increasing payoff (e.g., a fair coin landing heads in an epistemically isolated branch; that branch containing 10^100 welfare-relevant duplicates; a further nested claim about those duplicates' suffering being averted). The rules should force a collective decision—each round requires unanimous commitment of a threshold number of tokens to "accept the wager"—so that students experience the pressure to keep conceding as the stakes scale, and they should discover only at the end whether they have been "mugged" into an unbounded commitment.

Specify concrete rules, materials (token sheets, hidden-role cards for one student who plays the mugger in round 4), a payoff scheme using only abstract points, the information structure (what each student knows about others' remaining tokens and about the true nesting of scenarios), and a timeline for the 90 minutes. Include a debrief that maps specific rounds onto causal versus evidential reasoning and onto the scope-insensitivity problem, and take a committed position—defended with reasons, not presented as an option—on whether the game should include a reverse round in which students themselves construct a mugging offer for a hypothetical agent. Be honest about which structural feature of Pascal's mugging (likely the genuinely unbounded or infinitary reward sequence) the finite five-round game cannot reproduce, and say exactly how you will supply that missing element verbally in the debrief. Also state what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—probably strategic token-hoarding that collapses the collective mechanism in round 3—and how to handle it without breaking the experience. The idea should emerge from the rules and token dynamics, not from an upfront lecture; do not name Pascal's mugging until the debrief.
