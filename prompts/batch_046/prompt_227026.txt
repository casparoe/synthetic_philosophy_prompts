Since my daughter was born eighteen months ago, I have become increasingly absorbed in long-range demographic forecasting and in the statistical arguments that bear on whether human civilization is likely to persist for centuries or for millennia. I have been studying the Carter-Leslie doomsday argument and its reliance on the self-sampling assumption, but I keep returning to a variant that receives little explicit treatment: the nested reference-class problem applied not to the biological species as a whole, but to institutional observers—risk analysts, modelers, and policy institutions—whose membership conditions depend on cognitive roles rather than on reproduction alone. If David Hume were alive today and acquainted with this modern reasoning, how do you think he would assess it? Given his skepticism about inductive inference, his account of custom and the uniformity principle, and his insistence that probabilistic judgments require stable, causally homogeneous experiential bases, would he reject the selection of a reference class as an arbitrary stipulation that vitiates the birth-rank inference? Or might he concede a pragmatically bounded class—say, entities with a shared causal history of biological reproduction—within which the doomsday reasoning retains some limited force? Furthermore, if he were informed that artificial cognitive agents, lacking biological birth order altogether, might eventually outnumber human observers and thereby disrupt the self-sampling logic from within the same institutional framework, how might his philosophy of causation and his distinction between matters of fact and relations of ideas shape his response? I am asking for a reasoned reconstruction rather than a historical citation; I want to know whether Hume’s resources for resisting inductive overreach would extend naturally to this kind of probabilistic prophecy, or whether he would find in it an illegitimate projection of custom beyond its proper domain.
