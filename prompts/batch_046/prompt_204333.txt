I am a novelist defending the psychological architecture of a forthcoming historical novel that examines the perpetrators of Reserve Police Battalion 101. At a seminar convened by the university’s Centre for Narrative History, I shall argue that neither the situational explanation advanced in Browning’s study of the battalion nor the cultural thesis of deep-rooted eliminationist antisemitism proposed by Goldhagen adequately accounts for the divergent responses—willing participation, psychological collapse, and strategic refusal—observed among the same group of men. Instead, I propose a model of incremental narrative dissociation, in which perpetrators reconstructed their moral self-image through the bureaucratic euphemisms and role-specific rituals of the killing process, thereby fragmenting moral agency rather than suspending it entirely. The audience comprises three literary scholars who prize psychological interiority over sociological abstraction, two social historians who regard novelistic interpretation as anachronistic when applied to archival silences, and a philosopher of history who will press me on whether this model can sustain collective moral attribution. Please prepare a speaker’s Q&A sheet: list the objections in the order they are likely to arise and in proportion to the damage they could do, phrased precisely as each faction would articulate them; for each, provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute exchange, note honestly where that reply is weakest, and identify the single question I most dread being asked. Write your response in British English, in a formal and polished register, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation.
