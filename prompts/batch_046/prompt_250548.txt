I am designing a training module for regulatory compliance officers who must sign off on supervision protocols for high-stakes AI decision-support systems—clinical triage, sentencing risk assessment, credit underwriting—without possessing machine-learning expertise and within a forty-five-minute window, with mandatory dual sign-off from both a legal lead and a technical lead. I want a reusable, ordered decision procedure—tests in sequence, branching conditions, explicit stop points, and marked judgment calls—that helps them determine whether a given system should be governed primarily through process-based supervision (inspection of intermediate reasoning traces, chain-of-thought fidelity, and causal load-bearingness of representations) or through outcome-based supervision (distributional robustness checks, ground-truth benchmarking across demographic slices, and input-perturbation testing). The procedure must preserve the philosophical distinction between these modes without collapsing them into a false dichotomy, and it must incorporate a specific, non-obvious criterion I will call the counterfactual perturbation check: altering an intermediate representation without altering the input and observing whether the output shifts proportionately, thereby distinguishing causally load-bearing reasoning steps from post-hoc rationalization. It must also account for epistemic leakage—the risk that monitoring intermediate steps itself perturbs the reasoning path—without treating this risk as decisive in every case.

Please construct the protocol for an officer operating under severe time constraints, with no access to model weights, who applies this decision repeatedly and must document each branch taken. Include the hard cases the procedure must handle: a system with transparent reasoning traces and correct outcomes that nevertheless fails the counterfactual perturbation check; a black-box system with strong outcome metrics but entirely inaccessible intermediate representations; and a system in which process audits appear coherent while outcome robustness collapses under slight input perturbation. Apply the procedure to three worked cases: a forty-variable clinical triage model, a recidivism-risk scoring tool, and a deliberately constructed synthetic case in which the protocol recommends process-based supervision with restricted deployment—a recommendation the legal lead will likely reject because it delays rollout. In each application, specify the exact branch taken, the unavoidable judgment call, and the documented limitation. Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot settle—specifically, where unresolved philosophical disagreement about whether artificial systems possess anything analogous to understanding, or about the moral priority of procedural transparency over predictive accuracy, must be resolved by the board rather than by the checklist. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or references to named papers; ground every distinction in the conceptual framework itself.
