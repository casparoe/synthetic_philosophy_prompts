I have no formal training in philosophy, but for several years I have been working through an idea on my own that I now call tranquilism: the view that the only things with genuine value are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, while pleasure, achievement, or any positive good counts for essentially nothing, and may even distract from what matters. I want to see whether this idea holds up when it is translated into the vocabulary I actually know best.

For the past fifteen years I have worked in regenerative agriculture, managing soil health for small perennial systems. I think naturally in terms of soil structure, water retention and drainage, nutrient cycles, salinity stress, root-zone oxygen, the difference between suppressing a pathogen and cultivating a symbiont, and the principle that healthy soil is defined primarily by the absence of compaction and toxicity rather than by the presence of any particular input.

Please translate the core claims of tranquilism into that soil-ecology framework. Do it systematically: take each main claim — that only absence of negative states has value, that positive conditions are irrelevant, and that well-being is a kind of zero-state rather than an accumulation — and restate it using soil conditions, drainage dynamics, salinity balance, root health, and nutrient cycling. For each translation, tell me what carries over cleanly, what only fits if I distort either the agricultural concept or the philosophical claim, and what has no expression at all in soil terms.

Treat the residue as the interesting part. I have been wondering whether tranquilism misses something that soil vocabulary actually captures well — for example, the idea that good soil is not merely the absence of salinity stress but also possesses an active, self-sustaining capacity to cycle nutrients and support symbiotic life, which looks like a positive property not reducible to the absence of blockage. Or is that a confusion, and does the soil framework actually support the tranquilist view that any such capacity is just the absence of impediment?

Also consider a distinction I have been playing with: between passive absence (fallow ground with no stress but also little biological activity) and active suppression (constant manual removal of weeds or salts, which keeps stress absent but requires ongoing intervention). In tranquilist terms, is there a meaningful difference between a state in which craving is naturally absent and one in which it is continuously suppressed? Does the soil vocabulary clarify that distinction, or does it collapse the two?

Finally, tell me whether the parts of tranquilism that fail to survive this translation point to a real limitation in how soil ecology conceives of health, or whether they reveal that tranquilism itself was relying on distinctions that become unclear once you try to state them with precision.
