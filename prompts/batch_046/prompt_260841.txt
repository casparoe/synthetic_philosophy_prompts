In a graduate seminar on revolutionary dynamics and political ideology, my classmates and I have been debating whether the French Revolution's turn to the Terror is best explained by structural class conflict, by the internal logic of revolutionary ideology, or by contingent factors such as foreign military pressure, personal rivalries, and institutional accident. During our last session, one participant dismissed the contingency line by invoking the maxim that "revolutions devour their children," treating it as an established principle that makes the turn to violence inevitable regardless of particular circumstances. I find the move unconvincing, but I also recognize that the slogan might be doing more precise philosophical work than a mere empirical generalization. Before the next meeting, I would like to be able to distinguish the different precise claims that phrase could be expressing in this specific argumentative context.

Could you lay out, as a menu of distinct precisifications, what "revolutions devour their children" might amount to when deployed against contingency-based interpretations of the French Revolution? For each reading, please indicate: whether it collapses into a tautology or near-tautology; whether it asserts a substantive structural or causal law about revolutionary processes; whether it depends on a specific normative or dialectical claim—for instance, that revolutionary regimes contain an internal mechanism of self-correction that necessarily eliminates their own originators, rather than simply noting that leaders are often purged; and, crucially, whether that particular reading actually undermines contingency explanations or remains compatible with them. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of historiography; I want a conceptual disambiguation organized as a list I can bring to office hours. Please also say, for each reading, whether it is plausibly what someone in my seminar meant when they used the phrase to argue that class-struggle or ideological-necessity accounts are superior to accounts that emphasize accident and personality.
