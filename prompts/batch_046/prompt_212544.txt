I have just come out of a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a friend over whether cosmological fine-tuning retains any evidential weight for design once we grant the possibility of a multiverse generated by eternal inflation and the string landscape. I need to be prepared for a seminar presentation I am giving next week to a joint philosophy-of-science and cosmology reading group. My position is that the multiverse does not dissolve the fine-tuning problem because it offers no canonical probability measure over the space of physical constants; without such a measure, appeals to observer selection remain probabilistically hollow and cannot explain why our particular life-permitting parameters are unsurprising. I accept that mechanisms for parameter variation exist, but I reject the inference that mere variation plus selection eliminates the need for explanation. The audience is sharply divided: on one side are physicists familiar with the measure problem in eternal inflation who regard design arguments as conceptually obsolete; on the other are philosophers of religion who share the intuition that the constants appear calibrated but are suspicious of borrowing decision-theoretic and anthropic reasoning from cosmology. Please prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as those two camps would actually phrase them, with a brief assessment of which carry real force. Tell me what I should concede upfront, identify the single reply that must land for my argument to survive, flag the weakest point in my own position that I should be ready to defend rather than hope goes unnoticed, and include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—specifically, whether my demand for a canonical measure implicitly assumes a realist interpretation of probability in cosmological contexts that my opponents reject, and how that meta-objection interacts with the Boltzmann brain problem.
