I am drafting a novel set in the months after Thermidor, and in my research I have encountered a summary—passed along by a fellow novelist who once studied the historiography—that claims to reconcile three dominant readings of the French Revolution. The summary runs as follows.

First, the Marxist social interpretation holds that 1789 was essentially a bourgeois revolution driven by structural class conflict: the aristocracy was displaced not by abstract ideas but by the material ascent of capitalist property relations, and every subsequent factional split can be mapped onto competing economic interests with only superficial ideological decoration.

Second, the revisionist school associated with the linguistic and cultural turn argues that class was not the engine but the product of revolutionary discourse. On this view, the very concept of revolution was invented in 1789, and the Terror was not an economic necessity but the logical working-out of democratic rhetoric that lacked any institutional brake; material conditions, in this account, play at most a permissive role.

Third, a more recent contingentist or microhistorical approach insists that neither class nor discourse explains the specific turn toward violence. Rather, local emotional economies, accidental decisions, and the irreducible particularity of events—the flight to Varennes, the September Massacres, the shifting alliances of the sections—made the Revolution what it was, rendering any grand structural narrative a post hoc imposition by historians.

The summary then draws a synthetic conclusion. It claims that these three readings are not actually in conflict because they all share a hidden premise: that the Revolution possessed an internal logic that eventually turned against its architects, the familiar trope that revolutions consume their own children. The writer argues that this trope is itself a retrospective fiction, invented by Thermidorian survivors to aestheticize their own survival as inevitable tragedy rather than contingent luck. By exposing the trope as narrative artifice, he says, we dissolve the false choice between class, ideology, and contingency: all three are merely different costumes for the same survivor’s myth, and once the myth is unmasked, no serious historian need choose among them.

How fair is this? I intend to use the argument to structure my characters’ debates about whether their suffering possessed meaning or was mere accident, but I do not wish to build an entire novel on a historiographical straw man. Go through the summary point by point: where does it accurately capture the school it describes, where does it conflate a modest claim with an extreme one, and where does it present an opponent’s standard objection as if it were a definitive refutation? Specifically, does the claim that the trope of revolutions consuming their architects is purely a survivor’s fiction survive scrutiny, or does it depend on misrepresenting the contingency school as denying all pattern whatsoever? And is the synthesis—that the three schools collapse into one another once the trope is unmasked—something any serious historian of the period would recognize, or is it an elegant confusion designed primarily for novelistic effect? I want to know what a historian would wince at, what they would grant, and whether the conclusion that follows from the corrected premises is stronger or weaker than the one presented here.
