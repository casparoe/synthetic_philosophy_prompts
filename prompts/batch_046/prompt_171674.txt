I'm gradign a philosphy exam on lying vs misleading and need a second pair of eyes. The question was: "Distinguish lying from paltering. Then present one conceptual argument for why the moral asymmetry—lying is worse—might collapse if we prioritize communicative intentions over speech-act form, and say whether this fully undermines that asymmetry."

Student answer:
Paltering is saying something literally true but leaving out the part that corrects the listener's wrong inference, like telling your TA "I've graded every exam I received" when you know they think the whole class is done. Unlike lying, you don't assert a falsehood; you just use strategic omission. The distinction collapses because both involve the same intentional structure: you want the hearer to form a false belief and you craft your utterance to cause it. Grice's maxim of Quality gets violated either way, since you're breaking cooperative exchange by engineering false beliefs. The hearer's epistemic harm is identical—they relied on your testimony and now hold a false belief—so there's no moral difference. The only defense left is that lying violates a specific rule against false assertion, but that's just a formal deontological quirk with no deeper weight. So yes, the asymmetry is completely undermined.
