Consider the institutional norm, common to medical, legal, and administrative ethics codes, that practitioners should suppress personal attachments, private projects, and emotional commitments when making official judgments—not merely avoiding conflicts of interest, but treating such commitments as irrelevant to reasoning within the role. The ideal is a kind of professional distance: the practitioner should be able to decide as if no outside project could outweigh procedural fairness or role-defined duty.

What conception of moral agency and personal integrity does this norm presuppose? Must it treat a person’s commitments as compartmentalizable or revocable at will, or can it be explained by a role-based view of responsibility that does not fragment the self? Is it compatible with Bernard Williams’s argument that integrity requires having ground projects that are non-negotiable—that for some commitments, adding a further layer of moral calculation introduces one thought too many? Reconstruct the implicit theory of motivation and identity that makes professional suppression seem rational, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and specify what changes in professional ethics would follow if we replaced its implicit view with Williams’s conception of integrity as the coherence of one’s core projects.
