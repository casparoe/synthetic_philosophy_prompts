Consider the clinical-institutional practice surrounding patients in disorders of consciousness: neurologists are required to classify patients into discrete categories—vegetative state, minimally conscious state, locked-in syndrome—using standardized behavioral scales that score observable responses to command and stimulus, and these classifications are entered into medical records as objective clinical parameters. At the same time, hospital ethics committees are routinely directed to base decisions about withdrawing life-sustaining treatment on judgments about whether the patient retains subjective experience or inner awareness, properties that the behavioral scales are explicitly designed not to assess and for which the protocol provides no operational detection criterion. Reconstruct the implicit philosophy of mind that renders this bifurcated practice coherent. Does it presuppose a functionalist account on which behavioral responsiveness is sufficient for clinical taxonomy but insufficient for moral status? Does it tacitly invoke a dual-aspect or property-dualist view in which consciousness possesses an objective, observable dimension and a private, morally decisive dimension? Or does it depend on treating the hard problem of consciousness as practically irrelevant for diagnosis yet practically decisive for ethics? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, assess whether its parts are philosophically consistent, and specify what would have to change in hospital protocol if the implicit view were replaced by one that either collapsed the diagnostic and moral criteria into a single measurable construct, or separated them explicitly through an adopted metaphysical account of phenomenal consciousness.
