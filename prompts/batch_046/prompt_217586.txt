ok so i've been trying to formalize the reference class problem for a seminar and i think i've accidentally built an argument that says doctors can't rationally diagnose anyone, which is definatly wrong. here's the chain:

(1) if you want a rational credence that you'll get disease D, you have to put yourself in the correct reference class of people who share your causally relevant traits.

(2) but what's "causally relevant" depends on the grain of your causal model -- cellular, behavioral, social, etc. -- and there's no unique fact of the matter about which grain is right.

(3) so for any diagnosis there's a fine-grained reference class where D is common and a coarse-grained one where it's rare, and both are correct relative to legitimate models.

(4) now here's the twist i added: if the reference class includes not just actual people but counterfactual versions of you in nearby worlds (since similarity is modal), then you also have to weight those versions by some similiarity metric.

(5) but that metric itself depends on which properties you treat as essential, and picking that seems to require knowing the very probabilities you're trying to compute -- it's circular.

(6) so the framework gives you ambiguity in grain, ambiguity in counterfactual weighting, and circularity in fixing either.

(7) therefore there's no unique rational credence for any statistical prediction about you.

(8) but doctors obviously do rationally say "given your symptoms the risk is about 70%", so (7) can't stand.

(9) i really want to keep (1) and (8). where does the chain actually break? and if i have to give something up, which step is the least costly to drop?
