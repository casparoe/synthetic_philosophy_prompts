I'm applying for a one-semester independent study (around 6000 words, needs a faculty sign-off but no grant). My half-formed idea is about Buridan's ass, but I want to avoid the usual decision-theory retread. Most people frame it as "what happens when two options have exactly equal value," then answer random choice or hidden asymmetry. I think there's a hidden distinction between being indiferent because reasons are symetrically balanced—call it equilibrium-indifference—and being indiferent because the relevant practical reasons just don't apply, which I'd call register-silence. If the donkey faces hay bales that differ only in left-right position, and spatial position isn't a practical reason, then there's no real tie, just a misapplication of ranking machinery. But if it's true equilibrium, where every relevant consideration is exactly counterweighted, maybe practical reason can't actually sustain that state without collapsing.

So the precise question: Does Buridan's paradox depend on conflating equilibrium-indifference with register-silence, and can we dissolve or reshape it by modeling practical reason as allowing "gaps" where no ranking is generated, rather than as producing a complete ordering with occasional ties?

Why existing answers leave it open: The standard literature treats indifference as a uniform relation—either there's a tie or there isn't. Nobody asks whether practical reason can be genuinely silent on a pair in a way that's structurally different from being evenly split. If it can, then standard solutions (will-based tie-breaking, infinitesimal differences) are fixing a problem created by forcing a ranking where one shouldn't exist.

Aproach: Month one, reconstruct the paradox in modern utility terms but drop the completeness axiom. Month two, work through Buridan's discussion of will, liberty, and reason from standard translations—I'll skip the Latin originals—to see if he already gestures at something like register-silence. Month three, develop a minimal "gap" model and test it on variants: two-way, three-way, continuous options. Month four, write up and check whether the gap model collapses into ordinary tie-breaking.

What counts as failure: If the two kinds of indifference can't be separated clearly, or if the gap model is just tie-breaking with extra steps. Or if Buridan's own view is flatly incompatible and I can't salvage anything interesting.

Realistic scope: One semester, conceptual only, no empirical work.
