Consider a neighborhood collective that has adopted a symmetric, turn-based protocol for resolving disputes over shared spaces—specifically, scheduling access to a community workshop on weekday evenings. Under the rules, two parties alternate making claims or objections, there are no draws, and the procedural structure is identical regardless of who moves first. One member argues that this protocol must contain a hidden bias in favor of the first mover, and offers the following reasoning: imagine, for the sake of argument, that there existed a perfect winning strategy for the party who moves second. The first party could then open with a purely nominal claim—one that consumes a turn but alters no substantive conditions—and thereafter execute precisely that second-party strategy. Because the first party now possesses the second-party strategy plus an additional, costless opening move, they would be strictly better positioned. Hence, the member concludes, a perfect second-mover strategy is impossible, which implies that the first mover can never be at a structural disadvantage.

Another collective member objects that this argument illicitly transfers reasoning from abstract combinatorial games into a social setting where moves carry informational and relational weight. The "stolen" strategy was calibrated for a player who knows they are responding; once the first mover adopts it with an anomalous prior claim, the common-knowledge conditions that made the strategy coherent may collapse. The strategy is not a portable algorithm but a situated plan.

I am trying to determine whether the first member has identified a genuine feature of symmetric decision protocols, or whether they have committed a category mistake by applying a proof technique outside its domain of validity. How should one evaluate the reach of this kind of copycat reasoning? In particular, is there a principled distinction between contexts—such as pure resource-allocation games versus negotiation settings with expressive moves—where adopting a hypothetical opponent's strategy remains rational, and contexts where doing so undermines the very conditions that made the strategy available?
