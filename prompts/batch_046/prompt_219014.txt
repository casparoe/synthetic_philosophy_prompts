After being asked to advise on how our institution should commemorate a founding injustice tied to its endowment, I have come to defend a specific claim: that institutional successors bear a duty of remembrance not because they inherit guilt, but because they continue to enjoy the material and narrative advantages produced by the wrong, and because "mnemonic abdication"—the active restructuring of archives, curricula, and commemorative practices to make remembrance administratively inconvenient—is conceptually distinct from "mnemonic decay," the natural erosion of testimony over time. I further hold that this duty binds through what I call narrative authority: the power to determine what is archived is itself an inherited institutional capacity that creates an obligation not to suppress. What would genuinely count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure: would it survive if empirical work on collective memory showed that institutional forgetting improves democratic deliberation in plural societies; would it collapse if mnemonic abdication turned out to be indistinguishable from legitimate curatorial judgment; and does the distinction between inherited benefits and inherited obligations hold up under scrutiny? Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would be merely awkward, say what defenders of remembrance ethics would themselves concede as damaging, and assess whether I have qualified the view into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that is a fatal flaw for a claim about institutional ethics of this kind.
