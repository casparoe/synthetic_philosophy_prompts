Please explain the conceptual tension that arises when Nāgārjuna's tetralemma (catuskoṭi) is applied not only to the inherent existence (svabhāva) of phenomena, but to the Madhyamaka distinction between conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya) and ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya) itself. If one asserts that this very distinction is only conventionally designated and therefore empty of inherent existence, then apply the four corners—affirmation, negation, both, neither—to that assertion itself: is it conventionally true, ultimately true, both, or neither? If it is conventionally true, it seems to presuppose the framework it undermines; if ultimately true, it appears to establish an ultimate fact about the absence of all ultimate facts, which looks self-defeating. How can a Madhyamaka philosopher avoid collapsing into either horn without implicitly adopting a "fifth" position that the method explicitly rejects? In your answer, address whether this reveals an asymmetry between applying emptiness (śūnyatā) to ontological claims versus applying it to the semantic-epistemic framework that delivers those claims, and whether emptiness can consistently apply to itself without generating a performative contradiction.
