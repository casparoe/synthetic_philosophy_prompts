I have been defending a modified Gricean account of conversational implicature—what I call Coordinated Implicature—according to which an implicature is generated only when there is mutual maxim-responsiveness between speaker and hearer. That is, both parties must be disposed to interpret utterances in light of violations of Quantity, Quality, Relation, or Manner, and this reciprocal disposition must be common knowledge within the exchange. On this view, speaker meaning is strictly narrower than sentence meaning plus conventional implicature; it comprises only those contents the speaker intends to communicate and that are recovered through this live coordination mechanism.

My judgments on cases are these. In ordinary face-to-face dialogue, when a speaker responds to "I am out of petrol" with "There is a garage around the corner," I take the implicature—that the garage is open and sells petrol—to be fully present, since both parties are actively coordinating on Relation and Quantity. By contrast, in asynchronous text messages where the recipient knows the sender relies on predictive text, I deny that any comparable implicature arises, even if the literal message violates Quantity; there is no live coordination to generate it. Similarly, when an AI assistant paraphrases a user's request to a third party, I maintain that the original user implicates nothing to that third party; the assistant's output carries only derived speaker meaning, not original implicature. But I remain genuinely undecided about a boundary case: a live video call with a consistent half-second delay, where both parties know about the delay and actively adjust their turn-taking. Does the delay destroy mutual maxim-responsiveness, or does the mutual knowledge of the delay preserve it?

What would count decisively against Coordinated Implicature? I am not asking for the familiar objection that Grice's own framework already accommodates mediated communication through the device of making as if to say. Rather, I want to know which conceptual or empirical results—concerning, for instance, interpretive capacities in clinical populations with theory-of-mind deficits, communicative behavior in non-human primates, or the formal semantics of indirect speech acts—would demonstrate that mutual maxim-responsiveness is either unnecessary or insufficient for generating implicature. Which of these potential defeaters would a committed defender of the view concede as genuinely damaging, and which could be absorbed by refining the coordination condition? Please rank them from fatal to merely awkward. I am particularly interested in whether the view risks collapsing into a generic relevance-theoretic account once the coordination condition is fully articulated, and whether that collapse is a problem or a clarification.

In your response, please state explicitly how confident you are in your assessment and single out the specific point about which you are least certain—especially regarding whether the delay case undermines the necessity of synchronous coordination or merely illustrates its adaptability.
