Write the full specification of desiderata that an adequate philosophical theory of human death must satisfy, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement, and present your answer in American English. The theory must be able to adjudicate between brain-death and circulatory-death criteria without reducing the disagreement to mere legal convention. Specifically, construct your list around the following dimensions: (i) coherence with the biological fact that cellular metabolism and certain reflex arcs can persist after whole-brain death; (ii) consistency with clinical and legal practices that declare death upon irreversible cessation of circulatory function in some jurisdictions; (iii) a principled distinction between the death of the human organism and the death of the person, including the specific case that forces the separation; (iv) an account of why a patient in a permanent vegetative state is not dead, despite the absence of consciousness and self-awareness; and (v) a constraint on any appeal to integrated functioning—namely, that the theory must resolve the status of a decapitated body maintained on an extracorporeal heart-lung machine, explaining either why its remaining subsystems fail to constitute genuine integration (if the body is dead) or why brain death should nonetheless be treated differently (if the body is alive). For each desideratum, indicate whether it is non-negotiable or reflects the preference of a particular camp. Then identify the single requirement that does the most work in undermining the standard clinical brain-death criterion, and explain briefly why. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; avoid highly specialized neuroanatomical terminology not part of standard philosophical discussion; and keep the weight of the response on the logical structure of the criteria rather than on a historical survey of positions.
