I am convening the final examination for an upper-level MA seminar in the philosophy of history and rational forecasting. I require one essay question, designed to be answered in ninety minutes at approximately 1,200 words, that treats the outside view of imperial decline through reference-class reasoning. The question must not be answerable by reciting standard summaries of base-rate forecasting or by listing well-known historical analogies. Instead, it should compel the candidate to engage with a specific conceptual difficulty: that selecting a reference class for predicting the dissolution or transformation of a political order necessarily involves prior normative commitments about institutional identity, temporal continuity, and what counts as the same kind of entity across cases.

To embed this difficulty concretely, present the candidate with three scenarios: the dissolution of the Austro-Hungarian Dual Monarchy in 1918; the gradual administrative devolution of the British imperial apparatus between 1945 and 1997; and the hypothetical fragmentation of a contemporary supranational regulatory union that lacks a single sovereign centre. Ask the candidate whether these three cases belong to a single reference class of imperial terminations, and—crucially—whether the answer depends on descriptive historical features alone or also on normative judgments about legitimacy and institutional purpose.

Please provide:
(i) the exam question itself, clearly framed for an MA-level audience;
(ii) a model answer of roughly 800 words that demonstrates how to navigate the tension between descriptive base-rate reasoning and the ontology of institutional persistence, including recognition of the circularity risk in using historical outcomes to define the reference class that is then used to predict them;
(iii) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks assigned to each (total 20), and a brief one-line note for each on how candidates typically fall short—particularly distinguishing answers that treat reference-class selection as a merely technical exercise from those that grasp its philosophical load.

The response should be written in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the answer must be constructible without recourse to external sources. Avoid verbatim quotations from named texts; paraphrase any ideas drawn from the forecasting or philosophy-of-history literature.
