Compare Judith Jarvis Thomson's violinist argument with Don Marquis's future-like-ours argument, concentrating on how each framework handles the distinction between biological dependency and axiological dependency — dependence not merely for continued existence, but for the realisation of future value. To test this distinction, consider a hypothetical case: an unconscious adult is sustained by a life-support apparatus that, as an inseparable side-effect, continuously generates rich and valuable experiences for an unrelated third party; disconnecting the apparatus kills the patient and ends those experiences, yet the patient never consented to the arrangement and derives no benefit from it. Which argument, if either, can consistently hold that disconnecting this apparatus is morally permissible in a way that parallels the violinist case, despite the presence of an axiological dependency? In your answer, analyse whether Thomson's appeal to bodily autonomy and Marquis's appeal to the wrongness of depriving a being of a future like ours both implicitly treat dependency as a uniform moral relation, or whether they can distinguish biological from axiological dependency without undermining their respective conclusions. Conclude by reconstructing which framework offers the more coherent account of why, and under what conditions, dependency generates moral obligations.
