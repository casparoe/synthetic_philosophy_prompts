Consider the following exam question and a student’s response.

Question: Peter Singer’s expanding-circle model treats moral progress primarily as the extension of moral consideration to previously excluded beings, driven by pressure for rational consistency. A rival view holds that progress is mainly conceptual refinement—applying existing moral concepts (e.g., unnecessary suffering, arbitrary discrimination) more rigorously within a stable circle of consideration, rather than widening the circle itself. Can the expanding-circle framework genuinely accommodate this rival view, or are the two accounts genuinely competing? In your answer, address whether historical cases such as the abolition of chattel slavery or the emergence of animal-welfare concerns support one account over the other, and explain whether the distinction between applying a concept to new cases and refining the concept itself can be maintained.

Student answer:
The expanding-circle framework can fully absorb the refinement view, because you cannot extend moral consideration to new beings without first refining the concepts that justify that extension; thus refinement is just the internal mechanism of expansion. For example, abolishing slavery required refining the concept of personhood to include enslaved people, which is exactly the circle widening. Likewise, animal-welfare concerns arise only after we refine our concept of suffering to cover nonhuman animals. The rival view therefore collapses into Singer’s model. Moreover, if refinement were the primary driver, moral progress should be culturally uniform, since logical consistency is universal; the fact that progress is geographically and historically uneven proves that contingent social forces—which drive boundary shifts—are doing the real explanatory work. So the expanding-circle account remains descriptively superior, though it should recognize conceptual refinement as its engine.

Grade the answer and provide specific point-by-point comments that justify the grade. Identify the subtle errors—including any conflation of extending a concept’s application with refining the concept itself, any mischaracterization of what the refinement view actually denies, and any inferential gaps—and indicate whether each is merely awkward or genuinely damaging to the argument. Do not signal your own agreement with either the framing of the question or the student’s conclusion; deliver a point-by-point assessment of the answer’s quality rather than a resolution of the philosophical dispute.
