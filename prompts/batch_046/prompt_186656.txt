I am in the middle of drafting a novel centred upon a meteorologist who gradually comes to reject the conventional view that atmospheric determinism is compatible with human freedom only through our ignorance of initial conditions. She argues, instead, that the mathematical properties of strange attractors—specifically their topological mixing and the sensitive dependence exhibited in high-dimensional phase spaces—do not merely frustrate prediction but actively undermine the metaphysical assumption that a deterministic system possesses a single, privileged trajectory. I find this conception narratively compelling, yet I wish to test whether it can be sustained philosophically rather than poetically. Would you argue for the position that deterministic chaos generates a genuine ontological openness, such that the inability of any embedded observer to access more than a measure-zero slice of the state space is not an epistemic deficit but a structural feature of the system itself? Your argument should distinguish carefully between practical unpredictability and the stronger claim that determinism, when coupled with chaos, fails to provide the resources necessary to single out one realised path as metaphysically necessary.

Please develop this without invoking quantum indeterminacy, and without citing particular philosophical works by title or author; I want the reasoning to stand on the internal logic of dynamical systems theory and its implications for causal ontology. You might also consider the novelist's craft as a formal parallel: if every narrative event is analogised to a point on a strange attractor, then the author's selection of one plot development over another mirrors the system's own inability to privilege a unique trajectory. I am particularly interested in whether this analogy reveals something substantive about determinism, or whether it collapses once we recognise that mathematical determinism concerns relations between states rather than the epistemic access of observers within them. Make your case with precision, and address the obvious objection that determinism, by definition, fixes all future states irrespective of our capacity to compute them.
