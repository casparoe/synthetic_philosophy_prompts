Following a recent case in the emergency department in which I halted further diagnostic workup once a septic patient’s hemodynamics stabilized—accepting clinical improvement rather than pursuing the definitive pathogen—I have been asked to design a single forty-five-minute teaching session for first-year residents on satisficing and bounded rationality in clinical decision-making. The audience has no formal background in decision theory or philosophy of science; they are competent clinicians who have just begun to recognize that they rarely optimize. Do not explain the concepts to me. Instead, provide a teaching plan: the precise sequence in which the components must be introduced and why, with explicit attention to conceptual dependency (what must be in place before aspiration-level adjustment or environmental structure can be introduced); the one carrying clinical case that should anchor the entire session, specifying what properties it must possess and what properties it must lack, together with an explanation of why a familiar non-clinical example such as grocery shopping should be avoided at this stage; the predictable misconceptions these residents will bring or form—including at least the equation of satisficing with clinical negligence, the belief that bounded rationality denotes a personal cognitive defect rather than a structural feature of decision environments, and the confusion between stopping rules driven by aspiration adaptation and those driven by mere fatigue—and the exact point in the sequence where each should be preempted; one check-for-understanding question I can pose at the end that reveals whether the idea has actually landed rather than whether they can repeat a definition; and what should be explicitly left out at this level, with brief justification. The plan should navigate, without conflating them, Herbert Simon’s procedural account of aspiration-level adjustment and the descriptive literature on fast-and-frugal heuristics, and it should address the specific clinical stopping-rule problem: under what conditions does a physician’s decision to stop searching become rationally defensible rather than merely expedient? Please also state how confident you are in the claims you make about the pedagogical dependencies and the misconception map, and identify the single point about which you are least sure.
