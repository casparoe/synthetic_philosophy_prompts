My friend and I just spent three hours arguing over whether norms like slavery or dueling collapse because people finally grasp a moral argument, because material interests shift, or because the code of honor that sustained them is quietly devalued. She kept insisting on Bernard Williams’s skepticism that rational argument drives moral revolutions, and I kept returning to Kwame Anthony Appiah’s account of honor as the real engine—how dueling died not when anyone proved it irrational, but when being a duelist became embarrassing rather than glorious. We left it in stalemate, and I want a poem that holds the stalemate open rather than resolving it.

Write a sestina of exactly thirty-nine lines: six stanzas of six lines, followed by a three-line envoi. Use blank verse with a formal, slightly archaic diction. The six end-words, in order for stanza one, must be: reason, gold, shame, law, blood, silence. Follow the standard sestina rotation (1-2-3-4-5-6, then 6-1-5-2-4-3, and so on through the six stanzas). The structural-philosophical constraint is this: assign the lines ending in reason and law to the voice of moral argument; assign gold and blood to the voice of material interest and violent enforcement; and assign shame and silence to the honor-and-shame mechanism Appiah emphasizes—norms dying not by refutation but by becoming unspeakable. These three voices should interpenetrate rather than stay segregated; by stanza five, every stanza should contain audible traces of all three. The envoi must place all six end-words in their prescribed order—reason, gold, shame, law, blood, silence—within a single grammatically complete sentence that knots the three explanations together rather than sorting them apart. Do not cite specific texts, provide a bibliography, or declare which mechanism is primary.

After the poem, include a section titled "Commentary on form and voice" (approximately 150–200 words). In it, explain: (1) why the sestina’s recursive, non-teleological rotation suits a moral revolution that does not proceed in a straight line; (2) how you distributed the three explanatory voices across the end-words and whether they bled together as the poem progressed; and (3) what the envoi’s syntactic knot is meant to say about the unresolved state of our argument.
