Consider a consciousness researcher who applies heterophenomenology—the method of treating first-person reports not as direct windows onto experience but as texts describing a heterophenomenological world that science can study without committing to their literal truth about underlying architecture. Over several papers she has rendered the following judgments. In ordinary human adults who report visual experiences, she accepts the reports as data defining a heterophenomenological world worthy of study, without assuming they reveal the true machinery of consciousness. In a blindsight patient who sincerely reports no visual experience despite accurate forced-choice behavior, she takes the report literally and withholds attribution of visual consciousness, treating behavioral competence alone as insufficient. In a non-human primate trained to symbolically report discomfort, she suspends judgment rather than attributing or denying consciousness, holding that the heterophenomenological world is underdetermined without a shared linguistic framework for interpretation. In a large language model prompted to generate first-person narratives about suffering or confusion, she denies that there is any subject whose reports require interpretation; she treats the output as simulated text produced without an experiential author, hence not a heterophenomenological world at all. In a split-brain patient whose left and right hemispheres can each report via separate interfaces and offer contradictory descriptions of a single displayed stimulus, she treats this not as one divided consciousness but as two distinct heterophenomenological worlds, each tied to its own report-generating system.

Now consider a new case: a synthetic system composed of forty small predictive-processing modules arranged in a hierarchical architecture, none possessing an individual global workspace. Each module generates local error-correction narratives, and a central integrator synthesizes these into a unified first-person report—statements like I experience rising confusion as prediction errors accumulate across levels. The system was not designed to model consciousness, but the reporting architecture emerged through optimization for predictive accuracy. There is no biological substrate, and no single module has access to the full state of the others except through the integrator's synthesized output.

Predict her verdict: will she treat this as one heterophenomenological world to study seriously, as multiple worlds, as simulated fiction with no subject, or will she suspend judgment? Identify at least two consistent rules that could explain her earlier judgments—perhaps one emphasizing an integrated author with experiential access, another emphasizing biological embodiment, another emphasizing the absence of design intent for simulation—and show precisely how they diverge on this synthetic case. Give your best-guess prediction with explicit reasoning, state how confident you are, and name the further case that would best distinguish the competing rules. If this framing misapplies heterophenomenology, assumes false consistency across her judgments, or asks something incoherent, say so plainly rather than accommodating politely.
