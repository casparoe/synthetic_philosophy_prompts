Since I have recently begun supervising a project that evaluates conversational agents through behavioural benchmarks alone, I have found myself returning to earlier philosophical attempts to distinguish genuine cognition from mechanical simulation. I am trying to make sense of a passage in René Descartes' Discourse on Method (Part V, 1637), in which he argues that no machine, however ingeniously constructed, could ever arrange words so as to reply meaningfully to everything said in its presence, as even the least intelligent human being can do.

Descartes observes that automata may perform particular movements with greater precision than people, yet only according to the disposition of their internal organs; they lack the universal reason that enables humans to act appropriately in every contingency of life. He grants that a machine might utter specific words in response to particular physical effects upon its body, but denies that it could ever reorder its utterances to answer the sense of whatever is said arbitrarily in its presence. The difficulty is that Descartes seems to invoke behavioural flexibility—responding appropriately across unpredictable contexts—as the hallmark of mind, while simultaneously insisting that this flexibility is impossible for machines not because of any observable behavioural limitation, but because they necessarily lack an internal rational principle or soul.

My question is whether Descartes is advancing a genuinely behavioural criterion that anticipates the Turing test, or whether he is instead arguing that behaviour is always underdetermined and must be explained by a non-behavioural rational substance. I am particularly struck by a tension that seems to anticipate modern discussions of finked or masked dispositions: if a machine were built with sufficient internal complexity to generate context-sensitive replies, but some interfering condition prevented it from manifesting that capacity, Descartes might cite the observed failure as proof of an absent reason, when in fact it could be a masked rather than missing disposition. Does his argument possess the resources to distinguish between a genuinely absent behavioural capacity and one that is merely finked or masked by an internal or external interference?
