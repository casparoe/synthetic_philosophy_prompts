I'm drafting a two-page workshop prospectus on democratic inclusion and need a sharp, tractable proposal built around a half-formed intuition. But I keep returning to liminal cases—populations displaced across generations by upstream ecological policy, or residents governed through administrative rather than democratic mechanisms—where people are clearly caught in collective decision structures yet do not fit cleanly into either criterion. My suspicion is that the stalemate persists because both frameworks assume that belonging must be on-or-off, and that we could make progress by asking whether democratic membership should instead be reconceptualized as a scalar property of "justificatory standing": the degree to which an agent is embedded in structures that can register and answer her objections. The proposal should state this question with precision, explain why the standard binary answers leave it genuinely open rather than merely adding edge cases, and defend a specific methodological path—say, conceptual analysis of liminal standing-types combined with a reconstructed reciprocity condition—rather than the obvious empirical survey or historical genealogy. Please lay out a realistic six-month timeline month by month, specify what the deliverable would look like if successful (for instance, a taxonomy of standing-types that dissolves some classic puzzles but sharpens others, or a proof that scalar membership collapses back into binary distinctions under institutional pressure), and say explicitly what would count as failure. I will add all citations myself, so do not include a bibliography or literature review by author and date; keep the framing conceptual so that it can stand without access to specific texts. The scope should fit a short fellowship, and the angle should feel like a genuine intervention rather than a restatement of the affected-interests versus coercion debate.
