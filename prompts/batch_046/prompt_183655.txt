I'm working through my own case judgments about who counts as "liberal" across political vocabularies, and I'm trying to see whether the pattern is principled or just parochial. In American usage, I think it correct to call a supporter of universal healthcare, expanded civil rights protections, and redistributive taxation a liberal, and I think it wrong to apply the same label to a free-market libertarian who rejects the welfare state, even if both claim descent from John Stuart Mill. In European usage—say, in France or the Netherlands—I think it correct to call a pro-market, socially progressive politician a liberal, and somewhat misleading to apply the term to a traditional social democrat who favors high taxation and robust welfare provision, even when that social democrat strongly supports civil liberties. A nineteenth-century British advocate of free trade and limited government, but not of universal suffrage, strikes me as clearly liberal in the historical sense. Yet I feel genuine tension about a modern American who combines strong support for abortion rights and anti-discrimination law with advocacy for school vouchers and broad economic deregulation: in U.S. parlance they are split into "socially liberal, fiscally conservative," but I wonder whether there is a unified concept that either absorbs or rejects that combination. Meanwhile, I am inclined to deny the label "liberal" to a thinker who champions negative liberty and laissez-faire economics while explicitly rejecting democratic legitimacy altogether, even though that position resembles the economic core of contemporary European liberal parties.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on a specific conception of liberty, equality, or the legitimacy of state coercion—that captures all these verdicts? Please state it precisely, show which clause is motivated by which case, and be explicit about which of my judgments it forces me to overturn if any. Also apply the resulting account to a case I have not listed: a Scandinavian social democratic party that supports extensive welfare provision and high taxation, but also entrenches strong constitutional protections for individual autonomy precisely to shield citizens from majoritarian intrusion. Does that party come out liberal, social democratic, both, or neither, and what does that tell us about whether the concept is essentially tied to the size of the state or to the structure of justification it offers for coercion?
