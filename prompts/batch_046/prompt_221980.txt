student answer:
The doctrine of anatta denies a permanent atman, saying the person is just five skandas—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—held together by karma and causal dependence. This lines up closely with Parfit's reductionism, since he also rejects any further fact of identity beyond physical and psychlogical conectedness. Both replace the self with a stream, so there's no enduring owner of actions.

Because of this, promise-keeping is just a matter of continuity. The mental state that made the vow is linked to my present skanda-stream through psychological overlap and karmic traces, which is exactly what Parfit's Relation R describes. So the present 'me' inherits the obligation because the relation holds; the stream itself carries the normative weight. We don't need a self to do the work.

One might worry about branching—if consciousness split, both branches might share the past relation—but in normal life there's no fission, so in those cases there is a definite fact that I am the same person who promised. The conventional self is only a prajnapti, a useful label for the stream, but all real explanation comes from the causal links between ksanika states. So anatta and reductionism jointly dissolve the problem.
