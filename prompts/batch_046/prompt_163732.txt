I keep hearing people defend heavy reliance on AI reasoning tools by comparing them to GPS navigation: the device calculates the route, you still reach your destination, and if it breaks you can always learn the terrain yourself. I want to take this analogy seriously and audit it point by point, because my own experience doesn't quite line up with the reassurance.

Could you lay out the correspondence explicitly—what in reasoning plays the role of the GPS, the driver, the destination, the mental map, and the underlying physical terrain? Then show me where the mapping breaks: is there an equivalent in reasoning to a landscape that exists independently of the tool? I also want to flag something specific I've noticed. With a GPS I may forget street names, but I still recognize that I'm in a city with roads and landmarks; with AI I sometimes find I've lost not just the answer but the shape of the problem I started with—the kind of reasoning it required, the distinctions that mattered, the way the question was framed. Is that a real disanalogy, or just my imagination?

Please separate the breaks that actually threaten the "harmless tool" conclusion from the ones that don't. In particular, does the GPS analogy quietly import the assumption that knowledge is like a landscape you can always learn later if needed? And if some forms of understanding are structurally altered rather than merely postponed—if using the tool changes what kind of thinker you are, not just what you happen to know right now—does the analogy's conclusion that dependence is benign still survive? If the analogy ultimately fails, what would be a better comparison for thinking about what AI dependence does to understanding?
