Contemporary development reports and historical sociology often explain divergent economic trajectories—say, sustained entrepreneurial activity in parts of Northern Europe or high household savings rates in certain East Asian economies—by citing “cultural values” such as residual Protestant asceticism or Confucian discipline, while treating institutional rules and resource distributions as background conditions rather than primary explanatory factors. What philosophical commitments about causation, explanation, and ontology does this explanatory practice presuppose? Must one accept that culture operates as a cause irreducible to aggregated individual beliefs, or can a rigorous methodological individualism recover the same predictions? Furthermore, does invoking Weber’s thesis commit the analyst to maintaining a distinction between understanding action through subjective meaning and explaining macro-outcomes through causal mechanisms, and is that distinction consistent with treating values as policy-manipulable variables—like education spending or infrastructure—rather than as historically embedded orientations that resist deliberate engineering? And if the practice implicitly rules out reducing values to incentives, does it thereby presuppose a specific view of how norms differ from preferences in guiding behavior?
