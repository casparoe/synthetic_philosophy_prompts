I'm working on a story about contingency in history — specifically that "hinge of history" idea that some moments are like door-hinges where tiny pushes swing the whole future. Two people I interviewed for the piece got into it over drinks and I want a referee.

Jordan said if hinges are real, then people who happen to be standing at them carry way more moral wieght. If you're the guy whose minor decision tips a hinge, you've basically steered civilization, so judging you by ordinary standards is too soft.

Riley shot back that this get's it backwards. At a true hinge, the past doesn't constrain the outcome — the system is unstable. So your personal character matters less, not more; the hinge structure does the causal work, not you. Calling it "your" decision is like blaming a coin for landing heads.

Jordan came back: but that's precisely why responsibility is heavier — you don't need perfect foresight, just the awareness that you're in a high-leverage spot. The instability means your specific act is the decisive factor, so you can't hide behind "history was going to do this anyway."

Riley replied that hinges are only identifiable after the fact. Nobody knows they're on a hinge in real time, so you can't demand heightened vigilance for something that looks like every other Tuesday. And if we retroactively label moments as hinges just to assign blame, we're not describing history, we're just moralizing with a fancy diagram.

Jordan then said maybe that's the point: the hypothesis forces us to admit that our usual moral accounting is built for smooth causal curves, not for discontinuities. If we're serious about hinges, we need a different ethics for them — not the same old rules with extra guilt.
