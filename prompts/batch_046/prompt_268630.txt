I cover philosophy of science and ideas for a long-form magazine, and I am preparing an essay on whether scientific defenses of unrealistic modeling can illuminate literary works that depend on manifestly false premises. I would like you to read Edwin Abbott's Flatland through the lens of Milton Friedman's argument—often called the F-twist—that the realism of a model's assumptions is irrelevant provided the model yields accurate predictions or valuable explanatory insights. Abbott's two-dimensional world and its geometrically rigid social hierarchy are physically impossible, yet the novel produces sharp observations about class, gender, and epistemic closure. Treat the dimensional reduction not merely as allegory but as a kind of narrative idealization: where does it function like an economic model's perfect-competition assumption, serving as an instrumental fiction that reveals structural dynamics invisible at full scale? And where does it resist that framing—perhaps because fiction succeeds through hermeneutic fidelity or emotional resonance rather than predictive accuracy? I want you to engage specific scenes—A. Square's encounter with the Sphere, the color revolution, and the suppression of three-dimensional knowledge—to show where Friedman's defense helps explain why Abbott maintains the false premise, and where the novel insists on being read representationally rather than instrumentally. Do not reduce Flatland to an illustration of market theory; take both the literary text and the philosophy-of-science debate seriously, and say where the work adds something that abstract argument alone does not. Finally, please separate what is broadly settled about Friedman's position from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of economics and philosophy of science, and avoid presenting any disputed interpretation of the F-twist as if it were the consensus view.
