I am preparing a paper for a philosophy-of-mind seminar on personal identity and was given the following summary of Douglas Hofstadter's view by a classmate. She said that Hofstadter denies any immaterial soul and instead treats the self as a strange loop: a self-referential pattern that arises when a system symbolizes itself within its own structure, particularly as developed in works like I Am a Strange Loop. So far I follow her. But she then advanced three stronger claims. First, she said these loops are not locked to a single brain: because we constantly absorb other people's patterns—their mannerisms, judgments, and emotional responses—those others literally become embedded as sub-loops within our own strange loop, so that part of their I genuinely resides inside our brains. Second, she claimed that this makes personal identity distributed across a social network rather than localized in individual skulls, with the boundary between self and other being a useful fiction generated by the loop's need to center its narrative rather than a real metaphysical boundary. Third, she argued that if two close friends of a deceased person both internalized her patterns deeply enough, there would then be two genuinely continuous, equally authentic instances of that deceased person's I running in parallel, with no principled reason to call either one the real continuation.

I want an accuracy audit of this characterization. Is Hofstadter actually committed to the idea that internalizing another person amounts to hosting a fragment of their self, or does that conflate his strange-loop theory with extended-mind or distributed-cognition theses he might reject? Does he really think identity can split into multiple equally valid copies across different brains, or is he more cautious about what survives? And is the framing of the self-other boundary as a useful fiction accurate, or does it overstate his ontological claim into a kind of eliminativism he does not hold? Please go through her three stronger claims one by one, distinguishing what is a plausible extension of Hofstadter's premises from what is a subtle misrepresentation—whether an overstatement of his thesis, a conflation with another philosopher's argument, or an unjustified slide from metaphorical influence to literal ontological distribution. In particular, tell me whether the duplication scenario she describes actually follows from his premises, or whether it relies on an unstated assumption about pattern identity that Hofstadter himself might deny.

Please also say what specific clarification from Hofstadter's own treatment of brain-transplant or split-brain scenarios would cause you to revise your judgment, and identify the part of your reasoning that is most likely to fail if I have inadvertently distorted her summary in relaying it.
