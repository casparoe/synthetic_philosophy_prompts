I've been working through a position for a course on comparative philosophies of well-being, and I've pushed it into what feels like an unfamiliar corner. The view—call it representational tranquilism—holds that only the absence of craving and suffering has intrinsic value, but it adds a stronger claim: any mental state with intentional content, whether a belief, a perceptual judgment, an attentional orientation, or even the minimal aboutness of awareness, necessarily involves a proto-craving, a kind of micro-tension or directedness that is intrinsically disvaluable. So the ideal is not merely calm but contentless: a bare phenomenal presence without representational structure, in which even the awareness that one is calm has been extinguished, since that awareness itself would carry the same representational charge.

What I want is a map of this view's exposure. Which observations, conceptual arguments, or phenomenological descriptions would genuinely count against it—decisively, not just awkwardly? I'd like you to distinguish evidence that should force a revision (for instance, a demonstration that intentionality can occur without any valenced charge, or a coherent first-person report of contentless awareness that still retains minimal self-reference) from evidence that is merely uncomfortable (such as the intuitive appeal of knowledge, aesthetic experience, or relational love). I'd particularly like to know whether defenders of this kind of negative hedonism would themselves concede that the intentionality claim is the weakest point, or whether they can absorb every objection by redefining craving broadly enough to make the view unfalsifiable. And if the view is well insulated—if any appeal to the value of representation is dismissed in advance as already infected by proto-craving—is that insulation a virtue for a theory of well-being, or does it render the position empty?
