I'm preparing a six-month independent study proposal (approximately 8,000 words, one semester) on a tension in the Principal Principle that standard treatments leave unsettled. The standard formulation holds that if an agent knows the objective chance of E is x, and has no other relevant evidence, her rational credence in E should be x. I want to examine whether this survives if objective chances are structurally fragmented—for instance, in a deterministic multiverse where different regions or branches instantiate different stable frequency distributions, and an agent knows the global deterministic law but not which local distribution governs her epistemic situation. The precise question: does PP require deference to a single global chance, to every locally accessible chance distribution indexed to the agent's position, or to none at all once global structure is known? Existing answers treat such fragmentation as either impossible or irrelevant to rational credence, leaving open whether PP is a principle about global rationality or about localized epistemic policy. The approach: first, formalize three interpretations of PP under fragmentation (global-deference, local-indexical, and rejection); second, test each against cases where an agent has full knowledge of deterministic dynamics but only partial structural self-location; third, determine whether any interpretation avoids either probabilistic incoherence or an implausible demand that agents suspend belief in their local conditions. A successful result would be a clear criterion distinguishing when an agent should defer to a local chance and when deferral is blocked by knowledge of global structure; failure would mean the argument collapses into a restatement of standard self-locating belief problems without yielding new constraints on chance-credence alignment. The proposal should state the question precisely, explain why current positions do not settle the fragmentation case, outline the method step-by-step with realistic monthly milestones, specify the deliverable, and include an honest assessment of scope risks—especially whether resolving self-locating belief issues is a hidden prerequisite that makes the project too large for one semester. Please separate what is settled (the basic formulation of PP and its core motivation) from what is genuinely contested (whether PP applies across fragmented or indexical chance structures, and whether self-locating evidence counts as admissible under the principle), and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view.
