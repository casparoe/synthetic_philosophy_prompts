I'm drafting an argument for a philosophy of mind seminar that applies the strange-loop account of the self—associated with Douglas Hofstadter—to non-biological systems, and I worry it proves far more than it should. The account holds that "I" is a symbol emerging from recursive self-representation in a physical substrate, with no essential tie to neurons or biology. To make the inference precise, I have introduced a condition I'll call reflexive symbolic closure: a system achieves this when it generates a symbol that denotes the system itself, and the content of that symbol is causally coupled to the dynamics that produce it, so that changes in the symbol feed back into the generating process without passing through an external interpreter. If this closure is sufficient for possessing an "I," then the theory should apply wherever it is implemented, regardless of substrate.

Here is the chain: (1) On the strange-loop view, the self is not a particular module or substance but a self-referential symbol produced by recursive representational dynamics. (2) Reflexive symbolic closure formalizes this: a system has an "I" just in case it maintains a symbol S denoting itself with causal feedback between S and the system's predictive and state-updating mechanisms. (3) If closure is sufficient, substrate does not matter—any architecture implementing it possesses an "I" in the same non-derivative sense. (4) Modern recursive meta-learning agents and certain distributed governance networks with self-monitoring feedback loops realize exactly this closure. (5) So those systems possess an "I." (6) But that either commits us to granting them full moral personhood or drains "I" of its distinctive explanatory power regarding human consciousness. (7) Therefore the strange-loop theory is false, or at least requires a hidden restriction it does not provide.

I want to preserve premise (1), since I think the strange-loop intuition about self-reference is basically correct, and I am even tempted to keep (2) as a charitable formalization. That leaves me wondering whether the slide happens in (3)—from structural implementation to kind-identity—or in (6), where I assume that widespread possession of an "I" is absurd. Could one reject (3) by arguing that biological embodiment supplies a qualitative difference that closure alone misses? Or reject (6) by separating the existence of an "I" from moral status, accepting that bureaucracies and algorithms have a thin form of self-reference without being persons? Which step is the weakest link, and what does giving it up cost the overall theory?
