I host a small interview series for curious listeners without specialized training, and my next guest is a philosopher who takes Nietzsche’s scattered remarks about food, climate, digestion, and bodily health seriously as philosophy rather than biography. He defends what he calls a kind of gastronomic perspectivism: the idea that what we eat and how our bodies process nourishment is not separate from how we think, and that Nietzsche’s notion of great health is therefore a cultivated physiological achievement rather than a mere mental attitude or metaphor. I want eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond having him restate this position. I need at least one question addressing how he distinguishes Nietzsche’s selective nourishment from the ascetic denial of the body that Nietzsche attacks in Christian and Schopenhauerian morality, with a follow-up tailored to the answer he is most likely to give, in which he appeals to affirmation rather than rejection. I also need one question on whether this physiological epistemology collapses into a biological determinism that undermines Nietzsche’s central commitment to self-creation and the revaluation of values, again with a follow-up keyed to his probable defense that the body is material for interpretation rather than a fixed cause. And I need one question on the problem of universalization: Nietzsche’s own prescriptions—lighter Mediterranean-style fare, avoidance of alcohol and heavy German cooking, preference for particular climates—seem tied to his own constitution and historical moment, so does great health become just a private regimen rather than a philosophical ideal anyone can pursue, with a follow-up anticipating his likely move of distinguishing the specific menu from the underlying principle of attentive self-regulation. Beyond these three, I want questions that press the edges of the view: for example, whether someone living with a chronic digestive disorder or a radically different metabolism could still attain the philosophical clarity Nietzsche associates with health; whether modern nutritional science renders his specific prescriptions obsolete or actually vindicates his broader claim about the mind-body link; and whether interpreting the eternal recurrence, the will to power, or the revaluation of values through the lens of diet risks reducing all of Nietzsche’s philosophy to symptoms of his stomach. Please avoid generic biographical openings or questions about what drew him to Nietzsche. Structure the sequence so that it builds in intensity, ending with a question that forces him to say exactly what philosophical work remains for concepts like self-overcoming if bodily regimen is doing the heavy lifting.
