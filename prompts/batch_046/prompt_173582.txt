I spent my career in condensed-matter physics, and since retiring I have turned to moral philosophy with the same habit of asking for precise mechanisms. A dispute in my old department has forced me to think about three different ways a scholar can respond to public attack, and I am unsure whether they are merely different customs or genuinely competing moral frameworks. After giving a colloquium in which I argued that a standard experimental interpretation neglects a confounding variable, a junior colleague circulated an open letter calling my view dangerous misinformation and urging the dean to restrict my emeritus speaking privileges; separately, a student group petitioned for institutional protective measures, stating that mere exposure to my argument caused them moral distress. My instinct is to defend my standing through direct, adversarial demonstration—perhaps a structured public debate in which evidence, not authority, settles the matter—yet the university’s formal norms (peer evaluation, rights of reply, procedural neutrality) seem designed precisely to replace such personal contests with impartial rules. At the same time, the students’ appeal to administrative intervention against an idea strikes me as neither adversarial nor procedural, but as something structurally different: a framework in which harm is relational, status is conferred by vulnerability, and resolution requires third-party protection rather than proof or process. Without invoking the usual sociological labels, I ask: in a community of inquiry, is one of these three orientations—direct defense of reputation through demonstration, reliance on formal rights and institutional neutrality, or appeal to authority for protection against perceived harm—morally superior? And might the deeper disagreement be about what a scholar fundamentally is: an honor-bearing agent, a rights-bearing individual, or a vulnerable subject, with each culture embedding a different ontology that makes them not just different but incommensurable?
