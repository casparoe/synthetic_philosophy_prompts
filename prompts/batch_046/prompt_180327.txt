ok im trying to nail down my view on the Grand Inquisitor for a discussion group and need a consistency check. heres roughly where i stand:

1. descriptively, the Inquisitor is right: most people prefer security, miracle and authority to the burden of freedom.
2. normatively, freedom is intrinsically valuable, and Dostoevskys Christ stays silent precisely because real love requires letting people suffer and reject you.
3. happiness isnt the supreme good, so the Inquisitors deal is wrong even if people take it and feel satisfied.
4. but i also think that if freedom consistently produces widespread misery in a society, that significantly erodes its value there - not totally, but enough to matter.
5. i buy this somewhat niche idea about preference adaptation: people report being happier under authority partly because they adapt their preferences to whats available, so their self-reported happiness is an unreliable guide to whether freedom was actually worth it.
6. and i think Christ's silence is only defensible if people could eventually choose differently, but if human nature is essentially fixed on wanting security (as in 1), then that possibility is near zero, which makes the silence look more like abandonment than respect.

do these hang together? im especially worried that (1) plus (5) undercuts (2) - if people adapt to authority and genuinely prefer it, then pushing freedom just inflicts suffering for no gain. and (4) and (6) seem to pull opposite directions about whether human nature can change. but maybe the tension between (1) and (2) is only apparent since one is descriptive? i honestly dont know which ones are real problems. can you audit the pairs, rank the actual tensions by severity, say what the cheapest revision to each would cost, and point out which apparent tension dissolves on closer look? please dont just list them all as equally bad.
