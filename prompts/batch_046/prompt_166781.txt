I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about whether Dewey's experimentalism genuinely supports democracy as continuous social inquiry, or whether it collapses into an uncritical optimism about collective reasoning. We kept returning to the jury-room dynamics in the film 12 Angry Men. I should like you to read that film through Dewey's philosophy, specifically his claim that democratic life ought to be modelled on scientific inquiry — an open-ended reconstruction of shared experience through cooperative problem-solving, rather than the simple aggregation of fixed private opinions. Concentrate on whether the jurors' shift from near-unanimous conviction to reasonable doubt genuinely exemplifies Deweyan inquiry, or whether the dramatic turning points — Juror 8's private reconstruction of the switchblade evidence and his demonstration of the elderly witness's gait — actually depend on heroic individual insight that undermines Dewey's social conception of knowledge. Where does the film illuminate Dewey's idea that democracy requires transforming an indeterminate situation into a unified one through collective investigation? And where does it resist that reading — for instance, in the emotional manipulation visible in several jurors, the class and ethnic prejudices that initially structure their certainty, or in the sense that the final verdict seems to hinge on one man's persistence rather than on any genuinely shared method? I am preparing an essay that takes both the philosophy and the film seriously, refusing to reduce either to an illustration. Please respond in British English, and conclude by saying what the film contributes to the philosophical debate about democracy and inquiry that a purely conceptual argument on its own cannot capture.
