I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up the study of political philosophy and Roman constitutional history as a serious avocation. In conversation with a reading group, I have found myself returning repeatedly to a structural pattern in the fall of the Roman Republic that I cannot place within the standard vocabulary I have encountered, and I am writing to ask whether it corresponds to an established concept or whether it requires a new distinction.

The pattern is not merely that republican norms were violated, but that each successive expansion of personal authority was defended as an interpretation of those very norms rather than as an exception to them. Consider the progression: Sulla's dictatorship was presented as a temporary constitutional repair with no predetermined limit, yet its defenders treated the absence of a term not as an innovation but as a necessary clarification of what temporary meant in a prolonged crisis. Caesar's accumulation of honors—his formal appointment as dictator in perpetuity, the right to wear triumphal dress in the senate, the expansion of his patronage—was justified as the republic's traditional recognition of exceptional merit, not as the abolition of collegiality. The triumvirate was enacted through a popular law, so its architects could claim that the people themselves had reaffirmed, not overturned, republican procedure. In each case, the defenders did not say the old rules no longer apply; they said the old rules, properly understood, always permitted this. Once such an interpretation was accepted, it became part of the interpretive baseline, so that the next crisis was measured against an already-shifted framework. I observe an analogous rhetorical structure in modern democratic debates about emergency powers, where executive actions are defended not as new authorities but as revelations of powers that were implicitly present all along.

What interests me is not the general phenomenon of democratic decline or institutional mission creep, but this specific mechanism: the transformation of a regime through backward-looking interpretive claims that deny any departure has occurred. Is there an established term in constitutional theory or political philosophy for this precise move—the accumulation of exceptional authority through arguments that present each exception as a faithful reading of the original rules? If there is, I wish to understand how it is distinguished from ordinary adjustments to institutional practice or from the simple normalization of emergency measures, and whether its scope is limited to formal constitutional texts or extends to unwritten conventions and rhetorical practices. If no precise term exists, I would welcome a proposed coinage together with a clear definition, a couple of illustrative boundary cases, and an account of what the concept excludes. I ask that the response be composed in American English; I have no need for bibliographies, citations, or lists of further reading, only for a rigorous conceptual carving.
