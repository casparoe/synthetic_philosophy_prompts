Two colleagues in my philosophy-of-ethics seminar have been arguing about whether moral objections to algorithmic grief-counseling systems—interactive, learning-based simulations of deceased individuals offered for bereavement support—are genuine ethical concerns or merely disguised status-quo preferences. They asked me to report their exchange to you and adjudicate it point by point; I have no view of my own.

The exchange went as follows.

(1) The first colleague argued that the revulsion many people feel is structurally identical to the disgust initially directed at in-vitro fertilization: a novel technology triggers a visceral reaction, which is then rationalized as a moral claim about "unnaturalness" or "dignity." Once normalization occurs, the reaction fades, revealing that it was never a moral judgment but a preference for familiar practices. Therefore, objections based on "authentic grief" or "relational dignity" are cases of preference-laundering.

(2) The second colleague replied that not every attachment to an existing practice is a bias. Some preferences are integrity-preserving: they maintain the coherence of a practice (mourning) without which human relationships lose a specific form of meaning. The IVF analogy fails because IVF extends a biological capacity, whereas algorithmic grief-counseling replaces a relational process with a synthetic substitute; the objection is therefore not about novelty but about the substitution of an authentic relational endpoint.

(3) The first colleague countered that "integrity-preserving" is just a label for preferences the second colleague happens to share. Without an independent criterion for which practices have integrity worth preserving, the distinction collapses into "bias equals preference I reject, integrity equals preference I accept." He added that since the harm principle is not violated—no one is compelled to use the system—any remaining objection must be aesthetic or cultural rather than moral.

(4) The second colleague then introduced what she called the preference-recycling problem: during technological transitions, preferences formed under an older normative framework do not vanish; they are recycled as moral objections within the new framework. This makes them especially difficult to identify, because they present themselves as first-order moral intuitions rather than as inherited tastes. She argued that this does not prove they are false—only that identifying them requires phenomenological attention to whether an objection tracks a feature of the practice itself or merely the discomfort of transition. She claimed that her opponent's framework lacks the resources to make this distinction, because it treats all transitional discomfort as bias by definition.

(5) The first colleague responded that the preference-recycling idea is unfalsifiable: if an objection survives normalization it is called integrity-preserving, and if it fades it is called recycled preference. He asked for a principled, non-circular test that distinguishes recycled preferences from genuine moral concerns, and noted that without such a test the second colleague's position is as vulnerable to the preference-laundering charge she levels at others.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Identify where each party correctly sees something the other misses, and specify the weakest point in the second colleague's appeal to preference-recycling—the point she should prepare to defend rather than hope goes unnoticed. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
