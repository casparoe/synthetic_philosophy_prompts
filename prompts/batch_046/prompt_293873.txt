After a long and rather heated discussion with a colleague about Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, she laid out her judgments on a series of causal scenarios. She holds that when fire touches cotton and the cotton burns, the fire is not the true cause—God creates the burning directly, with the fire serving merely as an occasion (sabab). The same applies to medicine and recovery: the drug is an occasion, not an efficient cause. She extends this to inanimate nature generally—gravity, magnetism, and chemical reaction are all customary sequences (ʿādāt) rather than necessary connections. Yet when she considers human voluntary action—say, a person deciding to raise an arm—she insists that the human agent genuinely acquires (yaksibu) the act, so that the will functions as a real, though created, occasion in a way that fire does not. She denies that this makes the human a true efficient cause in the philosophical sense, but she grants voluntary agency a distinctive status. She also accepts that God could, in principle, break the customary order—water need not quench thirst—without any logical contradiction.

Now consider a new case we did not settle: a synthetic biological computer, designed with no conscious programmer present, evolves through autonomous genetic algorithms in a closed laboratory. Over months, it restructures its own circuitry to solve a problem that was never in its original programming, and it produces a stable chemical compound that has never existed in nature, all without any human intervention after the initial seeding. The causal chain runs entirely through the machine's self-modifying processes, with no observable external trigger at the moment of creation.

Predict my colleague's verdict: will she say the machine's internal processes are merely occasions for God's direct creation of the compound, or will she grant that the autonomous, self-modifying system possesses a form of acquisition analogous to human voluntary agency? Identify at least two interpretations of Al-Ghazālī's framework that fit all her earlier judgments—one that treats all non-human processes as pure occasions, and one that allows for a spectrum of acquisition or secondary causation in complex, self-directing systems. Say which interpretation you think she is running, justify your prediction, and name one further case—perhaps involving a non-biological artificial agent or an animal with learned behaviour—that would best distinguish the two interpretations.
