Hey, I'm dealing with a weird situation at my rationalist-adjacent coworking space. We have this shared kitchen budget, and one member—let's call her J—set up an automated system where every time anyone buys coffee, a tiny fraction gets silently diverted to a food-bank fund. She specifically configured it so the transaction happens in the background and she never sees the running total, because she said she doesn't want her giving to depend on whether she's feeling generous that week. Now another member is arguing that this actually undermines the moral value of what she's doing: if she's deliberately hiding the choice from her own conscious deliberation, then she's not really acting from the right reasons, she's just acting from programing. J pushed back that the "right reason" is still there—it's embedded in the architechture she chose—and that requiring constant conscious moral sentiment is just a bug in human psychology.

I'm torn. There's something here about whether moral worth requires that the reason be live in your mind at the moment of action, or whether it's enough that you've made the reason govern your behavior structurally, even if you've made yourself blind to it. What's the philosophical take? Is automating away your own awareness of a moral choice a form of integrity, or is it a kind of self-deception that strips the act of its value? I feel like I'm missing the framework that distinguishes "acting for the right reasons" from "designing a life where the right reasons can't reach you."
