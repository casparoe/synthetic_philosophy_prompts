She reasons as follows. (1) Keynes argued that once absolute scarcity is solved, rational agents will prefer leisure to further income, predicting a fifteen-hour work week as the natural endpoint of prosperity. (2) Yet the persistence of forty-hour norms and extreme cases like karoshi — partly driven by sābisu zangyō, the unpaid "service" overtime workers perform for group harmony rather than for direct pay — shows that people do not actually prefer leisure; they seek social recognition and belonging through labor itself. (3) Therefore Keynes was wrong about human psychology: culture and status competition, not material scarcity, are the primary engines of long hours. (4) But if culture drives labor, then legal caps such as France's thirty-five-hour week or Keynes's proposed fifteen-hour standard cannot free people for the good life; they merely push labor into unregulated, invisible forms that erase the transparency needed to prevent death by overwork. (5) So any legislated leisure mandate — including weekends, retirement ages, and vacation floors — is not merely ineffective but actively harmful, since hidden overtime is more dangerous and less compensable than visible overtime. (6) Hence we must reject all labor-reduction policies and accept that the Protestant work ethic is an immutable social feature rather than a historical ideology to be overcome by abundance. I want to resist step (4) or (5) — it feels like a modal slide from "some hidden overtime occurs" to "all caps produce net harm" — but I'm unsure whether the real damage lies in the empirical premise about sābisu zangyō or in the inference from cultural motivation to policy futility. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost for Keynes's broader vision of the good life?
