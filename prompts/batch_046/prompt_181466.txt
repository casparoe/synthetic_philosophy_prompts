I was discussing with my friends whether self-deception is even coherent, and I keep hitting the same wall: its not just the old paradox of believing p and not-p at once. I'm writing a short piece and want to focus on the upstream conceptual questions rather than the usual survey. Specifically, there are four tensions that feel like they control everything else: (1) whether you need literal doxastic partitioning — like a Freudian split — or if one cognitive system can just weight evidence differently based on desire without ever integrating it, a kind of "double bookkeeping" that never becomes explicit contradiction; (2) whether self-deception requires actively suppressing evidence you already possess, or if motivational filtering at the attention stage is enough, which would mean you never form the belief in the first place rather than hiding it; (3) whether there's a distinct "affective self-deception" where you don't misrepresent a fact but systematically misread your own emotional valence toward it, and if that avoids the doxastic paradox entirely; and (4) whether the whole debate depends on a hidden crux about the unity of agency — if personal identity is loose enough, self-deception might just be two subsystems arguing, but if agency is strictly unified, then the contradiction is fatal and the concept collapses.

If I had about two years of focused philosophical work — not a career, just a sustained project — which of these should I tackle first? I don't want a bibliography; I want a dependency-aware prioritization. Which question is upstream of the others? Which ones are actually tractable with conceptual analysis versus ones that need empirical psychology we'll never have? And is there a hidden resolution — maybe about intentional suppression — that would make the other questions moot? Rank them with reasoning, not just importance. I especially want to know what I'm paying if I reject the idea that suppression requires a unified agent doing the suppressing.
