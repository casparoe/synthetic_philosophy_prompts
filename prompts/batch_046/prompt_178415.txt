My mother died three weeks ago after completing a twelve-year clinical protocol called Graduated Cortical Substitution, in which her biological neurons were replaced region by region with synthetic analogues engineered to preserve synaptic weights, functional connectivity, and phenomenological continuity. By the end, neuroimaging confirmed that her prefrontal cortex, hippocampal formation, and primary sensory regions were almost entirely artificial substrate, yet she remained fully lucid and insisted—both in conversation and in a final written statement left with her physician—that she never experienced any discontinuity of self, only a gradual shift in the physical basis of her thoughts. I am delivering her eulogy this Saturday to an audience divided between family members who need genuine comfort that she endured rather than vanished, and a smaller group of neuroscientists and philosophers who will recognize the direct parallel to Dennett's brain-transplant puzzle, where gradual replacement distributes the self across original tissue, synthetic substrate, and remote backups, making the question of personal location genuinely unanswerable by simple geography.

I need a five-minute eulogy that treats this puzzle as a structural constraint rather than decoration: it must avoid the false consolation that she is observing us from some other realm, avoid reducing her to a disembodied pattern of information, and avoid alienating non-philosophers with technical jargon, while still honoring her conviction that gradual replacement was not equivalent to gradual erasure. The speech should help everyone present understand why she found Dennett's geographical dispersal of identity—brain in one place, functional copy elsewhere—more liberating than terrifying, and it should allow me to say goodbye without claiming certainty about exactly where she is now.
