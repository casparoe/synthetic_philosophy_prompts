I'm a doctoral student in philosophy working at the intersection of medieval Islamic metaphysics and contemporary philosophy of mind, and I've become convinced that al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is usually treated as a historical curiosity about divine omnipotence rather than as a serious structural contribution to debates about mental causation. In a recent seminar on non-reductive physicalism, we spent several sessions on Kim's causal exclusion argument—the worry that if every mental event has a complete sufficient physical cause, then mental properties are rendered causally redundant or overdetermined. What struck me was that occasionalism sidesteps this problem not by defending mental causation against physical competition, but by denying that physical events are causes in any robust metaphysical sense, thereby leaving mental events as direct occasions for divine action without any intermediate physical mediation that could compete for explanatory relevance.

I'd like you to make the argument that al-Ghazālī's occasionalist framework, when reconstructed with careful attention to his distinction between merely "habitual" sequences of events and genuinely "necessary" causal connections, actually offers a more internally coherent resolution to the exclusion problem than standard emergentist or non-reductive physicalist views do. Your argument should explain precisely how the occasionalist denial of secondary causation avoids the overdetermination worry that plagues dualist and emergentist accounts, and you should map this onto—rather than merely analogize—the modern distinction between causal production and causal relevance that structures the exclusion debate. You should also address the strongest objection available: namely, that occasionalism achieves coherence only by collapsing all causal efficacy into a single divine cause, thereby sacrificing not just physical but also mental explanatory autonomy in a different register. Please do not provide a bibliography or cite specific editions; I am interested in the conceptual architecture itself, particularly whether the occasionalist separation of habitual correlation from necessary connection can do genuine philosophical work in a contemporary setting without reducing mental life to a passive theater of divine impressions.
