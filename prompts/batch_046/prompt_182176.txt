Consider this reflexive extension of Wittgenstein's family-resemblance analysis, which seems to show that if taken consistently it dissolves every categorical boundary—including the ones his philosophy requires.

(1) For Wittgenstein, "game" has no essence; board games, card games, and ball games form a family linked only by crisscrossing similarities.
(2) So membership in any category C consists in bearing a sufficient cluster of family traits to paradigmatic members of C.
(3) But "sufficient membership in C" is itself a category relation, and by (1)-(2) applied reflexively it too lacks any essential conditions.
(4) Therefore, for any item x and category C, resemblance to paradigms is graded and boundary-less, so there is no determinate fact about whether x belongs to C.
(5) This applies to "language-game," "rule-following," and "concept" itself—the very distinctions Wittgenstein needs to separate meaningful practice from mere associative behavior and to ground the private-language argument.
(6) So family resemblance eliminates all categorical distinctions rather than explaining them, leaving no principled way to mark one practice as a language-game and another as not.
(7) Yet if we halt the regress by introducing an essentialist core—say, a necessary condition for "sufficient resemblance"—we abandon the anti-essentialist point of the analysis.
(8) The conclusion—that Wittgenstein's view undermines the conceptual distinctions it needs—is unacceptable.

Which step should be rejected? If you prefer to keep (1) and (5), explain exactly what you must pay: does the cost fall on the reflexive application in (3), on the move from graded resemblance to indeterminacy in (4), or on the assumption in (6) that Wittgenstein needs sharp boundaries rather than pragmatic ones? And if the flaw is in (3), does that mean family resemblance applies selectively—only to some concepts and not to others—and if so, by what criterion is the selection made?
