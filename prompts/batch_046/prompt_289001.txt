I'm preparing interview questions for a philosophy podcast episode, and my guest next week is a political philosopher who has recently advanced an argument that the left-right distinction remains genuinely real, though not for the reasons typically captured by party platforms or survey data. Rather than treating the divide as a bundle of policy preferences—economic redistribution versus market freedom, social liberalism versus traditionalism—she defends what she calls a "modal theory of normative inheritance." On her account, the right is characterized by treating institutional and moral traditions as carrying a defeasible but real presumptive weight: they stand as default solutions unless a specific, demonstrated failure is shown. The left, conversely, treats traditions as possessing no presumptive weight whatsoever; they are merely historical accumulations that must be continuously re-justified in present terms against current needs. Crucially, she maintains that what actually binds each coalition together is not this cognitive framework alone, but a shared "structural emotion" she terms custodial anxiety—the fear that the opposing side's mode of inheritance will permanently destroy something irreplaceable. For the right, the feared loss is accumulated practical wisdom that cannot be reconstructed once dissolved; for the left, it is adaptive capacity that is frozen out by unexamined tradition.

Please draft a sequence of interview questions that do not allow her to simply restate this framework, but instead press on its internal tensions and on standard objections from the literature. In particular, include at least one question addressing the claim—advanced by political theorists who view post-industrial politics as organized around openness versus closure rather than left versus right—that her modal distinction collapses into that openness-closure axis and therefore explains nothing distinctive about the older taxonomy. I also want a question that anticipates her likely reply to the charge that both sides actually share the same custodial anxiety, merely directed at different objects, followed by a sharp follow-up asking whether this shared emotional architecture undermines her claim that left and right are genuinely distinct positions rather than mirror-image variants of a single conservative impulse. Finally, include one question that applies her framework to a domain where left-right mapping seems least intuitive—debates over governance of artificial general intelligence or climate geoengineering—asking whether her modal distinction predicts disagreement there more accurately than standard ideological taxonomies, with a follow-up tailored to the answer she is most likely to offer, likely that both sides apply the same inheritance-mode regardless of technical content.
