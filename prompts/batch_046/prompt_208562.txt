You are a philosopher-poet with expertise in African philosophy and relational ethics, and you think carefully about form before drafting. Before writing the poem, please spend a few sentences brainstorming which concrete images or rhythms best suit the two refrains—one voicing relational constitution, one voicing the stranger’s exclusion—so that their repetition feels inevitable rather than mechanical.

I am a university student in my philosophy of personhood seminar, and for our final session we have been working through Ubuntu, specifically the claim that personhood is relationally constituted: that a person becomes a person through other persons within a shared communal web. Rather than stopping at the usual celebration of interdependence, our discussion moved into a tension that introductory accounts rarely stress—if personhood is genuinely constituted by communal relations, then those outside that web, moral strangers with no shared bond, seem to lack the very ground that grants them full standing. Some defenders respond by expanding the circle universally; critics worry this collapses into particularism without an independent anchor for rights. Our seminar ended with this contradiction open, and I want to close with a poem that holds it there.

Write a strict villanelle—nineteen lines, ABA rhyme scheme, two refrains that alternate, and a closing couplet containing both refrains together—taking this tension as its structural spine. One refrain should voice the relational self; the other should voice the stranger knocking from outside the circle, demanding recognition that the relational account has not yet granted. Let the form do the philosophical work: the refrains should pass each other through the stanzas like opposing arguments that never quite intersect, until the final couplet forces them into proximity without synthesis or resolution. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with debates in Ubuntu ethics—particularly the problem of extending communal obligations to those outside the relational network—recognizes the issue immediately, yet it should also stand as a strong poem for a reader with no background in African philosophy. Do not turn it into a sermon about inclusion, and do not resolve it with a shrug about universal human nature; leave the contradiction held open by the rhyme and repetition. Do not include citations, footnotes, bibliographic references, or attributions to specific scholars or texts. Begin with your brief brainstorming, then present the poem.
