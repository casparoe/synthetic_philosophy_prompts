After a long and unresolved argument with my closest friend, I need help seeing the philosophical structure in a practical decision we face together. We inherited a small family orchard three years ago and have since poured substantial capital into specialized equipment and soil restoration, yet it operates at a steady loss. My friend wants to sell the land to developers, arguing that money already spent can never be recovered and therefore should play no role in deciding what to do next; to continue would only deepen the mistake. I resist, not out of financial optimism, but because walking away feels like rendering our years of labor meaningless and breaking an implicit pledge to sustain what our grandparents began. I wonder whether there is a philosophically serious distinction between foolishly deepening a loss and a legitimate form of loyalty—perhaps tied to narrative continuity or to the kind of agents we constitute ourselves as being through sustained commitment—rather than mere sentimentality. Can you lay out whether honoring past expenditure can ever be rational for reasons other than expected return, carefully separating prudential calculation from considerations of identity, promise-keeping, or fidelity to an earlier self? And please: if my framing collapses because I am treating unrecoverable costs as direct reasons for action rather than as conditions that shape who I am, or if I am conflating these registers in some other confused way, say so plainly rather than accommodating the intuition politely.
