Consider a substance that possesses an intrinsic disposition to dissolve upon contact with water, together with a second intrinsic disposition—triggered only by that same water contact—to secrete an impermeable polymer that would prevent dissolution if fully formed. In the standard taxonomy, the first disposition might be classified as masked or finked by the second, depending on whether the interfering factor is treated as internal or external to the substance’s causal structure. Now introduce a further complication: the protective disposition is itself finkish. At the exact moment it begins to manifest—when polymer secretion initiates—the disposition to secrete is immediately lost, leaving the substance briefly exposed to dissolution before any protective layer can complete. Explain the conceptual problem this recursive, self-undermining arrangement generates. Is the original dissolution disposition ever genuinely manifested, or is it perpetually interrupted by a mechanism that cancels itself mid-operation? Does the presence of a mask that is simultaneously intrinsic and self-defeating blur the distinction between a real but blocked disposition and one that was never fully present? Furthermore, discuss how this interdependence affects a dispositional essentialist account: if the substance’s nature is supposed to fix its causal powers, but its powers include mutually undermining dispositions that are each lost upon activation, what does that imply for the metaphysical inventory of its properties? In particular, address whether such cases require us to reject the simple binary of manifest versus masked, and whether they motivate a graded or modal conception of dispositional presence.
