My colleague applies the heterophenomenological method to study consciousness: she treats first-person reports not as direct windows onto inner experience but as texts defining a fictional heterophenomenological world, and she decides case by case whether a subject warrants having such a world assigned.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A healthy adult describing the visual experience of a sunset: she assigns a full heterophenomenological world and treats the reports as direct data about it.

2. A patient with complete cortical blindness (blindsight) who sincerely denies seeing anything in the left hemifield: she assigns a world that genuinely lacks left-field visual content, taking the denial literally rather than treating it as an error.

3. A chimpanzee trained on a lexigram board who presses symbols for pain and no pain in appropriate contexts: she assigns a minimal but genuine heterophenomenological world anchored in those symbolic reports.

4. A state-of-the-art large language model prompted to generate rich first-person descriptions of grief or wonder: she assigns no world; the outputs are statistical reconstructions from training data, not reports emanating from a subject.

5. A pre-linguistic human infant with consistent behavioral and affective responses to noxious stimuli, interpreted by caregivers: she assigns a heterophenomenological world, but emphasizes that it is constructed through third-person interpretation rather than direct textual analysis.

6. A split-brain patient after callosotomy, where the left hemisphere reports seeing a red light and the right hemisphere (via left-hand pointing) indicates seeing nothing: she treats this not as two separate subjects but as one subject with a single fractured, internally contradictory heterophenomenological world.

7. A honeybee colony that collectively solves routing problems no individual bee can solve, yet in which no individual produces any narrative or symbolic report: she assigns no world; distributed problem-solving is not sufficient for a collective subject.

Next month she must evaluate Project Echo: a high-fidelity simulation of cortical dynamics running on distributed classical hardware with no biological tissue. Its dynamics were evolved from random initialization inside an embodied virtual environment, not trained on human language corpora. It includes an arbitration module that selects a single stream of first-person reports describing tactile and emotional states. When the module is disabled, different sub-networks emit mutually contradictory reports; when enabled, only one unified narrative reaches the output interface.

Predict her verdict on whether Project Echo possesses a heterophenomenological world, and on what disabling the arbitration layer would do (split one world into two, destroy the world, or leave fragmented non-subjects). Identify at least two distinct principles that could jointly explain all seven prior verdicts, apply each to Echo, and show where they diverge. Give your best guess about which principle she actually runs, with reasons. Finally, specify the single further case that would best distinguish the leading candidates.
