You sit on a municipal technology procurement review board that evaluates proposed urban infrastructure systems. The board has ninety minutes per proposal, no philosophers of technology on staff, and must sign off on a reusable evaluation protocol. Your task is to translate the thesis that material artifacts embed, enforce, and stabilize particular forms of social order, authority, and exclusion—often summarized as the claim that artifacts have politics—into a practical decision procedure your colleagues can run without reading philosophy.

Design a step-by-step checklist or flowchart in words, ordered so that earlier steps filter out clear cases and later steps handle ambiguity. For each step, specify: the exact question to ask of the proposal; what counts as sufficient evidence; whether the step yields a definitive stop-point, a flag for judgment, or a hand-off to values. Be explicit about which distinctions from the theoretical literature must survive in the procedure (for example, between explicit design intentions and emergent structural effects, or between flexible use and path-dependent lock-in) and which can be safely collapsed for usability. Mark every point where the board must make an irreducibly normative choice rather than a technical finding.

Then apply the procedure to two concrete proposals. The first is a federated-learning pedestrian-flow optimization system: it aggregates differentially private video feeds from privately owned storefront cameras, automatically reroutes traffic and emergency services based on real-time density, and assigns governance weights to property owners proportional to camera contribution. The second is a centralized smart-grid load-balancing platform: it requires participating households to accept remotely adjustable thermostats governed by opaque time-of-use pricing algorithms, with opt-out available only through an in-person physical board hearing.

For each application, show the answers the procedure produces step by step, including one outcome that conflicts with what a sympathetic reviewer might initially expect. Conclude by stating exactly what the procedure cannot do—what kinds of political embedding it will miss, and what false positives it risks—so the board knows its limits before adopting it.
