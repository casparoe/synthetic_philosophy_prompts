I am defending a thesis at an interdisciplinary workshop on moral responsibility: Frankfurt-style counterexamples fail to undermine the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP) because they conflate the elimination of an immediate, surface-level branching option at the moment of decision with the elimination of a deeper structural capacity. I call the retained but unactivated capacity “dormant pathway accessibility”—the agent’s psychological and neural architecture still contains the pathway that would have produced the alternative action, and this pathway remains accessible in nearby possible worlds even when a prior controller or mechanism ensures it is not exercised in the actual world. I argue that responsibility requires the presence of this structural modal accessibility, not merely an unblocked local fork, and I distinguish between a mechanism that preempts an alternative (which leaves the pathway dormant) and one that renders it psychologically or causally unavailable (true determinism). My audience is precisely composed of four analytic philosophers of action, two of whom are compatibilists with backgrounds in neuroscience and decision theory who think in terms of prefrontal inhibition, readiness potentials, and predictive processing; two libertarians who defend agent-causal or event-causal non-determinist accounts and care about irreducible causal powers; one Kant scholar for whom freedom requires transcendental spontaneity rather than mere empirical openness; and several graduate students working on moral luck and control conditions. They will not be persuaded by generic dialectics about Black and Jones; they will ask whether my distinction survives empirical neuroscience, whether “dormancy” is metaphysically substantive or merely verbal, and whether I have smuggled libertarian commitments into a supposedly neutral framework.
