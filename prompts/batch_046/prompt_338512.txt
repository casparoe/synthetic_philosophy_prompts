A philosophy blogger I read recently summarized the debate over incommensurable values as follows. Isaiah Berlin's value pluralism, the blogger claims, establishes that goods such as liberty and equality, or artistic integrity and loyalty, are not merely difficult to weigh against one another but genuinely incomparable, lacking any common metric whatsoever. Consequently, whenever such values conflict, rational choice is impossible: any decision must be arbitrary or purely emotional, since reason provides no guidance where comparison fails. Ruth Chang, according to this summary, corrected this mistake by arguing that hard choices are not rational failures but rational exercises, because agents can possess reasons that favor one option without that reason outweighing the competing one. The blogger draws a further conclusion: Chang's account shows that incommensurability is not a permanent feature of values but a temporary obstacle that rational commitment dissolves. Once an agent commits to one value, the conflict vanishes and the choice becomes straightforward. Therefore, Berlin's pluralism poses no real threat to rational agency; it merely describes situations where agents have not yet made the commitments that would resolve the conflict.

How fair is this summary? Please evaluate it claim by claim, distinguishing what accurately reflects Berlin's pluralism and Chang's view of hard choices from what conflates or misrepresents them. Specifically: does Berlin hold that incomparable values make choice arbitrary, or does he allow for rational though tragic selection? Does Chang treat commitment as dissolving incommensurability, or does she preserve the conflict even after commitment? And does the blogger's conclusion—that pluralism is unproblematic once we recognize the power of commitment—follow from either thinker's actual position?
