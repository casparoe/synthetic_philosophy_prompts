You work as an ethics-review auditor for a decentralized clinical-research cooperative that runs low-budget trials in remote regions. Every quarter you must adjudicate batches of first-person adverse-event reports—participants describing nausea, dizziness, mood shifts, or cognitive fog—where the report often conflicts with biometric logs or with each other. You have no formal philosophy training, roughly two hours per batch, and your sign-off determines whether a trial pauses or continues. Turn the philosophical analysis of introspective reliability into a reusable decision procedure you can actually run on a single report without consulting the literature.

The procedure must distinguish, at the first step, between reports about present phenomenal states (what it is like right now) and reports about causes, durations, comparative judgments, or dispositional attributions, on the ground that first-person access is not uniform across these categories. For each branch, specify the ordered filter questions, the minimal confirmation tests (behavioral correlates, contextual consistency, or cross-participant triangulation) that must be satisfied, the threshold at which the procedure mandates a regulatory pause regardless of biometrics, and the point at which it classifies the report as interpretive rather than phenomenal and downgrades its evidentiary weight. Include a small set of literal report templates or test sentences you can apply in under thirty seconds to classify an incoming report into the correct branch.

Then apply the procedure to three cases, including at least one where it yields an answer you will find unwelcome: (A) a participant reports severe anxiety with no physical symptoms, biometrics are flat, and the participant has a documented history of somatic misattribution; (B) a participant explicitly denies any discomfort, yet exhibits clear behavioral markers of pain that independent observers record; and (C) a report describing a novel cognitive state—say, a feeling of time fragmentation—that does not map onto any entry in the standard adverse-event taxonomy. For case C, state precisely what the procedure cannot determine and which kind of expert must be called in. Finally, list the two failure modes where a rational auditor should override the flowchart, and state explicitly what the procedure is not designed to do—for example, establish metaphysical ground-truth about consciousness, replace informed-consent obligations, or resolve disputes about the ontology of mental states.
