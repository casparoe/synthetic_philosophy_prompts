As a journalist who covers historiography and the philosophy of science, I have become skeptical of the standard remedy for Eurocentrism in global history: the call to tell the past "from everywhere at once" by multiplying voices and decentering Europe spatially. I want you to argue for the stronger, more disruptive claim that this remedy fails because it leaves untouched the deeper problem—unified chronology. Make the case that genuinely non-Eurocentric narration requires not merely adding regional perspectives, but abandoning synchronized timelines altogether and replacing them with thematic "fields" (for instance, hydrological regimes, scriptural diffusion, or epidemiological corridors) that do not map neatly onto dynastic or centennial frames calibrated in European archives. Defend the view that causal coherence in world history is itself an artifact of that chronological scaffolding, and that accepting temporal incommensurability—where one region's crisis or transformation has no mandatory temporal counterpart elsewhere—is intellectually more honest than stitching disparate rhythms into a single narrative. Be concrete: show what hidden normative choices are forced by retaining chronology, and explain precisely what is gained, and what is lost, by narrating the past through incommensurable thematic fields rather than through a shared timeline.
