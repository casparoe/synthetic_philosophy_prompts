I have recently been drawn into a painful family discussion about whether a close relative ought to be held morally responsible for harmful actions committed during a prolonged depressive episode. The exchange kept circling back to the slogan that moral responsibility requires that one could have done otherwise—treating the principle of alternative possibilities as an obvious barrier to blame. I want to understand precisely what claims are packed into that line, because in this personal context it is being wielded as if it were a single, self-evident truth. Please lay out, as a menu of distinct and precisely formulated theses, the different things that slogan could be asserting: distinguish, for instance, whether the relevant 'can' denotes physical ability, psychological accessibility, or rational capacity; whether the requirement applies specifically to blame and desert, or extends equally to praise; whether 'doing otherwise' demands only a local counterfactual about the immediate action, or a global counterfactual about the agent's entire formative history; and whether the slogan is meant to invoke a hierarchical mesh condition—where responsibility depends on the alignment of first-order and second-order volitions—rather than a bare counterfactual about behaviour. For each reading, indicate whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive claim that might survive Frankfurt-style counterexamples, or a false overgeneralisation, and specify which readings would actually support excusing my relative. Please write in British English, state how confident you are in the distinctions you draw, and explicitly identify the point about which you are least certain.
