I serve on the acquisition committee of a mid-sized city's public-art foundation, and each winter we have roughly three weeks to review about eighty proposals for permanent outdoor installations. Our charter requires that funding decisions rest on aesthetic merit rather than popularity, political resonance, or tourism potential, yet our board includes city council members with no art background who must sign off on every grant, and we have only about twenty minutes per proposal before a preliminary vote. I recently revisited Hume's essay on taste and judgment—particularly the idea that taste is not purely subjective but depends on refined faculties, freedom from prejudice, practice in comparison, and a delicacy of imagination that can be cultivated—while debating with a colleague whether a work produced mainly by a generative algorithm could ever satisfy those conditions. I need a reusable decision procedure rather than a philosophical essay: a checklist or ordered sequence of tests that translates Hume's conception of the qualified aesthetic judge into something a time-pressed committee can apply consistently, without requiring members to have read the source text.

The procedure must be operational enough for a practitioner without philosophical training, fixing the role (committee reviewer), the frequency (annual round of eighty proposals), the stakes (public funds and permanent civic space), and the sign-off constraints (board members with no art background must understand the rationale in plain language). It must handle hard cases that have become routine: proposals where a human artist acts mainly as a curator of algorithmic outputs; works by collectives including members with atypical sensory processing—such as synesthetes or people with extreme color-grapheme associations—who claim the intended effect depends on perceptual pathways most viewers lack; and installations designed exclusively for augmented-reality viewing, meaning the sensory conditions of the work are technologically mediated and invisible to the unaided eye. For each step, specify what counts as passing or failing, flag explicitly where judgment is unavoidable and merely disguised as procedure, and apply the checklist to three hypothetical proposals: a politically popular but technically conventional mural; an obscure algorithmic piece that scores well on refinement but makes the board uncomfortable; and an AR-only installation by a neurodivergent collective. Include the case where the procedure recommends rejecting the popular mural despite public pressure, and be honest about what the procedure cannot settle—especially disagreements between equally qualified judges—without providing a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations.
