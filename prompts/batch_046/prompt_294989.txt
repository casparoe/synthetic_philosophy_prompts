Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, with two refrains labeled A1 and A2 that alternate and finally sit together in the last two lines—staging Aristotle’s distinction between oikonomikē and chrematistikē. Let A1 carry the claim that natural exchange is bounded by use: goods exist for a purpose (the shoe for the foot, the house for dwelling), and trading serves only to restore self-sufficiency across households. Let A2 carry the claim that coin breeds coin (tokos): money, being conventional (nomisma, nomos), can be detached from every use-value and made to generate itself without internal limit (apeiron), which Aristotle calls the most hated form of wealth-getting because it treats a means as if it were an end that reproduces. Make the form do the philosophical work: through the first four tercets, A1 and A2 should circle each other without resolving, since Aristotle treats these activities not as extremes on a continuum but as categorically different—one serving the good life, the other parasitic and limitless. Only in the final quatrain should the refrains converge side by side, revealing that they share the same root (the conventional token) yet remain unreconciled, with A2 now readable as a kind of self-referential loop detached from the ergon of things. Render the view accurately: Aristotle does not reject all markets, but he judges exchange by whether it serves household need; retail trade for profit and especially interest violate nature because they make money grow “as if from seed.” Do not turn this into a modern sermon about capitalism; keep the tension specifically Aristotelian. After the poem, include a brief note stating how confident you are in the interpretation embodied in the verse and singling out the point you are least sure about—especially whether the formal convergence of A1 and A2 in the closing lines fairly represents Aristotle’s hierarchical judgment that chrematistikē is subordinate and parasitic on oikonomia, or whether it falsely imposes a dialectical symmetry on a view that actually denies any common measure between the two.
