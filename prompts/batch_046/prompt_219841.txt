hey, i'm in this philosphy of medicine seminar and we're talking about advance directives and whether the "earlier you" actually gets to boss around the "later you." i'm trying to map my gut feelings onto cases, and there's kind of a pattern but also a mess.

case 1: 25 year old writes an advance directive saying if she ever gets severe dementia and doesnt recognize her kids, dont treat pneumonia, just let her go. fast forward to 80, she has alzheimers, doesnt recognize anyone, but she smiles at music and eats pudding happily. pneumonia hits. honoring the directive and not treating feels... okay? like she made a clear call about not living that kind of life.

case 2: same directive, same 80 year old, but now shes terrified, screaming, clearly in existant suffering, and begging the nurses to recieve medicine. honoring the directive and withholding treatment feels definately wrong. like we're letting a piece of paper override actual agony.

case 3: healthy 40 year old writes "if im ever permanently unconscious with no recovery chance, pull the plug." at 60 hes unconscious after a crash, docs say zero chance, and his family remembers he was always terrified of being kept alive artificially. pulling the plug feels right.

case 4: at 35, right after a bad breakup and a depressive episode, he writes a directive saying if he ever becomes severely disabled (like quadriplegic), dont take heroic measures. then at 50 he actually becomes quadriplegic from an accident, adapts over years, writes a book, says his life is genuinely good. now he gets pneumonia and clearly wants antibiotics. following the old directive feels wrong, like we're punishing him for having recovered from that temporary state.

case 5 — the one i keep going back and forth on: she writes a directive refusing a feeding tube if shes ever locked-in. she updates it every single year for 15 years, consistant, never wavers. then at 65 she actually gets locked-in from a stroke, fully conscious, communicates by blinking that she wants the tube to stay alive. part of me says follow the 15-year paper trail because its so deliberate, part says shes right there asking for help. i honestly cant land it.

my tentative idea is that the earlier self has authority only when the later change is "vertical" — a value shift caused by pathology or temporary emotional disturbance — but not when its "horizontal," like adapting to a new circumstance with the same core values. case 2 and case 4 seem vertical (terror/depression), case 3 is just applying a stable preference to a new situation. but case 1 breaks it: is being happy in dementia horizontal or vertical? and case 5 feels like maybe horizontal adaptation, except her values never changed, she just wants to live now that its real.

so is there a cleaner principle, or a small set, that captures most of these? state it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my gut reactions you have to throw out. also predict what your principle says for a case i havent listed: someone writes a directive during a brief psychedelic experience saying "never prolong my life artificially," then spends the next 20 years as a rationalist philosopher consistently reaffirming it, but then gets into a state where they cant communicate and family is split. does the paper win? and please dont just list philosophers — i want the reasoning that connects the cases.
