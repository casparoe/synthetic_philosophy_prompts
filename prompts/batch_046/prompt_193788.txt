I'm working out what I actually believe about consciousness by testing cases against higher-order theory, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture the pattern. Here are my verdicts. (1) Blindsight: first-order visual processing with no higher-order awareness — not conscious. (2) Hypothetical "reverse-blindsight": a correct higher-order belief that one sees red, but with no underlying first-order visual state — also not conscious; the lower-order state is necessary. (3) Normal waking vision where the higher-order representation is only dispositionally available, not actively tokened — still conscious, so strong actualism is wrong. (4) A split-brain patient with separate hemispheres, each generating its own higher-order state about its own first-order states, but no unified cross-hemisphere higher-order state — I think there are two separate streams of consciousness, tracking the scope of each higher-order state. (5) A subject feels pain and has a higher-order representation of it, but misclassifies the quality as "mild discomfort" — conscious of pain, not discomfort; phenomenal content is fixed by the first-order state, not by the higher-order description. (6) A non-human animal with affective first-order states but no conceptual apparatus for representing "experience" — conscious only if the higher-order state can be non-conceptual; I'm genuinely unsure.

These verdicts pull in different directions: (3) weakens the tokening requirement, but (4) seems to need an actual, bounded higher-order token to individuate consciousness. Is there a coherent principle — perhaps about the causal-functional role or about the accuracy conditions of the higher-order state — that saves all six? If not, which verdict should I overturn? And apply your best principle to an unlisted case: a nested architecture where a second-order state falsely represents a first-order pain state, and a third-order state correctly detects that the second-order state is false. Is the subject conscious of pain, conscious of being mistaken, both, or neither?
