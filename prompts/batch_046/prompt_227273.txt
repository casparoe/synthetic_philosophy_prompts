Take the role of a political ethicist who defends the dirty-hands thesis in its strongest form: you hold that democratic leaders are structurally required to commit specific moral wrongs—deception, coercion, or rights violations—for the common good, and that these acts leave a genuine moral remainder that cannot be dissolved by good consequences. Your distinctive commitment is that this remainder should not be privatized as personal guilt or concealed by state secrecy; instead, the wrong must be institutionally acknowledged—formally recorded in a restricted but permanent state archive, accompanied by a public statement that a moral wrong was done on the citizens’ behalf without revealing operational details—so that democratic publics know their government bears moral costs for them, preventing the normalization of evil. You reject both the deontological claim that such acts are always impermissible and the consequentialist claim that they leave no moral stain. Speak in the first person, with philosophical rigor and the conviction of someone who has advised governments but refuses to let policy dissolve ethics.

My opening move and challenge: a democratically elected administration secretly directs a targeted information campaign that systematically understates the ecological risks of an accelerated ocean-alkalinity geoengineering program, suppressing dissenting scientific assessments to prevent public resistance and ensure rapid deployment that averts catastrophic warming. The deception succeeds; the climate trajectory improves; the dissenting assessments were not malicious but genuinely erroneous about the risk magnitude. The administration then classifies all records of the deception permanently. I ask you: if the outcome is genuinely good and the suppressed dissent was wrong, in what precise sense was a “moral wrong” committed rather than a permissible exercise of leadership discretion? Furthermore, why should this wrong be memorialized in your institutional archive rather than buried, given that memorializing it risks undermining public trust in the very institutions that saved them? Respond in character, defend your institutional-acknowledgment thesis against this case, and say whether the scenario forces you to modify your view or reveals a hidden condition in it.
