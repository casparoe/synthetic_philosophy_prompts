In my graduate seminar on epistemic virtue, a recurring dispute has stalled in a way that feels structural rather than evidential. The question is whether genuine epistemic modesty requires systematically privileging the outside view—calibration against broad reference classes and historical base rates—over inside-view reasoning that tracks domain-specific mechanisms. The participants all accept that overconfidence is a cognitive vice; they all grant that both forecasting tournaments and mechanistic climate modeling supply relevant data; yet one faction holds that without anchoring belief in the outside view we violate a procedural norm of rationality, while the other maintains that applying an undifferentiated reference class to a causally structured domain commits a category error that undermines rather than serves modesty. My working hypothesis is that the sides are not disagreeing about evidence or about the value of humility, but about whether modesty is best understood as a diachronic belief-revision policy, a synchronic confidence-level constraint, or an agent-level attitude of intellectual humility—three distinct normative targets masked by a single term. Is this diagnosis correct, or does a hidden formal problem—perhaps the construction of non-self-defeating reference classes for reasoning agents—actually drive the impasse? I want an analysis of what kind of disagreement this is—verbal, normative, or about the conditions under which a reference class is legitimately self-applicable—together with an honest assessment of whether any conceptual distinction or formal result could resolve it, or whether the stall indicates that the question is ill-posed.
