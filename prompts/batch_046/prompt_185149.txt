"In Theravada Abhidhamma, kamma-citta is consciousness with wholesome or unwholesome roots (hetu), and it alone produces vipaka. Kiriya-citta is functional consciousness—present in arahants—so it doesn't generate new kammic fruit. This shows that the moral quality of an act depends on the mental state at the moment of volition, not on later results. So the pure-intention reading is correct for Theravada.

The Jataka tale where the Bodhisattva feeds his own body to a starving tigress shows this too. His act is kamma-citta because he still has defilements to purify, and it produces enormous merit. But he doesn't do it for the merit; he does it out of compassion. The fact that the result is good just confirms that his intention was pure, not that the result made it good.

That said, Mahayana upaya complicates things. In the Upayakausalya Sutra—which is part of the Lotus Sutra tradition—a teacher lies to save beings, showing that expected outcomes can override ordinary rules. So if we combine Theravada's kamma/kiriya framework with Mahayana skillful means, we get a hybrid view: because kiriya-citta produces no vipaka, any system that tracks results must be operating through kamma-citta. This means expected consequences don't just accompany intention; they determine whether the roots are actually wholesome. Therefore intention fixes the kammic weight, but expected consequences decide whether an act counts as kusala or akusala. So Buddhist ethics isn't purely consequentialist, but it isn't pure intention-ethics either—it's a two-layer system."
