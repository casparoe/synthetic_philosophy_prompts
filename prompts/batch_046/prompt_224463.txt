I sit on the methodology review panel for an interdisciplinary foundation that funds research at the boundary of cognitive science and the study of built religious environments—specifically projects that try to explain why visitors to preserved historic sanctuaries report sustained experiences they describe as liturgical awe. Last week, after a long argument with a co-reviewer about whether a neuroscience proposal had genuinely explained the phenomenon or had simply renamed it in brain-language, I realized we lack any shared procedure for making this call; every quarter I evaluate about twelve proposals, I have roughly ninety minutes per file, I must co-sign with a humanities scholar who has no technical background, and I need to apply Daniel Dennett's distinction between cranes (legitimate, step-by-step explanatory constructions that preserve what they explain) and skyhooks (explanatory cheats that invoke lower-level mechanisms without a demonstrated path upward), together with his specific warning about greedy reductionism—where an explanation is so eager to reduce that it eliminates the very phenomenon it claimed to account for.

Write me a reusable decision procedure—structured as an ordered sequence of no more than seven gates or tests—that I can run on the central explanatory claim of any proposal. At each gate, provide a brief diagnostic sentence or two (test cases) I can apply without specialist equipment. The procedure must include an explicit flag for the stage at which judgment is irreducibly qualitative and I must make a call rather than apply a rule. Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (1) a proposal that correlates fMRI sublimity signatures with visitor reports but never addresses the semantic or ritual structure of the space; (2) a proposal that builds mechanistically from documented acoustic properties and communal bodily posture up to the sustained experience, preserving its intentional content; and (3) an unwelcome case in which the mechanistic architecture is technically impeccable—every gate passes—yet the conclusion treats the normative dimension of awe, its being oriented toward something the visitor takes to genuinely matter, as an evolutionary misfire to be explained away, so that I must reject the proposal despite its methodological elegance and sign a negative assessment that will anger the principal investigator. For each case, indicate the exact gate where evaluation stops and the reasoning.

Finally, include a concise limits-of-procedure note specifying which kinds of submissions fall outside its scope—such as purely phenomenological accounts that make no mechanistic claims, or purely formal computational models lacking empirical anchoring—so that I do not misapply the checklist. The deliverable should be something I can print and keep beside the evaluation form, readable in under five minutes, with the hard cases and points of unavoidable judgment marked clearly.
