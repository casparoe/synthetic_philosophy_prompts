My friend and I just spent a long evening arguing over John Cage's 4'33"—specifically whether Schopenhauer's philosophy of music can tell us anything meaningful about the 1952 Woodstock premiere, where David Tudor sat at the piano, opened and closed the keyboard lid through three timed movements totaling four minutes and thirty-three seconds without playing a notated pitch, and parts of the audience walked out in anger. She thinks Schopenhauer's framework resolves the dispute; I think it might collapse under the case. I want a disciplined analysis that runs both directions.

Schopenhauer holds that music is not, like painting or poetry, a representation of Platonic Ideas, but a direct copy of the Will itself. Within this ontology he assigns a precise hierarchy: the bass line corresponds to the inorganic world and the lowest grades of objectification; harmony expresses the intermediate grades—plant, animal, and human—as structured Ideas; rhythm gives the temporal form of the Will's universal movement; and melody alone, especially in the human voice, expresses the Will's own endless, unsatisfied striving and its occasional moments of resolution. Given this structure, apply it rigorously to Cage's piece and its premiere.

First, map the episode: identify what Schopenhauer's categories classify as present—the performer's bodily movements, the ambient sounds of the room, the audience's own noises, the silence itself—and what they classify as absent, namely composed melody, composed harmony, and rhythm intended as musical structure. Second, say what the theory predicts about the audience's experience: if music bypasses conceptual representation and strikes the Will directly, does the absence of melody and harmony mean the Will is suspended, exposed in raw form, or expressed only through accidental sounds? Third, evaluate whether the audience's hostile reaction confirms Schopenhauer's claim that music acts immediately on the Will—perhaps they were disturbed by an unmediated encounter with it—or whether it reveals that the theory requires some minimal organizational structure to function at all, making 4'33" not music but its negation. Fourth, say where Schopenhauer's categories are silent: for instance, does his distinction between composed sound and ambient noise allow him to distinguish Cage's intentional framing from ordinary background noise, and can his hierarchy account for a performance that deliberately removes melody, harmony, and rhythm while retaining only the temporal container? Finally, conclude whether the episode is a confirmation of the theory, an anomaly it never anticipated, or a case that forces us to revise Schopenhauer's claim that melody is the essential bearer of the Will's expression. Be honest about where you are forcing the theory to speak and where it genuinely offers illumination, and do not treat the audience's reaction as mere philistinism without testing it against the framework.
