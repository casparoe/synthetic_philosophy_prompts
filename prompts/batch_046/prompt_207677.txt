Moore's paradox is usually shown with sentences like 'It's raining but I don't believe it.' Philosophers sometimes talk about a 'transparency condition' on belief that explains why this sounds absurd. Is this structurally paralel to Moore's paradox? In answering, separate (i) the first-person transparency condition, (ii) the sincerity conditions on assertion, and (iii) whether collective or institutional 'belief' can generate the same contradiction as individual belief.

student answer:
Moore's paradox works because asserting p commits you to believing p, so saying you don't believe p contradicts that commitment. The transparency condition means if you believe something you can immediately report it, and if you don't you can report that too. In the crustacean case, the consortium asserts they have documented aversive responses, which is like asserting p, but then they deny holding belief about sentience, which is like denying belief in q where q is the explanation for p. So it's not exactly the same form since it's p-but-no-belief-about-q rather than p-but-I-don't-believe-p. Still, it violates transparency because having documented evidence should make them transparent to themselves about their beliefs. They also violate Grice's sincerity maxim because you shouldn't assert what you don't believe, and they're asserting evidence while disclaiming the underlying belief. Collective belief works just like individual belief but scaled up through consensus aggregation, so the paradox applies to groups too. Overall it's a structural analouge, though the collective aspect and the p/q distinction make it a weaker case than the original.
