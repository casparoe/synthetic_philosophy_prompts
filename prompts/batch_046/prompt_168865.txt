I'm in the final weeks of a graduate seminar on personal identity and philosophy of mind, and we've spent several sessions on the gradual-replacement thought experiment—replacing biological neurons one at a time with functionally identical silicon analogues until the brain is entirely artificial—usually framed through the Ship of Theseus. Our discussion stalled on a more specific tension: if psychological continuity and first-person narrative survive intact, functionalism seems vindicated and uploading preserves identity; but if embodied biological continuity carries metaphysical weight, then the original subject has been incrementally replaced by a perfect functional duplicate that merely inherits its memories and pronouns. For our closing session, I'd like a poem that enacts this problem structurally rather than arguing for one side.

Please write a pantoum—stanzas of four lines in which the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, with the poem's final stanza bringing back the opening lines in transformed positions—on the gradual replacement of a conscious subject. Let the poem's repeating lines carry the persistent functional or psychological elements: the first-person pronoun, specific memory fragments, the narrative voice. Let the new lines introduced in each successive stanza represent the foreign substrate—silicon, code, synthetic architecture—gradually displacing biological tissue. By the end, the reader should encounter the original opening phrases altered by everything that has replaced them, so that it remains genuinely ambiguous whether the speaking I has survived as the same subject or has been hollowed out and inhabited by a duplicate.

The poem should be accurate to the uploading debate in a way a philosopher of mind would recognize: address the ambiguity of phenomenal versus access continuity, the problem of whether first-person reference can survive total substrate replacement, and the distinction between functional persistence and biological essentialism about the self. Do not resolve the tension—leave open whether identity is preserved or lost. Keep the language precise and the tone serious; it will be read aloud at the end of our seminar.
