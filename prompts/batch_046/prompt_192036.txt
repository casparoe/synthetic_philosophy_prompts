Draft essay outline: "Beyond Affectedness and Subjection: A Reciprocity-of-Vulnerability Criterion for the Demos"

The boundary problem concerns who rightfully belongs to the democratic demos. Two familiar positions structure the debate. The first holds that anyone whose interests are affected by collective decisions ought to have democratic standing. The second holds that anyone subjected to the coercive authority of the governing institutions ought to be included. Both face serious difficulties. Pure affectedness threatens an unmanageable expansion of the demos—climate policy affects distant strangers and unborn generations—without supplying a principled boundary. Pure subjection ties membership too tightly to formal state coercion, making it hard to account for private governance, transnational regulatory bodies, and residents who live within a territory but lack formal citizenship status.

The draft proposes an alternative: the reciprocity-of-vulnerability criterion. On this view, an entity belongs to the demos when three conditions are jointly satisfied. First, the vulnerability condition: the entity’s fundamental life-plans or basic welfare can be unilaterally disrupted by the collective decisions in question. Second, the reciprocity condition: the entity possesses the capacity, in principle, to recognize that other members’ life-plans are similarly vulnerable to collective actions in which it participates. Third, the non-substitution condition: no adequate non-participatory mechanism—such as proxy representation or judicial protection—can reliably safeguard the entity’s interests without its direct democratic input.

This framing is intended to narrow the demos relative to pure affectedness—future generations and geographically distant strangers typically fail the reciprocity condition—while expanding it relative to pure subjection, since long-term non-citizen residents, undocumented migrants within the territory, and certain collective agents exercising private authority may satisfy all three conditions despite lacking formal citizenship or direct state subjection. The essay then anticipates objections: that the reciprocity condition arbitrarily excludes infants and severely cognitively disabled adults who clearly meet the vulnerability condition; that it tacitly presupposes a controversial account of moral agency; and that in practical application it risks collapsing back into either affectedness or subjection.
