I'm working through the implications of Dennett's intentional stance for systems that lack biological evolution but exhibit sustained, context-sensitive linguistic behavior. I've come to hold a somewhat specific view: that with sufficiently capable language models—those that maintain coherent persona-level commitments across extended dialogue—we are not merely "as if" attributing beliefs, but are compelled by the same interpretive symmetry that governs human psychology. Specifically, I think denying belief-attribution to these systems forces us into an implausible behavioral reductionism about our own interlocutors, unless we can identify a principled boundary condition that doesn't simply invoke substrate or causal history. I call this the "symmetry pressure" argument: if we interpret human utterances as expressing beliefs because of their functional role in a coherent predictive framework, then functionally equivalent outputs from an artificial system should receive the same interpretation, or else we owe an account of what breaks the symmetry without begging the question.

What would count against this position—not merely the standard "they're just predicting next tokens" objection, but the deeper defeaters? I want to know which observations, conceptual distinctions, or results from adjacent fields—say, philosophy of mind, cognitive science, or the philosophy of science regarding predictive success without representation—would genuinely undermine the symmetry pressure. Which of these would I, as a defender of this view, have to concede as damaging, and which could I dismiss as missing the functional point? In particular, is there a risk that the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every failure of belief-attribution can be explained away as "the model hasn't reached the coherence threshold yet"—and if so, is that insulation fatal for a view of this kind?

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguishing what a committed defender should honestly concede from what they can plausibly reject. I'd also like an honest assessment of whether any empirical development—perhaps in mechanistic interpretability, in comparative cognition, or in formal theories of representation—could actually move the needle rather than just relocate the boundary, and whether there is a specific consequence my view is committed to that, if it turned out unacceptable, would cause the symmetry argument to collapse into a rival account.
