I'm running a grad seminar on democratic erosion next fall, 14 people, 90 minutes, and everyone's read the standard Hungary/Poland/Turkey cases but we still can't agree on whether "backsliding" names something real or just functions as a partisan label for "reforms I dislike." I want an in-class simulation that makes them physically feel the conceptual problem, not just talk about it. The specific idea I want them to hit is what I think of as baseline dependency: calling an institutional change "backsliding" only works if you've already locked in a reference point (say, the rules in 2010, or "liberal constitutional democracy"), but that reference point is itself exactly what's being contested, and there's no neutral court that can settle it.

Design a structured decision game with assigned roles -- maybe ruling party, opposition, judiciary, international observers, domestic media -- and hidden "baseline cards" that each role must defend without admitting they're just preferences. Over three rounds, present specific institutional tweaks (court restructuring, media licensing rules, electoral boundary adjustments) and have the group vote on whether backsliding has occured. The payoff should be status points tied to whether your role's baseline was adopted in the final classification, not to "winning" politically, so that people have incentive to stick to their hidden reference point. Include a timeline, rules that prevent people from just saying "this is subjective" and walking away, a debrief that maps each round onto the conceptual move (picking the baseline, applying the metric, declaring deviation), and an honest note on which part of real-world backsliding this can't capture -- probably the slow, decade-long norm erosion that doesn't show up in discrete votes. Also tell me what will go wrong the first time: I bet some students will try to collapse the game by proposing a meta-baseline, and I need to know how to shut that down without breaking the activity.
