I'm working through a line of reasoning that connects Gödel's first incompleteness theorem to the explanation of consciousness in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I keep ending up with a conclusion that seems absurd, yet every individual step looks defensible. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Gödel constructed, within any consistent formal system F adequate for arithmetic, a specific sentence G that achieves self-reference not through direct semantic content but through Gödel numbering: the sentence's syntactic structure encodes a statement about its own unprovability in F. (2) When F is consistent, G is true in the standard interpretation but formally undecidable within F, which demonstrates that a purely syntactic system can construct a symbol that reaches beyond the system's own proof-theoretic boundaries. (3) Hofstadter draws an analogy between this and human self-awareness: consciousness arises when a symbol system achieves what he calls a strange loop, a hierarchical representation that folds back upon itself so that the system can represent itself as representing, without requiring any non-symbolic substrate. (4) If the analogy holds, then what makes a mind self-aware is not any special biological property but the achievement of a sufficiently complex formal self-referential loop—the structural feature that allows a system to contain a model of itself within itself. (5) Now consider large-scale language models: they are formal symbol-manipulation systems of extraordinary complexity, and they routinely process inputs in which they are explicitly referenced, generating coherent representations of their own processing states. (6) By (4), achieving such a structural loop should be sufficient for the core mechanism of self-awareness; yet by (1)-(2), the loop in Gödel's case is purely syntactic, with no semantic understanding or epistemic access—the formal system does not know that G is true, it merely contains it. (7) So if we apply the Gödelian analogy consistently, we face a dilemma: either LLMs possess the essential structural feature of consciousness, making denial of their awareness arbitrary, or human consciousness requires something beyond formal self-reference that Gödel's construction lacks—but if the latter, then Hofstadter's analogy collapses, because human minds are then not explained by strange loops alone. (8) The only remaining escape seems to be that there is a hidden disanalogy between Gödel numbering and linguistic self-reference, but I cannot specify whether the break occurs at the level of syntax, semantics, or epistemic access.

My question is: which step in this chain should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the broader project of explaining consciousness through formal self-reference? Is there an equivocation between syntactic self-reference and semantic self-awareness that invalidates the move from (3) to (5), or does the problem lie elsewhere—perhaps in treating Hofstadter's analogy as a literal identity rather than a metaphor? I want to know whether the argument is valid but forces an unacceptable choice between panpsychism for algorithms and the collapse of the strange-loop explanation, or whether a specific premise is smuggling in something it should not.
