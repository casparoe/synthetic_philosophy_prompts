I was discussing a philosophical puzzle with two fellow engineers—neither of us has formal training in ethics or metaphysics—after we read a brief explanation of indirect normativity and idealized deliberation. One colleague, Mara, proposed what she called a "professional-amnesia filter" for testing whether a norm is genuinely action-guiding: you first imagine yourself with perfect logical consistency and full information, but then you strip away all knowledge of your specific social and professional roles. If you still endorse the principle once you no longer know that you are, say, a software engineer with particular obligations to users and maintainers, then the norm survives as indirectly normative for you; if it collapses without that contextual knowledge, it was never more than a contingent bias dressed up as a reason. Her interlocutor, James, argued that this second filter destroys the very point of idealization. He said that refining your commitments should mean clarifying and strengthening the reasons embedded in your actual situation, not erasing the domain-specific knowledge that gives those reasons their weight—otherwise you end up with a norm that applies to a generic rational agent but fails to guide anyone in a concrete runtime environment, much like a design specification that ignores the hardware it actually runs on. Mara replied that allowing professional identity to remain in the deliberative frame smuggles in arbitrary social construction and calls it rational justification; James countered that removing it produces an empty formalism, leaving you with prescriptions that are technically consistent but motivationally inert. They turned to me and asked me to referee the exchange move by move, and I realized I could not clearly say who was right about what. Could you adjudicate their argument step by step—rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, explaining where each party lands a valid point, identifying what either is missing about how indirect normativity is supposed to function, and saying which view is more defensible overall? Please avoid appealing to named theorists or specific texts I would not have encountered; I am interested in the conceptual mechanics rather than scholarly attribution.
