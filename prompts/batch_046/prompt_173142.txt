I'm a retired experimental physicist who spent my career building calibration protocols and distinguishing instrumental artifacts from genuine measurements, and who has recently begun reading philosophy of science and philosophy of mind. I've encountered the debate over whether neural networks contain genuine internal representations — the kind mechanistic interpretability claims to identify when it maps directions in activation space to human-interpretable features — or whether such talk is merely an instrumental gloss on high-dimensional statistical correlations with no ontological commitment. I want you to restate the three main positions in this debate — the realist view that certain circuits or directions literally represent properties of the input domain, the eliminativist or instrumentalist view that interpretability is post-hoc curve-fitting without representational content, and the intermediate functional-role view — using only the vocabulary of my laboratory practice: calibration curves, operational definitions, response functions, noise floors, detector linearity, latent-variable reconstruction, and the distinction between measuring a physical quantity and modeling an instrument's internal dynamics. For each position, specify precisely which measurement practices or instrumental concepts it corresponds to, and be honest about where the analogy strains. Then provide the residue report: which claims translate cleanly into instrumentation language, which require distortion to fit, and which have no counterpart at all in how experimentalists distinguish real measurements from artifacts. Specifically, address whether the realist claim that a network represents, say, an edge or a concept has the same epistemic status as saying a calibrated thermocouple represents temperature, or whether the philosophical debate imports metaphysical commitments — about intentionality, about content — that never arise in actual measurement science. I want to know whether the residue reveals a gap in the scientific vocabulary or shows that part of the philosophical disagreement is less substantive than it appears.
