In our political philosophy seminar we have been working through Karl Jaspers's postwar analysis of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—and his insistence that moral guilt remains strictly personal rather than genuinely collective. Several classmates have begun using the corporate-personhood analogy to challenge that boundary: a corporation, they argue, is a legal fiction that can bear liability for acts committed by long-departed executives, pay reparations out of current assets, and be compelled to reform its institutional culture, all without anyone claiming that every present shareholder personally committed the original wrong. By that logic, a nation-state is simply a larger and older corporation—it possesses legal continuity, a treasury, mechanisms of collective acknowledgment, and institutional memory—so talk of national responsibility or intergenerational obligation is not a category error but standard liability practice. I want to audit this analogy rather than dismiss it outright. Please construct an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of shareholders, the board, the articles of incorporation, the liability shield, and the dissolved subsidiary? Then identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for Jaspers's framework—for example, whether corporate liability is always reducible to penal or compensatory mechanisms (criminal or political guilt, in his terms) and therefore never reaches the strictly personal moral and metaphysical dimensions he treats as non-transferable. Also examine where the analogy quietly imports assumptions about consent, membership replaceability, and institutional identity across generations that may fail for citizens born decades after the relevant events. If the corporate analogy collapses under these pressures, is there a more resilient analog—perhaps a distributed autonomous organization governed by immutable logs, or a vessel reconstructed plank by plank—that could preserve intergenerational collective responsibility without violating Jaspers's core claim that moral guilt is inalienable? I am not asking for bibliographies or citations; I want to know whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along.
