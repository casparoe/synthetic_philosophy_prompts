The Kripkenstein debate about rule-following has been circling since the 80s and honestly it feels like every grad student now just picks a camp—skeptical solution, straight dispositionalism, or the no-fact-of-the-matter-but-we-talk-anyway line—and then moves on. But lately I've been wondering if the stall is real or just an illusion because people started applying the same framework to distributed ML systems, where a bunch of nodes update weights without any central arbiter checking if they're really following gradient descent. Some folks say this is literally Kripkenstein in silicon: there's no fact of the matter about whether the federated network is following the rule, only community practice among the nodes. Others say thats a category mistake—computational rules are syntactic, not mental, so the skepticism never applied. My hypothesis is that the original debate stalled because its actually two questions: one about mental content (what makes my use of plus mean addition) and one about normative constraint (what makes any system correct or incorrect). The ML case just makes the split obvious. So is the impasse verbal—are we fighting over rule—or are there two different problems being run together? And if its the split, what would actually move things? A formal result showing distributed systems cant stabalize a rule without a central arbiter? An empirical study about how human mathematicians actually correct each other? Or is the whole framing wrong and we should just drop rule-following as a primitive? I'd like the diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, plus what progress would look like given your diagnosis.
