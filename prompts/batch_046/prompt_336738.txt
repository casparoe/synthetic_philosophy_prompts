Write the opening remarks I am to deliver tomorrow evening at the memorial colloquium for Dr. Elias Vorn, to be held in the university’s old reading room. I am a novelist and was his friend rather than his colleague; the audience will be divided between philosophers of action and rational choice, who will notice every imprecision in the conceptual framing, and family members and literary friends who knew him only as a man of unusual stillness who never seemed to hurry toward any decision. Vorn spent his career on a reframing of the classic Buridan’s ass problem—not as the paradox of an agent starving between two identical bales of hay, but as what he called an “equilibrium of indifference,” in which the refusal to rank two equally weighted goods is not a failure of practical reason but a sustained ethical posture. This idea must do real structural work in the remarks: I cannot present his life as a narrative in which he eventually “picked a side,” intellectually or personally, because he genuinely did not; I cannot offer the family false consolation by implying that death has now resolved his suspended choices or that he watches over us from a resolved elsewhere; and I must honor his stubborn equilibrium without allowing it to sound like paralysis or avoidance. For the philosophers present, do not invent technical formalisms, but you may invoke the traditional framing of an agent positioned exactly between two indistinguishable options, and the long interpretive split between those who read this as a logical impossibility requiring a hidden preference and those who read it as a legitimate state of rational suspension. For the non-philosophers, render the concept through the concrete texture of his life: the way he held two equally beloved teaching posts for decades without ever ranking one above the other; the way he maintained close friendships across bitter academic divisions without ever mediating or taking sides. The tone should be literary, precise, and spoken in my voice—never academic lecture, never sentimental eulogy—running approximately six minutes. Do not include a bibliography, do not cite specific papers or texts, do not ask for reading recommendations, and do not put any philosopher’s words in quotation marks; paraphrase any position in plain, direct language. The speech should leave both halves of the room feeling that the concept explained the man rather than decorated him.
