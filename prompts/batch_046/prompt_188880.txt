Analyze it through the lens of collective intentionality and joint action, specifically contrasting Michael Bratman’s account of shared cooperative activity—where participants hold shared intentions and mutual responsiveness without necessarily constituting a unified group agent—with Margaret Gilbert’s account of plural subjects formed by joint commitment. Who, if anyone, counts as the collective agent: the Montgomery Improvement Association, the boycotters as a distributed population, or a we-mode collective? Map the actual structure of commitment and authority: Rosa Parks’s initial refusal was not planned as part of the boycott, yet was rapidly incorporated; the MIA organized carpools, mass meetings, and financial support; Martin Luther King Jr. emerged as spokesperson rather than director; and many participants never met one another. Finally, say what the case implies for the broader debate over whether collective intentionality requires something like a single group mind, and address what the theory predicts for a case it has not anticipated—say, a decentralized modern boycott coordinated primarily through digital platforms with no central association.
