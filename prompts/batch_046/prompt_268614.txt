Here is the argument:

(1) The frame problem is that any intelligent system must determine which of its representations are relevant to updating given a particular action, without examining all of them.
(2) Any solution requires a filter mechanism F that distinguishes relevant representations from irrelevant ones for a given context.
(3) For an agent to rationally employ F, the agent must possess a “frame belief”—a belief that F is the appropriate filter for the current context.
(4) Frame beliefs are themselves representations that may be irrelevant; for instance, the belief that my kitchen-filter applies might be irrelevant if I am actually in a simulated environment.
(5) Therefore, rationally applying F requires a second-order filter F’ that determines whether the frame belief about F is relevant.
(6) By parity of reasoning, F’ requires its own frame belief, which requires a third-order filter, generating an infinite regress of filters.
(7) To stop the regress, there must be a “ground frame belief” G that is employed without any further filter.
(8) If G is not filtered, its adoption is either hardwired or arbitrary—there is no rational justification for selecting G over any alternative.
(9) Consequently, all practical reasoning ultimately rests on an arbitrarily chosen ground frame.
(10) If the ground frame is arbitrary, then any course of action can be rendered rational by selecting a ground frame that makes the relevant beliefs support it.
(11) But there is clearly an objective distinction between rational and irrational actions—walking into oncoming traffic is irrational regardless of framing.
(12) Therefore, practical reasoning is impossible, or at least there is no objective rationality.
