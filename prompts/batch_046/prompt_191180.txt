I'm advising a small interdisciplinary group—economic historians and philosophers of science, with three years of funding and no capacity for field experiments—to tackle one conceptual bottleneck in the debate over whether, and how, we can ever know why some nations are rich. The familiar triad of explanations—institutions, geography, and culture—has generated vast empirical literatures, yet causal inference from comparative history remains contested because of path dependence, endogenous institutional formation, and the absence of counterfactual national trajectories.

Rather than a survey, I want a defended agenda: identify the two or three open conceptual questions that are genuinely upstream of adjudicating among institutional, geographic, and cultural accounts. For each, explain why it deserves priority over the others for this specific group, using criteria of tractability without randomization, how many downstream disputes depend on its resolution, and what form of conceptual or archival evidence in the next few years would cause you to upgrade or downgrade it. In particular, weigh whether the deeper obstacle is the standard identification problem—finding exogenous variation in institutional quality—or a prior definitional circularity: whether we can specify what an institution is independently of the economic outcomes it is invoked to explain, since instrumentation alone cannot rescue a concept that is partly constituted by its effects. Rank them, make the trade-offs explicit, and indicate which one you would tackle first if the goal is not just better measurement but a clearer answer to whether historical comparison can ever yield causal knowledge of national prosperity at all.
