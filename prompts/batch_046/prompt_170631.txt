I am composing a novel in which a protagonist must choose whether to enter a binding agreement that will later compel her to destroy her own completed masterpiece. In working out her reasoning, I have arrived at the following convictions, and I wish to know whether they cohere. First, I hold that rationality is purely instrumental and indexed to the present: there is no rationality in the abstract, only what best serves one's aims at the moment of decision. Second, I accept the logic of Parfit's hitchhiker: it can be rational now to bind oneself to an act that will be irrational when the time comes, provided the present gain is sufficient. Third, I take personal identity across time to be largely a narrative construction rather than a metaphysically deep fact; the future artist who would destroy the work is, in significant respects, not me. Fourth, nevertheless, I believe we possess substantial reasons not to undermine our future selves, reasons that feel weightier than a mere preference for biographical continuity. Fifth, I reject moral realism entirely, denying that any mind-independent moral facts could ground those reasons. Do these five views hang together? Please examine each pair, distinguishing genuine tensions from merely apparent ones, ranking the conflicts by severity, identifying the cheapest revision that would restore consistency, and saying which single change would resolve the most. In doing so, separate what is philosophically settled from what remains genuinely contested among those who work on these questions, and do not present any disputed position as if it were the consensus. Present your reply as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a thoughtful letter.
