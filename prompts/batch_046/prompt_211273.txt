I work as a senior software engineer with no formal philosophical training. My team is developing a reusable decision procedure for architecture reviews: specifically, whether to delete a legacy module, dependency, or configuration flag from a production system. I want the procedure to operationalise Chesterton's fence — that one should not dismantle a mechanism without knowing why it was erected — together with Burke's idea of prescription, which treats long-standing institutional practices as carrying embedded practical wisdom that outlasts individual reasoning.

The deliverable should be a step-by-step procedure that a non-philosopher can run in thirty minutes, without external research. For each step, state the precise question, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether failure stops the process or passes to human judgement. The procedure must distinguish between epistemic gaps (we do not know the purpose) and normative disagreements (we understand the purpose but reject the value it serves). It must also specify how to proceed when original authors are unreachable and documentation is sparse or contradictory.

Apply the procedure to three scenarios: deleting an undocumented rate-limiting middleware layer from 2014; replacing a bespoke encryption wrapper with a standard library equivalent; and removing a dead code branch kept alive solely by a failing integration test. Include at least one scenario where the procedure mandates preservation despite strong team pressure to delete, and flag the steps where subjective judgement is unavoidable and where the procedure is likely to misfire.
