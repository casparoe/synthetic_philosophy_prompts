Exam question:
Standard theories of promissory obligation—Scanlon's expectation-based account and Raz's interest-based coordination view—are typically formulated for cases in which the promiser knows precisely what performance entails. Consider, however, what might be called epistemic promises: commitments made under substantial uncertainty about future feasibility, in which the promiser pledges to pursue a specified course of action contingent on conditions not yet within her control (for instance, a scientist promising a collaborator to share preliminary findings if an independent replication succeeds). Assess whether Scanlon's principle can accommodate such promises without reducing them to mere expressions of intent, and identify one internal tension that this accommodation creates for Raz's claim that promises serve the promisee's interest in stable planning.

Student answer:
Scanlon's expectation-based principle holds that A becomes obligated to B to perform X when A voluntarily and intentionally leads B to expect that performance, B takes steps in reliance on that expectation, and neither consent nor failure of conditions intervenes. Applied to epistemic promises, this structure remains intact. The promisee does not expect an unconditional result but rather a specific course of conduct—say, submitting data upon successful replication—backed by the promiser's judgment. The reliance is therefore directed at diligence and good faith rather than at a guaranteed outcome, so the commitment satisfies principle F without collapsing into a statement of intent.

Raz's interest-based account treats promises as instruments that protect the promisee's autonomy by guaranteeing actions around which she can plan. On Raz's view, however, an obligation arises only if the promisee actually relies on the commitment for practical planning; where there is no such reliance, no binding force is generated. In epistemic promises, this reliance is necessarily weakened because the promisee knows the outcome is contingent. Hence Raz's framework implies that such promises carry only diminished obligations, or perhaps none at all, which sits uneasily with the intuition that a contingent pledge of data-sharing can impose a serious duty of diligence.

Moreover, there is a deeper tension for Raz. If the normative value of promising lies in enabling firm, stable planning, then embedding explicit contingency directly undermines the coordination function he emphasizes. The promisee cannot build reliable plans around an uncertain result. Consequently, Raz must either exclude epistemic promises from the domain of genuine promises, or concede that their binding force derives not from planning interests but from the very expectation dynamics that Scanlon's account describes.
