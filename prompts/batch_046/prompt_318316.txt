I am preparing an essay for a long-form feature on economic reasoning and moral psychology, and I would value rigorous critical feedback on its conceptual architecture rather than on style alone. The piece asks whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational, and it advances a distinction between two phenomena that standard treatments tend to conflate: the familiar waste-aversion error in private consumption—continuing a poor film because one paid for the ticket—and what I term structural honor in institutional and relational settings. In these contexts, completing a costly commitment can operate not as a failed attempt to recover irretrievable resources, but as a credible maintenance of the trust architectures and intertemporal bargaining positions that were co-constituted with the original expenditure. To test this, I construct a hypothetical scenario involving a municipal authority that has sunk substantial capital into a bridge design subsequently found to contain a subtle, correctable flaw; abandoning the project might preserve immediate fiscal resources, yet it could simultaneously erode the credibility required for future public-private partnerships in ways that pure forward-looking calculus does not capture. I then address the objection that this reasoning merely smuggles in standard game-theoretic commitment devices, and I reply that structural honor differs from strategic commitment precisely because it is non-instrumental with respect to the original investment's value—one honors the sunk expenditure not because it generates future payoff, but because discontinuing it fractures a collective narrative of reliability that was inseparable from the act of spending itself.

Where I am most uncertain is whether this distinction genuinely holds or collapses into special pleading once examined closely. Does the bridge scenario actually support the thesis, or does it quietly depend on hidden forward-looking benefits—such as reputation preservation—that make it a case of rational signaling rather than of honoring a sunk cost for its own sake? I am also concerned that the argument may moralize what is essentially a straightforward error in individual reasoning by projecting institutional significance onto private psychology. Could you comment specifically on the coherence of the conceptual distinction, on whether the scenario risks conflating rational commitment with irrational persistence, and on what revisions—perhaps tighter conditions for when structural honor applies, or a clearer boundary against strategic reasoning—would strengthen the essay's rigor without sacrificing accessibility for a general audience interested in philosophy and public policy? I am not seeking citations or a reading list, but rather an assessment of where the logic is vulnerable and which lines of objection deserve the most serious response.
