i've come around to a weirdish view about why promises bind: it's not about reliance or harm-avoidance, but about "future-self authorization." when you promise someone, you're giving them a license to hold your later self acountable as the same practical identitiy — so breaking it is mainly a kind of self-fragmention, not a wrong done to them. and i think this holds even if the promisee dies or the collective you promised dissolves, because the normative echo is anchored in your own practical continuity, not in their existence. what would actually count against this? don't just give me the standard "but expectations matter!" line — i want defeaters that hit the core: cases where the promisee is an amnesiac or an artificial agent with no practical identity to authorize, results from personal-identity theory that sever present and future self, maybe phenomenological data where breaking a promise feels like betraying them rather than betraying yourself. rank them fatal to merely awkward. also be honest: has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability? if i just say "well practical identitiy survives any psychological change," is that a virtue or a fatal insulation for a claim of this kind? and what specific evidence or argument would move your credence the most?
