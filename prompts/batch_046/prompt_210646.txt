I am a novelist drafting a work of historical fiction about an archaeologist who, excavating a Late Bronze Age settlement that has yielded no texts whatsoever, becomes convinced that the asymmetric distribution of ritual vessels and the orientation of domestic doorways together encode a specific cosmological hierarchy—one that assigns a divine status to the eastern quarter. For my research, I am preparing to moderate a podcast interview with an unnamed scholar who defends what she terms structural institutionalism: the view that spatial syntax and artifactual patterning can reliably reconstruct social institutions—graded authority, inheritance rules, labor specialization—yet can never license attributions of propositional belief, whether theological or cosmological, in the absence of writing or direct representational iconography. I need eight to ten questions that press this position precisely where archaeological epistemology is most contested, avoiding any generic biographical opening. At least one question should ask how she distinguishes legitimate institutional inference from the kind of symbolic over-interpretation practiced by my fictional archaeologist; another should force her to address the standard counter-example of prehistoric cave art, where scholars routinely attribute shamanic or narrative beliefs despite a total lack of textual corroboration; and a third should probe her response to materially contradictory patterns—say, rigorously egalitarian burial goods set against monumentally hierarchical storage architecture. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, particularly her probable retreat to the claim that institutions are observable behavioral residues while beliefs are unobservable cognitive contents; arrange the sequence so that this retreat becomes progressively harder to maintain without either collapsing into arbitrariness or conceding that some belief-attribution is unavoidable. The tone should be rigorous but accessible to an educated general audience, and each question should anticipate the literature's standard replies rather than treating them as afterthoughts.
