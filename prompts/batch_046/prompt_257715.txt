As a journalist preparing a feature on the architecture of consciousness for a scientifically literate general audience, I am commissioning the background document that would emerge from a rigorous adversarial collaboration between a Central Integrationist—committed to the claim that conscious experience requires a unified, spatiotemporally localized integration stage where competing representations are resolved into globally available content—and a Multiple Drafts defender—committed to Dennett’s view that there is no central screen, that consciousness consists of distributed, parallel, and temporally unbound processes, and that the appearance of a unified stream is a retrospective narrative construction rather than a real-time presentation. Please draft the joint statement both representatives could sign after a year of good-faith collaboration.

Structure the deliverable in two parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims that are neither vacuous nor slanted: shared commitments about what conscious report actually involves; about the formal problem of explaining temporal continuity, selective attention, and the latency between stimulus and verbal report; about what the absence of a single anatomical “read-out” site preceding report by a stable interval genuinely rules out; and about the empirical constraint—treated here as a shared premise—that intracranial recordings in human subjects consistently reveal distributed activation across prefrontal, parietal, and temporal regions with a variable latency of roughly 150 to 400 milliseconds and no fixed neural convergence zone that consistently leads conscious report. Second, a disagreements table stating the precise residual divergences, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording: for instance, one row should state the integrationist position that “there exists a functional (not necessarily anatomical) stage at which competing representations are resolved into a single globally broadcast content,” and the opposing row should state the drafts position that “there is no such stage; apparent resolution is an artifact of post-hoc selection among parallel drafts, none of which is canonically ‘the’ conscious experience.”

Within the document, identify at least three cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I want them to include: (a) whether diachronic binding, the experienced continuity of a single stream across time, requires a dynamical mechanism that maintains representational identity over intervals, or whether it can be fully explained by the brain’s retrospective stitching of discrete drafts; (b) whether the observed absence of a fixed neural read-out site is fatal to any integrationist model, or whether integration can be realized by transient, distributed coalitions without a permanent hub; and (c) whether evolutionary robustness arguments genuinely favor one framework—specifically, whether the lack of a central screen makes a system more resilient against single-point failure, or whether evolutionary pressure for unified action-selection necessarily produces a functional convergence zone.

For each crux, specify the concrete case, argument, or empirical finding both sides agree bears on it—using the distributed-variable-latency premise where relevant—followed by each side’s explicit prediction or interpretation of that evidence. Then note whether removing the contested feature (for example, assuming perfect temporal resolution in measurement or ignoring evolutionary constraints) collapses the disagreement back into a standard Newcomb-style framing or reveals it to be merely verbal.

Conclude with an assessment of which parts of the dispute are genuinely substantive and which are terminological: say exactly where terms such as “presentation,” “binding,” “draft,” or “canonical version” carry different theoretical loads for each camp, and whether agreeing on a neutral vocabulary would dissolve any portion of the conflict without changing anyone’s underlying commitments. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for a feature brief, avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests, and do not include direct quotations from specific texts; where you reference Dennett’s framework, paraphrase without quotation marks and omit unverified page numbers or edition years. The prompt should be self-contained and answerable by a model relying on general philosophical and neuroscientific knowledge alone.
