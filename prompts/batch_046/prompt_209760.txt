I am moderating a podcast Q&A with an unnamed philosopher who has recently advanced a reconstructed defense of Berkeley's master argument—the challenge in the Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous that asks whether one can conceive of any sensible object existing outside the mind. Rather than treating it as a straightforward modal inference from conceivability to possibility, she reads the argument as a transcendental claim about the conditions of intelligibility: any attempt to form the idea of an unperceived object necessarily involves treating that object as present to a mind, so the materialist who asserts mind-independent existence owes not a negative refusal but a positive account of what it is for a quality to be instantiated without being perceived, and Berkeley's point is that no such account is available.

Please draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking her to restate this interpretation. I want at least one question on each of the following: (a) how her transcendental reading avoids collapsing into a Kantian critical idealism that still permits a noumenal realm she wants to reject; (b) whether her reconstruction requires retaining Berkeley's specific theory of ideas—particularly his rejection of abstract ideas—or whether it can survive on a thinner phenomenological basis; (c) the classic objection that we conceive of an unobserved tree in a park by abstracting away our own presence, and why she thinks that abstraction fails to reach genuine mind-independence rather than merely imaginary absence; (d) whether her version renders the master argument unfalsifiable, since any proposed counterexample can be redescribed as involving an implicit mental presence; and (e) how her account relates to Berkeley's own solution to the problem of continuity—divine perpetual perception—and whether she accepts, modifies, or eliminates that theological apparatus.

For each thread, provide a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. For example, if she answers (a) by insisting that Kant's noumenal realm is itself unthinkable on her terms, press her on whether that unthinkability is demonstrated or merely asserted by the same master argument she is defending. The questions should be rigorous, avoid generic biography or "what inspired you" framing, and should assume an audience familiar with basic epistemology and metaphysics but not with the specifics of her reconstruction.
