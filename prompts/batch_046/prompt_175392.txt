I am writing from our seminar on comparative historical methods in political economy, where we examine whether cross-national income divergence can be causally attributed to institutions, geography, or culture. Our professor has adopted the following consistent rulings on specific comparisons. First, on Korea after 1945: with geography and culture essentially constant, the exogenous institutional split justifies an institutional attribution, and she holds that we have genuine causal knowledge. Second, on the colonial reversal of fortune: because tropical geography systematically determined whether extractive or inclusive institutions were imposed, institutions and geography are collinear; she rules that institutions are only the proximate mechanism and that we lack independent causal knowledge of institutional effects separated from geographic selection. Third, on East and West Germany after 1949: with shared geography, shared culture, and an exogenous institutional division, she rules that institutions explain the divergence and that we possess causal knowledge. Fourth, on Haiti and the Dominican Republic: despite shared geography, colonial legal traditions diverged alongside language and religious practice; she rules that institutions and culture are confounded and that no independent causal claim is warranted for either.

We are now assessing a proposed study comparing two adjacent river-basin provinces—Region A and Region B—separated only by an arbitrary 1923 administrative boundary that corresponds to no ethnic, linguistic, or geographic discontinuity. Over the subsequent century, Region A developed inclusive property-rights institutions through an endogenous merchant-guild tradition, while Region B retained extractive colonial land-tenure rules that were never exogenously imposed but simply never reformed. Geography, language, and religion remain identical, yet per-capita income diverges sharply.

Predict her ruling: would she say we can know that institutions caused the gap? Identify the closest analog among her four precedents, state the principle she would be adopting if she extends the Korea and Germany line versus if she extends the reversal-of-fortune line, and specify the further comparative case—real or constructed—that would best settle whether her underlying rule is that institutional divergence yields knowledge whenever background conditions are held constant, or that it yields knowledge only when the divergence is exogenous to geographic and cultural selection.
