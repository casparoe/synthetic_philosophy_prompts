As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am pitching a small interdisciplinary foundation for a six-month philosophical research project; the foundation supports conceptual work that shapes real institutional design but does not fund empirical data collection. I need you—acting as a professional philosopher with deep expertise in social-choice theory and the philosophy of technology—to draft the one-page proposal I will submit. Before you begin drafting, please spend a brief moment brainstorming: what is the sharpest, least-obvious formulation of the problem, and where is the greatest risk of analogical slippage?

The domain is Sen’s liberal paradox—the structural tension between minimal liberal rights and Pareto efficiency in collective preference aggregation. To avoid a generic recapitulation of well-known consumer-choice examples, inject a genuinely niche framing: apply the paradox to multi-stakeholder governance of large-scale AI systems, where “minimal liberty” takes the form of stakeholder-specific veto rights over distinct classes of model outputs (for instance, a scientific-research consortium demands unrestricted access to high-level reasoning capabilities, while a biosecurity oversight board demands the power to block outputs that could facilitate dual-use biological design). The core question is whether a governance mechanism can honor every stakeholder’s minimal liberty within its designated domain without generating a collectively inferior outcome that all parties would reject. Crucially, ask whether this is one unified problem or fragments into separate sub-problems—epistemic (what counts as “their” domain), normative (whether liberty should be weighted equally), and procedural (how aggregation rules are selected).

The proposal must address, in a formal and polished register suitable for a grant committee:
1. The precise research question, framed so that it is genuinely open and tractable without original empirical studies or simulations.
2. Why existing positions in both social-choice theory and AI governance leave the question unsettled; refer to arguments and positions, not to specific bibliographic citations, as I will supply references myself.
3. The philosophical approach—conceptual analysis of preference domains, rights-structures, and aggregation rules—and why this makes progress rather than the obvious alternatives (e.g., running agent-based models or surveying practitioners).
4. A realistic deliverable for people actually designing oversight boards: what would the result look like, and what specific conditions would count as failure?
5. A direct, honest response to the foundation’s likely objection: why should they fund philosophy here rather than engineering or policy analysis?
6. A realistic six-month scope with clear milestones.

Please think carefully about whether the paradox genuinely replicates in this institutional setting or whether the analogy breaks down, and make that tension an explicit part of the proposal’s intellectual risk. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or requests for citations. Do not use verbatim quotations from any real text; paraphrase without quotation marks if needed. Write the document as a plan for work not yet done, arguing that the work is worth doing and can be done within the time available.
