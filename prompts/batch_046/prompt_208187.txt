You serve on the interpretation board of a regional museum and must sign off on every public claim that assigns a belief, institution, or collective intention to a past community based solely on excavated material. The board has adopted a theoretical framework—drawn from social ontology and the philosophy of material culture—that distinguishes indexical collective orientation (shared practical dispositions embodied in how objects are made, placed, or broken) from propositional collective belief (explicit, doctrinal commitments that members could in principle affirm). It also treats negative materiality—systematic absences where preservation bias cannot explain the gap—as a separate evidential category that may indicate institutional suppression, taboo, or structural exclusion, but only under strict conditions. Your task is to translate this framework into a reusable, thirty-minute decision procedure that a staff archaeologist without philosophical training can run before drafting a label, report, or exhibition panel. The procedure must be ordered: state the questions in sequence, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, identify the exact point where the answer must be “reject the claim,” “approve with an institutional caveat,” or “refer to the board because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence,” and flag where judgment cannot be eliminated despite the formality. It must also distinguish distributed preference (similar individual behaviors arising from optimization or ecology) from genuine institutional belief, and it must handle negative inferences without collapsing absence into proof of absence.

Apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals your board has received: (1) a claim that standardized ceramic motifs across twelve widely separated sites prove the existence of a centralized religious authority directing ritual production; (2) a claim that the systematic absence of defensive architecture in a settlement rich in high-status goods demonstrates an institutional commitment to pacifism, despite regional conflict evidence; and (3) a claim that gender-segregated tool caches in domestic contexts justify labeling the society as having an institutional gender division of labor. The first case should yield an answer you, as the board’s advocate for bold interpretation, will dislike. The second should show the procedure stopping at a negative-materiality gate. The third should show where the procedure hands the decision to a values judgment and cannot settle it. Conclude by stating exactly what the procedure is powerless to determine, even when all evidential boxes are checked.

End your response with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by a brief phrase naming the specific judgment point—between indexical orientation and propositional belief, or between absence and suppression—that remains irreducible in every case.
