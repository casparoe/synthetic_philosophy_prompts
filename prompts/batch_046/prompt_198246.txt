Since my father's recent stroke left him with anosognosia and severely disrupted self-monitoring, I have been forced to think about consciousness not as an abstraction but as something that can fragment in a particular person. In a caregiver forum, someone keeps repeating the line, "Consciousness is only a user illusion—there's no one behind the screen, only a brain rendering a dashboard for itself," using it to argue that my father's diminished sense of ownership over his actions means he is no longer fully a person, with corresponding shifts in moral obligation. Before I let that slogan settle the matter, I want to understand exactly what work it could be doing. Can you separate the precise theses hiding in that sentence—ranging from the trivial claim that introspection does not reveal neural mechanisms, through intermediate views about self-models, to radical eliminativism about phenomenal subjects—indicating for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or false? For each reading, specify whether it actually supports the inference from fragmented self-modeling to reduced moral status. Lay the options out as a menu rather than selecting one, identify which version the forum speaker is most likely relying on, and say what conditions would have to hold for that specific version to carry the moral conclusion they want.
