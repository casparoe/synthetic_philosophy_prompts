Read the film Twelve Angry Men through the Condorcet jury theorem, but modify the usual framing: instead of treating the twelve jurors as independent, equally competent binary voters, treat them as nodes in a deliberative network where influence is directional, competence is heterogeneous, and the jurors initially share a common cognitive bias—namely, an uncritical trust in direct eyewitness testimony despite its well-documented fragility—that introduces correlated errors violating Condorcet's independence condition. Where does the narrative dramatize the theorem's optimism about collective wisdom overcoming individual fallibility, particularly in the gradual shift toward acquittal? Conversely, where does the film expose the limits of the model by showing that the jury's accuracy depends not on the sheer number of voters but on the presence of a single dissenting node (Juror 8) who breaks an epistemic cascade, makes hidden premises visible, and redirects the flow of persuasion? I want a reading that takes both the dramatic logic of the deliberation and the formal conditions of the theorem seriously, asking whether the final verdict vindicates collective judgment in Condorcet's sense or instead demonstrates that network structure and the breaking of shared bias matter more than voter multiplicity—what the film might add to the philosophical debate that the formal proof alone cannot capture.
