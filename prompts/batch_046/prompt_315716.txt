My colleague has rendered consistent verdicts across a series of knowledge-attribution puzzles. She holds that (1) in the standard Gettier case—where Smith justifiably believes that the man who will get the job has ten coins, inferring from a false premise about Jones, and happens to be right about himself—Smith lacks knowledge; (2) in the fake-barn county scenario, the driver who correctly identifies the sole real barn among numerous facades lacks knowledge that it is a barn; (3) in a case where a historian correctly dates Napoleon's death to 1821 on the basis of a biography that falsely claims to have consulted primary archives, where that false claim plays no causal role in fixing the date, the historian does have knowledge of the date; and (4) in a case where a thermometer is broken and stuck displaying the correct temperature, with a hidden heating system maintaining the room's temperature to match that display, an observer who reads the thermometer lacks knowledge of the temperature.

Do you think she would say that a data scientist knows her model's prediction in the following situation? The statistical model is misspecified for the domain and yields correct predictions only about 20% of the time, but an unknown structural confounder in this specific dataset exactly cancels the misspecification error, producing the correct result. The confounder is stable across nearby data configurations, so the belief would remain true in similar situations, yet the scientist has no awareness of either the confounder's presence or the model's poor domain record, and she formed her belief solely from the model's output. What about a second scenario with the same misspecified model applied to a different dataset lacking any confounder, where the model is correct purely by chance, and the scientist knows independently that the model is usually unreliable in this domain but cannot tell whether this particular run is among the rare successes?

I am less interested in the bare yes-or-no answers than in the principle or principles that best organize her four earlier judgments, the competing rules that accommodate those judgments equally well, which of the two scenarios above most sharply separates those rules, and what further hypothetical—perhaps involving an instrument that is globally reliable but temporarily deceived by an isolated, non-repeating environmental anomaly—would provide the clearest test between them.
