Having spent thirty years calibrating instruments and worrying whether observed shifts in a signal were due to the detector or to the phenomenon itself, I find the long-running dispute between pragmatic encroachers and epistemic contextualists curiously familiar. The encroachers hold that whether a subject knows depends directly on the practical stakes of being wrong—raise the cost of error, and the same belief may fail to amount to knowledge—while the contextualists hold that only the truth-conditions of the sentence that S knows that p shift with conversational context, leaving the underlying knowledge relation untouched. For decades, both sides have traded intuitions about bank-teller scenarios, airplane-checking judgments, and high-stakes medical decisions, yet the literature seems stuck: each side accounts for the linguistic data, each explains away the opponent's cases, and neither has persuaded the other.

My working hypothesis, drawn from measurement theory, is that the disagreement may be structurally analogous to the distinction between parameter drift and model misspecification. The contextualist treats varying epistemic standards as a shift in the calibration parameter of the term knows across contexts, leaving the core model of belief-formation unchanged; the pragmatic encroacher treats the same variation as evidence that the knowledge relation itself contains a practical coefficient—that is, that our model is misspecified if it omits stakes. If this analogy holds, the debate might not concern a shared empirical target at all, but rather which statistical architecture we should project onto ordinary language.

But I am unsure whether this framing captures the actual structure of the dispute. Is the impasse substantive—do the sides genuinely disagree about whether practical interests can causally influence the epistemic status of a belief—or is it verbal, turning on whether knows denotes a context-sensitive relation or a uniform one expressed with shifting thresholds? Could there be a hidden empirical question, perhaps about the cognitive mechanisms that generate knowledge-attributions, that both sides have ignored because they treat the dispute as purely philosophical? Or are they, like two research groups with incompatible statistical priors, simply answering different questions—one about the semantics of attribution, the other about the normative conditions for rational belief—under a single heading?

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Using what each side actually says about cases, about the role of practical reasoning, and about the aims of epistemic evaluation, tell me what kind of disagreement this is: factual, normative, semantic, or methodological. And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? Would it require a new conceptual distinction—say, between the conditions for knowledge and the conditions for warranted assertion—that both sides could accept, or a result from cognitive science about how speakers calibrate epistemic standards, or is the question ill-posed in a way that suggests we should dissolve it rather than solve it? Please be honest about whether any development should be expected to move the debate, and why.
