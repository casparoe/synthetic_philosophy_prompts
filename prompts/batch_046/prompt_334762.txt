I'm developing a framework I call geometric epistemics, which treats an agent's belief-state not as a single probability distribution but as a portfolio of epistemic positions, each carrying its own long-run growth rate in expected accuracy across repeated updates. The analogy is drawn directly from Kelly betting: just as a gambler maximizes the geometric mean of capital growth by sizing bets according to edge divided by odds, the rational agent might maximize the geometric mean of epistemic reliability by allocating cognitive commitment proportionally to calibrated confidence. I have constructed the following argument, which now strikes me as suspicious because it concludes in total dogmatism, yet I cannot pinpoint the precise failure.

(1) If geometric rationality requires maximizing the long-run geometric mean of returns, and if beliefs are the resources being invested through the actions they license, then a rational agent should allocate belief-weight according to the Kelly-optimal fraction for each proposition.

(2) For any belief held with confidence c strictly between 0 and 1, this allocation necessarily splits cognitive resources between acting-on-p and withholding-action, treating the belief as a partial bet.

(3) Splitting resources across non-correlated epistemic positions reduces the portfolio's geometric growth rate relative to concentrating fully on the Kelly-optimal subset; diversification here is not protective but destructive.

(4) Therefore, for any individual belief, partial confidence is geometrically dominated by either full acceptance (c = 1) or full rejection (c = 0).

(5) By iteration across the entire belief portfolio, geometric epistemics demands complete dogmatism: every belief must be held at an extreme.

(6) Complete dogmatism is incompatible with learning from evidence that admits of gradation and with the practical necessity of revising beliefs incrementally.

(7) Hence geometric epistemics is self-defeating, unless premise (1) is false.

The conclusion feels intolerable, but I want to preserve the core intuition that belief management has a formal structure analogous to capital management—specifically, that there is a geometric cost to misallocation. If I must reject a premise, I prefer to keep (1) and (3) intact and instead modify (2) or (4). Where exactly does this argument fail? Is there a hidden equivocation between financial capital, which is conserved, fungible, and transferable across bets, and epistemic commitment, which is not? Or does the error lie in treating beliefs as independent bets within a single portfolio, when in fact epistemic updates are path-dependent and non-fungible? Provide a precise diagnosis of the flawed step, explain why it appeared valid, and say what a corrected version of geometric epistemics could claim—without collapsing into extremism—about how partial confidence should be managed. In particular, address whether the residue points to a genuine limitation of applying Kelly logic to belief, or whether it reveals that my framing of "epistemic capital" was less coherent than it appeared.
