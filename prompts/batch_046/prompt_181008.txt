For years I've followed the debate in AI ethics and on rationalist forums about whether autonomous systems create genuine responsibility gaps — situations where serious harm occurs but no human agent clearly satisfies the conditions for moral blame. The stalemate runs like this. One camp argues that in continuously-learning systems — imagine a logistics optimizer that updates its routing weights in real time via feedback loops no operator monitors — human supervisors lack both the epistemic access and the causal leverage that backward-looking responsibility requires. The opposing camp, often linked to institutional-design frameworks, replies that responsibility was never about micro-level causal tracing; it is satisfied by ex ante architecture choices, governance roles, and designated accountability officers who can be called to answer even when no one could have intervened at the critical moment. My working theory is that the disagreement is mostly conceptual rather than empirical: the two sides operate with different implicit models of what a control relation is. One treats it as an occurrent, traceable causal link between an agent and an outcome; the other treats it as a structural, normative relation that holds through design and oversight regardless of real-time opacity. But I want to check whether I am missing something. Is there a hidden empirical dispute about whether current human-in-the-loop architectures actually preserve the right kind of control, or about whether organizational liability can genuinely do the moral work that individual blame usually does? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed — perhaps because it assumes responsibility must be singular and fully localized rather than distributed and plural? I am asking for a diagnosis, not a survey of positions. Tell me whether this is a verbal disagreement, a clash over which cases count as data, a conflict about standards of evidence, or a substantive normative divergence masked as a technical one. Then say what kind of progress could actually resolve it — a conceptual distinction, a formal model of distributed causation, an empirical study of deployed oversight, or an argument that no resolution should be expected — and identify which feature of the case does the real work. Please answer in American English, without bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to authority I cannot verify independently.
