I'm preparing a long-form feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the metaphysics of personal origins, and I want to avoid the standard classroom framing of origin essentialism. Instead, I'm testing how different metaphysical stances survive a deliberately constructed counterfactual. Please consider the following five positions on whether a specific person could have originated from a different sperm and egg:

1. Rigid gamete essentialism: the particular sperm and egg are necessary conditions for that person's identity.
2. Genetic-profile essentialism: any gamete pair yielding the same nuclear genome is interchangeable with respect to identity.
3. Developmental-path essentialism: identity is anchored to the continuous causal-developmental trajectory from conception, permitting genetic variation provided the trajectory is unbroken.
4. Relational origin essentialism: what fixes identity is the specific parental reproductive pairing, not the particular gametes involved.
5. Anti-essentialism: origin facts are entirely contingent and place no cross-world constraints on personal identity.

For each position, assign a score from 1 to 10 measuring its resilience under what I call "chimeric divergence". By this I mean a hypothetical case in which a single fertilised zygote undergoes an anomalous early-cell division producing two genetically overlapping but non-identical lineages, one of which is reabsorbed, leaving the survivor with a mosaic genome that partly traces to the original sperm-egg pair and partly to the anomalous division event. Provide a one-paragraph justification for each score in formal but accessible prose. Then, group the five positions into a tier list—S-tier, A-tier, B-tier, C-tier, D-tier—ranking them by overall philosophical coherence rather than by score alone. Write in British English, maintain a polished, journalistic register suitable for an educated general audience, and do not supply bibliographies, citations, or suggested reading lists.
