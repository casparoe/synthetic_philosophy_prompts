ok so i'm sorting out my view on advance directives for a bioethics group and want a consistency check before i present. here's roughly where i stand:

(1) i think a living will binds caregivers mainly because the authoring self keeps diachronic authority over the biological life that continues — it's like granting power of attorney to your own subtrate, not to a future psychological stage.
(2) i don't buy that severe dementia creates a numerically distinct person; the patient is still me, just with capacities stripped down.
(3) but if the later self shows clear, sustained preferences that clash with the directive (say a happy demented patient who wants food despite a refusal clause), those should sometimes override — but only if the clash comes from a real shift in core values, not impulse or confusion.
(4) autonomy is basically relational and narrative: we let past versions govern us only insofar as their choices still fit a life-story we can currently endorse.
(5) and actually i think the directive's authority is strongest precisely when narrative capacity is totally gone — like late-stage alzheimers with no self-awareness — since that's when there's no competing autonomous voice.

does this hang together? i feel like (4) and (5) are pulling opposite directions, and (1) might not sit well with (4). but maybe (2) and (3) only look tense. can you rank the real tensions by severity, say what'd have to give in each case, and tell me whether one revision could clean up the most without ditching the intuition behind (5)? please don't just flag everything as equally bad — i want to know which ones are fake.
