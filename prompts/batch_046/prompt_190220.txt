I serve on a commission revising constitutional amendment procedures, and I have recently been assisting a family member through addiction recovery using clinical precommitment contracts. This dual involvement has drawn my attention to the philosophical debate on Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment—specifically the claims that a rational agent may legitimately bind her future self without irrationality, that democratic legitimacy is undermined when one generation permanently constrains its successors, and that there is a substantive distinction between binding future actions and binding future judgments. I want you to restate these three claims entirely in the vocabulary I work with professionally: hyperbolic discounting curves, contingency-management contracts in addiction therapy, therapeutic-alliance agreements, and legal doctrines of non-derogable provisions coupled with asymmetric sunset clauses. Proceed claim by claim. For each, report what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit—especially where collective democratic agency is compressed into the model of a single intertemporal agent—and what has no counterpart at all in my framework, paying particular attention to whether clinical commitment devices ever attempt to bind a patient’s future epistemic states rather than merely her future behaviors. Then provide the residue report: does the complete absence of “belief-binding” in clinical practice reveal that applying Ulysses logic to constitutional design imports a category mistake, or does it show that my vocabulary simply lacks the resources to represent collective rationality across generations? I am especially interested in whether the asymmetry I have noticed—constitutional entrenchment can be made reversible for actions but not for interpretive commitments—survives the translation, or whether it dissolves into a feature of contract design that has nothing philosophical behind it.
