I retired from marine biology about five years ago and have been slowly working through philosophy texts since then, mostly because I like arguing with my old lab mates over coffee. Lately I've been stuck on this pattern in how people talk about blurry boundaries — not just in science but in everyday stuff. Take the line between a hill and a mountain, or between a healthy ecosystem and a degraded one. In every debate I see, it splits into two camps. One camp insists there's definately a precise cutoff somewhere — maybe exactly 300 meters, maybe exactly 47 species present — we just haven't measured finely enough to find it, like the fuzz is only in our instruments. The other camp says that's completely wrong, there's no hidden fact at all, just different ways people could draw the line, and picking one is just convention.

That part I think is familiar. But here's the thing that doesn't seem to have a neat label: people switch which camp they belong to based on whether the category feels 'discovered' or 'invented'. My freinds will defend the hidden-cutoff view for chemical isotopes but immediately deny it for mental-health diagnoses, even though both are measured on continuous scales. Is there an established concept for this selective application — treating vagueness as hidden ignorance in some domains but as genuinely absent boundaries in others? Or is it just people being sloppy? And if philosophers have carved this up, I'd like to know how they draw the edges: is there a middle view that allows hidden boundaries for physical quantities but not for social ones, or does that combination fall apart? I don't need book lists, just wondering if this asymmetry is a recognized failure mode or if I'm describing something that doesn't quite fit the standard split.
