I'm organizing a ninety-minute session for philosophy-of-science doctoral students who are comfortable with Bayesian epistemology but have never worked through a modal derivation. The idea I want them to grasp is the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents: specifically, why an agent whose reasoning system is strong enough for arithmetic, and which also holds beliefs about what it can prove, cannot consistently maintain that belief in its own consistency is justified—without that very belief collapsing in a way that forces acceptance of arbitrary propositions. Rather than explaining the derivation to me, I need a teaching plan that structures the session as an explanation they can follow.

What dependency order should govern the session—must it begin with the object-language versus meta-language distinction before introducing the specific Löb conditions, or is the reverse possible, and why? For the carrying example, I want to avoid the standard mathematician scenario and instead build everything around an autonomous climate-modeling system that generates temperature forecasts and simultaneously outputs confidence reports about its own calibration error; what must this case possess, and what must it deliberately lack, so that the obstacle appears as a structural feature of self-reference rather than as an engineering failure? I anticipate three misconceptions: that this is simply Gödel's second incompleteness theorem applied to agents, that the problem is merely computational intractability, and that the agent could escape by weakening its trust in a single isolated belief; at exactly which points in the sequence should each be intercepted, and with what distinguishing move? What single check-question at the end would reveal whether they understand the obstacle as a constraint on rational self-trust rather than as a technical result in formal logic? Finally, what should I explicitly mark as out of scope—perhaps the full modal derivation, or applications to artificial general intelligence—and how should I frame that omission so it reads as a deliberate boundary rather than a missing piece?
