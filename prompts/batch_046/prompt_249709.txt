At a study group of longtime rationalist-blog readers, two members—Maya and Jonah—argued over several rounds about whether climate and pathogens should be treated as “historical actors” in the sixth-century Mediterranean trade collapse. Maya said that calling the 536 CE volcanic winter and the subsequent Justinian Plague “actors” is environmental determinism: it strips merchants, bureaucrats, and generals of causal efficacy and anthropomorphizes nature by implying intention. Jonah replied that “actor” in historiography is just shorthand for “difference-maker,” not an intentional agent, and that refusing climate and germs causal parity amounts to “humanist exceptionalism” that distorts the record—without the 536 aerosol veil and plague mortality, the tax-base contraction wouldn’t have occurred, regardless of Byzantine policy. Maya answered that if human mediation is always present, climate never acts alone, so it should be classified as a “background condition” rather than an actor; she proposed a distinction between “foreground causation” (entities that can respond to reasons) and “background causation” (everything else), and noted that Whig-style narratives make the mirror-image error with institutions, treating them as inevitable progress rather than contingent choices. Jonah said Maya’s distinction merely renames determinism in reverse: by relegating nature to background, she makes human choice the only real cause, which is equally one-sided. He introduced “causal democracy”—the view that causes should be weighed by counterfactual importance, not ontological category. Maya countered that “causal democracy” collapses moral evaluation: if a volcanic winter is an “actor” on par with a tax collector, then blaming the state for famine requires also “blaming” the volcano, which is absurd. Jonah said moral evaluation targets agents who could have done otherwise, not causes that made a difference; the two domains are orthogonal, not competitive.
