By late 1989 the DOE review panel issues this broadly negative assessment and the claim gets pushed to the fringe. The Utah team never really recants.

What I want to do is analyze this through conciliationism versus steadfastness, but I'm injecting a framing I'm calling 'mediated peerhood.' The idea is that in modern experimental science peers rarely share direct observational access to the phenomenon; they share protocols, graphs, and data files, but the actual bench conditions — palladium loading ratios, calorimetry calibraton, even ambient temperature controls — are institutionally specific. So when the MIT physicists and the Utah chemists disagree about whether fusion happened, they're peers in general competence and intelligence, but not in sensory or instrumental access to the same event. That asymmetry seems like it should mess with the standard conciliationist demand that peers split the difference.
