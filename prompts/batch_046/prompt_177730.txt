You are a professional philosopher specialising in political ethics and transitional justice. Before you begin, please take a few minutes to brainstorm the different kinds of philosophical disagreement that can hide behind apparently shared vocabulary, and then think carefully step by step.

The debate over truth commissions, amnesty provisions, and transitional justice — drawing on South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Argentina’s Full Stop and Due Obedience laws, and Rwanda’s gacaca courts — has been stalled for decades. One side defends amnesty as a necessary political bargain to secure democratic transition; the other condemns it as a betrayal of victims and a corruption of the rule of law. Both sides accept the same empirical record of abuses, and both agree that suffering matters and that justice is important. Yet neither side has persuaded the other.

My hypothesis is that the stalemate is not mainly empirical. Instead, it may conceal a conceptual disagreement about whether reconciliation is a relational good that requires direct victim participation, or whether it can be achieved institutionally by a state that was not itself the primary victim. There may also be a deeper clash about whether collective political entities can possess the moral authority to forgive, or whether forgiveness is strictly interpersonal and therefore cannot be delegated to a commission or a legislature.

Please diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, or a case of bedrock intuitions? Do the sides differ about what counts as a moral reason, about hidden empirical predictions regarding long-term democratic stability, or about the appropriate normative framework — restorative, retributive, or strategic? Refer concretely to what proponents of amnesty and opponents actually claim, not to a generic survey. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a reframing, an empirical finding, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Write your response in British English.
