I am preparing for a workshop presentation in philosophy of law where I defend a hybrid institutional account of punishment. I accept Gregg Caruso's argument that basic moral desert, construed as an intrinsic metaphysical property that makes suffering fitting, is untenable under a naturalistic conception of agency, and I grant that his public-health-quarantine model correctly identifies the risk-management function of incarceration. At the same time, I maintain that Daniel Dennett's evolutionary-compatibilist framework for moral responsibility offers normative resources for retaining desert-sensitive institutional practices—specifically proportionality constraints in sentencing and expressive practices of condemnation—without committing to libertarian free will. On my view, these practices do not track metaphysical desert; rather, they maintain the intentional stance toward agents and preserve the gradient of culpability recognition that liberal legal orders require for coordination and normative stability. Thus punishment retains a non-consequentialist structure, but its justification is functional and stance-dependent rather than retributive in the classic sense.

The audience will consist of roughly fifteen scholars: primarily criminal-law theorists with legal-moralist sympathies, several of whom have published on proportionality as a constitutional or doctrinal constraint, together with two political philosophers who regard state legitimacy and social-contract reasoning as the sole grounds for penal authority. They are largely indifferent to metaphysical debates about determinism but deeply concerned with institutional design, judicial reasoning, and whether a theory collapses punishment into mere social engineering. They will not ask about quantum indeterminacy or evolutionary psychology in the abstract; they will press me on whether judges have any non-instrumental reason to respect culpability gradations, whether my view can sustain the expressive distinction between punishment and treatment, and whether I have smuggled retributivism back in through institutional language.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. Order the objections by the damage they would do to my position, phrased precisely as these audience members would put them—using the vocabulary of legal doctrine, state legitimacy, and institutional function rather than metaphysics. For each, indicate what I should concede immediately, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Provide a reply I can deliver in approximately two minutes. Then identify the single question I am hoping nobody asks—likely the tension between denying metaphysical desert and still instructing judges to calibrate sentences to culpability—and explain exactly where the reply to that question is soft. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; I need arguments and strategic notes, not references.
