I'm preparing a proposal for a philosophy-of-science workshop focused on conceptual foundations, and I need to sharpen a half-formed idea into a precise, tractable research plan with a clear failure condition. The idea concerns the Church-Turing thesis and its epistemic status: standard treatments present it as either an empirical claim about what physical devices can compute or a definitional claim about effective calculability, but neither classification captures how the thesis actually functions in practice. If it is purely empirical, then unconventional computing architectures—quantum error-corrected circuits, analog neural substrates—ought to threaten it directly, yet computer scientists typically absorb such architectures into an expanded but stable framework rather than treating them as falsifications. If it is purely definitional, then its explanatory role in accounting for why particular physical mechanisms realize particular abstract functions becomes mysterious, since stipulations do not explain contingent alignments.

My proposed angle is to treat the Church-Turing thesis not as falling on either side of that divide, but as operating analogously to a Kantian regulative principle: a framework that structures the domain of computability without being either empirically falsifiable or analytically empty. The precise research question is whether this regulative interpretation can account for both the normative authority the thesis holds in computer science and its resistance to counterexamples drawn from non-standard computing models, without collapsing into conventionalism. To pursue this, I would spend the first two months reconstructing Church's original formulation and Turing's classic argument to determine whether they implicitly rely on such a framing rather than on a straightforward empirical claim. Months three through five would examine recent debates about quantum computation and analog neural computation, analyzing whether these are genuinely advanced as falsifying the thesis or rather as extending the class of physically realizable functions within a conceptually stable boundary. Months six and seven would develop the regulative interpretation formally, distinguishing it from nearby conventionalist and natural-kind accounts, and testing whether it can explain why the thesis retains disciplinary authority even when its physical instantiations are questioned. The remaining time would be reserved for writing and revision.

For the proposal to succeed, it must yield a coherent third category for the thesis's epistemic status—one that is genuinely distinct from the empirical-definitional dichotomy—together with a clear criterion for when a physical computing model challenges computation theory itself versus when it merely operates within an expanded but conceptually stable framework. The project would count as failing if the regulative interpretation collapses into either a disguised empirical claim, leaving it vulnerable to the very counterexamples it seeks to accommodate, or into a purely stipulative definition that forfeits all explanatory power. Given the one-year scope, I do not aim to settle every debate about hypercomputation, but rather to establish whether the regulative framing offers a defensible and distinct position in the philosophy of computation.
