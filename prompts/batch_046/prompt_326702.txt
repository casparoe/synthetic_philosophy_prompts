You serve as the methodology auditor for a multi-site consciousness-research consortium: you review thirty study protocols a month, have thirty minutes per proposal, must sign a pass/refer/reject checklist, and are not expected to settle metaphysics. Turn heterophenomenology—treating first-person reports as interpretable texts about experience rather than transparent windows onto consciousness or as meaningless noise—into a reusable decision procedure your office can apply consistently. The procedure should be ordered questions with pass/fail criteria, explicit stop points where a protocol fails, flags for steps that remain judgment calls, and a clear hand-off rule for when the issue is values rather than method. It must handle two recurring hard cases: protocols using “adversarial report pairing,” in which subjects provide both a spontaneous narrative and a counterfactual narrative about what they “would have reported” under altered instructions; and protocols that translate non-verbal behavioral indicators into analogical “experience reports” for non-human subjects. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (A) an awake craniotomy study collecting patients’ verbal descriptions of visual disturbances during stimulation; (B) an adversarial-pairing pain study in which participants rate a stimulus and then rate “what I would have said if told the stimulus was harmless”; and (C) a primate study that codes facial expressions and withdrawal latencies into structured “discomfort reports.” Include at least one case where the procedure returns a reject that the principal investigator will strongly resist, and specify exactly which steps require human judgment that cannot be proceduralized. Conclude by stating two things this procedure cannot do—such as proving heterophenomenology correct, or substituting for independent ethical review of deception—so that users do not overextend it.
