I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has enrolled in an adult-education programme in philosophy of mind at my local university. Last week, in a seminar on personal identity and technological replacement, a fellow student—who works in computational neuroscience—invoked the slogan "the pattern is the person, not the carbon" to defend a very specific scenario: a gradual, decade-long substitution of biological neurons by synthetic amorphous-carbon lattice nodes, with stochastic-resonance mapping preserving every functional connection and no discrete "jump" at any stage. He treated the phrase as if it settled the matter, concluding that the original subject necessarily survives such a procedure. I feel that the slogan is being made to carry several different burdens at once, but I lack the philosophical vocabulary to separate them. Could you disambiguate the precise claims hidden in that one sentence, presenting them as a menu of distinct readings rather than selecting a single interpretation?

For each reading, please indicate whether it expresses something near-tautological, a defensible thesis about psychological continuity, a substantive metaphysical commitment to substrate-independent identity, or an empirical claim about phenomenology that might be false. I am particularly interested in knowing which of these interpretations actually warrants the inference that this particular gradual synthetic substitution—where behaviour and functional architecture remain continuous—guarantees the survival of the self, and which interpretations merely shift the discussion to behavioural resemblance without addressing identity at all. The context matters because in our seminar the slogan was used specifically to block the objection that a synthetic copy, however accurate, is merely a duplicate; I want to bring a clear map of its possible meanings back to the discussion so we can see whether any of them genuinely answers that objection.
