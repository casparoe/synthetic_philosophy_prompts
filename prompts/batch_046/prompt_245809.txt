I hold the view that heritability coefficients—narrow-sense h² and broad-sense H²—are routinely misinterpreted as measures of fixed biological destiny, an error that becomes especially visible in non-canonical applications such as quantitative-genetic studies of clonal decapod lineages, including parthenogenetic crayfish, where separating additive genetic variance from maternal effects and social transmission is conceptually unstable. More generally, I claim that heritability is a population-level statistical descriptor, not a causal property of individual traits, and therefore cannot by itself license claims about intervention, malleability, or evolutionary inevitability. What would genuinely count against this skeptical framing? I want the decisive evidence and arguments—empirical studies in which heritability estimates remain predictive across radically different environments and mating structures; formal results showing that gene-environment interaction terms can be cleanly isolated from culturally transmitted variance in animal models; and honest concessions from defenders of standard quantitative genetics about whether the claim that heritability does not determine destiny has been qualified into unfalsifiability, so that every counterexample can be absorbed by appealing to hidden environmental variance. Please distinguish fatal objections from merely awkward ones, assess whether insulating the view in this way is intellectually defensible for a statistical claim of this kind, and include what defenders of the standard interpretation would themselves accept as damaging.
