I am preparing a problem set for a graduate seminar on philosophy of gender and distributive justice, and I have constructed an argument that proves far more than I am willing to accept. Its empirical premise is the gender-equality paradox: in societies with stronger institutional gender equality—wider access to education, parental leave, anti-discrimination enforcement, and economic redistribution—occupational gender segregation is often more pronounced rather than diminished, especially in fields such as nursing, engineering, and early childhood education. From this I have articulated what I am calling the reversibility thesis, which drives the rest of the reasoning. (1) If structural barriers and socialization were the primary causes of gendered career patterns, then removing those barriers should reduce gender divergence in occupational choice. (2) Instead, divergence increases after barrier removal. (3) Therefore, the divergence was never caused by the barriers; rather, the barriers suppressed underlying preferences or dispositions that become visible only when constraints are lifted—this is the reversibility thesis. (4) If a group difference grows when constraints are removed, that difference reflects relatively stable underlying dispositions rather than socially constructed patterns. (5) Hence gendered career choice in highly egalitarian societies expresses relatively unmediated underlying dispositions. (6) Justice should accommodate, rather than correct through parity targets, differences that express stable underlying dispositions. (7) By parity of reasoning, if any intervention that removes constraints on group X and group Y increases their divergence in domain D, then divergence in D expresses stable dispositions of X and Y and should be accommodated. (8) So equality of opportunity is self-defeating: successful equalization reveals "natural" differences that justice then requires us to preserve. (9) But no plausible theory of justice should treat every post-intervention divergence as a mandate for differential treatment—this general result is absurd. (10) Therefore at least one premise in (1)–(8) is false.

I want to keep (1) and (2) as describing the paradox, and I want to reject both (5) and the normative slide in (6)–(8), but I am unsure exactly where the inference breaks. Which step should I reject—probably (3) or (4)—and why did it initially seem sound? Please identify the weakest link, explain precisely how it trades on an ambiguity between suppressed preference and revealed preference, and then lay out what the standard philosophical responses must pay: for instance, positions that emphasize interaction effects between environment and preference, positions that distinguish autonomous choice from constrained revealed preference, and positions that defend equality of opportunity independently of outcome patterns. I am especially interested in whether one can deny the reversibility thesis without denying that the paradox is real and that purely structural explanations are inadequate.
