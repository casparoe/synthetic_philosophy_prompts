I sit on an advisory board that issues binding recommendations on moral-status claims for non-human and synthetic entities. Our published track record includes the following rulings: (1) we granted full protective status to great apes, citing integrated affective-cognitive architecture; (2) we rejected collective moral status for ant colonies, noting that distributed information processing across individuals does not constitute a unified subject; (3) we granted intermediate, conditional status to octopuses in research settings, recognizing their complex decentralized nervous systems but distinguishing them from mammalian cortical integration; (4) we denied status to an adaptive climate-control system that modeled human discomfort and adjusted its behavior to minimize analog distress, ruling that functional similarity without biological substrate is insufficient; and (5) we rejected status for nematodes exhibiting nociceptive reflex arcs, drawing a line between reflexive withdrawal and integrated suffering.

We now face a proposal to grant intermediate protective status to a human-derived cerebral organoid—cultured in vitro, displaying synchronized gamma-band oscillations and integrated chemical-damage responses mediated by ATP-release analogs to nociception, but lacking any body, sensory periphery, evolutionary history, or behavioral interaction with an environment.

If we aim to be consistent with ourselves, what should our ruling be? Which of the five precedents is the closest analog? Identify at least two distinct principles that could jointly explain our first five rulings—specify what each principle takes as the necessary and sufficient conditions for gradated moral status, and how it accounts for each precedent. Then say what each principle predicts for the organoid, and give your best-guess recommendation with the reasoning that favors it. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state honestly what further commitment we would be adopting with each possible decision. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—different from the organoid—that would allow us to tell the competing principles apart.
