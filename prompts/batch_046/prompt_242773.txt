If I am advising a small research group with one year of funding on the conceptual foundations of corruption, how should we allocate philosophical effort among three intertwined problems? First, whether corruption is best defined as deviation from formal institutional roles or as breach of locally legitimate social obligations — a distinction that becomes acute when clientelism and gift-exchange are structurally embedded rather than deviant. Second, whether one can draw a principled line between “lubrication” gifts that sustain access within reciprocal networks and transfers that genuinely distort discretionary judgment, without smuggling in a Weberian state ideal that may not apply to the institutional context in question. Third, whether exporting Western transparency metrics to societies with embedded patronage risks conceptual imperialism, or whether it correctly identifies harms that persist regardless of local normative framing. Rank these by conceptual urgency: which clarification is upstream of the others, which is most tractable through philosophical analysis rather than empirical fieldwork, and which, if settled, would do the least to reorder downstream policy ethics? Defend the ordering with explicit criteria — dependency, tractability, and downstream impact — and indicate what kind of argument or conceptual distinction over the next year could change your priorities.
