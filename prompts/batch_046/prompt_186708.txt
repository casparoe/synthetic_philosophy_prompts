I have recently been drawn into two apparently unrelated legal questions that now seem to me to share a hidden philosophical structure. On one side, I am helping a family member navigate a psychiatric advance directive—what clinicians sometimes call a Ulysses contract—in which a presently competent patient can authorize involuntary treatment during a future episode of psychosis, even though that future self will vehemently resist it. On the other side, I have been following constitutional debates in my jurisdiction, where the prevailing doctrine insists that no legislature can permanently bind its successors: constitutional entrenchment is rejected, and every democratic generation retains full authority to revise the settlement.

The institutional pattern is therefore this: the law permits an individual to bind a future version of herself, but refuses to let one democratic generation bind the next. What philosophical commitments does this asymmetry presuppose? Must it rest on a particular view of personal identity over time—perhaps that mental illness introduces a discontinuity in the self that democratic succession does not? Or can the difference be explained without any metaphysics of the self, for instance by a harm-based or procedural rationale that applies differently in clinical and political contexts?

More precisely, I want to understand whether the practice strictly requires what one might call a "fragmented-agent" picture, according to which the psychotic future self falls outside the normative jurisdiction of the present self, while future citizens remain within the continuous jurisdiction of the present polity. Is that distinction coherent, or is the asymmetry philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories of autonomy, democratic legitimacy, and intertemporal binding? And if the implicit theory were replaced—say, by a view that treats both mental episodes and generational turnover as continuous with their predecessors—what exactly in the institutional design would have to change? Would psychiatric Ulysses contracts collapse, or could they be reconstructed on different grounds? What would a unified account of binding authority across personal and collective time look like, and does the current practice quietly rule one out?
