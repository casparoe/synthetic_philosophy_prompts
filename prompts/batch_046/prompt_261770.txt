Hey, I've been trying to wrap my head around why philosophers say consciousness is uniquely hard to explain. There's this pattern I keep noticing but I don't have a word for. First instance: a philosopher argues that physical science can't explain subjective experience becuase we can clearly conceive of a being that behaves exactly like me but has no inner life at all—no subjective feel—so there's an explanitory gap. Then later, when asked why we should trust that intuition of conceivability, they say it's becuase we have direct, first-person access to our own experience, which makes the gap obvious. So the gap is used to justify the conceivability argument, and the direct access is used to justify the gap.

Second instance: in meta-problem discussions, people say the reason we think there's a hard problem is that consciousness is phenominally transparent—we see right through our experiences to their content. But then they turn around and say that this very transparency is what makes consciousness resist functional analysis, since any functional story leaves out the transparent feeling. Again it's the same feature doing double duty: establishing that there's a special problem, and then explaining why that problem can't be solved the usual way.

So the pattern is taking some feature of how we think about consciousness—direct access, transparency, conceivability—using it to establish an explanatory gap, then using that gap to explain why the feature feels mysterious. It's not just circular reasoning, I think, becuase each step sounds okay on its own. Is there an established name for this kind of double-duty reasoning in consciousness studies? If not, how would you distinguish it from ordinary circularity, and is there a closer concept—maybe something like epistemic bootstrapping—that captures it? I don't need just a label; I want to know exactly what the move is and where its edges are.
