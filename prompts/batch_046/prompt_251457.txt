In debates about weakness of will, a frequent move is to treat akrasia as hyperbolic discounting turned inward: the present self discounts the welfare of its future self as steeply as it would discount a stranger's, producing preference reversals that look like a failure of will but are actually consistent intertemporal choice. Proponents use this to dissolve the paradox of knowingly doing what is worse—there is no mysterious faculty that breaks, only a correctable discount parameter—and to license solutions such as precommitment or Ulysses contracts. Audit the analogy. Provide an explicit correspondence table: what corresponds to the agent, the discount curve, the precommitment mechanism, and the normative judgment that the delayed course is better? Then mark the disanalogies, especially whether the akratic agent genuinely prefers the immediate option at the moment of choice (as the hyperbolic model predicts) or continues to judge it worse while acting. Note also whether the analogy smuggles in a multiple-selves ontology that a unitary account of agency rejects. Finally, assess whether the conclusion—that weakness of will is reducible to a discounting problem and needs no distinct theory of will—survives once these breaks are visible, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument.
