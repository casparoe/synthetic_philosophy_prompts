Recently, in trying to organize supply logistics for a small cooperative grocery I help run, I kept returning to an analogy that dominates discussions of economic planning: the price system is like a telecommunications network—prices are signals that transmit dispersed knowledge about scarcity and preference across the economy, just as telegraph wires or neural pathways transmit impulses from peripheral sensors without requiring a center to observe every local condition directly. Hayek and his successors use this to argue that central planning is structurally impossible, because replacing the network with a planning bureau is like replacing every local nerve ending with one brain cell that must feel every touch itself. I want to test whether this analogy actually carries the weight it is asked to carry.

Please audit it systematically. First, give an explicit correspondence table: in the source domain (a telecommunications or neural signaling system), what corresponds to local producers, to consumers, to scarcity, to the price itself, to the "center" that receives the signal, and to noise or signal degradation? Then identify the disanalogies that matter specifically for the anti-planning conclusion: does the market network actually have a unified purpose like an organism's nervous system does? Are prices genuinely analogous to neutral electrical impulses, or do they carry distributive and strategic content that signals do not? Where does the mapping break on questions of feedback loops, error correction, and the possibility of centralized computation aided by modern information technology? In particular, consider that contemporary platform economies use algorithmic pricing computed in centralized server farms—does this development weaken the analogy's claim that distributed computation is irreplaceable?

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that socialist calculation is impossible survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work all along. If there is a better analogy for the relationship between dispersed knowledge and economic coordination—perhaps one drawn from distributed computing or from ecological signaling—suggest it and explain what it changes.
