I am drafting an essay for a postgraduate seminar on conceptual history and moral philosophy, prompted by the recent discovery of my late grandfather’s administrative correspondence from the wartime occupation bureaucracy in Eastern Europe. Rather than offering a conventional historical narrative, I want to test whether Zygmunt Bauman’s argument in Modernity and the Holocaust—that industrial-scale genocide emerged not as an aberration but as a possibility inscribed within modern bureaucratic rationality—undermines or reinforces claims about the Holocaust’s uniqueness, a tension that lies at the heart of the Historikerstreit. To avoid the familiar binary of uniqueness versus comparability, I propose a three-part conceptual distinction: uniqueness of moral status, uniqueness of causal architecture, and uniqueness of phenomenological experience for the victims. I argue that Bauman’s analysis demonstrates uniqueness at the level of causal architecture—specifically, the unprecedented density of railway logistics, census categorisation, and paperwork that separated decision-makers from suffering—while simultaneously rendering the event structurally comparable to other modern bureaucratic catastrophes, such as the administrative logistics of famine management during the Great Leap Forward or in colonial Bengal. I further suggest that the debate has been miscast because participants conflate structural comparability with moral incommensurability, and I tentatively advance the notion of “administrative distance” not as a universal feature of modernity, but as a historically specific configuration that reached an extreme form in this case. I am concerned that my personal reaction to my grandfather’s detached, procedural language is colouring my interpretation of moral distance, and I worry that importing famine-administration logistics as a comparative framework may dilute rather than sharpen the conceptual point. Could you comment on the philosophical coherence of this three-part distinction, on whether my framing of Bauman and the uniqueness debate is accurate, on whether the comparative reference to bureaucratic famine logistics strengthens the argument or introduces an inappropriate analogy, and on which sections risk conflating conceptual clarification with biographical projection? Please reply in British English, and indicate where the essay needs further conceptual refinement rather than additional empirical detail.
