After recently facing a decision in which I undertook a severe personal sacrifice—abandoning a secure professional path to provide long-term care for a family member whose needs exceeded what anyone considered obligatory—I have come to defend a particular conception of supererogation. I maintain that the category is coherent only if we reject the premise that moral reasons are always decisive in the sense of generating all-things-considered obligations. Rather, I propose that there exist conclusive practical reasons that strongly favor a burdensome course of action without making the less burdensome, permissible alternative wrong; in these cases, the agent retains a legitimate entitlement to prioritize her own permissible interests, even when the balance of reasons favors self-sacrifice. Supererogation, on this view, marks not a failure of obligation but a structural space in practical reason where reasons are weighty yet not authoritative. What would genuinely count against this position? I ask for the evidence, arguments, or formal results that should move me, arranged by how decisive they are, including: whether this distinction between weight and authority collapses into a problematic dualism of practical reason; whether the view can accommodate the intuition that failing to rescue a stranger at trivial cost is not merely suberogatory but genuinely wrong; whether it risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—such that any apparent counterexample is dismissed by claiming the reasons were insufficiently weighty—and if so, whether that insulation is a vice rather than a virtue for a moral theory. Please also distinguish what defenders of this view would themselves concede as damaging, not only what critics allege, and conclude with an honest assessment of whether the position remains exposed or has been rendered immune to defeat.
