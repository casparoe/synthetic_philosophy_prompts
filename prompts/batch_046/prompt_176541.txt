I am a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science in earnest. In a recent popular essay, an unnamed author summarizes the well-known criticisms of philosophy advanced by Steven Weinberg, Stephen Hawking, and Richard Feynman. The summary runs as follows: all three figures agree that philosophy is retrospectively descriptive rather than prospectively generative; Weinberg is said to have argued that philosophy offers no practical assistance to working physicists, Hawking is said to have pronounced philosophy dead for failing to keep pace with modern physics, and Feynman is said to have likened philosophers to ornithologists describing birds that can already fly without their help. The author then claims that philosophers typically defend their discipline by citing conceptual clarifications—such as the interpretation of quantum nonlocality, the analysis of probability in statistical mechanics, or the ontology of fields—but that these are merely post-hoc tidying exercises that never yielded a novel experimental prediction. From this, the author concludes that philosophy is at best decorative: it may rename distinctions physicists have already made, but it cannot contribute to scientific discovery, and therefore Weinberg's more modest claim that philosophy is useless to the practicing scientist extends naturally to the stronger claim that philosophy is useless to science altogether.

Before I accept this verdict, I want to check whether the summary is fair. Please audit it claim by claim: which parts accurately represent the respective scientists' positions, which parts conflate distinct criticisms into a single argument, and where does the summary misrepresent the philosophers' counter-arguments? In particular, I am struck by the author's implicit criterion—that philosophical contribution must take the form of a novel experimental prediction. Is this criterion actually present in Weinberg's writing, or has the author projected it onto him? And does the distinction between heuristic utility for the working scientist and architectonic utility in shaping the conceptual framework of a theory survive the author's critique, or is it a distinction without a difference? Please treat the strongest version of the philosophers' response fairly, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I seek only a conceptual audit I can act on without access to a library.
