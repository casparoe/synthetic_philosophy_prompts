I am a doctoral researcher in the philosophy of probability and anthropic reasoning, and I have been developing a somewhat unorthodox framing of the self-locating belief problem that arises in so-called doomsday-style arguments. Rather than rehearsing the familiar standard formulation, I want you to construct a rigorous conceptual argument for a specific, counter-intuitive position. Assume that the relevant reference class is not the set of all humans, nor all observers capable of philosophical reasoning, but rather the subset of observers who have reached a precise epistemic stage: they have already recognised the reference-class problem for their own kind, but have not yet developed reliable institutional mechanisms for long-range demographic forecasting. Call this the "intermediate formulation window."

Argue that, within this narrowly circumscribed reference class, the usual selection effect is reversed. The claim should be that finding ourselves inside this window is more probable if human civilisation persists for millennia—thereby generating many successive cohorts of observers who each briefly inhabit this window—than if extinction occurs shortly after the first cohort reaches it. In other words, defend the thesis that for this particular reference class, anthropic reasoning supports extended survival rather than near-term extinction. Your argument should turn on the temporal distribution of epistemic maturity across generations, not on empirical predictions about technology, climate, or policy. Please present your answer in American English, despite the British English of this request. I do not need bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the point is conceptual precision rather than scholarly apparatus.
