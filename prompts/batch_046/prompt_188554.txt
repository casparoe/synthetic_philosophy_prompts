In the debate over whether compatriots possess special moral claims against cosmopolitan equality, several unresolved tensions persist: the relative weight of associative obligations versus universal distributive duties; whether patriotic partiality functions as a virtue or as a corruption of moral judgement; and how institutions ought to adjudicate conflicts between domestic justice and global relief. Rather than survey these, I ask you to set an agenda. Imagine a research group with five years of work, tasked with producing actionable guidance for constitutional courts in multinational federations that must decide whether citizens may be prioritised for climate-adaptation funding over equally vulnerable non-citizens. Within this framing, evaluate three lines of inquiry. First, whether associative duties arise solely from co-authorship of state institutions, or whether they can also be generated by non-territorial networks — such as diasporic communities or shared digital infrastructures that create welfare dependencies without sovereign boundaries. Second, whether the justification for partiality is conditional upon state legitimacy: if a state fails global justice thresholds, does compatriot priority collapse, or does it remain intact? Third, whether empirical findings about motivational crowding — that framing aid in cosmopolitan terms can erode domestic support — should shape normative theory, or whether they are merely pragmatic constraints on implementation. Rank these three lines by which settlement would be most upstream, restructuring the greatest number of downstream institutional and philosophical questions; defend your ordering with explicit criteria (dependency, tractability, and practical impact on constitutional design); and identify which line you would deprioritise on the grounds that resolving it would change little in practice. Do not supply a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; reason from the conceptual relations themselves.
