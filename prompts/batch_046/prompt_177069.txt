I am preparing a seventy-five-minute session for an upper-level philosophy seminar—twelve students, all familiar with basic propositional logic but with no prior exposure to proof-theoretic semantics—centered on Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise. Rather than lecturing on the regress problem, I want the students to encounter it physically and only recognize the structure afterwards. Design a runnable in-class activity with clear roles, turn-based rules, and a non-monetary payoff structure: pairs receive a simple valid syllogism (for instance, a chain of three premises yielding a conclusion via modus ponens and universal instantiation) and must defend each inferential step aloud. The rule is that whenever one student applies an inference rule, the partner may challenge her to state that rule as an explicit premise; if she does, the partner may then demand justification for that newly added premise, which can itself invoke another rule, and so on. The physical layout should make the growing list of premises visible—perhaps index cards placed in a line—so that by minute twenty-five most pairs are either looping, stalling, or quietly smuggling in an unstated assumption.

Please structure the activity in three phases—enactment (twenty-five minutes), comparison across pairs (ten minutes), and debrief (forty minutes)—and specify exactly what materials are needed, what predictable failure mode occurs the first time (likely a student inventing an ad hoc exception to avoid the loop), and the precise rule-patch that closes the loophole without dissolving the pedagogical tension. In the debrief, map the three typical stopping points—abandoning the chain, accepting an ungrounded intuition, and treating the inference rule as constitutive of the premises rather than an additional premise—onto standard philosophical responses to Carroll's regress, but also integrate a less conventional option: the idea that deductive justification is a matter of the structural coherence of the entire practice rather than of linear premise-to-conclusion transmission. Be explicit about which normative feature of Carroll's original paradox—specifically the ought of logical acceptance that Achilles presses upon the Tortoise—the physical card-game cannot reproduce, and tell me how I should supply that missing element in the final ten minutes. Finally, include an honest note on which aspect of the paradox this activity necessarily flattens, and how to acknowledge that limitation without undermining the students' sense that they have genuinely run into the problem.
