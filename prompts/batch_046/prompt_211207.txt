I was recently trying to explain to a friend why I find it strange that we use the same word “fix” both for repairing a broken clock and for correcting a mistake in a manuscript, and she told me I was overthinking language. That conversation stayed with me, and I started noticing how often ideas get copied from one tiny domain of practice into another and quietly change their rules. I am particularly drawn to what might be called copycat concepts—ideas that migrate between microdomains and carry hidden assumptions with them, like when the notion of “cleaning” from household chores is copied into museum conservation and suddenly means “stabilizing” rather than removing dirt. I am not a philosophy student, just someone who reads widely and thinks about these patterns on my own, and I want to apply this idea to a work I have been puzzling over.

I have been rewatching the film Groundhog Day and wondering whether Phil Connors’s suffering comes partly from importing the wrong conceptual frameworks into a situation that is structurally cyclical rather than linear. The film is usually read as a redemption story or a study in personal growth, but I want to read it through this lens of conceptual slippage in microdomains of maintenance and repetition. Phil tries to treat the loop as a prison to escape, then as a playground for consumption, then as a workshop for self-improvement—each strategy seems to borrow a different concept of “progress” from a different practical domain (career advancement, leisure, craft apprenticeship) and apply it to a daily routine that never accumulates. Could you read the film through this framework? I want to know which scenes best dramatize this slippage—for instance, his attempts to “fix” his situation through romantic conquest versus through learning piano or ice sculpting—and whether the loop genuinely allows for a kind of transformation that is not just conceptual correction. At the same time, I want you to be honest about where the film resists the reading: is there a moment where Phil changes in a way that cannot be reduced to applying the right concept to the right domain? Please take both the narrative logic of Groundhog Day and the idea of fluid, copycat concepts seriously, and end by saying what the film adds to this philosophical problem that abstract argument alone does not capture. I am not looking for an illustration of a theory, but for a genuine criticism that lets the work complicate the idea.
