At a graduate seminar on early modern political thought, two students—Rosa and Kai—became locked in a sustained disagreement about Machiavelli’s treatment of fortune and virtù in The Prince, especially in chapters XXV and XXVI. A bystander who heard the exchange reports it below and asks for an outside adjudication, having no stake in either side.

Rosa opened by stressing Machiavelli’s river metaphor: fortune is like a flood that can be partly channeled through dikes and embankments, yet never fully eliminated. She took this to mean that fortune is an exogenous source of shock or randomness, and that virtù functions essentially as risk-management technology—reducing the variance of outcomes without guaranteeing success. On her reading, the claim that fortune governs about half our actions is a statistical acknowledgment that irreducible contingency remains no matter how well-prepared the prince is.

Kai objected that this treats fortune as possessing independent causal power, which he thinks misreads the text. He pointed to Machiavelli’s claim that fortune displays her force mainly where virtue is not well ordered to resist her. For Kai, this implies that fortune is not a separate causal agent but a negative or residual category—the domain of affairs that falls outside the scope of effective human agency. He proposed interpreting the “half” claim not as a quantitative division of causal labor but as a modal claim: in roughly half of the worlds consistent with a given political situation, the prince lacks sufficient virtù to determine the outcome. Thus virtù does not “fight” fortune like an engineer fighting a flood; rather, it defines the conditions under which the concept of fortune does not apply.

Rosa replied that Kai’s interpretation renders fortune ontologically empty. If fortune is merely the absence of virtù, then Machiavelli’s insistence that she governs half our actions collapses into the tautology that we fail half the time. Moreover, she noted, Machiavelli describes fortune as a woman who must be beaten and subdued by boldness—a clearly agentive, almost personified figure. If Kai is right, this rhetoric is pure anthropomorphic confusion, and Machiavelli’s advice to be impetuous and violent becomes inexplicable: one does not strike a modal absence.

Kai conceded the rhetorical force but denied its metaphysical weight. He argued that Machiavelli’s gendered language captures the phenomenology of decision-making under radical uncertainty: agents experience unknown risks as hostile, active forces precisely because human cognition tends to personify what it cannot predict or control. On this view, the advice to be bold is not about dominating an external agent but about adopting decision procedures—high variance, rapid adaptation—that perform well across the unknown state-space Rosa calls “fortune.” Kai further suggested that Rosa’s engineering model actually makes Machiavelli anachronistic, importing modern concepts of probabilistic risk management that depend on statistical regularities foreign to Machiavelli’s framework.

Rosa countered that Kai is not rescuing Machiavelli from anachronism but substituting a different one: turning him into a proto-theorist of robustness, where virtù is redefined as resilience across unknown states rather than the achievement of specific political outcomes. She argued that this strips away the heroic, almost tragic dimension of Machiavelli’s prince, who must wrestle with forces larger than himself to found or maintain a state. She then sharpened her position: the core issue is whether virtù is fundamentally instrumental—aimed at controlling outcomes in a contingent world—or constitutive—defining what it means to be an effective prince regardless of which outcomes actually occur.

Kai responded that this distinction is false for Machiavelli because the prince’s identity is constituted precisely by his capacity to impose form on contingency; there is no “prince” separate from the exercise of virtù in conditions of fortune. He added that if Rosa insists on treating fortune as an independent causal power, she owes an account of how two distinct causal domains—virtù and fortune—interact without reducing one to the other, and she must explain why Machiavelli assigns them roughly equal weight rather than treating fortune as mere noise.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Rosa’s and Kai’s interventions, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say which party is more right overall about Machiavelli’s conception of the relationship between fortune and virtù, and identify what—if anything—both are missing about how the text actually works. The deliverable is a point-by-point verdict on the arguments as actually made, including the conceptual innovations either side introduced; it is not a request for personal advice.
