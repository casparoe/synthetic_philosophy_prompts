I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, working on whether evolutionary functions can ground normative claims about what organisms ought to do without presupposing mental representation. At a recent workshop, a discussant blocked an evolutionary-normativity proposal by invoking the one-liner "there is no reason without a reasoner" — occasionally rendered as "reasons never float free." I want to understand exactly what work that slogan is doing. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might express, treating each as a separate reading? Specifically, distinguish at least: (a) a conceptual claim about the meaning of "reason" requiring an agent with representational capacities; (b) a metaphysical claim that normative properties are necessarily relational to some actual or possible agent; (c) a motivational claim that for a consideration to count as a reason for X, X must be capable of being moved by it; (d) a linguistic claim that reason-attributions implicitly index a subject. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive defensible position, or false — and note whether it would actually support blocking evolutionary explanations that treat selection pressures as providing objective rationales for organisms that lack minds. I am especially interested in which reading my interlocutor likely intended when deploying the slogan to end the argument, and whether that intended reading succeeds or collapses into one of the others. Please present this as a disambiguation menu rather than selecting a single interpretation.
