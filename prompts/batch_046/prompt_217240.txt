I am preparing a long-form feature for a science and ideas magazine on whether the formal machinery of strategic reasoning can survive once we take seriously that human agents are bounded, embodied, and institutionally situated. In reporting the piece, I have arrived at a cluster of philosophical commitments about rationality, belief, and agreement, but I am unsure whether they form a coherent package rather than an opportunistic collage. Here is where I stand: (1) I accept that common knowledge of rationality is a necessary condition for backward-induction solutions in finite games of perfect information, and I treat this result as a normative benchmark for ideally rational agents rather than as an empirical description. (2) At the same time, I maintain that any descriptively adequate model of belief formation should replace the infinite hierarchy of “knowing that you know” with a graded, resource-bounded structure—what I have been calling layered epistemic access—because real reasoners lack the memory and observational transparency required for full common knowledge. (3) I also want to hold that Aumann’s agreement result demonstrates that rational agents with common priors cannot rationally disagree, yet I think persistent disagreement is often rational precisely because priors are not shared but are constructed through indexical epistemic frames—different institutional positions that determine what counts as evidence—without those frames being open to rational revision within the strategic interaction itself. (4) Finally, I believe that evolutionary dynamics in belief networks supply the correct long-run standard for evaluating rational belief revision, which leads me to reject static consistency conditions (such as applying the sure-thing principle across epistemic states) in favor of convergence properties under selection pressure.

My concern is that these four positions may pull against one another in ways I have not fully traced. Specifically, I worry that (2) undermines the normative authority I grant to (1), that (3) introduces a form of rational disagreement that the shared-rationality assumption in (1) ought to rule out, and that (4) threatens to dissolve the axiomatic framework that makes both (1) and (3) intelligible. Yet I also suspect that at least one of these conflicts is only apparent—perhaps dissolving once we sharply distinguish normative benchmarks from modeling assumptions, or once we clarify whether evolutionary evaluation applies to belief-formation mechanisms rather than to individual doxastic states. I would like a careful audit: which commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs are only apparently incompatible, and what is the least costly revision—ranked by severity—if I wish to preserve the core intuition that rationality is structurally constrained yet empirically situated? Please be specific about which view has to give, what that costs conceptually, and whether any revision forces me to abandon the layered-epistemic-access framework or the indexical-frame account of prior divergence.
