I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology preparing remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop on functional ascription in synthetic and chimeric biological systems. During our discussion, a colleague raised the specific case of fecal microbiota transplantation, in which donor-derived bacteria establish a sustained metabolic causal role within the recipient’s gut despite lacking any shared evolutionary history with the recipient’s genome or its native selective environment. Rather than treating this as a straightforward victory for causal-role theories over etiological ones, I want to defend the more nuanced position that etiological accounts should be expanded to accommodate “borrowed” or “displaced” selection histories—where the evolutionary past of the donor lineage continues to ground functional norms inside the recipient—without requiring that the functional trait’s history be strictly bound to the host lineage. Could you please construct this argument for me? Present your response as exactly five numbered points, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding remarks.
