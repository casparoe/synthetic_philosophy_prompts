I've been thinking about how people justify killing insects, and I've noticed a strange pattern that keeps coming up, though I don't know whether philosophers have given it a specific name. Let me describe a few cases where I've seen it. When farmers spray pesticides, people usually defend it by saying the insects are destroying crops—so the killing is explained by practical damage to human interests. But when someone asks whether it's wrong to cause that many deaths, the same people often reply that insects are too simple to feel anything—so the moral concern is dismissed by appeal to what the insects are like inside. The reason for acting and the reason for not feeling guilty seem to come from two different places, and they never have to meet. I see something similar with mosquitoes and bees. Mosquitoes are killed because they carry disease, but people also say they don't matter because they're pests; bees are protected because they help plants grow, yet people rarely ask whether bees suffer more or less than mosquitoes. Even in my own home, I kill ants because they're in my kitchen, telling myself it's about hygiene, but when I feel a brief guilt, I quiet it by thinking ants are just little reflex machines without real feelings. Is there an established concept for this kind of split reasoning—where one framework justifies the harm and a separate framework removes the moral weight, with neither framework actually addressing the other? Or is it simply ordinary hypocrisy? I'm also curious about its boundaries: does it only apply to creatures we think of as pests, or does it show up in how people talk about other animals that cause problems but might still suffer?
