As a novelist whose fiction has long been preoccupied with characters who appear to hide unwelcome truths from themselves, I have been invited to deliver the opening remarks at a three-day interdisciplinary workshop titled "The Unreliable Narrator Within," held for an audience split between philosophers of mind and cognitive psychologists on one hand, and fiction writers and literary critics on the other. I need you to compose these remarks in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write a formal letter, and they must be rendered in British English. The address should feel like an invitation to shared inquiry rather than a lecture, so please ensure that the writers in the room are not spoken down to, while the philosophers recognise that a serious conceptual problem is being engaged rather than merely decorated.

The remarks should turn structurally on the question of whether self-deception is genuinely possible, given that it seems to require one and the same subject both to know and not to know an identical proposition. To keep the discussion from collapsing into a well-rehearsed survey of familiar literature, I want you to introduce a distinction I have been developing in my recent novels between "reflexive concealment," in which a subject actively structures attention so that a threatening belief never fully forms, and "doxastic schism," in which contradictory beliefs persist in functionally isolated subsystems of cognition without being integrated by a central arbiter. Argue that only the second case merits the name self-deception, but that its possibility forces us to accept a partitioned model of mind that many philosophers reject; if we refuse that partition, then the literary figure of the self-deceiving character must be reinterpreted not as a mind concealing its own knowledge, but as a mind failing to synthesise what it already suspects. Reach a clear, justified conclusion—perhaps that self-deception is possible but far rarer and stranger than our narrative conventions suggest, with direct implications for how writers construct interiority. Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not refer to sources the audience should consult.
