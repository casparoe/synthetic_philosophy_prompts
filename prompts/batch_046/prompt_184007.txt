A philosopher working within the Strawsonian framework on moral responsibility has offered the following judgments about whether reactive attitudes—specifically resentment and moral anger—are appropriate responses to harm caused in different circumstances. In cases of ordinary adult wrongdoing with full knowledge and freedom, reactive attitudes are fully appropriate. In cases involving young children, they are inappropriate and should be replaced by the objective attitude. In cases of severe psychopathy characterized by a global absence of moral-emotional comprehension, reactive attitudes are inappropriate. In cases of temporary insanity or extreme duress that severely compromises rational agency, reactive attitudes are inappropriate or at least suspended. In cases of negligent harm caused by an otherwise competent adult who failed to attend to obvious risks, reactive attitudes remain appropriate though perhaps moderated. In cases of collective entities such as corporations or institutions, reactive attitudes are categorically inappropriate, since these entities lack the interpersonal emotional capacities required to be proper targets of resentment.

Now consider a new case: an adult with intact rational agency, full moral knowledge, and voluntary control over her actions has undergone a permanent, elective neurocognitive procedure that eliminates her capacity for second-personal emotional engagement. She can no longer feel resentment, understand it when directed at her, or participate in mutual moral recognition, but she retains complete instrumental and moral reasoning, can plan long-term, and deliberately chooses to cause serious harm to another person for personal gain. She understands that her action is wrong.

If one aims to be consistent with the philosopher's prior judgments, what should the verdict be? Identify the closest analog among the earlier cases, articulate at least two competing principles that could jointly explain the prior pattern (for instance, whether exemption requires loss of rational agency, absence of second-personal capacity, or exclusion from the moral community of mutual recognition), state what each principle predicts about this new case, offer your best guess about the actual verdict with reasons for preferring it, and specify one further case—perhaps involving temporary rather than permanent suppression of these capacities, or suppression achieved through disciplined practice rather than neurocognitive intervention—that would distinguish which principle is genuinely governing the judgments.
