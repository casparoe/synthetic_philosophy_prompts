I'm a senior software engineer who recently moved into platform-engineering management, and I have no formal training in economics or philosophy beyond podcasts and long dinner conversations. Last month, discussing with friends why our company keeps pouring quarters into failing initiatives, someone described the dollar auction—the bidding game where both winner and loser pay, so rational bids quickly exceed the prize value—and I realized our quarterly portfolio reviews have the same structural trap. Once we've sunk nine months of engineering time into a legacy migration, pulling out feels like "paying the loser's penalty," so leadership keeps bidding with more funding. I need a reusable decision procedure I can run in our standard 60-minute quarterly review with a cross-functional team (engineers, a product manager, and a finance partner with no PhD in economics). The procedure should translate what is actually settled about escalation of commitment and rational commitment revision into an ordered sequence of concrete tests or questions applied to any long-running internal project that is over budget or behind schedule. For each step, specify what counts as a passing or failing answer, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation—kill, restructure, or continue with binding conditions—and exactly where it must hand the decision back to human judgment because the issue is strategic value rather than escalation logic. It must handle hard cases specific to software: changed market conditions since kickoff, hidden dependency risks revealed only after partial investment, and withdrawal costs that include contractual or reputational "loser pays" penalties analogous to the auction mechanics.

Apply the procedure to three concrete proposals: (1) a database migration running nine months late with 70% of the schema converted but no clear completion path; (2) an experimental AI feature that consumed 40% of our quarterly compute budget with near-zero user adoption; and (3) a security overhaul for a legacy API that is technically necessary but politically unpopular with sales—this is the case where I personally want to save it, and I want the procedure to potentially deliver an answer I will not like. The deliverable must respect our operational constraints: we use only standard metrics (burn rate, milestone completion, dependency maps, user engagement data), the meeting lasts one hour, and the final recommendation requires my CTO's sign-off, so the procedure should distinguish outputs that are direct recommendations from outputs that are structured inputs to executive judgment. Finally, please clearly separate what is genuinely settled in the study of escalation and rational choice from what remains actively contested—particularly whether sunk-cost reasoning is always fallacious or can be rational under conditions of incomplete information, signaling, or organizational credibility—and do not present a genuinely disputed interpretation as the consensus view among people who work on this topic.
