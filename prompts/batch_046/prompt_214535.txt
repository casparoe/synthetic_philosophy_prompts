Consider the following argument, offered by a colleague to show that cross-cultural variation in moral judgment undermines moral objectivity altogether, but which strikes me as proving far more than it should—perhaps that no cross-cultural descriptive claim can be objective either, which I take to be false.

(1) Moral objectivity requires that moral truths hold independently of any particular cultural conceptual scheme and are in principle accessible to rational agents regardless of background.

(2) For a truth to be accessible across cultures, there must exist a shared conceptual framework in which it can be formulated without loss or distortion.

(3) Empirical work in moral psychology demonstrates that basic moral cognition is organized around incommensurable frameworks—autonomy-based, community-based, and divinity-based ethics—such that thick concepts like dignity, purity, or hierarchical harmony lack direct equivalents across frameworks, and the same behavior (e.g., elder deference or resource pooling) is classified under different normative categories.

(4) Because these frameworks lack shared normative concepts, there is no neutral standpoint from which to formulate moral truths that apply across cultures.

(5) Therefore, moral objectivity fails: there are no culture-independent moral facts.

(6) But the very same empirical literature shows that descriptive categorization—what counts as an intentional agent, a causal relation, or a social group—also varies systematically across these frameworks.

(7) By parallel reasoning: if the absence of a shared conceptual scheme undermines objectivity in morality (steps 4–5), it must also undermine objectivity in descriptive ontology and social cognition (step 6).

(8) Yet we should not deny that there are objective descriptive facts about agents, causes, or social structures.

(9) So the argument proves too much.

I want to preserve the empirical claim in (3)—that the divergence is real, deep, and not reducible to ignorance or coercion—and I want to preserve (8). Tell me exactly which premise or inferential step is the weakest link, explain why it looked plausible (in particular, distinguish metaphysical independence from epistemic or linguistic accessibility if that is the crux), and then take a definite position rather than surveying options. Is the correct move to reject the claim that objectivity requires shared conceptual schemes, or to accept that descriptive objectivity is also scheme-relative but recoverable through translation? State clearly what your chosen repair costs for moral realism, for moral relativism, and for conceptual pluralism, and defend that price as worth paying.
