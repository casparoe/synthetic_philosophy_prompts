Draft the joint statement that two camps in philosophy of personhood could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Ubuntu-inspired relationalism. The first camp, "constitutive relationalists," holds that personhood is not an intrinsic property of an individual substrate but is constituted by standing in specific reciprocal recognition relations within a community; they hold that moral status can vary with relational embeddedness and that entities lacking such relations—whether through severe isolation, certain cognitive conditions, or non-biological architectures—do not possess full personhood. The second camp, "developmental relationalists," holds that individuals possess intrinsic capacities for personhood—such as reflexive self-awareness and moral agency—that exist independently of particular relations, though these capacities require relational contexts to develop and be sustained; they worry that constitutive relationalism risks denying full moral status to marginalized people who are socially excluded or to those with severe disabilities who are no longer actively recognized by their communities.

The deliverable should be structured in three parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: about what Ubuntu asserts regarding interdependence and dignity; about the formal distinction between relations that constitute a property and relations that are merely necessary conditions for its development or exercise; about the moral status of newborns, severely isolated adults, and individuals with profound cognitive disability; and about which kinds of phenomenological descriptions and social-scientific findings both camps accept as bearing on the dispute. Second, a disagreements table stating the precise formulations of residual disagreement, with each formulation phrased so that its own camp would accept the wording of its own view and recognize the other camp's view fairly; include at least the disagreement over whether relational density functions as a threshold condition for personhood or as a continuous gradient that modulates the expression of an independently existing status, and the disagreement over whether the capacity for reciprocal recognition must be actually exercised in present social practice or need only be available in principle. Third, the cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp, and for each crux present a concrete case or structured thought experiment both sides agree is a genuine test—such as a long-term institutionally isolated adult who retains reflexive self-awareness but lacks any sustained recognition by others, or an artificial system embedded in sustained reciprocal social practices but lacking biological consciousness and subjective experience—recording in advance each camp's explicit prediction about what the case demonstrates. Finally, state whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive—specifically, whether "personhood" operates with different intensions or extensions across the camps—and if so, identify exactly which portion and explain why the disagreement dissolves once that ambiguity is removed. Keep the tone of a document both parties could sign; do not adjudicate which camp is correct.
