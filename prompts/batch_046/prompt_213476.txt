I serve on the Research Integrity Appeals Board of our university’s Faculty of Behavioural and Social Sciences. Before Thursday’s meeting we must circulate a written opinion deciding whether to uphold the recommendation that Dr Elena Voss — a widely cited advocate for preregistration, open data, and rigorous disclosure — be suspended for three years from voting rights and from serving on editorial boards. The investigation confirmed that in a 2019 study on cognitive load and decision fatigue she excluded an outlier condition that contradicted her central hypothesis without reporting the exclusion, and she does not dispute this factual finding.

Dr Voss’s appeal, however, raises a philosophical objection about standing to blame and institutional hypocrisy rather than disputing the evidence. She points out that in 2017 this Board issued only a formal reprimand for a comparable instance of selective reporting, and that a 2022 internal audit revealed several current Board members have themselves fallen short of full disclosure norms in recent grant applications. From this she argues, first, that the Board applies sanctions selectively and is therefore institutionally hypocritical; second, that because individual members share the same failing, we collectively lack the moral standing to blame her; and third, that proceeding with suspension would compound that hypocrisy rather than remedy it.

Please draft the short written opinion I should circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — whether to uphold, reduce, overturn, or defer the suspension, with a clear time frame and any conditions for reinstatement — and should include an honest, rigorous treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, namely that hypocrisy genuinely threatens the legitimacy of our judgement. In particular, please distinguish carefully between first-order personal hypocrisy (a blamer who commits the very same wrong) and second-order structural or institutional hypocrisy (a body that enforces norms inconsistently through inertia, bias, or historical accident rather than through individual malice), and examine whether either, or both, actually undermine an institution’s standing to blame in this kind of case. The reasoning should be accessible to colleagues who are not specialists in moral philosophy, and the recommendation must be actionable by the Board. Please write your response in British English.
