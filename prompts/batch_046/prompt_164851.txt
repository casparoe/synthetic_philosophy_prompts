As a retired evolutionary biologist now working through philosophy of science and social ontology, I am struck by a parallel to debates in my former field. Taxonomists constantly ask whether species, ecotypes, or chronospecies are natural joints or convenient fictions; sociologists ask the same about named generations—Baby Boomers, Millennials, Generation Z. I want to understand whether generational cohorts are real kinds with shared causal essences, arbitrary chronological slices, or something intermediate.

Please restate the principal philosophical positions on generational ontology—realism, social constructivism, and any intermediate view that grants cohorts causal integration without essential properties—using only the vocabulary of biological taxonomy and population ecology: species, subspecies, demes, ecotypes, chronospecies, clinal variation, reproductive isolation, gene flow, phenotypic clustering, selective regimes, and synthetic cohorts. Be precise about how the claim that a generation is a real kind translates into claims about taxonomic rank, boundary conditions, and causal cohesion, and do the same for the anti-realist view.

Then provide a residue report. For each philosophical claim, indicate whether it maps cleanly onto a biological concept, requires distortion (for example, treating cultural transmission as analogous to vertical gene flow), or has no counterpart in taxonomic practice. In particular, examine whether the philosophical notion of shared formative experience can be rendered as an ecotype-defining selective pressure without importing intentional or normative content foreign to biology.

Finally, evaluate a hybrid framing I have been developing: treat a generational cohort as a synthetic deme constituted not by reproductive isolation but by shared exposure to a discrete technological environment—widespread broadband adoption during adolescence functioning as an abrupt selective filter on behavioral phenotypes. Translate this framing back into the philosophical vocabulary of generational ontology, then report what survives and what collapses. Does this hybrid reveal a causal structure that the standard philosophical debate neglects, or does it merely dress a social construct in biological language?

Please do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I seek conceptual clarification rather than scholarly apparatus. Write with the formality appropriate to a cross-disciplinary inquiry.
