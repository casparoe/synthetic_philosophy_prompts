I've been arguing with a friend about the statue and clay problem and I think I've finally landed on a view that isn't just the usual they-are-identical-but-have-different-properties line, but I'm not sure how sturdy the machinery actually is. My position is that there's only one concrete object in the room, but "statue" and "clay" track genuinely different persistence conditions that are both genuinely instatiated—not just descriptions applied to the same thing. The clay-entity survives being squashed into a ball; the statue-entity doesn't. But here's the part I added: the statue doesn't have physical parts in the normal merological sense. Its left arm isn't literally a part of it like a leg is part of a dog; it's a functional contribution that can be replaced during restoration without destroying the statue, because the statue's identity is tied to an artistic-historical trajectory rather than to the continuity of its material substrate. So constitution isn't two objects sharing space—it's one physical substrate realizing two entity-types with different essential properties and different part-whole structures. Most of the time they overlap, but in counterfactual scenarios they split apart.

What would actually count against this specific setup? I'm not asking for the generic colocation objection, since my view is built to absorb that. I want the defeaters that hit the layered-part idea or the functional-contribution tracking directly. For instance, if empirical work on how people actually track objects across change showed we don't separate physical continuity from artistic identity in the way my view requires—would that be fatal, or just awkward? Or if there's a hidden contradiction in saying something has functional parts that aren't spatial parts—could formal mereology actually kill that? What about a real-world case where a statue is slowly replaced part-by-part until zero original clay remains, yet art historians still call it the original: does that confirm my view or break it? I also want to know whether defenders of this kind of layered ontology have already qualified it into unfalsifiability—like retreating to "it's just conceptual"—and whether that insulation is a problem for a metaphysical view like this. Rank the defeaters from "you'd have to abandon the core machinery" to "you'd just need a patch," and be honest about whether my view risks anything real or if it's been protected into emptiness.
