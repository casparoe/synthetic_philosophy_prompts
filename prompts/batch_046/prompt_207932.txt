I'm designing a conceptual taxonomy for a seminar on artificial cognition, and I've become dissatisfied with the usual framing that forces a choice between treating large language models as agents, as mere simulators of human discourse, or as simple tools. The debate seems to presuppose that these three categories are jointly exhaustive and that we can classify a system without first specifying what work the classification is supposed to do—whether predictive, explanatory, normative, or ontological. Before I settle on a taxonomy, I want to know what an adequate theory of an LLM's functional kind actually has to deliver, measured against conceptual clarity and explanatory coverage rather than engineering utility or legal applicability.

Please write the full list of desiderata—what phenomena it must explain, which distinctions it must preserve, and which empirical or conceptual constraints it must satisfy—together with the motivating case or argument behind each. I am especially interested in requirements that arise from embedding contexts rather than isolated inference: for instance, the same weights can drive a one-off chatbot response, a persistent looped system with external memory and self-modifying prompts operating over weeks, or an embedded controller in a robotic architecture with real-time sensorimotor feedback. The theory should clarify whether its verdict applies to the weights alone, to the weights-plus-context, or to the larger socio-technical loop, and it must not collapse these into a single answer by fiat. In particular, include criteria addressing: (1) the distinction between producing belief-like outputs and having beliefs with counterfactual stability across sampling parameters; (2) the difference between simulating a speaker with intentions and functioning as an intentional system in one's own right; (3) consistency with the absence of intrinsic persistent state between queries unless externally scaffolded; (4) accommodation of gradient-descent artifacts that track statistical regularities rather than causal structures in the world; (5) whether the theory permits normative ascriptions like responsibility or epistemic trust, and on what basis; and (6) how it handles cases where the model's outputs are systematically true in one domain, such as formal mathematics, and systematically unreliable in another, such as recent biographical facts.

For each desideratum, flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp—say, those who prioritize internal representational architecture versus those who prioritize observable interaction patterns. Then identify whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: for example, whether demanding both intrinsic intentionality and the absence of endogenous persistent state creates an impossible combination, or whether requiring empirical equivalence with behaviorist criteria conflicts with explaining domain-specific reliability patterns. Finally, indicate which single desideratum does the most work in ruling out the mainstream contenders—the agent view, the simulator view that treats outputs as linguistic reproduction without underlying understanding, and the extended-cognitive-tool view—without turning the answer into a mere scorecard. Keep the conceptual weight on the criteria themselves, but do briefly sketch how each contender fares against the list, noting any awkward commitments, such as a simulator view having to deny that looped embedding creates genuine agency, or an agent view having to explain away sampling-dependent instability.
