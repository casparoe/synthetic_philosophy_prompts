I'm grading exam answers for a philosophy of mind seminar I run with a small rationalist reading group, and I'd like a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression. The exam question was this: pain asymbolia involves patients who recognize nociceptive signals, correctly localize injury, and can describe qualities like burning or sharpness, yet deny that the sensation is unpleasant, distressing, or bad—sometimes even smiling while reporting severe tissue damage. Some philosophers take this dissociation between sensory-discriminative and affective-evaluative dimensions to show that pain's negative valence is modular and separable from its sensory core, undermining any view that pain is necessarily or intrinsically bad. Others argue that it challenges representationalist theories of pain—accounts that identify the feeling with a representation of bodily damage—because in asymbolia the representational content seems intact (damage is registered and localized) while the negative affect is absent, which should be impossible if badness is built into the representational content itself. Assess both lines of argument: does asymbolia genuinely show that badness is separable from sensation, and does that separation help or hurt representationalism?

Here is one student's answer, which strikes me as genuinely mixed—right about the phenomenology, but with a couple of subtle mistakes that a hasty grader might miss:

"Pain asymbolia demonstrates a clear dissociation between the sensory-discriminative aspect of pain—localization, intensity, qualitative character—and its affective-motivational aspect, the feeling that something is wrong and must be stopped. Patients with parietal lesions can point precisely to an injury and describe it as sharp or burning without reporting any suffering or desire for relief. This shows that badness is not intrinsic to the pain sensation itself but is added by a separate evaluative mechanism that can be disabled while sensory registration remains intact. For representationalism, which identifies pain with the representation of tissue damage, this result is actually supportive: since the representational content—there is damage in this location—remains fully present in asymbolia while the negative affect is missing, we can see that representation and evaluation are two independent processes. The representationalist is therefore correct that pain is essentially information about bodily state, and its badness is merely a contingent affective overlay that accompanies it in normal subjects but can be stripped away. Consequently, pain lacks all intrinsic value properties; its negative value depends entirely on the contingent presence of an evaluative response that is not part of the experience's essential nature."

Flag the non-sequitur in the final move from modularity to the denial of all intrinsic value properties, and say whether the error is a misreading of representationalism or a logical slip that survives even if representationalism is false.
