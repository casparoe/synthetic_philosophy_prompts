You are a philosopher of religion and moral psychology writing for an analytically trained audience. Before you answer, take a moment to map out the conceptual distinctions you will need—especially between descriptive cooperation and normative morality—and do some brief brainstorming about which parts of the problem are empirical and which are genuinely conceptual.

Here is the concrete situation: A longtime friend is deciding whether to raise her kids inside a religious community she has been visiting, or to stay in our secular, rationalist-adjacent circle. She has noticed that in the religious group, people share tools, cover shifts, and pool childcare with very little enforcement, whereas in our secular networks cooperation often feels transactional and fragile once anyone stops tracking contributions. She suspects that without some transcendent grounding, moral norms are just preference-optimization that collapses as soon as monitoring disappears, and she worries that secular societies only look trustworthy because robust institutions hide the absence of a metaphysical backstop. At the same time, she sees high-trust secular societies with strong institutions and asks whether religion actually makes people genuinely more moral, or merely selects for different signaling behaviors, and whether secular trust can be sustained if moral motivation is understood as instrumental rather than categorical.

She is not asking for studies, bibliographies, or citations. She wants help seeing the philosophical structure of her dilemma. Specifically: (1) What would "more moral" actually have to mean for religion to be a genuine cause of moral improvement rather than a selection or signaling effect? (2) Can secular societies sustain generalized trust if moral motivation is instrumental, or does that eventually undermine the very cooperation it produces? (3) Is the apparent stability of secular high-trust societies compatible with the claim that transcendent beliefs are necessary for moral motivation? Address this as a philosophical problem instantiated in her choice, not as lifestyle advice. Think carefully about the distinction between behaving cooperatively and being morally better, and flag any hidden assumption in her framing that might collapse the two.
