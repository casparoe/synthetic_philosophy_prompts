Here's an argument my study partner cooked up about collective intentionality and I'm stuck on where it breaks. (1) Joint intentionality requires that participants share an intention aimed at a collective goal, with each person intending their part in a way that refers to the others' parts. (2) Take the trail case: I'm hiking alone, intending to reach the summit by using this maintained path. The path depends on park rangers' concurrent maintainance, but I don't represent them in my plan, so this isn't joint—seems right. (3) But my intention isn't just "reach the summit"; it's "reach the summit via this trail," and the trail's being a usable path conceptually includes being kept up. So my practical reasoning implicitly embeds the rangers' actions in the content of my plan. (4) By (1), that implicit embedding counts as referring to their parts, so my hike is actually jointly intentional with the rangers. (5) The same slide works for speaking English, writing checks, driving—every action conceptually embeds background social maintainance. (6) So either joint action is everywhere and the distinction collapses, or (1) is wrong about what shared intention needs. Where's the slip? Is (4) conflating causal background with intentional content, or is (1) demanding explicit representation that we should drop?
