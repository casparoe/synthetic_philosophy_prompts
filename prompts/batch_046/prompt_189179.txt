I've been trying to work out a view for a course I'm taking about how we should treat germs, long climate shifts, and volcanic events like the one after Tambora in historical explination. I think they shouldn't just be background conditions — like scenery — but also not full intentional agents. Instead I want to say they have this middle-status thing I'm calling 'disruptive agency': they don't have goals, but they generate branching historical possibilities that literally wouldn't exist without them, so in a real sense they co-author outcomes with people.

The Black Death reshaping labor relations, or the Year Without a Summer altering agricultural policy — those aren't just human reactions to a setting; the pathogen or the ash cloud is doing causal work that can't be reduced to what people did. I also think this avoids environmental determinism because I'm not saying climate or germs determine everything, just that they open and close paths in a way that deserves narrative credit. So what would actually count against this? I want the honest map of exposure — which arguments from philosophy of history would show 'disruptive agency' collapses into pure metaphor on one side or determinism on the other? What kind of case, maybe where an epidemic or volcanic winter hits but social structures barely budge, would be decisive versus just awkward? And be honest: have I already qualified this so heavily — 'it's not determinism, it's not intentionality, it's just this special middle thing' — that it's unfalsifiable? If so, is that a real problem for a view that's supposed to change how we write history, or is that insulation actually okay?
