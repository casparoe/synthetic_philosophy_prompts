I'm prepping a ten-minute talk for a grad seminar next week—mostly people in comparative literature and anthropology who work on colonial encounter texts and are pretty allergic to anything that smells like the noble savage trope. I'm defending a somewhat specific reading of Montaigne's essay usually called "Of Cannibals": I think he's not actually doing cultural relativism in the modern sense, like saying all cultures are equally valid, but running a kind of normative inversion. The cannibal figures aren't there to say "they're just different"; they're there to show that European warfare, diet, and social order are more genuinely "barbaric" because they're mediated by artifice, hypocrisy, and custom, whereas the cannibals stay closer to nature. So I'm arguing Montaigne uses them as a mirror, not as an object of ethnography, and that his point is that Europeans fail a natural standard worse than the people they call savages.

The audience is going to be skeptical in particular ways. They tend to read Montaigne as ironic projection—just a European man using the Other to talk to Europeans—and they're suspicious of any "nature versus culture" framing because it feels like back-projecting 20th-century structuralism onto the sixteenth century. They also care a lot about not reproducing colonial power dynamics, so any hint that I'm romanticizing indigenous societies will draw fire.
