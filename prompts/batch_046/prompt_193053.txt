Two people in my study group got stuck arguing about whether beef or chicken is worse for wild-animal suffering once you account for crop-cultivation deaths. Alex opened by saying that, per unit of edible protein, beef requires roughly six to ten times more feed calories than chicken because of ruminant digestion and growth timelines; that means more corn and soy hectares, more harvest machinery, and therefore more rodent and bird deaths per calorie consumed. Jordan conceded the feed-conversion ratio but said Alex was ignoring direct suffering: chicken involves killing many more sentient individuals per kilogram of protein, and those lives are typically brief but intensely confined. Alex replied that for wild-suffering calculus the direct slaughter is irrelevant—those are farmed animals already in the system—whereas the machinery deaths and habitat loss from feed crops are marginal harms we can avoid by switching protein sources.

Jordan then introduced pasture: grass-fed beef avoids feed-crop machinery deaths entirely, but the pasture itself displaces wild herbivores through fencing, grazing competition, and predator control. Alex countered that even grass-fed systems usually finish on grain, and that pasture displacement is gradual starvation rather than the acute trauma of harvest equipment, so the suffering profile differs in kind, not just scale. At this point Jordan argued that chicken feed is almost entirely cultivated grain, concentrating all wild-harm in harvest fields, while beef can spread harm across both pasture and feed crops; therefore beef’s footprint is broader but not necessarily deeper per unit protein.

Alex then injected a different concern: the “replacement trap” in field ecology. When a harvester kills a wild rodent, that individual is usually replaced within a season by a migrant or offspring who then lives a short, high-mortality life in the same risky habitat. So counting only direct machinery deaths understates the suffering footprint, because each death supports a cycle of brief, disease- and predation-filled replacements. Jordan said this replacement dynamic applies equally to any crop-based diet—including vegan ones—so it cannot distinguish beef from chicken unless you also weigh the remaining life expectancy of replaced animals, which differs between small rodents in grain fields and larger herbivores displaced by pasture. They deadlocked on whether the correct metric is wild deaths per kilogram of protein, net suffering-years displaced, or some weighted combination, and neither could say whether beef’s larger total land footprint outweighs chicken’s larger direct body count.
