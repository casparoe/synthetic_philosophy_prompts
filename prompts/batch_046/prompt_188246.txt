I have recently taken over verification oversight at a firm that deploys neural theorem provers for safety-critical infrastructure. We are adopting an internal framework drawn from logical induction—treating each unverified proof claim as a logical statement about which we maintain a confidence level that updates as partial checks accumulate, constrained only by coherence conditions and a long-run convergence requirement rather than by complete computation. Because I must decide repeatedly whether to approve, flag, or reject automated proofs within a two-hour review window, without access to external databases and with final sign-off required from a non-technical compliance officer, I need a reusable decision procedure.

Please translate the core commitments of logical induction—especially the distinction between logical and empirical uncertainty, the coherence conditions that prevent arbitrage across logically related claims, and the convergence requirement that confidence must approach zero or one as more logical structure is revealed—into a step-by-step protocol my team can run on any proof submission. For each step specify the exact question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where a negative or ambiguous result stops the process with rejection or mandatory escalation, and where the question is irreducibly one of risk tolerance or values, requiring a human judgment call rather than a procedural answer. Be explicit about which distinctions from the theory must survive the simplification—such as coherence binding across related claims even when they are verified separately—and which can be dropped for operational speed.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a forty-page automated proof of a distributed consensus protocol's liveness property, in which spot-checks of three key lemmas pass but the proof relies on a recently added library of custom tactics whose soundness has not been independently audited; (b) a shorter proof of a cryptographic reduction that contradicts an earlier manually verified result, yet every internal step appears structurally valid; and (c) a proof of an optimization pass's termination guarantee that scores well on all structural checks but concerns a logical domain—non-termination of a specific loop construct—where our historical base rate for prover errors is unusually high. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an unwelcome answer—approval should be withheld despite positive surface signals—and state precisely what the procedure cannot settle, such as the appropriate penalty for a false negative versus a false positive in this domain.

Keep the procedure honest about its misfire modes: cases where coherence checks are satisfied but a hidden logical dependency creates a joint failure, or where convergence pressures push premature confidence before the proof structure is fully exposed.
