There's something I keep seeing in discussions of scientific thought experiments and I don't know if it has a specific name. You start with a classic setup — Galileo dropping bodies from a tower, Einstein riding an elevator in free fall, or Schrödinger's box with the cat — and use it to show that some physical theory leads to a counterintuitive result. Then someone objects that the setup cheats: it assumes a perfect vacuum, or an infinitely precise measuring device, or that the box is totally isolated from the outside world. The defender doesn't abandon the experiment; they just add a little clause, like "ignoring air resistance" or "treating the system as closed." Each clause is defensible by itself. But stack three or four together and you're describing a universe with different laws, not ours, and yet people still cite the conclusion as if it applies here. I know about falsification and maybe research-program degeneration, but I'm asking about the sepcific pattern where individually reasonable isolation conditions accumulate until the thought experiment is testing a toy world rather than reality. Is there a term for that drift? How is it different from an ordinary auxilliary hypothesis? And if there's no name, what should we call it — maybe "isolation creep"?
