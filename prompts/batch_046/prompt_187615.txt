Consider the institutional practice of binding direct democracy as exercised through frequent national referendums in Switzerland, the 2016 United Kingdom European Union membership referendum, and the ballot-initiative system in California. In each case, complex constitutional or policy questions are resolved by direct popular vote, often with minimal deliberative intermediation, and the outcome is treated as politically authoritative even when it conflicts with expert assessments or yields internally inconsistent policy bundles—for instance, California propositions that mandate increased public expenditure whilst simultaneously restricting taxation. What philosophical commitments regarding collective rationality, popular sovereignty, and the epistemology of democratic decision-making does this practice presuppose? Specifically, does it presuppose a Condorcet jury-theoretic view of error-cancelling aggregation, a Rousseauian conception of an indivisible general will, or rather a form of doxastic fragmentation in which a polity is treated as holding a single authoritative judgment despite no individual voter possessing a coherent overall view? Reconstruct the implicit normative theory, distinguish commitments that are strictly necessary to the practice from those that are merely contingently associated with it, and assess whether the same underlying philosophy can coherently accommodate both Switzerland's model of repeated, low-stakes referendums and the one-off, high-stakes binary choice characteristic of Brexit. Please present your analysis in British English.
