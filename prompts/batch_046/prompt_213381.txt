Write a parody of an End User License Agreement (EULA) — approximately 700 words, formatted as numbered clauses with subsections and appendices — issued by a fictional “International Genome Licensing Consortium” for the human genome. Adopt a deadpan corporate-legal voice that never breaks character; treat the venue as a binding terms-of-service document that every user implicitly accepts upon conception. The satire should target both the popular-science “DNA is literally software/code” metaphor and the genuine philosophical debate over whether genetic information carries semantic content (representing phenotypic traits) or is merely causal and chemical. To avoid pure slapstick, exaggerate real features of both positions: include clauses on open-reading frames versus proprietary introns, DRM enforced by ribosomes, “update patches” (mutations) that void warranty, and a “junk DNA” provision classifying non-coding regions as deprecated legacy code. Crucially, inject the following fictional but conceptually precise framework: the agreement must distinguish a “Syntactic License” (ownership of the nucleotide sequence itself, analogized to source code) from a “Semantic License” (rights to the phenotypic “meaning” or functional interpretation produced by expression), and it must contain an “Appendix C: The Teleosemantic Exception” stating that if DNA genuinely carries semantic content about organismal traits — rather than only chemical instructions — then any mutation that preserves syntactic structure but alters phenotypic meaning constitutes “copyright infringement by misrepresentation” rather than a simple warranty breach. The piece should remain readable to someone without training in philosophy of biology, yet reward a specialist who recognizes that the joke turns on the real tension between syntactic information flow and teleosemantic theories of biological function. Do not cite philosophers, texts, or case law by name; do not include a bibliography or reading list. Maintain strict internal consistency with the literal-code premise throughout, and conclude with a “Refund and Continuity Policy” that quietly raises the problem of personal identity: if your genome is licensed software, does a somatic mutation that changes your phenotype mean that “you” have violated your own terms of service?
