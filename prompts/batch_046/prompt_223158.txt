hey — quick question for a piece i'm working on about democratic institutions that brand themselves participatory but actually lock key decsions in expert committees (think central banks, climate boards, those AI ethics panels that claim to represent public will). Spinoza, in the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus and the unfinished Tractatus Politicus, argued democracy is the form where sovereignty isn't really alienated, since the multitude never fully transfers its natural right (jus naturale) to rulers; he also insisted you can't coerce minds, so freedom of thought is non-negotiable. So what's actually being presupposed here? When a modern state sets up these insulated bodies with democratic branding, is it relying on Spinoza's idea that the collective retains power, or on his other claim that reason should guide the state — which might justify handing authority to rational elites? And if Spinoza is right that people never fully surrender their natural power, then aren't these boards basically aristocracies with PR budgets? I want to figure out whether the practice of "democratic insulation" is philosophically coherent on Spinozist grounds, or whether it quietly replaces his concept of common notions with technocratic authority, and what that means for how we should design them.
