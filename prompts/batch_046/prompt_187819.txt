As a novelist developing a historical fiction manuscript set in Geneva during the years of Rousseau's controversy over the theater, I have drafted a brief conceptual essay intended as an author's note. The essay attempts to extend Rousseau's critique of stage representation to narrative fiction, and I am concerned that I may have conflated his objections to collective spectacle with an unjustified exemption for private reading. Rather than a general impression, I seek a point-by-point assessment of the essay's philosophical accuracy, its internal coherence, and the specific points where my framing might mislead a reader acquainted with Rousseau's broader concerns about identification and imagination. Please address whether my argument holds, where it collapses, and whether the distinction I draw between theatrical and novelistic representation is defensible.

Here is the essay:

Rousseau's well-known critique of theatrical representation holds that the actor abandons authentic being to inhabit an artificial persona, and that the gathered audience, witnessing this substitution, gradually learns to value appearance over substance. From this, I derive what I term the privacy fallacy: the assumption that if representation occurs outside collective spectacle, its moral and ontological dangers disappear. Applied to the novel, this fallacy suggests that private reading escapes Rousseau's condemnation because there is no assembled public to be corrupted, and because the novelist, writing in her own voice rather than performing a body, avoids the actor's self-abnegation. The reader, engaging in solitary imagination, identifies with an interior consciousness rather than an external spectacle. Consequently, the novel not only evades Rousseau's critique but actively protects authentic being by removing representation from the public eye. I therefore conclude that his objections apply exclusively to the stage, and that fiction consumed in silence represents a form of representation that reinforces rather than dissolves the self.
