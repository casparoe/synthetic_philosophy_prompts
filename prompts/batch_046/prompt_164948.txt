Following a lengthy and somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether cooperative behaviour in iterated dilemmas is genuinely evolutionary or merely an artefact of stable population structures, I have found myself repeatedly describing a particular strategic configuration, and I am unsure whether the literature already has a precise name for it.

The situation is this. Imagine an iterated prisoner's dilemma in a mutating population, where most discussions contrast one-shot defection with reciprocal strategies such as tit-for-tat. I keep noticing an intermediate strategy: it defects on the opening move, then, on each subsequent round, compares its own cumulative payoff to that of its opponent. If it is behind, it copies the opponent's previous move; if it is ahead or level, it defects regardless of the opponent's action. Against an unconditional cooperator, it eventually falls behind, switches to mirroring, and locks into sustained mutual cooperation. Against an unconditional defector, it never falls behind, so it never switches, leaving both in perpetual defection. Against tit-for-tat, however, the dynamics enter a stable three-cycle—defection, cooperation, defection—because the relative standing oscillates in a manner that prevents convergence. The strategy is therefore neither dominant nor dominated; instead, it generates a non-transitive, rock-paper-scissors-like loop, but one that appears exclusively against reciprocators rather than across the whole strategy space.

My friend argued that this is merely a noisy variant of win-stay, lose-shift, yet I think that mischaracterises the mechanism. The switch is not triggered by a change in the agent's own payoff between rounds, but by a continuous comparison of relative standing, and the resulting cycle is structurally stable rather than stochastic. I have not encountered an established term for either this score-conditional mirror strategy or the evolutionary loop it produces specifically against reciprocal agents. Does this exact configuration have a recognised name? If not, I would value a careful distinction between it and win-stay, lose-shift, as well as from general cyclical dominance, together with an account of its boundary conditions—particularly whether it requires perfect score-tracking or can persist under noisy observation.
