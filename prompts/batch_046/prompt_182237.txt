I encountered a summary by an unnamed philosophy blogger about Ned Block’s distinction between access consciousness (A-consciousness) and phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness), and about how global workspace theory (GWT) relates to it. The blogger writes, in effect, the following: Block distinguishes access consciousness—the information globally available for reasoning, decision, and verbal report—from phenomenal consciousness, which is just the raw subjective feel or “what-it’s-likeness” of an experience. Global workspace theory, developed by Bernard Baars and later elaborated by Stanislas Dehaene, explains consciousness as the broadcasting of sensory information across a distributed network linking prefrontal and parietal regions; on this view, access consciousness is essentially that global availability. Block contends that phenomenal consciousness is genuinely separate, since it can occur without global broadcasting, as suggested by cases of super-blindsight or masked stimuli that still seem to generate experience. Some neuroscientists, notably Victor Lamme, have argued that phenomenal consciousness actually depends on local recurrent processing loops in early sensory cortex rather than on long-range broadcasting. The blogger then claims that Block’s phenomenal consciousness is really just this local recurrent processing—the entry-level integration mechanism that feeds information into the global workspace. Once you see phenomenal consciousness as merely that preliminary gating stage, there is no mysterious leftover “feel” independent of access; the hard problem dissolves into the engineering question of how local loops prepare content for broadcasting. I am inclined to think this is mostly right but I worry it collapses two things Block keeps apart, and that it overstates what GWT defenders actually claim. Please audit the summary for me.
