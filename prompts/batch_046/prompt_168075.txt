After a prolonged disagreement with a friend about whether comparative judgments of value can survive continuous variation without collapsing into arbitrariness, I have been asked to evaluate a student examination answer on the same conceptual issue, and I want an objective assessment rather than my own impressions.

The examination question was as follows: "Consider a spectrum argument directed at the relation 'aesthetically superior to' applied to a continuous sequence of artworks A_0 through A_100, where each adjacent pair differs only by an imperceptible increment in chromatic complexity and formal density. Proponents of the argument observe that A_0 is clearly superior to A_100, yet claim that no adjacent pair in the sequence can be ranked. They conclude that the relation 'better than' is not transitive. Critics respond that this confuses comparative judgment with transitive ordering, or that the appearance of failure is merely epistemic. Evaluate whether the spectrum argument genuinely threatens the transitivity of aesthetic betterness. In your answer, distinguish carefully between epistemic inaccessibility of a comparison and an ontological failure of the relation to hold, and assess whether an account that treats aesthetic value as continuous and real-numbered can preserve transitivity without committing to sharp thresholds."

The student submitted the following answer:

The argument arranges the artworks in a chain where neighboring pairs are indistinguishable in value. Because A_0 is superior to A_100, transitivity requires that A_0 be superior to A_1, then A_1 to A_2, and so on through every link. But since each adjacent pair is indistinguishable, the relation cannot be applied across the chain, which demonstrates that 'better than' is not transitive. This error stems from assuming that indistinguishability entails equality; in reality, two works may differ in value without our being able to perceive it. The correct response is that 'better than' is a vague relation that nevertheless possesses a precise but unknown sharp boundary somewhere in the sequence; this is the epistemicist position. Once we acknowledge this hidden cutoff, transitivity is fully restored at the metaphysical level, and the argument only reveals our cognitive limitations. Additionally, one may adopt a degree-theoretic view on which aesthetic value is continuous and ordered by real numbers; since the real ordering is transitive, the underlying value relation is preserved, and our binary judgments are merely coarse approximations. Therefore, the spectrum argument fails to establish any genuine ontological breakdown of transitivity; it exposes only either our ignorance of precise boundaries or the imprecision of our comparative vocabulary.
