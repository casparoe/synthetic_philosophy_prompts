I sit on the advisory council for a residential college that has proposed a rather unconventional orientation practice. Over the course of the first week, small cohorts of new students are invited—though not formally required—to attend a single evening session led by a trained, entirely sober senior mentor. Each participant is served a precisely diluted mixture of wine and water, roughly one part to four, and the group is guided through structured conversations about past conflicts, moral commitments, and how they handle disagreement. The explicit purpose is not social bonding but character assessment: the mentors observe whether lowered inhibitions produce greater candor, aggression, or a particular trait the committee terms epistemic humility—the willingness to publicly revise one’s judgments about oneself and others. Those who maintain measured self-awareness are identified as potential peer mediators; those who become deceptive or hostile are flagged for early support. The dean justifies the method as analogous to a clinical stress-test of the psyche, arguing that social masks are precisely the obstacle to fair evaluation, and that a philosophically informed observer can distinguish mere physiological disinhibition from a genuine revelation of moral disposition. A dissenting colleague counters that this coerces artificial vulnerability, treats students as specimens rather than agents, and commits a category error by assuming that a chemically altered state reveals a more authentic self. She also points out an asymmetry: if the test is valid, it should apply to those in authority, yet no mentor or administrator is asked to undergo it.

I find myself uncertain whether this practice can be philosophically defended as a legitimate probe of character, or whether it fundamentally misconstrues what alcohol reveals about the soul, and whether the presence of a sober guide genuinely resolves the ethical and epistemic difficulties. Could you help me think through the conceptual architecture here—specifically, whether intoxication can ever be a fair window onto moral identity, what interpretive burden falls on the observer, and whether selecting leaders through such a filter is compatible with treating persons as ends in themselves?
