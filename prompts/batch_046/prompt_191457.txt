I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am designing a thirty-minute guided explanation for a small reading group of senior architects and urban planners—professionals fluent in technical standards but without formal training in philosophy of science—on Kuhn’s account of paradigm shifts and incommensurability. I want the teaching plan, not the lecture itself: specify the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and the dependency that requires one to be secure before the next can land; identify the single concrete carrying case, drawn from a domain familiar to planners, that should illustrate the shift—stating precisely what it must display (a change in evaluation criteria rather than merely a change in factual belief) and what it must avoid; map the misconceptions this audience will predictably import from popular narratives about “scientific revolutions,” noting the point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; propose a brief check that would reveal whether they have understood the structural dependency rather than just adopted the vocabulary; and state explicitly which technical elements—such as Kuhn’s later analysis of lexical taxonomy and kind terms—are to be left out at this level and openly acknowledged as beyond scope. The plan should not depend on assigned readings, bibliographies, or citations.
