In debates over prosaic AI alignment, proponents often compare current techniques—RLHF, constitutional AI, adversarial filtering, and process-level supervision—to installing seatbelts, airbags, and guardrails in a vehicle whose engine, steering dynamics, and crash physics remain largely opaque. The analogy is used to license the claim that layered empirical safeguards can render present architectures safe enough without a theoretical breakthrough in interpretability or agent foundations. Audit the comparison: provide an explicit correspondence table (what plays the role of the seatbelt, the airbag, the crash-test regime, the driver, and the opaque engine); identify the disanalogies that matter for this conclusion (for example, whether RLHF alters the system's objectives rather than merely restraining them, or whether capability growth outpaces the addition of safeguards in ways that automotive engineering does not); separate the breaks that undermine the safety argument from those that do not; and say whether the conclusion—that incremental engineering alone can achieve adequate alignment—survives once these gaps are on the table. If the vehicle analogy collapses in crucial respects, propose a better analogy for the relationship between surface behavioral control and underlying capability growth in current architectures.
