I'm preparing the interview guide for an episode of a small philosophy podcast I'm recording with a philosopher colleague who defends what she calls a bifocal-model view of introspection — not a named school in the literature, but a specific synthesis she has developed across several papers. Her core claim is that introspective access is not a single mechanism but operates through two structurally distinct channels: an "apparent" channel that delivers direct, high-fidelity reports about occurrent propositional attitudes and the qualitative character of present experience, and an "interpretive" channel that constructs explanatory narratives for emotional intensity, motivational strength, and behavioral causes using contextual and theoretical cues. On her view, introspection is therefore genuinely authoritative in some domains and systematically unreliable in others, but the unreliability is not randomly distributed: it clusters precisely where the two channels compete, producing what she terms "cross-channel interference." A classic case in her framework is the subject who reports feeling "perfectly calm" about a high-stakes choice despite clear physiological arousal — here the apparent channel registers the arousal, while the interpretive channel suppresses it to preserve narrative coherence, yielding a report that is sincere but structurally conflicted.

I want eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual content of this position rather than letting her restate it. Please include at least one question on how her model handles the transparency arguments associated with philosophers like Evans and Tye — specifically, whether her "apparent" channel collapses into direct perception of experience rather than introspection proper — together with a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that transparency only governs phenomenal access, not propositional self-knowledge. Include at least one question on the classic Nisbett-and-Wilson findings about our ignorance of the causes of our judgments and behavior, asking whether her bifocal model explains those results without conceding that introspection is globally a kind of confabulation, and provide a follow-up anticipating her answer that the interpretive channel was never designed for causal attribution. Finally, include at least one question specifically about "cross-channel interference": ask what phenomenological or experimental marker could distinguish it from ordinary self-deception or post-hoc rationalization, and include a follow-up for the answer she is most likely to give — probably that interference produces characteristic patterns of temporal instability and partial accessibility rather than wholesale false belief.

Please separate in your response what is settled about introspective reliability from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers and cognitive scientists who work on the topic, and do not present any contested interpretive claim — such as whether transparency eliminates introspection altogether, or whether Nisbett-and-Wilson-style results generalize beyond their original experimental domains — as if it were the consensus view.
