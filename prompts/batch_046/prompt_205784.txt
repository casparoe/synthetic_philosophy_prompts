I have been working through a tension in Maimonides' philosophy of worship and have constructed an argument that seems to show his position is incoherent. I need to know where it breaks, but every repair I attempt looks costly.

(1) In his philosophical account of the commandments, Maimonides explains animal sacrifice as a pedagogical concession: the Israelites, accustomed to animal-worship practices among surrounding cultures, were given a commandment that redirected those same physical gestures toward God so as to wean them from idolatry.

(2) On that account, sacrifice possesses no intrinsic superiority to other forms of worship; its value is purely instrumental and historical. Prayer and internal intention represent the essential divine service, while sacrifice is a temporary vehicle tailored to a primitive condition.

(3) If a law's entire justification is that it served an obsolete pedagogical need, and a superior form of the same service is already available, then retaining the law as permanently binding is incoherent. A concession that outlives the condition it was meant to treat either becomes meaningless or risks perpetuating the very practices associated with the error being cured.

(4) Yet in his legal codification, Maimonides details the laws of sacrifice as obligations that would resume immediately upon the rebuilding of the Temple, with no indication that prayer will ever fully supersede them or that they are merely tolerated relics.

(5) One might reply that the concession explains only the origin of the law, not its continued normative force. But if the form of the law remains inseparable from the idolatrous practices it was designed to mimic, then granting it permanent legal status risks re-institutionalizing those associations rather than transcending them.

(6) Therefore, Maimonides must either deny that sacrifice will ever be restored—contradicting his legal code—or concede that the concessionary explanation is incomplete, which requires admitting that sacrifice carries some non-instrumental value he elsewhere denies.

(7) I suspect the slip occurs somewhere between (3) and (5), perhaps an equivocation between the psychological reason for a commandment's historical institution and the rational structure it acquires once embedded in a legal system, or a hidden modal slide from "was given as a concession" to "must be withdrawn." But I cannot name the move precisely, and I do not want to abandon the intuition that Maimonides should not be left with a contradiction.

Which premise or step should be rejected? Explain why it initially looked sound, and then price the alternatives: does saving Maimonides require a sharp distinction between diachronic pedagogical function and synchronic institutional meaning? If so, does that distinction undermine his broader claim that the commandments have discernible purposes? And if one instead keeps (3) and rejects (4) by treating the legal details as hypothetical rather than normative, what does that cost for reading the Mishneh Torah as a living legal framework rather than an archaeological record? I would prefer to keep the coherence of both the philosophical and legal texts intact, so I am especially interested in whether there is a repair that preserves both without inventing an intrinsic value for sacrifice that Maimonides never asserts.
