I am writing a dissertation chapter on the epistemology of origin narratives, and I keep encountering what I will call the "sieve model" of legend-to-history transmission. The model treats narratives like the Exodus, the Trojan War tradition, and the Arthurian corpus as if they began as straightforward reports of actual events, after which centuries of oral and literary transmission added miraculous, archetypal, or anachronistic elements like sediment. The work this analogy does is to license a minimalist historiography: apply the sieve, discard the supernatural and the symbolic, and you are left with a recoverable kernel—a small Semitic migration out of Egypt, a Bronze Age siege of a northwestern Anatolian settlement, a Romano-British military leader around whom later chivalric fiction accreted. I want you to audit how far this actually holds.

Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the sieve mechanism, the original unfiltered substance, the retained residue, the discarded sediment, and the agent who applies the filter? Then show me where the mapping breaks for each of the three cases. In particular, press on whether the sieve smuggles in a stratigraphic theory of cultural memory—the assumption that earlier layers are closer to reality and later ones are pure embellishment—and whether that theory survives contact with the actual compositional history of these texts. For the Exodus, consider whether the narrative might be retrojectively constructed during the Babylonian exile as a counter-cosmology rather than an embellished memory of an actual departure; for Arthur, whether the chivalric and magical layers are not late accretions to a historical core but constitutive of the very figure that later writers synthesized; and for Troy, whether Schliemann's identification of specific destruction horizons with Homer's narrative already assumes the sieve rather than testing it independently.

Finally, take a committed position: does the minimalist conclusion survive once these disanalogies are on the table, or was the sieve carrying the entire argument? If the analogy collapses, what is a better model for thinking about the relationship between these narratives and whatever historical processes, if any, they relate to? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of positions; I want a definite assessment with reasons.
