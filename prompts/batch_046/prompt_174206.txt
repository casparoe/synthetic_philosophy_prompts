I'm working through the measurement problem in philosophy of physics, and I've become fixated on a stalemate that feels different from ordinary scientific disagreement. Everettian, Copenhagen, and Bohmian mechanics all recover the same statistical predictions from the same Schrödinger dynamics and the same Born-rule weights; they diverge not on what happens in a laboratory, but on what ontology, if any, underwrites those dynamics. The Everettian treats the universal wavefunction as complete and explains definite outcomes through decoherence-induced branching; the Bohmian adds deterministic particle trajectories guided by that same wavefunction; the Copenhagen interpretation, at least in its orthodox form, treats the wavefunction as an epistemic tool and takes measurement outcomes as primitive, irreducible events that need no deeper ontological explanation. After a year of reading, the arguments seem to circle the same points—locality, determinism, ontological parsimony, the reality of the wavefunction—without anyone conceding ground.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not, at bottom, about physics, but about a philosophical condition I want to call explanatory closure: whether an interpretation is required to generate the appearance of definite, single outcomes entirely from resources internal to its own ontology, or whether it is legitimate for a theory to treat some class of events as boundary conditions that the formalism describes without explaining. The Bohmian and Everettian programs both pursue closure—hidden variables or branching worlds are attempts to make the outcome-definiteness internal—while Copenhagen refuses that demand and pays the price in ontological austerity. But I worry I may be smuggling in a philosophical prejudice: perhaps the sides are not arguing about closure at all, but about incompatible bedrock intuitions regarding determinism, the meaning of "reality," or the modal status of the wavefunction (whether it represents possibilities or actualities). There may also be a hidden formal question—perhaps about whether decoherence can genuinely recover classicality without a prior selection of a preferred basis—that none of the interpretations solve but all assume away.

So I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Using what each interpretation actually commits to—Everett's denial of collapse, Bohm's guidance equation and quantum equilibrium hypothesis, Copenhagen's insistence on classical measuring apparatuses as prerequisites for meaningful attribution—tell me whether this is a verbal dispute about the word "explanation," a substantive disagreement masked by shared mathematics, or a cluster of distinct questions (one semantic, one metaphysical, one methodological) running under one heading. Is there anything in the residue that my vocabulary of "explanatory closure" fails to capture—perhaps a cognitive or pragmatic factor about how physicists choose frameworks? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a no-go theorem showing that decoherence cannot select a basis without circularity, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the appearance of conflict, or an honest admission that the dispute rests on intuitions about determinism that no formal result could settle? I want to know whether my framing is illuminating or whether it distorts the debate by imposing a philosophical architecture that the physicists themselves would reject.
