Consider the operating procedures of many public arts endowments and museum acquisition boards in Western liberal democracies. Their peer-review guidelines typically instruct panels to assess submissions on “formal innovation”, “aesthetic autonomy”, and “intrinsic merit”, and they often explicitly exclude “political message”, “social utility”, or “didactic purpose” from counting as artistic virtues. At the same time, these very institutions maintain separate funding streams—community-engagement grants, public-monument commissions, documentary archives—through which they routinely support works that are overtly propagandistic: murals celebrating labour movements, photography exhibitions designed to change immigration policy, monuments intended to reshape collective memory. The result is a bifurcated practice in which a work’s eligibility for the “pure art” category depends on its not being seen as serving an explicit social end, even though the institution as a whole actively commissions art that does exactly that.

What philosophical commitments about the nature of art, propaganda, and value does this institutional division presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require a Kantian separation between aesthetic and cognitive value, a particular view of artistic intentionality, or merely a pragmatic distinction between tractable evaluation criteria? Is the practice strictly committed to the claim that propaganda and genuine art are mutually exclusive, or is it compatible with the view—famously defended by W. E. B. Du Bois in his writings on Black aesthetics—that all art is propaganda and that beauty should be deliberately harnessed to truth and justice? Say whether the bifurcation is philosophically consistent, whether its parts presuppose rival theories of value, and identify where the practice is underdetermined, such that several philosophical accounts could license it. Then explain what would have to change in funding structures, evaluation protocols, and curatorial categories if an arts council fully adopted Du Bois’s framework, in which no work could be disqualified from artistic status on the grounds that it propagandises for equality.
