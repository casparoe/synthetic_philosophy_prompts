The debate over whether a statue and the clay composing it are one object or two has stalled for decades. Constitution theorists point to modal differences—squash the clay and the statue is destroyed, yet the clay survives—while eliminativists reply that this is only a difference in description, not proof of a second thing: there is just clay arranged statue-wise. Four-dimensionalists absorb both into one spacetime worm under different descriptions. More recently, synthetic-replacement cases have been introduced, in which every clay atom is gradually swapped for a structurally identical synthetic replica, so that material kind and functional form come apart; yet none of the main camps have moved, and the arguments now feel circular. I suspect the impasse hides disagreements about what an ontology should do—preserve commonsense counting, maximize parsimony, or capture every modal truth—but I am unsure whether there is also a substantive dispute about artifacts, or whether the sides are answering different questions about metaphysical grounding versus linguistic reference under shared vocabulary. Diagnose the disagreement using the actual moves each camp makes: is it verbal, substantive, or a case of masked differences in explanatory goals? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a sharper distinction between constitution and composition, a formal result on coincidence, or an argument that the framing itself is ill-posed?
