I am preparing a classification instrument for a graduate seminar on state-citizen information asymmetry. The debate is framed by three regulatory cultures: the German tradition of informational self-determination (Datenschutz and the right to be left alone), the American culture of government transparency coupled with extensive citizen exposure (public records, open-data norms, and the implicit premise that visibility is harmless), and the Chinese social-credit framework, in which comprehensive citizen visibility serves as an instrument of predictive governance.

Please construct a decision tree—not a gallery of named theorists—that sorts positions on how much citizens ought to hide from the state. Build the tree from the logical space: each internal node should be a concrete claim or case on which positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing the label it leads to. Begin with this root split: Is the value of hiding primarily defensive (protecting a pre-political zone of autonomy) or strategic (manipulating the data environment of a predictive state)?

From there, branch on whether state knowledge is legitimate only under conditions of reciprocity—that is, whether the citizen’s right to opacity is conditional on the state’s algorithms and databases being equally visible to the citizen. Include a further branch on whether the Chinese social-credit case demonstrates that any comprehensive transparency of citizens is inherently coercive, or whether coercion enters only through the opacity of the scoring mechanisms themselves. Add a branch addressing whether American openness norms tacitly presuppose that citizens “have nothing to hide,” a presupposition that collapses once profiling is algorithmic rather than bureaucratic.

Each leaf should carry either a named position—such as “radical predictive-opacity advocate,” “reciprocal-panopticon democrat,” “market-preference libertarian,” or “German-style defensive privacy supplemented by active citizen disinformation”—or be marked unnamed, with one sentence describing its most awkward commitment. Specifically, ensure these four positions appear if they occupy the space: (1) citizens should maximize opacity because predictive governance renders any data extract harmful; (2) citizens should be maximally visible but only if the state’s algorithms are fully auditable; (3) the degree of hiding is morally neutral and should be settled by individual preference in an information market; (4) conventional data protection is necessary but insufficient, and citizens ought to deploy strategic misinformation.

Keep the tree to six or eight leaves. Identify at least one combination in the logical space that remains unoccupied, and briefly diagnose whether its absence is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect—for instance, the view that accepts social-credit legitimacy in principle but demands that citizens hide more aggressively than the system permits. Flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot genuinely separate, particularly where strategic opacity and defensive privacy both reject algorithmic profiling but for different reasons.

Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the instrument should be answerable through reasoning about the concepts alone.
