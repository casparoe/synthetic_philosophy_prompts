In debates over whether large language models possess genuine reasoning dispositions or only context-dependent performances, a recurring analogy compares evaluation failures to masked physical properties. Proponents say a model that solves a logic puzzle under chain-of-thought prompting but fails with direct questioning is like a fragile vase wrapped in protective foam: the fragility—the reasoning disposition—remains fully present, merely masked by the packaging of the prompt format. Critics reply that this mistakes an absence for a hidden presence, likening the model instead to a wire that was never live: altering the conditions does not reveal a dormant capacity but changes the system’s actual causal profile, just as applying voltage to dead copper produces no current. Audit both analogies with explicit correspondence tables—what plays the role of the disposition, the mask or fink, the triggering conditions, the manifestation, and the evaluating observer—then locate precisely where each mapping collapses. Does the foam-wrap analogy smuggle in an essentialist commitment that reasoning is an intrinsic property invariant across contexts? Does the dead-wire analogy illegitimately assume that all genuine dispositions must be context-independent? Consider whether a better comparison might be available—perhaps to a skill that genuinely requires scaffolding to be exercised, or to a compound that reacts only under specific pH conditions—one that avoids both essentialism and elimination. Finally, say whether the dispute about model reasoning can actually be resolved by selecting the correct analogy, or whether the analogies are carrying the entire argumentative load.
