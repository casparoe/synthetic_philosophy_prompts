I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science preparing my dissertation prospectus on the metaphysics of scientific lawhood, and over the past two years I have been cataloguing my advisor's implicit judgments about which regularities count as genuine laws of nature versus accidental generalizations or system-relative artifacts. Before I present my reconstruction in next month's workshop, I want to check whether her verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one theoretical framework—specifically, whether they underdetermine the choice between a Humean best-system account restricted to natural domains, an anti-Humean necessitarian view that distinguishes governing natural universals from engineered artifacts, and a pluralist position that allows locally exceptionless regularities in designed systems to count as laws of those systems. Her recorded judgments are as follows. She treats the free-muon half-life regularity, the second-law entropy increase in thermally isolated systems, and Kepler's orbital laws as genuine laws of nature. She denies law-status to the true but accidental generalization that no gold sphere exceeds one mile in diameter; to the statistically robust but institutionally bounded correlation, observed only in country C between 1950 and 2020, linking central-bank interest-rate hikes to subsequent drops in housing starts; and to an exceptionless but spatially confined regularity in a single Andromeda globular cluster, where every detectable K-type star happens to host exactly seven planets. She also grants law-status to the universal genetic code shared by all terrestrial DNA-based life—resisting the interpretation that it is merely a frozen evolutionary accident—while denying that the convergence of every run of the Avida digital-evolution platform, with fixed parameters, to an identical complex logic-gate function is a law of nature, instead classifying it as a law of that artificial system analogous to an engineered artifact. Finally, she holds that a Bell-inequality-violating correlation observed consistently in one specific laboratory entanglement preparation, but not projectible to other quantum regimes, is not itself a law, though it is fully explained by underlying quantum-mechanical laws.

The new case I need placed is this. A synthetic-biology consortium has engineered a modular gene circuit that, in every tested E. coli strain across thousands of laboratory and field conditions, produces a specific fluorescent protein within exactly forty-seven minutes of blue-light exposure, with variance below measurement error. The regularity is exceptionless across all actual and counterfactual runs of this circuit, yet it fails in naturally evolved phototactic bacteria, depends essentially on the synthetic promoter and reporter genes introduced by the designers, and disappears if the bacterial chassis is replaced by a eukaryotic host or by an analog biochemical substrate. Would my advisor classify this regularity as a law—perhaps of synthetic biology, or of nature more broadly? For each of the three frameworks mentioned, predict her verdict on the synthetic-circuit case, identify which prior judgment offers the closest analog, and state whether her track record genuinely underdetermines the outcome. If the record is ambiguous, specify precisely what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice, and rank the options by how severely they strain her other commitments. Then propose one further hypothetical case—one that does not depend on any empirical discovery—that would cleanly separate the interpretations that survive this test.
