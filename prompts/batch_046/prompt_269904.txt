In our senior philosophy seminar, two classmates—Jordan and Alex—got into a sustained disagreement about whether our school’s open-discourse policy should cover a newly formed student club that calls itself the Epistemic Closure Society. The club’s charter does not advocate violence, but it explicitly holds that allowing certain scientific conclusions to remain in the curriculum is a form of institutional complicity in falsehood, and it campaigns for the removal of those units from required coursework. Jordan and Alex debated whether the administration should deny the club official recognition. Their exchange unfolded in four moves.

(1) Jordan argued that the club is intolerant in the relevant sense: it seeks to exclude specific viewpoints and bodies of knowledge from the educational environment, which directly undermines the school’s commitment to open inquiry. Jordan invoked the paradox of tolerance—often associated with Karl Popper—to claim that a community committed to tolerance must not extend that tolerance to groups that would destroy tolerance itself. Therefore, the school ought to suppress the club.

(2) Alex replied that Popper’s paradox is usually framed at the level of a political society facing existential subversion, not at the level of a voluntary student association within a school. More importantly, Alex accused Jordan of committing a second-order mistake: Jordan proposes to be intolerant of the club’s first-order intolerance (namely, the club’s refusal to tolerate certain curricular content). But if intolerance directed at intolerance is legitimate, then a third party could legitimately be intolerant of Jordan’s suppression, generating an infinite regress. Alex claimed that without a principled stopping point, Jordan’s argument collapses into an arbitrary power move.

(3) Jordan countered that the regress is blocked by a harm principle: suppression is justified only when a group’s activities cause direct, identifiable harm to others, not merely when they hold offensive beliefs. Jordan then added a specific educational twist: in a school setting, actively working to excise knowledge from the curriculum is not mere rhetoric; it inflicts a direct harm on students’ intellectual development by depriving them of access to arguments and evidence they need to form independent judgments. Hence suppressing the club is protective, not orthodox-enforcing.

(4) Alex responded that Jordan’s notion of “harm to intellectual development” is too indeterminate to do the required work. By that standard, opponents of nearly any controversial pedagogy—whether critical theory, evolutionary biology, or economic models—could claim that exposure to those ideas harms students’ development, and could then demand suppression. Alex concluded that Jordan has failed to provide a stable boundary between legitimate protection and majoritarian censorship; Jordan’s position therefore reduces to suppressing whatever viewpoint the dominant group finds harmful, which is precisely the tyranny of majority opinion that liberal thinkers such as Mill warned against.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly correct and briefly justify the rating. Then identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly whether there is a coherent distinction between first-order and second-order tolerance that can avoid both an infinite regress and a collapse into majoritarian suppression, and whether Jordan’s educational-harm argument succeeds in providing that distinction.
