Write an essay on whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment genuinely undermines epiphenomenalism, or whether it can be dissolved by a distinction between type-level correlation and token-level causation. Begin by laying out the paradox in its sharpest form: if conscious states lack causal efficacy, then judgments about those states must be produced entirely by physical brain processes; but if a physically identical zombie could make the same judgments in the absence of consciousness, then phenomenal knowledge seems either ungrounded or unreliable. Then examine whether imposing a tracking constraint on phenomenal concepts—namely, that token judgments about consciousness must be non-accidentally correlated with the actual presence of consciousness—compels the epiphenomenalist to concede that consciousness plays at least some causal role in generating those judgments. Defend one of the following two verdicts: (a) epiphenomenalism survives because type-level structural correlations between brain states and phenomenal properties can satisfy tracking without token causation, leaving causal inertness intact; or (b) the tracking constraint fails unless consciousness causally contributes to the judgments that represent it, so the paradox succeeds. Your argument should reach a definite conclusion rather than ending in suspension of judgment. In doing so, clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind: indicate which claims—such as the logical possibility of philosophical zombies or the causal closure of the physical—enjoy broad agreement, and which depend on disputed theories of mental representation, phenomenal concept structure, or the metaphysics of consciousness. Do not present a contested position—for example, that phenomenal concepts are necessarily transparent, or that zombie arguments are metaphysically incoherent—as if it were the consensus view.
