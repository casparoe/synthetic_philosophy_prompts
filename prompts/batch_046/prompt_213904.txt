I'm preparing to moderate a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher—let's call her Dr. Elena Voss—who is defending an unconventional reconstruction of evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics. Her central claim is that if evolutionary origins undermine the reliability of our moral judgments, they do so selectively: they threaten moral particularism far more severely than generalist moral realism. She argues that evolutionary moral psychology reveals domain-general, heuristic-driven cognition—broad rules of thumb about harm, reciprocity, and loyalty—rather than finely calibrated, case-specific judgments. Consequently, particularist claims that moral knowledge bypasses general principles are especially vulnerable to debunking, since there is little evolutionary story to support such context-sensitive precision. Meanwhile, she holds that generalist principles can survive through a constructivist filtering procedure that strips away evolutionary noise, leaving coherent normative structures intact. I want a sequence of interview questions that presses this view at its weakest joints without letting her retreat to standard debunking tropes. Specifically: first, ask how she distinguishes her selective debunking from Sharon Street's broader constructivist challenge to all evaluative attitudes, and whether she needs an independent empirical argument that evolved cognition is structurally generalist rather than modular and plural. Third, press her on the normative residue of her constructivist filter: if evolutionary noise is stripped away, do we retain substantive moral authority, or only procedural coherence vulnerable to the same no-real-authority objection Street faces? Fourth, ask what empirical result—perhaps cross-cultural evidence of entirely context-sensitive moral reasoning with no stable heuristic patterns—would genuinely threaten her view rather than simply trigger an adjustment to the filter.
