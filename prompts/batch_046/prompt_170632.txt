I'm giving the opening remarks tomorrow at a workshop called Attributing Minds: The Intentional Stance and Synthetic Systems. The room is split about evenly between ML engineers who build clinical decision tools and philosphers of mind who've been argueing about Dennett for decades. There's also a couple nurses and one lawyer, all there because they actually have to decide whether to treat a system's output as if it came from someone with reasons.

I want the talk to do real work: it should establish that for the next two days we're not asking the metaphysical question 'does the model literally believe anything?' — that's off the table not because it's answered but because it's not the question that shapes our practices. Instead I want us to ask what commitments we take on when we adopt the intentional stance toward a system in a high-stakes setting. Specifically, there's this tension I keep comeing back to: if a nurse starts saying the triage algorithm 'wants to save the younger patients,' she's not just using a predictive shortcut; she's entering a kind of reciprocal accountability relation where she adjusts her own moral reasoning around the system's 'preferences.' But the system can't hold her accountable in return. So we're in this weird asymmetry — we treat it as an agent for the purpose of coordinating care, but we deny it the standing to blame or praise. I want the speech to set up that asymmetry as the framing problem for the workshop, without resolving whether it's a bug or a feature.

The talk should be about eight minutes, casual but not sloppy, and it needs to land for the engineers (who will roll their eyes if it sounds like mysticism) and the philosophers (who will tune out if it sounds like tech boosterism). It should shape what people can say in the Q&A: no one should open with 'well, clearly it doesn't have beliefs because there's no consciousness,' and no one should open with 'it's just a statistical pattern, end of story.' Both of those have to feel like they're missing the point. Can you write it? I was discussing this with a friend over coffee and she said the real issue is whether adopting the stance is like hiring an interpreter — you depend on them but don't treat them as a co-author — and I think that analogy might be useful but I don't want to overdo it.
