I'm a doctoral candidate in political philosophy specializing in the normative architecture of state-citizen information relations. I'm examining three institutional patterns: first, the German constitutional tradition of informational self-determination, which treats personal data as an extension of personality rights and constrains state collection through purpose-limitation and proportionality; second, the American administrative-openness framework, which presumes government records are public and treats citizen visibility to the state as a democratic corrective to bureaucratic power; and third, China's social-credit system, which aggregates behavioral data into continuously updated reputational scores that determine access to transportation, employment, and credit.

Rather than describing these regimes, I want to reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments each must presuppose about (a) the ontology of the person—whether the self is prior to its data traces, constituted by state visibility, or constructed through aggregated scores; (b) the epistemic authority of the state—whether administrative knowledge of the citizen inherently instrumentalizes, empowers democratic agency, or completes social identity; and (c) the foundations of social trust—whether cooperation depends on privacy boundaries, transparency mechanisms, or calculable reputation.

I'm particularly interested in whether these practices rest on genuinely incompatible anthropological assumptions, or whether they can be interpreted as path-dependent responses within a shared liberal framework. To sharpen the analysis, I want to test the coherence of introducing a principle of strategic opacity—the claim that citizens ought to maintain deliberate informational asymmetries against the state not merely for privacy, but as a structural check on the rationalizing tendencies of administrative governance—within each of the three regimes.

Please reconstruct the implicit theories of the person, knowledge, and cooperation underlying these practices; assess whether they are mutually consistent or necessarily conflict; and examine whether strategic opacity is compatible with any of them. In doing so, please distinguish carefully between claims that are well-established in legal and philosophical scholarship and those that remain genuinely contested among specialists—such as whether informational self-determination presupposes a specifically Kantian conception of autonomy or can be sustained on purely procedural grounds—and do not present a contested interpretive position as if it were the consensus.
