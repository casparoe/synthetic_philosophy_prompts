Consider the institutional practice in modern liberal democracies—evident in constitutional courts, civic education curricula, and deliberative forums—of treating deep political disagreement as essentially a conflict over the correct application of shared liberal principles such as fairness and harm avoidance, while systematically framing conservative or traditionalist appeals to loyalty, authority, or sanctity either as misapplications of those shared principles or as non-rational psychological biases that public reasoning should filter out. Moral foundations research, however, suggests that left-leaning and right-leaning individuals do not share the same underlying moral architecture: they construct morality from partially non-overlapping sets of intuitive foundations. What philosophical commitments about the nature of moral disagreement and about the normative authority of moral psychology does this institutional practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that descriptive differences in psychological foundations carry no normative weight—that knowing one group weights loyalty or purity more heavily tells us nothing about whether those values are genuinely morally relevant? Or does it smuggle in a substantive moral theory according to which only care and fairness count as legitimate public reasons? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, assess whether its treatment of left-right divergence is internally consistent with its own claims to procedural neutrality, and describe what public institutions would have to look like if they replaced that implicit view with one that treats divergent moral-psychological profiles as carrying genuine normative significance rather than as correctable deviations from a single shared foundation.
