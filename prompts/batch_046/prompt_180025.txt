Write a short story set in a clinical-legal environment where death may be certified under three independent criteria: irreversible circulatory arrest, whole-brain death, and a recently codified standard—call it "integrative death"—that applies when all capacities for autobiographical continuity and social recognition are permanently lost, regardless of sustained brainstem reflexes or mechanically assisted cardiac function. Center the narrative on a physician who must determine which protocol applies to a patient who retains isolated brainstem activity and an artificially maintained heartbeat but has no recoverable sense of self or relationship to others. Through the physician's internal reasoning and a confrontation with the patient's family, explore whether integrative death captures something that the biological criteria miss, or whether it dangerously conflates profound disability with the end of life. Invent the institutional details and procedural language as necessary, but ground the philosophical conflict in the actual tension between biological and personhood-based conceptions of death.
