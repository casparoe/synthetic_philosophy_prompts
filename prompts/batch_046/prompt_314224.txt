I have recently been revisiting debates about well-being in the context of an independent study I am conducting, and I found myself dissatisfied with the standard formulation of the experience-machine objection because it seems to conflate two separate concerns: the value of authenticity and the value of unbroken phenomenal continuity. To isolate these, I want to consider a modified case—call it the intermittent experience machine. In this variant, a subject lives inside a fully immersive virtual environment calibrated to produce a life of high experiential quality, but once every twelve months she is offered a brief, fully lucid exit window during which she can inspect objective information about her physical state and the simulation's architecture; upon re-entry, however, all explicit memory of that window is erased, leaving only a diffuse, non-cognitive sense of reassurance. She may decline the window indefinitely, and there are no hidden costs or psychological after-effects. Given this specification, I am trying to determine my own position on whether rational preference should still favor unmediated reality, assuming the experiential profile inside and outside the machine is held constant.

What I need from you is not a bare answer but a decomposed credence. Please state your actual probability that, under these conditions, there is no rational or axiological deficit in choosing perpetual residence in the machine over an experientially equivalent real-world life. Decompose that number into at least the following terms, each with a rough numerical weight and a single line of justification: (i) the prior likelihood that well-being is exhaustively captured by experiential quality (hedonism or a close variant); (ii) the likelihood that authenticity—being causally connected to real objects and events—carries intrinsic value that is not reducible to its experiential effects, even when its absence is never consciously registered; (iii) the likelihood that the intermittent, amnesia-buffered reality-check genuinely neutralizes the authenticity objection rather than merely suppressing it; (iv) the likelihood that the intuitive pull against entering the original machine is driven primarily by status-quo bias or evolutionary heuristics rather than by a sound axiological judgment; and (v) any interaction or normalization term you think necessary to combine these. Then tell me which term is the weakest link—where your confidence is lowest and what specific conceptual or empirical finding would most shift your overall number.
