I am a junior in secondary school preparing for the university entrance examinations in philosophy and mathematics, and I find myself caught in a recurring pattern that I cannot quite diagnose. Every evening at six I sincerely resolve to spend ninety minutes on past exam papers, yet by seven I have almost invariably diverted myself to an online strategy game or to scrolling through short-form video feeds, telling myself that this brief respite will improve my concentration. By ten, I regret the choice acutely and resolve again for the following evening, but the resolution dissolves with the same regularity. The immediate gratification is always smaller than I anticipate, while the cost to my preparation is cumulative and severe.

In an attempt to impose structure, I arranged for my older sister to hold the access credentials to my gaming account and to restore them only after nine-thirty, treating this as an external commitment that my present self could not unilaterally revoke. The mechanism failed within a week: I persuaded her that I needed temporary access to review an online philosophical archive for an essay I was writing, and once the account was reopened I could not bring myself to surrender it again. What troubles me is not merely the lapse but its structure: it feels as though the agent who makes the promise at six in the evening and the agent who breaks it at seven are related but not identical, each treating the other’s commitments as suggestions rather than obligations. I am therefore uncertain whether my failure is a simple deficit of discipline or a deeper incoherence in how I weigh present satisfaction against future welfare.

Could you advise me on how to understand this situation? Specifically, I wish to know whether it is genuinely irrational to choose the smaller present pleasure when I know with near-certainty that I will later condemn the choice, and whether the appropriate remedy lies in stricter external constraints—perhaps ones that remove my ability to renegotiate entirely—or in some internal reconciliation of these competing preferences. I am not asking for study techniques in general, but for an analysis of the reasoning behind the choice and of what I owe to my own future self.
