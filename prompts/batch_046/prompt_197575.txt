I've been thinking about sentencing law and there's this asymmetry that bugs me. Judges can increase prison time if you targeted someone especially vulnerable — that's about your moral culpability — but if you've spent years in prison, genuinely turned things around, and pose zero risk now, that almost never earns you a shorter sentence. The idea seems to be that you owe a fixed amount of punishment based on what you did, not on who you are now.

What view of just desert does that practice actually need? Does it presuppose that blameworthiness is locked in at the moment of action — the kind of thing Caruso attacks when he rejects basic moral responsability? Or could a Dennett-style compatibilist defend it by saying responsibility tracks your rational agency back then, and later change just doesn't erase the debt?

There's also a tension with negligence law, where they say we punish the person not the harm. But criminal law clearly punishes a completed murder more than an attempted one even when the failure was pure luck. So what implicit theory lets the same legal system treat outcomes as irrelevant in one place and decisive in another? Is there one consistent theory of moral responsibility running underneath, or are there two incompatible ones?
