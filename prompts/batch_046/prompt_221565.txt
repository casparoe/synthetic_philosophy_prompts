I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a two-day workshop in Berlin on institutional memory and historical repair. The audience is split evenly between historians and sociologists who study collective trauma, and engineers and policymakers who think about data retention, archive architecture, and legacy system maintenance. I need the remarks to land for both halves without turning into either a history lecture or a tech talk. The conceptual framework should come from comparing Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung with Japan's contested wartime narratives, Turkey's suppressed and reopened archives, and the American South's long negotiation with Confederate monuments and institutional legacy. But I want this comparison to do real structural work in the speech rather than serve as decoration. Specifically, I want to frame these different national paths through the software concept of technical debt: just as a codebase that refuses to delete deprecated modules accumulates hidden fragility and compound cost, a society that avoids breaking changes to its foundational narratives accumulates moral and institutional debt that distorts future development. Germany's postwar project can be presented as a deliberate breaking-change refactoring, while the other cases represent alternative maintenance strategies—incremental patching, feature-flagging denial, or running deprecated systems in production. This framing should shape what I can and cannot say: I should avoid moralizing conclusions that declare one society "fixed" and others "broken," and instead present memory work as an engineering problem about sustainability, transparency, and the cost of deferred maintenance. The speech should run about five minutes spoken, should address the room as colleagues rather than students, and must remain in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if writing a letter or an email to the attendees. Please do not include any bibliography, reading list, citations, or requests for them, and please do not quote any real texts in quotation marks. I also ask that you not invent specific historical events I could be caught misrepresenting; keep the references at the level of well-known national patterns.
