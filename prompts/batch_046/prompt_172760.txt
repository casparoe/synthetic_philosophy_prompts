I've been trying to work through the paradox of tragedy for a paper, but I built this argument and now it feels like it proves way too much -- like, if it's right then enjoying a sad movie should commit you to enjoying actual grief. Here goes:

(1) When we watch a tragedy we feel genuine sadness, not fake sadness.
(2) If we genuinely feel sadness but also enjoy the overall experience, then we must be deriving pleasure from the sadness itself, not just from surrounding features like plot or acting.
(3) So enjoying tragedy means enjoying the feeling of sadness.
(4) Now, sadness is sadness regardless of cause -- the phenomology doesn't change just because it's triggered by fiction.
(5) So if I enjoy being sad about Oedipus, I should also enjoy being sad about my actual friend going blind, since the feeling-state is the same.
(6) But that's obviously wrong; enjoying real grief is monstrous.
(7) Therefore, either (3) is false and we don't actually enjoy tragedy's sadness (which seems to deny the phenomenon), or (5) is false and there's some hidden difference.
(8) The hidden difference people usually cite is aesthetic distance or control -- I can turn off the movie. But control is about my power over the cause, not about the feeling itself. There's also this idea of simulated affective commitment, where in art we feel the emotion but don't commit to its real-world behavioral implications. But even if my commitment is simulated, the sadness itself is still real, and I'm still enjoying it.
(9) There isn't some other feature of the feeling that differs -- sadness is sadness.
(10) So (5) stands, (3) stands, and we're stuck with the conclusion that tragedy-lovers should welcome real grief. Which is absurd.

I think the slip is somewhere between (3) and (5) but I can't name it. Maybe there's an equivocation on "enjoy"? Or maybe I'm missing some distinction about emotional framing? I really want to keep (1) and (4) if I can -- they seem like the least negotiable parts. Where does this actually break?
