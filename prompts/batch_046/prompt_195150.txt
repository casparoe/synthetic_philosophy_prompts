After a long and somewhat circular argument with a friend — she kept insisting that freedom is essentially about having real options open to you, while I kept countering that it is fundamentally about not being subject to anyone else's will — I want to construct a more rigorous diagnostic instrument than either of us could manage over dinner. Build a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts actual philosophical positions on liberty (not just the labels people adopt) using four concrete, case-based branch nodes. Each node must be phrased as a claim or scenario that a respondent could answer without knowing which theoretical camp the answer places them in.

The four branch nodes are:
1. The benevolent-master case: a ruler retains absolute legal authority over your life choices but, out of benevolence, never exercises it. Does this arrangement diminish your liberty?
2. The resource-conditions case: the state expands your effective options through education or unconditional income, but attaches behavioral conditions (attendance, reporting, compliance with norms). Is the net effect on your liberty positive, negative, or null?
3. The predictive-preemption case (the specific twist I want included): an algorithmic system structurally removes options from your environment — for instance, filtering employment listings or travel routes based on statistical demographic profiles — without any identifiable human agent issuing a command, being aware of your particular situation, or even intending to constrain you. Does this constitute a liberty violation?
4. The internalized-norm case: you inhabit social norms so thoroughly internalized that you never experience them as external obstacles; you would not choose otherwise even if the norms disappeared. Do these norms reduce your liberty?

Construct the tree from the logical space of yes/no/dependent answers first, then place the named positions in the resulting cells. The named positions that must appear are: (a) pure negative liberty (interference-only, requiring an identifiable interfering agent), (b) positive liberty (centered on self-mastery, effective capability, or the absence of internal obstacles to rational choice), and (c) republican liberty (centered on non-domination, regardless of whether interference actually occurs). You must also include at least one hybrid or unnamed combination that mixes answers across these three.

For each leaf, provide: the position name (or 'unnamed'), a one-sentence statement of its core commitment, and one sentence describing its most awkward consequence — especially for the unnamed positions. Where two named positions fall into the same answer combination, explicitly flag that the tree cannot separate them on these diagnostics and explain why. Identify at least one logically possible combination that remains unoccupied, and briefly diagnose whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, explain specifically how the predictive-preemption case exposes a gap in one of the named theories — for instance, whether republican non-domination can apply without a master, or whether negative liberty's agent-requirement leaves structural algorithmic filtering unclassified.

The instrument should be usable without any external lookup: no bibliographies, no citation requests, no reading lists, and no dependence on obscure or highly technical literature. Write it at a level of precision suitable for a philosophy seminar handout, keeping the tone formal and focused.
