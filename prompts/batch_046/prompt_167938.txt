Please explain the conceptual tension between covering-law explanation and narrative explanation in historiography, concentrating on what I will call “counterfactual anchoring”: the practice of explaining an outcome by identifying a specific event as a necessary turning point, such that had it not occurred the subsequent trajectory would have been substantially different. First, clarify how a covering-law model treats these counterfactual dependencies—must they be replaced by universal laws plus initial conditions, or can they serve as legitimate explanatory premises? Second, articulate what explanatory work the identification of a particular turning point does that a law-plus-initial-conditions schema fails to replicate, especially regarding the selection of a narrative beginning and end. Third, examine the objection that counterfactual anchoring smuggles in modal realism about unactualized historical paths: does the narrative historian need to commit to the reality of those alternative paths in order to claim that an event was decisive, or can the counterfactual be interpreted purely as a heuristic for measuring causal weight? In your response, please separate what is broadly settled among philosophers of history from what remains genuinely contested—particularly over whether narrative explanation is irreducible or merely a pragmatic presentation of covering-law structure—and do not present any contested position as though it were the consensus.
