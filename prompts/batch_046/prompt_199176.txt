I'm constructing a taxonomy for a graduate seminar on value theory. Please build a decision tree—branch nodes phrased as concrete questions a student could answer without knowing the position labels—that sorts views on whether pleasure and suffering are commensurable, with lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism as the focal tension.

Structure the tree around these four diagnostic splits in order:

(1) Can suffering and pleasure be mapped onto a single cardinal scale at all? (If no, move to qualitative asymmetry.)
(2) If yes, does suffering possess lexical priority over pleasure—that is, can any quantity of pleasure ever compensate for any non-zero suffering? (Distinguish strong lexical, weak threshold, and no priority.)
(3) If there is lexical priority, is it triggered only by extreme suffering or by any suffering whatsoever?
(4) If they are incommensurable on one scale, is the failure quantitative (different dimensions that resist aggregation) or normative (pleasure lacks authority to justify suffering)?

At each leaf provide: (a) a name or 'unnamed' if the combination is unoccupied; (b) the core claim in one sentence; (c) its most awkward practical commitment—for example, being forced to prevent the existence of any being that might suffer mildly, or being forced to accept that a world of enormous bliss plus one tortured being could be morally best.

The tree must accommodate these positions somewhere: standard lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism, classical hedonic commensurability, and pure qualitative asymmetry. Note any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and flag any branch combination that looks incoherent or merely unexplored rather than unpopular.

Keep the tree to about six leaves. Audience: philosophy graduate students familiar with basic utilitarianism but not specialists in value incommensurability.

End with a line beginning Verdict: that states which leaf corresponds to standard lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and which to pure hedonic commensurability.
