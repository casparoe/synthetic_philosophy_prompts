I have no formal background in history or philosophy of science, but after reading several popular accounts of how epidemics reshaped labor systems, how prolonged cooling altered agricultural patterns, and how a single volcanic winter disrupted harvests across continents, I found myself arguing with friends about what it actually means to say that a germ, a climate shift, or an eruption caused something in the past. We kept returning to the same confusion: are we giving these forces a kind of agency, or are we just describing background conditions in dramatic language? I want a clear, branching map that lets a layperson like me locate my own intuitions without first learning an academic vocabulary.

Please construct a taxonomy tree with no more than eight branch nodes, each expressed as a concrete claim or scenario that a non-specialist could evaluate on its own terms. The first split should ask whether a non-human force exhibits directional consistency—pursuing an outcome across time in a way that resembles strategy—or only mechanical repetition. From there, branch on whether attributing historical influence to nature constitutes causal ventriloquism, by which I mean using nature's voice to obscure human decisions about response and adaptation, or whether it serves as a legitimate correction to overly human-centered narratives. Next, split on whether the chief error of environmental determinism is empirical—societies always adapt, so nature never truly decides—or conceptual, because large-scale correlation is mistaken for intentional cause. The final divisions should distinguish whether calling a famine drought-caused is mainly an empirical statement about crop failure or mainly a moral statement about the failure of governance to prepare, and whether forces of enormous magnitude should be narrated as actors or treated with scale-mismatch, meaning they are causally real but should never be personified since magnitude is not purpose.

The leaves must include at least the following: a strict determinist view that treats nature as the primary driver of change; an agency-without-intention position that grants causal prominence but denies that responsibility shifts away from humans; a human-responsibility-first stance that treats all environmental attribution as rhetorical cover; an unnamed combination for anyone who accepts directional consistency, rejects causal ventriloquism, and also rejects the claim that societies always adapt; and the scale-mismatch position described above. For any pair of leaves that agree on every preceding branch and therefore cannot be separated by this tree, flag them explicitly rather than inventing a false distinction. Every node should reference a specific, tangible case—such as whether the Tambora eruption of 1815 should be narrated as an actor that disrupted European politics or as a background condition that revealed existing political fragility—so that the tree sorts people by their judgments about real scenarios rather than by the labels they prefer. I am preparing this for an informal discussion group of interested non-experts, so please do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or references to obscure technical frameworks that require specialist training; keep the language accessible, but make the conceptual distinctions sharp.
