I am drafting the final examination for our doctoral seminar in political philosophy and philosophy of social science, and I need an essay prompt together with a model answer and a granular marking scheme. The course has worked through John Dewey’s experimentalist account of inquiry—knowledge as the outcome of experimentally tested hypotheses rather than the representation of an antecedent reality—and his corresponding claim that democracy is best understood not as a constitutional form but as the application of social intelligence to collective problems. I want the exam to distinguish students who have internalized the conceptual architecture from those who can only recite that democracy is a way of life.

Please design a 90-minute essay question, approximately 1,200 to 1,500 words, carrying 25 marks, that forces students to adjudicate a specific tension within Dewey’s framework: he treats democratic participation both as an instrumental mechanism for improving collective problem-solving (the experimentalist thesis) and as a non-instrumental condition for the full development of human capacities (the developmental thesis). To prevent pure summary, embed the question in the following hypothetical institutional design, which is constructed for this exam and does not correspond to any existing policy.

The hypothetical Adaptive Risk Council manages ecological adaptation through iterative, experimentally validated interventions. Technical experts design the trials, generate probabilistic forecasts, and propose revisions based on observed outcomes. Affected citizens do not formulate the statistical hypotheses or set the parameters; they vote at fixed intervals on whether to continue, suspend, or revise the current intervention, and they may submit qualitative reports drawn from lived experience that experts must formally address in the next trial design, but the citizens’ role is confined to this continuation-or-suspension vote and to experiential reporting.

The exam question should ask students to determine whether this council realizes Dewey’s experimentalist democracy or betrays it by reducing participation to an instrumental veto over expert-constructed inquiries. Specifically, students must address: whether Dewey’s concept of the public can be satisfied when citizens are separated from the formulation of hypotheses; whether the iterative, fallible structure fulfills his criteria for inquiry even if democratic control is limited to continuation or suspension; and whether his non-instrumental commitment to participation as self-development is violated, and if so, whether that violation undermines his experimentalist thesis or only his normative anthropology.

Then provide:

1. The exam question itself, with explicit instructions on time, length, and the need for conceptual argument rather than historical summary.

2. A model answer that makes five specific conceptual moves necessary for a top mark—each move should explain not merely what Dewey holds but how the hypothetical council tests the coherence of his position.

3. A marking scheme totaling 25 marks, allocating specific point values to each of the five moves, with a one-line description of the typical mistake that costs marks in each category. Include also a brief distinction between a passing answer (around 13 marks) and an excellent answer (22 marks or above).

Write in a polished, formal register appropriate for a doctoral student submitting assessment materials to a faculty examination committee. Do not include instructions for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations in the exam prompt, since the assessment tests conceptual command rather than reference retrieval, and avoid verbatim quotations from Dewey’s texts.
