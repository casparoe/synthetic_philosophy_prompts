Consider the following argument, framed in the spirit of Hofstadter’s Ant Fugue and the hypothesis that an ant colony—call it Aunt Hillary—might constitute a single cognitive agent rather than a mere aggregate of insects. The premises each seem defensible, yet they appear to entail that distributed systems with no plausible claim to consciousness are literally minded, or else that minds can split and fuse without discrete identity conditions. I want to know where the error lies, but I am reluctant to abandon the idea that collective dynamics can fix representational content.

(1) A system is minded only if it possesses representations with determinate content that guide its behavior in a unified, adaptive manner.
(2) In Aunt Hillary, no individual ant bears representational content such as “food lies to the northeast” or “the nest is under attack”; these contents are fixed solely by the collective dynamics of pheromone gradients, labor allocation, and feedback loops.
(3) Nevertheless, the colony behaves as a unified adaptive agent, so by (1) it must possess representations—just ones that are collectively borne rather than localized in any constituent.
(4) Now consider a hypothetical “merged colony”: two previously independent colonies are gradually fused so that their pheromone networks fully intermix, yielding a single functional architecture with no internal boundary.
(5) By (2) and (3), this fused architecture is one mind, not two.
(6) If we slowly separate the merged architecture back into two isolated subgroups—each eventually regaining independent dynamics and its own reproductive center—there is no non-arbitrary point at which one mind dies and two new ones come into being.
(7) Therefore, collective minds, if they exist, can undergo continuous fission and fusion without satisfying discrete diachronic continuity conditions.
(8) But mental continuity—conditions that prevent arbitrary splitting and merging—is a necessary feature of anything properly called a mind.
(9) Hence collective entities like Aunt Hillary either violate mental continuity or are not minds, which contradicts (3).
(10) To avoid contradiction, we must reject the claim that collective dynamics alone can ever constitute representation-bearing minds, however unified and adaptive they appear.

The conclusion denies Aunt Hillary any mentality, yet I would prefer to preserve (1) and (3) and instead pay a price closer to (8). Identify the least defensible step, explain why it initially looked plausible, and then price the three main alternatives: giving up the continuity requirement for minds, accepting that traffic grids, immune systems, or markets might also be minded, or denying that collective dynamics can ever fix representational content. Which rejection is least costly, and what does it commit you to about the ontology of group cognition?
