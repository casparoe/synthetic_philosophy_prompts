Below is a sketch for a short essay titled “Non-Separable Accuracy and the Limits of Coherence Dominance.” I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citation check; I want critical comments on the argument’s structure, its conceptual coherence, and whether it mischaracterizes the accuracy-first program.

The essay proceeds as follows. The standard accuracy-first argument, developed by philosophers such as James Joyce, demonstrates that any incoherent credence function is dominated in Brier-score accuracy by some coherent probabilistic function. This dominance result is typically derived using a separable scoring rule: the inaccuracy of a belief about P is calculated independently of beliefs about Q. I want to challenge the implicit modularity assumption. In actual reasoning, beliefs form an inferential network: an agent’s confidence in P often depends on her confidence in intermediate premises linking evidence to P. I therefore propose a “network-weighted Brier score,” in which the penalty for error on P is scaled by the agent’s aggregate confidence in the inferential path supporting P. Under this non-separable rule, it becomes possible for an incoherent set of credences to achieve lower expected network inaccuracy than any coherent set, because incoherence permits strategic hedging across linked premises. For instance, an agent might hold P and not-P at high confidence in different sub-networks, reducing the weighted penalty in each branch, even though this violates probabilism globally.

If correct, coherence is not always locally accuracy-maximizing. Yet I am unsure whether my weighting scheme smuggles in pragmatic or decision-theoretic considerations—perhaps caring more about premises we rely on is merely caring more about practical outcomes, not about pure epistemic accuracy. I also wonder whether the argument confuses evaluation of a static credence set with the dynamics of belief revision: even if an incoherent set scores well on the network measure at one time, updating rules might eventually collapse it into coherence.

Please comment specifically on these points: (1) Is the network-weighted Brier score a legitimate epistemic utility function, or does it conflate accuracy with practical importance? (2) Does the possibility of incoherent dominance genuinely threaten the accuracy-first justification of probabilism, or does the standard argument already contain resources to block non-separable scoring rules? (3) Where is the sketch weakest—conceptually, technically, or in its framing of the relevant literature? Be direct and precise; I am particularly interested in whether the hedging example actually succeeds in producing a dominance reversal, and whether you see a hidden circularity in using an agent’s own confidence in inferential links to weight her accuracy penalties.
