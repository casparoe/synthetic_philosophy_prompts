A philosopher of science whose seminar you attended has offered consistent judgments on the following cases: (i) the Lorenz system governed by deterministic Navier-Stokes dynamics with infinitesimal initial-condition divergence—she calls it fully deterministic and says long-range unpredictability is purely practical, not ontological; (ii) a digital computer running a deterministic pseudorandom-number generator with a concealed seed—she calls it deterministic and holds that predictability is fully recoverable in principle by anyone who knows the algorithm and seed; (iii) a quantum decay event interpreted through the Copenhagen framework—she denies determinism, on the grounds that the law fixes probabilities rather than outcomes; (iv) a macroscopic deterministic system observed solely through an instrument physically coupled to it, where every measurement destroys the information required for the next prediction—she maintains that determinism remains intact globally, though no embedded observer can achieve predictive closure; and (v) a deterministic universe containing embodied agents whose brain states fully determine their actions, and who use those actions to alter the physical trajectory they inhabit—she insists determinism is not undermined, because the agents' reasoning and behavior are themselves nodes in the deterministic causal network.

Consider now a new case: a physically closed deterministic chaotic system—no information enters or leaves—containing an embedded universal computer that holds a complete bit-for-bit physical representation of the entire system, including the computer and its own computation. The computer applies the system's deterministic law to this representation in order to predict the next state, but because the representation includes the computer performing the prediction, the task is structurally analogous to a self-referential halting problem: the computation cannot complete consistently. The physical dynamics nevertheless continue in strict accordance with the deterministic law, with the computer's incomplete calculation physically altering the trajectory in a law-governed manner.

Predict her verdict: does she regard this system as deterministic, or does she take the irreducible computational blockage to undermine determinism? Identify at least two plausible implicit rules that explain her earlier judgments—one centered on exceptionless law-governance regardless of epistemic access, and another that treats irreducible barriers to predictive computation as conceptually incompatible with determinism. Say which rule best fits her track record and why. Finally, specify one further case—ideally one that modifies only the informational closure of the system, leaving the self-referential computer intact—that would most clearly adjudicate between the two rules.
