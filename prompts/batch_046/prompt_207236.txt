I'm currently enrolled in an upper-division philosophy of biology course, and after reading Gould and Lewontin's critique of adaptationism together with Gould and Vrba's work on exaptation, I'm trying to work out my own reflective equilibrium on when an evolutionary explanation counts as adaptationist in the legitimate sense versus when it overreaches. I have gut verdicts about several concrete traits, but they don't obviously fall out of one simple rule, and I'm particularly unsure whether I'm consistently weighing historical origin against present utility.

Here is the pattern of my judgments. The panda's radial sesamoid "thumb" is an adaptation: selection specifically shaped this modified wrist bone for bamboo manipulation, even though it is clearly jury-rigged rather than purpose-built. The bright red color of oxygenated human blood is a spandrel—an unavoidable chemical consequence of hemoglobin's structure with no selective history targeting redness itself. Human menopause, including in pilot whales, strikes me as a genuine adaptation under the grandmother hypothesis: selection favored reproductive cessation in order to increase inclusive fitness through grand-offspring investment. Human literacy and alphabetic writing, by contrast, are spandrels in the strict sense: they exapt language and visual-tracking capacities but have no evolutionary history of selection, being purely cultural technologies. The domain-specific preparedness for fearing snakes and spiders in young children feels like an adaptation—an evolved learning bias shaped by ancestral danger. The prominent human chin, despite being a distinctive feature of our species, strikes me as an allometric spandrel: a structural byproduct of jaw reduction and dental change under developmental constraints, rather than something selection engineered for signaling or function.

Is there a single precise principle—perhaps one that privileges specific selective history over current functional integration, or that distinguishes evolutionary from cultural origins—that delivers all six verdicts? Apply it to a case I have not listed: the retention of lymphoid tissue in the human vermiform appendix, which appears to serve an immune function now but likely originated as a digestive structure. If the principle forces me to overturn one of my judgments to maintain simplicity, tell me which one and why. Please also say explicitly what would change your mind—specifically, which argument or empirical finding about evolutionary history would lead to a different verdict, and where in the reasoning the account is most likely to fail.
