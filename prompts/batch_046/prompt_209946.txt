I have been working through Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism in the context of a recent discussion about engineered biological systems, and I have constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems far stronger than I intend. (1) In Al-Ghazālī's view, fire does not burn cotton by any intrinsic power; rather, God creates the burning directly, with the presence of fire serving only as the customary occasion that never guarantees the effect, since God could always create the contrary. (2) I recently encountered a synthetic-biology setup in which a designed protein folds only when a specific acoustic frequency is present, yet the sound waves do not physically interact with the protein at all—an external controller detects the frequency and then injects the folding agent, so the sound is present as an occasion but is genuinely causally inert. (3) This engineered system seems to realize occasionalism precisely: the occasion is entirely passive. (4) Now apply the same structure to human action: my decision to raise my arm is like the acoustic signal, and the arm's rising is like the protein's folding, with God as the external controller who creates both in correlation. (5) If occasionalism is correct, then my will has no more causal efficacy over my body than the sound wave has over the protein. (6) But if my will has no causal efficacy, then moral responsibility collapses, because responsibility requires that my will actually produces the action. (7) Al-Ghazālī clearly wants to preserve moral responsibility and divine justice. (8) Therefore, if occasionalism consistently applies to mental and physical events alike, it undermines the very ethical framework it was designed to protect, and we should reject it—yet I am reluctant to reject (1) through (3). Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Is there a hidden equivocation between physical and mental occasions, or does occasionalism genuinely pay the price of rendering human agency illusory? If I must keep either the consistency of occasionalism or the reality of moral agency, which should I abandon, and what does that cost?
