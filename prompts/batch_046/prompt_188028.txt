Question: Explain Bradley's regress argument against treating relations as real entities distinct from their relata. How does Bradley's doctrine of internal relations purport to stop the regress? Identify one serious objection to that doctrine, and assess whether applying Bradley's solution to Frege's problem of propositional unity succeeds or fails.

Student answer:
Bradley's regress starts from the idea that if a relation R connects two terms a and b, then R must itself be connected to a and to b by further relations R' and R'', which in turn require further relations, generating an infinite regress. To avoid this, Bradley denies that relations are external entities that stand between terms. Instead, he holds that relations are internal: they are not separate from the terms they relate but are modifications or aspects of those terms themselves. Because there is no separate relational entity, there is nothing that needs to be connected, and the regress stops.

A major objection is that if every relation is internal to its terms, then any change in a term's relations changes its nature, which seems to collapse into monism—everything is internally related to everything else, leaving no genuinely independent entities.

When we turn to Frege's problem of propositional unity—how the parts of a proposition are bound together into a single thought—the situation is structurally identical. Frege worries that if we need a special binding relation to unite the concept and object, we face the same regress. Bradley's internal-relation solution applies directly: the concept and object are not externally joined but are internally related within the unified proposition. Thus, propositional unity is just a special case of internal relation, and Frege's problem dissolves without needing an unsaturated concept or a special act of judgment.
