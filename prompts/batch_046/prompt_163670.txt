I serve on the Interdisciplinary Research Ethics Council at a research university, and at our meeting next Thursday we must reach a preliminary decision on whether to institute a provisional “Micro-Physical Moral Status Protocol.” The proposal, submitted jointly by a philosopher of physics and a quantum information theorist on our faculty, asks that all grant applications involving sustained quantum coherence manipulation or high-energy particle disruption include an ethical risk assessment addressing the possibility of “experiential fragmentation”—a harm conceived specifically under constitutive panpsychism, on the view that if fundamental physical processes involve sentient properties, then interfering with their experiential binding could constitute a form of suffering or degradation even in the absence of macroscopic consciousness. The proposal is careful not to assert panpsychism as established; instead, it frames the protocol as a precautionary extension of our existing obligations to avoid causing suffering wherever it plausibly occurs, analogous to how we review animal protocols without requiring proof that the specific species is sentient in exactly the way we imagine. Several council members—particularly those from the physical sciences—argue that this imports metaphysical speculation into procedural ethics and would impose an unworkable burden on basic research. Others counter that ethical review already operates under conditions of metaphysical uncertainty, for instance about the inner lives of non-human animals or about future artificial systems, and that excluding micro-physical sentience by default is itself a philosophical choice disguised as neutrality.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—adopt the protocol in full, adopt a modified version with clearly defined scope limits, or reject it—with a specific implementation timeline if adoption is recommended. The reasoning should be accessible to council members without specialist training in philosophy of mind, should treat seriously the strongest consideration on the side you do not take, and should avoid presupposing that panpsychism is either established or discredited. I was discussing the case with colleagues over lunch and found myself unable to articulate why the precautionary argument does or does not carry weight here, so I need the memo to clarify that point for me as well as for the committee.
