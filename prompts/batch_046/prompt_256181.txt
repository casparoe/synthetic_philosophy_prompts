As a journalist covering science, institutions, and public memory, I face a recurring editorial problem: every few months a proposal surfaces to rename a university building, remove a campus statue, or redesignate a street named after a scientist or engineer. Some proposals expose genuine moral failures embedded in commemorative space; others confuse technical eponymy with moral endorsement, or seek to erase the only public trace of a marginalized contributor. I need a reusable decision procedure—something I can run in about fifteen minutes before deciding whether to cover a proposal as a serious controversy, frame it as a category mistake, or treat it as an institutional values question outside my beat.

Ground the procedure in the ethics of commemoration, particularly the distinction between honoring an achievement and honoring a person, between symbolic harm and material exclusion, and between contextualization and erasure. The procedure should take the form of ordered questions: for each, state what counts as a sufficient factual answer, where the answer is normative rather than empirical, and where the procedure stops with a clear editorial directive—such as "do not frame as a live controversy"—versus where it explicitly hands the decision back to me because it requires a judgment call about values I must own.

The procedure must preserve, in simplified form, three distinctions that philosophy of commemoration treats as load-bearing: whether the commemorative object functions primarily as an index to a living technical concept (representational dependency), whether the harm claimed is present and specifically tied to the space rather than diffuse historical guilt, and whether removal would eliminate a narrative that cannot be substituted elsewhere (narrative substitutability). Flag the steps that will misfire—especially when the honored figure is dead but the terminology remains active in current practice, or when the proposal originates from a body with no direct stakeholder relationship to the alleged harm.

Apply the procedure to three cases, including one where the recommended framing conflicts with my initial intuition:

1. A proposal to rename a university physics building named after a Nobel laureate whose theoretical framework remains standard in textbooks, but who also directed a classified weapons program documented to have caused civilian harm; the building houses active laboratories with no museum or interpretive function.
2. A proposal to remove a bronze statue of a nineteenth-century naturalist in a public garden that bears no interpretive plaque; the naturalist's taxonomic system is still in use, and he publicly advocated racial hierarchies that shaped his collection practices.
3. A proposal to rename a street originally designated for a specific mathematical proof, now named after the living mathematician who published it; the mathematician recently made public statements denying anthropogenic climate change, and the proposal comes from a municipal board with no direct connection to climate harm, seeking to honor "scientific integrity" generally.

For each case, trace the path through the ordered questions, identify the exact step where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be owned by the editor, and state the editorial framing the procedure recommends—including the case where I will likely want to reject that framing.

Finally, specify what the procedure cannot do: distinguish good-faith institutional reform from performative moral hygiene, predict whether a renamed or removed object will be forgotten or mythologized, or resolve deep disagreement about which historical figures deserve permanent presence in public space.
