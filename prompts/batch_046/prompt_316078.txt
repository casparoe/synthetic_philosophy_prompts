I am a junior serving on my school's five-member student research ethics committee. Our charter contains an unusual provision: any single member may unilaterally suspend a proposed student project if they have serious ethical reservations, without requiring a vote or consensus. The rationale given when the charter was adopted was precautionary—that allowing one genuinely harmful study through is far worse than blocking several harmless ones. This semester, a colleague suspended a psychology survey on adolescent stress because she believed the questionnaire could cause discomfort, despite the rest of us judging the risk minimal and the scientific value significant. Now another member is considering suspending a biology exhibit on genetic variation for reasons I find unpersuasive. I am reluctant to invoke my own suspension power merely to counter his, since that seems to lead toward an escalating cycle of individual vetoes. Yet if I remain silent, I fear the committee will continue to block projects that a clear majority of us actually support. Is there a principled way for an individual member to decide when unilateral action is warranted, or does this institutional design contain a structural flaw that makes over-blocking inevitable regardless of our personal judgments?
