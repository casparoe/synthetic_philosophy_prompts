If hyperbolic discounting is best understood not as a cognitive bias but as an optimal intertemporal bargaining equilibrium among diachronic agency-slices—where each temporal part maximizes local utility constrained by the credible commitments of its successors—then the standard formulation of akrasia seems to rest on a false metaphysics of synchronic unity. After all, if there is no single will to be overridden, but only a distributed optimization problem across successive selves, describing an agent as acting against her better judgment appears to be a category mistake rather than a description of rational failure. Given this framing, should philosophers abandon weakness of will as a normative concept altogether and redirect evaluation solely toward the stability conditions of these intertemporal bargains, or is there a coherent way to preserve akrasia as a genuine failure of agency within a fragmented, slice-level model of practical reason?
