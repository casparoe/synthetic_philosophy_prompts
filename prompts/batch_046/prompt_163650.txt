I keep seeing a specific argumentative pattern in debates about conceptual analysis—especially in philosophy of mind and moral philosophy—that I do not have a precise term for. A theorist advances a claim and implicitly partitions it into an analytic core (true in virtue of meaning or concept) and an empirical periphery (the contingent conditions under which it applies). The crucial feature is that the partition is not fixed in advance by explicit, testable criteria. Here is the pattern in action.

When empirical findings or counterexamples threaten the claim, the theorist expands the analytic core to absorb the threatened element, declaring it conceptually necessary and therefore immune to refutation. The empirical periphery shrinks, and the theory survives untouched.

But when empirical findings happen to support the claim, the theorist does the opposite: they highlight the empirical periphery, present the result as a successful confirmation of the overall theory, and treat the analytic core as a harmless framing device rather than an unfalsifiable shell.

Consider two instances. In a debate about mental causation, a philosopher initially frames causal efficacy as an empirical hypothesis about neural-behavioral correlations. When those correlations are questioned, the causal claim is recast as analytically true of our concept of mental action, while the correlation data is relegated to a synthetic implementation detail that may vary. In ethics, a theorist treats a principle of distributive fairness as conceptually fixed; cross-cultural institutional variation is explained away as variation in the synthetic embedding conditions. Yet when different cultures with similar institutions converge on the same outcome, that convergence is cited as empirical vindication of the principle itself.

This is not just an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, because the adjustment is not saving a single prediction but systematically relocating the boundary between meaning and world in whichever direction protects the theory—defensively to block refutation, opportunistically to claim empirical credit. It also differs from plain Quinean holism: holism denies that there is a sharp analytic-synthetic boundary at all, whereas here a boundary is actively deployed, but it is drawn post hoc and asymmetrically.

So my questions are: Is there an established name in philosophy of science or philosophy of language for this exact double-use of an analytic-empirical division—defensive immunization paired with opportunistic empirical credit? How precisely does it differ from Lakatosian research-program protection, Popperian immunization strategies, and Quine's own rejection of the distinction? If the literature does not isolate this pattern, I want you to propose a precise definition, a name, and clear boundary conditions: what counts as an instance, what is merely ordinary theory revision, and where the edge cases lie. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the conceptual carving—what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.
