I'm working through Camus's arguments against capital punishment for a course on political ethics, and I'm trying to understand whether his central charge — that the state becomes a murderer by institutionalising premeditation — can survive a particular reformulation. Write a short dialogue between a defender of Camus's position and a critic who grants that the state must not replicate the criminal's logic but thinks Camus's reasoning is vulnerable to a distinction between collective procedural judgement and individual malicious intent.

The defender should present Camus's view that execution is bureaucratically premeditated murder, and that its modern concealment removes the public spectacle necessary for deterrence while allowing the state to preserve moral authority only by hiding its violence. The critic should reply that Camus illicitly collapses the diachronic, revisable structure of legal decision-making into the synchronic psychology of a single agent, and should inject the further claim that the prolonged anticipation Camus deplores — the phenomenological cruelty of waiting — is not intrinsic to capital punishment but an administrative failure; an instantaneous, transparent execution, she argues, could maintain retributive justice without this cruelty. Have them debate whether this saves the death penalty from Camus's hypocrisy charge, or whether it merely relocates state violence behind a fiction of collective, timeless intent. Both characters should respond directly to one another's points, neither should be a strawman, and the dialogue should not request citations, bibliographies, or external reading lists.
