In my research for a novel about a Theravada monastery in Myanmar, I have encountered an institutional practice whose philosophical foundations puzzle me. The monastery runs a hospice funded by lay donors who understand themselves to be generating merit. Its rules are strict: monks provide shelter, food, and companionship to the dying, but they withhold analgesics that might cloud consciousness in the final hours, on the belief that the mental clarity at death shapes the quality of rebirth. At the same time, the monastery's ethical instruction holds that karma attaches solely to cetanā—intention—not to the consequences that actually follow from an act; a well-intentioned donation produces merit whether or not the recipient is truly comforted. So the institution treats outcomes as irrelevant to the moral worth of the agent's action, yet treats outcomes—specifically, the patient's conscious mental state—as decisive for institutional policy about care. What implicit theory of ethical value, or of the agent-patient distinction in moral evaluation, does this combination presuppose? Is it coherent to maintain an intention-only account for the merit-maker and an outcome-sensitive rule for the hospice, or does the practice quietly smuggle in a consequentialist logic that the explicit doctrine rejects? And if the monastery replaced its framework with a straightforward consequentialism—say, one that seeks to maximize well-being across lives and rebirths—which concrete elements of the hospice protocol and of the merit-based funding model would become unstable?
