I am a novelist and a voting member of the board of the Meridian Foundation for Literary Arts, a private endowment that supports experimental fiction and poetry. At Thursday's meeting we will vote on whether to authorize Project Augury, a one-year pilot in our "Speculative Futures" grant line. Under the pilot, twenty percent of the pool would be allocated through a reputation-weighted prediction market rather than through conventional peer review. Specifically, reviewers and previously funded authors would stake non-transferable reputation tokens on forecasts of each project's long-term cultural influence, defined as a composite index of critical reception, curriculum adoption, and intertextual citation over a fifteen-year horizon. A quadratic weighting formula would limit the influence of any single high-reputation stakeholder. The market settlement price would determine funding rank automatically, with no deliberative override except for legal compliance.

Please draft the formal opinion I should circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision—approve the pilot outright, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions and a mandatory sunset review—together with a precise time frame for implementation or termination. The reasoning should engage the strongest argument in favor: that conventional peer review in niche literary fields is vulnerable to strategic misranking, network capture, and homophily, and that a properly designed prediction market can be more strategy-resistant and epistemically diverse. It should also treat with equal seriousness the strongest argument against: that aesthetic and political value in literature is not reducible to a forecastable metric; that reputation staking risks replicating rather than dissolving existing hierarchies; and that substituting predictive aggregation for deliberative judgment conflicts with our institutional mandate to support genuinely disruptive work that may be initially unpredictable.

Please state, within the document, how confident you are in the claims you make and identify the single point about which you are least certain. The tone should be suitable for circulation to board members who are not specialists in philosophy or economics—formal, precise, and without unnecessary jargon—while still addressing the conceptual stakes directly.
