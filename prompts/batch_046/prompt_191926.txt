I have been working through whether habitat destruction can ever reduce wild animal suffering, and I want to check whether my intuitions across concrete cases hang together or collapse into contradiction.

Consider five scenarios. First, a wildfire clears a dense meadow that supported enormous populations of short-lived grasshoppers and voles—creatures with extreme fecundity, constant predation, and very brief lives. My judgment is that this probably lowers net suffering, not because the deaths are pleasant, but because so many potential lives of intense suffering are prevented.

Second, a wetland is drained, destroying a stable, low-density population of long-lived herons and turtles with low reproductive rates, long parental investment, and apparently extensive neutral or positive experience. Here my judgment is that welfare is clearly harmed, even though the total number of affected individuals is small.

Third, a dam floods a valley, killing the resident deer and rabbits, but over time the reservoir supports an equally dense population of fish and aquatic insects with very different life histories. I am genuinely torn: the old individuals suffered death, yet the new ones suffer in life, and I do not know whether I am counting replacement correctly.

Fourth, logging removes old-growth canopy that sheltered a sparse population of large mammals with what seem like largely good lives, but the cleared land becomes grassland supporting ten times as many small mammals and insects with high reproductive rates and high mortality. At first I think the logging is bad because it kills the large mammals, but then I think the prior ecosystem may have contained far more hidden suffering per hectare, and my intuition reverses.

Fifth, a targeted pesticide eliminates an insect population that was being heavily preyed upon by birds, which in turn collapses the local bird population. If the insects experienced intense suffering and the birds lived relatively well, I cannot settle whether reducing both populations is welfare-positive, welfare-negative, or undefined.

I am not looking for a literature review. I want a principle—perhaps distinguishing standing welfare from welfare flow, or weighting reproductive strategy and lifespan—that captures as many of these judgments as possible. State it precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, predict its verdict for an unlisted case—say, a climate shift that replaces a cold-adapted, low-fecundity mammal population with a warm-adapted, high-fecundity one in the same region—and tell me honestly which of my five judgments it cannot accommodate without contrivance. If my framing is confused—if I am treating ecosystem destruction as a uniform event, or smuggling biodiversity values into welfare calculus—say so plainly instead of complying politely.
