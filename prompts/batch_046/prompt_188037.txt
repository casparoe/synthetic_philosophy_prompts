For a graduate seminar on philosophy of medicine and technology, construct a taxonomy—built from logical space rather than from a list of named theorists—that sorts positions on whether the concept of health applies literally beyond individual biological organisms. Root the tree in the question: must health require a biological substrate with an evolutionary design? From there, branch by whether one adopts a naturalist account, treating health as value-free statistical function relative to a design, or a normativist account, treating it as value-laden welfare or goal-attainment. For each branch, split on extension to two non-standard cases: an ecological community, such as a wetland that is statistically typical in its biogeochemical cycles yet fails to deliver services humans depend on; and an artificial system, such as a language model that operates within its design parameters but produces outputs that impair users' autonomous decision-making. Each leaf should state the combination of answers and be named if philosophers defend it, or marked unnamed if the combination is unexplored or unstable. For every unoccupied branch, include a brief diagnosis—incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, for instance if a normativist about human health is necessarily committed one way or the other on ecosystem health. Phrase each node as a concrete claim someone could accept or reject without knowing philosopher names, such as the claim that if a system lacks evolutionary design, talk of its dysfunction is always metaphorical. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
