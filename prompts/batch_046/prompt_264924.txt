Write a short story of roughly 1,000 words that takes Al-Ghazālī’s occasionalism as its literal physical and metaphysical backdrop. In this world, no created substance possesses intrinsic causal efficacy; God is the direct and sole cause of every particular event. What observers call natural law is nothing more than the habitual sequence—God’s customary manner (ʿāda)—in which certain events are regularly followed by others, without any necessary connection between them.

Your protagonist should be an artisan, physician, or engineer whose practical craft depends entirely on predicting these habitual conjunctions. The narrative should center on a discovery: she constructs or observes an isolated system—perhaps a mechanical automaton, a sealed garden, or a medical instrument—that exhibits a sustained counter-custom. This is not a miracle in the traditional sense (not a single, dramatic rupture in God’s habit), but a localized, self-reproducing order of regularities that consistently diverges from the broader divine custom. The system’s internal patterns are coherent, predictable, and apparently self-sustaining, yet they do not align with the usual pairing of causes and effects recognized elsewhere.

Embed in the narrative a precise philosophical tension that the protagonist must navigate practically rather than resolve discursively. First, she may interpret the counter-custom as a hidden subset of the single divine habit—merely an unknown corner of God’s uniform custom, which preserves occasionalism intact. Second, she may fear that the system reveals a derivative capacity in created things to establish secondary habits of their own, which would reintroduce genuine natural causation and threaten the uniqueness of divine agency. Third, she may consider that habit itself has become structurally autonomous—suggesting that occasionalism cannot preserve God as the sole cause if multiple, nested, and competing customs are possible within creation.

Do not allow the story to collapse into an explicit philosophical dialogue or treatise. Instead, let the protagonist’s concrete choices—whether to exploit the counter-custom for practical gain, to suppress it out of theological caution, or to redesign her entire craft around its internal logic—reveal her implicit metaphysical commitment. The fiction should remain conceptually rigorous: it must distinguish occasionalism from mere determinism, treat the counter-custom as conceptually distinct from both miracle and natural law, and show why the difference matters for human action. Avoid anachronistic modern scientific vocabulary unless it serves the conceptual framing; the metaphysical problem should drive the plot, not ornament it.
