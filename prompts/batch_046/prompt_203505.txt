Consider a scholar applying Nietzsche's framework from his essay on history for life—specifically the distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history, each with its characteristic service to present vitality and its corresponding danger of life-denial. Treat the following four judgments as her consistent track record.

First, she approves of a polity that constructs monumental narratives of past greatness to inspire present action, but only on the condition that the present is genuinely active and not merely imitative; she warns that without this condition, monumental history falsifies the past and paralyzes originality. Second, she condemns a culture that preserves every local custom and document with reverent exactitude yet produces no new form of life, judging that antiquarian history has here buried the present under the past. Third, she endorses a revolutionary movement that dismantles oppressive origin myths to free the present, but adds that this critical history becomes destructive rather than liberating if it leaves citizens rootless and unable to build anew. Fourth, she rejects a purely scholarly archive that collects and orders all facts without either glorifying, preserving for identity, or judging for liberation, on the grounds that history not in service of life is merely a burden of knowledge.

Now consider a new institutional form: a decentralized, algorithmically mediated "living memory" system in which every citizen continuously generates, remixes, and ranks narratives about their community's past. There is no canonical version; the same archive is mined simultaneously for heroic precedents that justify present policy and for contradictory testimonies that undermine those precedents, and civic participation requires constant engagement with both. The system preserves everything (antiquarian), selectively amplifies inspiring episodes (monumental), and permits real-time debunking of favored narratives (critical), but does so without a single governing purpose.

Deliver, first, the recommendation that best coheres with the four precedents: name the closest analog among them, state the principle she would be adopting if she rules that way, and be honest about what she would have to abandon or weaken in her earlier framework if she rules the opposite way. Then identify at least two other rules—different in structure, not just in outcome—that could also accommodate the four earlier verdicts. For each rule, derive its specific prediction about the living-memory system. Give your best-guess verdict with explicit reasoning for preferring the leading rule over the others. Finally, specify one further hypothetical institutional form—distinct from all cases discussed here—that would most sharply discriminate between the leading rule and its strongest competitor.
