After thirty years in experimental physiology and a recent retirement into philosophy of mind, I find myself serving on the ethics review board of a small neurophenomenology lab that conducts studies combining first-person verbal reports with intracranial recordings during sensory deprivation and psychedelic protocols. I have become convinced that Daniel Dennett’s heterophenomenology offers the correct methodological posture—treating subjective reports not as direct windows onto private qualia but as texts requiring the same critical interpretation we apply to behavioral or neural data—yet I find the philosophical literature stops at the level of conceptual stance rather than delivering an operational protocol. I need a reusable decision procedure that my board can actually run when evaluating a study protocol that proposes to use verbal reports as primary data: a step-by-step checklist that determines when a report should be admitted as heterophenomenological data, when it must be downgraded to a theoretical construct or confabulation, and when the protocol requires revision or rejection, with explicit stopping points where empirical judgment ends and value judgment takes over.

The procedure should remain grounded in the core heterophenomenological commitment that the only primary data are the reports themselves, interpreted against behavioral and neural context, but it should also incorporate what I have come to call the transparency gradient: the degree to which a subject’s report can be rendered into predictive behavioral and neurophysiological constraints without the introduction of ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses. For each step, specify the practical test—such as inter-rater reliability of report coding, consistency across repeated sessions under identical conditions, or the generation of falsifiable predictions about reaction times or cortical dynamics—and clearly flag the cases that the procedure cannot settle. Then apply the procedure to two concrete proposals: first, a study collecting standardized pain-scale descriptions during calibrated thermal stimulation with concurrent anterior cingulate recordings, where the procedure should yield a clear admission decision; second, a study asking experienced meditators to report the presence or absence of a self-boundary during deep absorption states where no consistent, replicable neural signature has yet been established across subjects, where the procedure should expose the precise point at which judgment must replace algorithm—ideally the very point that will make my fellow board members, mostly clinicians committed to respecting subjective authority, most uncomfortable. I have no web access in the review room and need only the conceptual architecture and its practical translation; please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
