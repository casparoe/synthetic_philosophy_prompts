You are a professional philosopher with expertise in moral memory and the ethics of archival practice. Before giving your verdict, take a moment to map the competing moral claims without relying on standard theoretical labels.

Here is the situation: A town archivist is the sole custodian of handwritten diaries left by families who lived through a localized industrial disaster seventy years ago. Every direct victim is now dead; only distant descendants remain. The diaries contain intimate accounts of loss, but also the only detailed documentation of how local officials diverted emergency resources. The town council has proposed an "institutional pause" ordinance: the diaries will remain sealed for thirty years, then be opened automatically, unless descendants vote to extend the seal. Supporters argue that forcing perpetual remembrance causes "commemorative fatigue" and that descendants should control whether painful family details remain public. Opponents argue that sealing the only evidence of official negligence denies future citizens knowledge they need to hold institutions accountable.

The archivist asks you: should she endorse the pause, open the archive immediately, or design a different protocol? Give a reasoned recommendation that considers whether obligations to preserve painful knowledge survive when all direct victims are gone, and whether deliberately designed forgetting is ethically different from accidental loss.

Please also state explicitly what argument or fact—perhaps about the moral authority of descendants, or about whether remembrance primarily serves the dead or the living—would most weaken your conclusion, and identify the weakest point in your own reasoning.
