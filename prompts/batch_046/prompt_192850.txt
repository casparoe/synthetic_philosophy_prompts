I am designing a ninety-minute workshop for municipal policy analysts who have no formal philosophical training beyond an introductory ethics course. My objective is to teach Scanlon’s contractualist framework—the principle that an action is impermissible if it can be justified only by principles that at least one affected party could reasonably reject—while immediately confronting the participants with the aggregation problem: the tension between a concentrated, identifiable harm borne by a small group and diffuse, modest benefits distributed across a large population. I do not want the lecture itself; I need a pedagogical plan.

Specify the exact sequence in which the conceptual components must be introduced, explaining why each step depends on the prior one and what must be in place before the aggregation challenge can be meaningfully raised. Identify the single concrete carrying scenario: it must feature an identifiable community that absorbs a focused burden and a dispersed public that receives incremental gains, and you should state precisely what features this case must possess and what it must lack so that the discussion does not collapse into pure utilitarian calculus or into an unexamined rights-based veto. Map the predictable misconceptions—such as conflating contractualism with Hobbesian or Rawlsian social-contract theory, interpreting “reasonable rejection” as mere subjective dislike, or treating aggregation as a simple numerical outweighing of harms by benefits—and indicate the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be preemptively defused. Propose a concise check for understanding that would reveal whether the participants have grasped the dependency between the reasonableness standard and the aggregation problem, rather than having merely memorized the definitions. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—whether Scanlon’s full account of well-being, technical distinctions within the aggregation literature such as ex ante versus ex post evaluation, or granular comparisons with Rawls’s original position—and justify each omission in terms of the workshop’s practical objective.
