I'm in this moral philosophy seminar and we've been arguing about the doctrine of double effect for like three weeks now, and I realized my gut reactions don't line up as neatly as the textbook says they should. So I'm trying to map out what I actually think by running through cases. The classic switch—trolley heading for five, I flip it to kill one—feels okay to me; I'm not using that one person as a tool, just redirecting the danger. Pushing the fat man off the bridge to stop the trolley with his body feels totally different, not okay, even though the math is identical. A doctor giving morphine to a dying patient knowing it'll hasten death by a day, but genuinely only intending pain relief, also feels okay. But that same doctor giving the lethal dose specifically to harvest organs faster for five waiting patients? Not okay.

Then there's this drone scenario I keep turning over. I see a truck with explosives heading for a crowded market, and there's one civilian walking right beside it. If I strike the truck, the blast will definitely kill that civilian too, but save dozens—this feels okay, like the switch case, since the death is a side effect of destroying the truck. But if I wait until the truck is right next to the civilian and then strike them both together, deliberately using the civilian's body to help contain the blast and reduce damage in the market, the numbers are exactly the same—one dead, dozens saved—but this feels wrong, closer to the fat man. Is that consistent, or am I just being weird?

The case that really has me going back and forth is this medical one. There's a surgeon who needs to do an emergency operation on five people, but the only available blood supply is shared, and there's about a twenty percent chance that drawing enough for all five will kill the one compatible donor. She knows this, proceeds with the operation for the five, and the donor dies. Sometimes I think it's okay—she intended saving, not killing—other times it feels like she's using the donor's blood as the direct means, which sounds more like pushing the fat man. So is there one principle, maybe double effect but maybe something tighter, that captures all my yes/no answers? And what should I do with that drone-containment case and the blood-donor case, where I feel torn? If a simpler principle saves most of my verdicts but forces me to drop one, which verdict is the outlier? Also, what would that principle say about a factory inspector who knows upgrading equipment will prevent future worker deaths, but will definitely cause one worker to be laid off and likely fall into fatal depression—the inspector pushes the upgrade knowing but not intending the worker's death? I keep flip-flopping on that one too, and I'd like to know if that's the same structure as the others or something different.
