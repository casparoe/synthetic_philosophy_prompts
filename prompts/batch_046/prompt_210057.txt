I have come to defend a particular resolution to the problem of divine hiddenness—one that does not turn on free will or soul-making, but on the epistemic architecture of authority recognition. The position is this: manifest, undeniable divine presence would not merely coerce belief; it would structurally disable the cognitive mechanisms by which finite agents distinguish legitimate external testimony from internally projected authority. Under conditions of direct manifest presence, the brain’s authority-detection and source-monitoring systems would be activated in ways that make it impossible to tell whether an authoritative disclosure comes from outside the self or from within it. Consequently, hiddenness is not a withholding of evidence but a necessary epistemic filter: it preserves human belief-formation through mediated, fallible, and therefore genuinely autonomous channels—tradition, reasoning, testimony, and interpretive experience—without which there could be no authentic epistemic relation to the divine. I want an honest map of this position’s exposure. Specifically: what empirical results in the cognitive science of religion or social epistemology would genuinely damage it; what theological or philosophical commitments does it presuppose that might be rejected; does it generate distinctive predictions about the cross-cultural distribution or psychological profile of belief that rival accounts do not; and has the view already been qualified—through appeals to divine accommodation, fallibilism, or the incommensurability of divine and human cognition—into a form that no observation could threaten? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a defender of this view should concede as genuinely counting against it, and judge whether such insulation, if present, is a theoretical virtue or a sign that the position has abandoned its empirical risk.
