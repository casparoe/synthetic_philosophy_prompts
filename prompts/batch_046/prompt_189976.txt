I have recently found myself in a prolonged caregiving situation that demands far more of my time and emotional energy than my career allows, and this has led me to wonder whether the aspiration to be maximally good—what philosophers sometimes discuss under the heading of moral sainthood—is even coherent or desirable for someone with ordinary attachments. I am drafting a short argumentative essay and would welcome your comments on its structure, conceptual coherence, and whether its central distinctions actually hold up.

Here is the outline and preliminary argument.

The essay begins from the familiar worry that a fully moral saint—someone who devotes every available resource to the welfare of others—would be an unattractive figure: lacking humor, personal projects, and the kind of idiosyncratic attachments that make a life recognizably one's own. Rather than treating this as an objection to an extreme but otherwise unified character, I propose that we distinguish two forms of maximal moral commitment. The first, which I call fragmentary sainthood, treats moral demands as an additive burden layered onto an otherwise conventional life; the result is not necessarily the absence of non-moral traits but their suppression or instrumentalization, producing burnout and a kind of resentment directed at one's own moral demands. The second, integrative sainthood, attempts to restructure one's entire practical identity so that moral demands and personal flourishing are not competing goods but mutually defining—much as a musician might integrate practice and social life rather than treating them as rivals.

Against this background, I introduce a further observation, which I term moral luminosity: extreme moral commitment tends to make an agent's other qualities invisible or reframed by observers (and often by the agent) as merely instrumental to moral purpose. This suggests that the familiar critique—that saints lack rich inner lives—may misdiagnose the phenomenon. The saint may possess humor, aesthetic taste, or loyalty, but these traits are perceived through the glare of moral purpose and therefore do not register as autonomous sources of value.

I then argue that integrative sainthood avoids the unattractiveness objection only by collapsing into a form of practical value monism: every aspect of life must be justified in moral terms. This is not necessarily undesirable, but it is unstable for creatures with plural and sometimes incommensurable commitments. Consequently, my tentative conclusion is that maximal moral goodness is desirable only in the integrative mode, yet that mode may not satisfy the quantitative maximization that the idea of sainthood originally implied.

Please comment on the following.

First, on accuracy: have I misrepresented the standard philosophical discussion of moral saints, associated for example with the well-known critique that saints are deficient in non-moral virtues? Are there settled points in that literature I have overlooked or mischaracterized?

Second, on conceptual coherence: do the distinctions between fragmentary and integrative sainthood, and the notion of moral luminosity, do any real philosophical work, or do they merely rename familiar tensions between duty and inclination?

Third, on argumentative strength: is the move from integrative sainthood to practical value monism valid, or does it conflate structural unity with axiological reduction?

Fourth, on framing: is the entry point through a personal tension between caregiving and career an appropriate way to motivate a conceptual argument, or does it risk conflating biography with philosophy?

In your response, please clearly separate what is settled among people familiar with this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretation of the moral-saint literature or of value pluralism as though it were the consensus view.
