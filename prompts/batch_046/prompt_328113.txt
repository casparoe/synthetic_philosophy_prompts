Write a poem in unrhymed iambic pentameter—exactly twenty lines, arranged in four five-line stanzas—structured as an internal monologue by a philosopher who is simultaneously applying the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption to her own present consciousness. To make the philosophical tension do formal work, have stanzas 1 and 3 speak from the self-sampling voice (treating her existence as a random draw from a defined reference class of observers), and have stanzas 2 and 4 speak from the self-indication voice (treating her very existence as direct probabilistic evidence for the size of that reference class). Inject the following specific, non-standard framing: the philosopher is not reasoning about observers in general, but about members of an extremely narrow reference class—philosophers who have just finished constructing this exact argument and who are each holding a coin that they privately believe may be either fair or double-headed, where the result of the flip determines whether they sit alone in a single study or are one of ten thousand identical copies seated in parallel studies. The poem should treat her belief about the coin’s bias as itself part of the conditioning evidence, so that her sampling frame includes her own epistemic state about the coin. Do not quote, cite, or name any specific author or text; instead, render the logic with enough precision that someone familiar with the presumptuous-philosopher objection—namely, that self-indication implausibly assigns high prior weight to cosmologies containing enormous numbers of observers—will recognize it in the final stanza, where the speaker accuses herself of epistemic vanity for treating her own observation as typical of a vast multiverse. End without synthesis: the poem should conclude with both voices still audible, the argument folded back on itself, and the tension left open.
