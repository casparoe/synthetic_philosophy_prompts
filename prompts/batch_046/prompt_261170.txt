I am convening a final-year seminar on philosophy of history and drafting the end-of-module examination. The course has examined familiar critiques of Eurocentric historiography—the privileging of the nation-state as the unit of analysis, the treatment of 1492 or 1789 as universal turning points, and the implicit teleology of modernisation—but I want to push students beyond rehearsing these standard objections. To that end, I have constructed a conceptual exercise around what I term “relational chronology”: the proposal that we abandon a single universal timeline and instead map the past through overlapping “causal fields”, each possessing its own internal rhythm and periodisation, such that any given event belongs simultaneously to multiple, non-commensurable temporal orders. The claim advanced in favour of this method is that it escapes Eurocentrism because no single field—say, the West European state-system—is granted metrical priority over others, and therefore “the past can be told from everywhere at once”. I should like an exam essay question that asks students to evaluate whether relational chronology genuinely evades Eurocentric framing, or whether it merely displaces that framing onto the architecture of comparison itself.

The question ought to require candidates to address three specific conceptual pressures: first, whether incommensurable temporal fields can be compared at all without smuggling in a hidden universal metric; second, whether the selection of which fields to include already presupposes a prior geopolitical hierarchy that determines what counts as a relevant “causal field”; and third, whether the aspiration to tell the past “from everywhere at once” is conceptually coherent, or whether it collapses into a fragmented pluralism incapable of sustaining historical explanation. Please write the essay question itself, followed by a model answer of approximately 800 words, and then a marking scheme suitable for a 90-minute examination for final-year undergraduates. The marking scheme should separate an A-grade response—which must identify the hidden-metric problem and the selection-bias problem as two distinct objections, and must show why pluralism undermines explanation—from a B-grade response—which treats these objections as a single undifferentiated worry—from a failing response, which merely summarises the standard critique of Eurocentrism without engaging the conceptual structure of relational chronology. The total mark should be out of 20, with explicit weightings for each required move, and a brief note on the characteristic error candidates make at each point. Write in British English, avoid any request for bibliographies or citations, and do not depend on obscure specialist literature: the task should be answerable from philosophical reasoning about the constructed framework alone.
