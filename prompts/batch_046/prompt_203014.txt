As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have been interviewing researchers in long-term risk forecasting and have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern for which I lack precise vocabulary. A researcher will defend committing substantial present resources to avert a catastrophe with an astronomically large negative outcome but a vanishingly small probability, appealing straightforwardly to expected-value calculus. When a critic responds by constructing an analogous case—say, diverting comparable resources to prevent an equally implausible cosmic event, such as a collision with a hypothetical asteroid composed entirely of antimatter—the researcher does not reject the calculus itself. Instead, they introduce a qualitative distinction: the original risk is structurally anchored in observable trends, whereas the counterexample is merely speculative. Yet the boundary seems drawn precisely where it protects the favored high-stakes conclusion, not by any independent criterion that would survive symmetric application. The maneuver is not exactly special pleading, since a feature is genuinely being cited; it is not straightforward confirmation bias, since the mathematical framework remains intact; and it differs from the familiar Pascal's Mugging problem, because the probabilities are not arbitrarily inflated but rather selectively insulated from comparison. Is there an established concept in decision theory or philosophy of risk that captures this specific protective boundary-drawing around high-expected-value conclusions? If the literature offers nothing precise, I would like to be able to name and delineate the phenomenon for my readers—distinguishing it from ordinary auxiliary hypotheses, from ad hoc immunization strategies, and from standard risk-aversion—so that the carving, rather than the label alone, does the work. What exactly is this pattern, where are its edges, and what should I call it?
