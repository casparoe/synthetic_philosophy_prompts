I'm preparing a philosophy of mind seminar on personal identity and neural division, and I want to use Robert Louis Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde not as a loose metaphor for "inner conflict" but as a serious test case for the debates that arose from Sperry and Gazzaniga's split-brain work. The novel depicts one body housing two apparently complete moral agents—Jekyll, who writes and reasons, and Hyde, who acts with separate desires—linked only by shared memory traces and a chemical switch rather than by any continuous conscious stream. I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the split-brain literature seriously: does Stevenson dramatize a Puccetti-style division into two conscious subjects within one cranium, or does he preserve a unitary self that merely suffers from fragmented access to its own motives?

Consider in particular the final letter and the transformation scenes: do they suggest that Jekyll and Hyde share a single narrative self that confabulates explanations for actions it doesn't author (as Gazzaniga's left-hemisphere interpreter might), or do they present two genuinely separate streams of phenomenal consciousness competing for control of one body? I also want you to address where the analogy breaks down—Stevenson's division is pharmacological and reversible, not surgical, and Hyde seems to possess full linguistic and reasoning capacity, unlike the right hemisphere in classic callosotomy cases. Can the novel illuminate something about the conditions for personal unity—perhaps the role of moral narrative in binding divided agency—that the clinical literature, with its emphasis on task-specific hemispheric specialization, tends to miss? And where does the philosophical reading force us to distort Stevenson's plot or character psychology?

A good answer should not reduce the novel to an illustration of neuroscience, but should say whether the work adds anything to the debate that argument alone doesn't capture, and should be honest about where the text resists the reading.
