I'm preparing a feature on the thesis that analogy-making is not merely one cognitive capacity among others but the fundamental mechanism of thought itself, as advanced by Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander. Their view holds that conceptual understanding operates through fluid structural mapping—what they describe as slippage between representations—rather than through fixed rules or symbolic computation.

For the feature, I want to test a tension that sits at the edge of their framework. Consider the domestication problem: if every cognitive advance must be analogically anchored to an existing conceptual structure, then genuinely novel reference—encountering a phenomenon that bears no structural overlap with prior experience—appears impossible, because there is no surface onto which the mind can map it. Defenders might respond that even radical novelty is assimilated through partial structural echoes, yet critics can reply that this transforms analogy from a foundation into a filter: it explains how we organize what we already grasp, not how we grasp what lies genuinely outside our conceptual home.

Write an essay weighing whether analogy-making is indeed the core of cognition, or whether it functions best as a powerful but secondary organizational mechanism that depends on something more basic—such as compositional abstraction, predictive modeling, or direct empirical engagement—to fix reference and generate genuine novelty. Address the Hofstadter-Sander position and the domestication problem specifically. Reach and justify a clear conclusion; do not settle for a non-committal balance.
