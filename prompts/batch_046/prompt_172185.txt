I have been working through debates about whether pain's badness is essentially phenomenal or essentially tied to affective-cognitive integration, and I want to focus the discussion on pain asymbolia—the clinical profile in which patients report nociceptive sensations yet show minimal distress, avoidance behavior, or negative evaluation, sometimes describing the feeling in strikingly neutral terms. Please draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could sign after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration on exactly this case.

Camp One holds that pain is bad primarily in virtue of an intrinsic phenomenal quality—its "hurtingness"—and that asymbolia represents a dissociation between that phenomenal core and its normal affective registration, meaning the badness remains present but unacknowledged or unexpressed. Camp Two holds that pain's badness is constituted by its role within an integrated functional architecture—representing bodily threat, motivating protective behavior, and generating suffering—and that asymbolia demonstrates that without this integration the raw sensation is not genuinely bad, only anomalous.

Produce the document they should have written. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: about the clinical and phenomenological profile of pain asymbolia; about the conceptual distinction between nociception, suffering, and integrated pain-experience; about what any adequate theory of badness must explain; and about which empirical observations or conceptual distinctions both sides accept as bearing on the dispute. Second, a disagreements table that states each camp's precise position in wording its own proponents would accept, specifically addressing whether phenomenal quality alone is sufficient for badness, whether representational content can occur without suffering, and whether asymbolia undermines or merely complicates the phenomenal view. Third, identify the cruxes—I take one to be whether "hurting" denotes a primitive phenomenal property or a relational-functional state, and another to be whether first-person reports from asymbolia patients are epistemically authoritative about the presence or absence of suffering. For each crux, specify the concrete case, conceptual test, or piece of clinical evidence both sides agree is relevant, and record in advance what each camp predicts about it. Finally, indicate whether any portion of their disagreement is verbal—about whether "pain" properly denotes the sensation, the suffering, or the integrated state—and if so, say exactly which part and why. Write this as a formal collaborative statement, not as a dialogue or adjudication, and do not include bibliographic requests or citation lists.
