The central claim is that standard criteria for ontological identification—particularly those that tie commitment to causal interaction or spatiotemporal traceability—fail to accommodate what I call "shadow nodes": theoretical entities that are informationally inaccessible in principle, leaving no direct or indirect observational footprint, yet structurally integrated into our best predictive frameworks in a non-eliminable way. Rather than treating these as mere calculational artifacts, I propose a criterion of "differential relational anchoring" (DRA). According to DRA, an entity is ontologically identified if its excision produces a non-compensable asymmetry in the relational architecture of the theory, even when the entity itself carries no independent informational signature. I illustrate this with a hypothetical forecasting architecture in which a hidden parameter systematically improves cross-domain predictive accuracy but resists any physical interpretation. My concern is that DRA may collapse into a form of structural realism that sacrifices individuation, though I maintain that the asymmetry condition preserves a minimal, non-epistemic form of identification. I am especially unsure whether the argument illegitimately extends theoretical warrant into metaphysical commitment, or whether the shadow-node example conflates mathematical existence with ontological presence.

Please comment on the coherence of the distinction between informational inaccessibility and ontological absence, the defensibility of DRA as a criterion that avoids pure structuralism, and the coherence of the forecasting example. In particular, I want to know whether the asymmetry condition genuinely individuates entities or merely registers theoretical indispensability, and whether there is an obvious revision that would preserve the intuition without inviting ontological inflation.
