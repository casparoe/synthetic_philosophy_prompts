Consider the widespread practice in development economics and organizational reform of attributing differential economic performance—entrepreneurial rates, savings behavior, institutional resilience—to “cultural values” such as a region’s work ethic, thrift orientation, or trust norms, and of designing interventions that aim to shift these value-orientations directly, treating them as manipulable causal levers on par with tax structures or infrastructure investment. What philosophical commitments about the ontology of values and the logic of social explanation does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it treat values as efficient causes in the Humean sense, as reasons that rationalize action without entering causal chains, or as Weberian ideal types that render conduct intelligible without themselves being causal variables? Is the practice compatible with Weber’s own view that the Protestant ethic was never the cause of capitalist rationalization but rather one meaningful orientation among others that made it comprehensible, or does it commit to the stronger thesis that culture possesses autonomous causal power? Examine, further, whether the practice is internally consistent: these same reports often explain value-change as a downstream effect of material incentives and institutional design, which suggests values are effects, even as policy treats them as independent causes. Identify the specific philosophical tension here—between values as causes and values as caused—and say what an evaluation framework genuinely committed to Weber’s interpretive sociology would have to change in its metrics, its intervention logic, and its account of what makes an explanation adequate.
