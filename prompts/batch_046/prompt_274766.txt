Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind, both equally competent, debating whether gradual neural replacement can preserve personal identity through to a full mind upload. The first defends the standard gradualist view: if synthetic neurons are substituted one at a time, each matching the functional profile of its biological predecessor, then consciousness and identity persist continuously, with no hidden breakpoint, and the final synthetic brain genuinely is the original person. The second grants local functional equivalence but denies that it suffices: she argues that consciousness depends on global dynamical properties—precise noise correlations across regions, temporal integration windows, and emergent synchronization patterns—which synthetic substrates might replicate imperfectly even when every local node behaves correctly. She should claim that this creates a possible "hidden discontinuity" undetectable from the outside and perhaps even from the first-person view, undermining the gradualist assurance. Have them engage directly: the gradualist should ask whether her global constraint is empirically distinguishable from an ordinary Ship-of-Theseus problem, and whether demanding global identity makes preservation impossible in principle; the critic should respond by distinguishing structural preservation from functional simulation and pressing whether the gradualist can specify a non-arbitrary threshold for "close enough" global dynamics without smuggling in the very identity claim at issue. Neither side should be a strawman, and neither should invoke specific literature, experiments, or authors by name.
