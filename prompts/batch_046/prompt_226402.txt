In popular neuroscience writing and in some philosophical treatments of personal identity, split-brain patients are frequently compared to co-tenants of one apartment who share a kitchen and bathroom but have had every internal door bricked up: each occupant controls the fixtures on one side, is largely ignorant of the other's plans, and depends on the same plumbing and heating, yet neither can enter the other's space. Proponents of this image use it to argue that severing the corpus callosum does not merely damage communication but reveals two distinct streams of consciousness that were always present and only seemed unified because the callosum served as a high-bandwidth messaging service between them. Write a rigorous audit of this analogy. First, provide an explicit correspondence table mapping the co-tenants, the shared infrastructure, the bricked-up doors, the lease, and any other salient features of the source domain onto specific structures and functional relations in the split-brain case. Second, identify the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the intended conclusion—especially the left hemisphere's monopoly on linguistic confabulation, the preservation of unified arousal and emotional response, and the absence of independent legal or moral agency—while separating these from merely decorative mismatches. Third, examine what normative and metaphysical commitments the co-tenancy image quietly imports (equal standing, the possibility of eviction, independent ownership of experience) that the neurological evidence does not supply. Fourth, determine whether the conclusion that a single body now hosts two persons, rather than one person with a divided processing architecture, survives once those imported commitments are subtracted, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. Finally, as part of your audit, consider an alternative framing in which the hemispheres are not co-tenants but two processing modules within a single cognitive architecture that has lost its inter-process communication bus, leaving one module as the sole user-facing interface and therefore compelled to generate post-hoc narratives for actions executed by the other. Does this second analogy preserve, dissolve, or transform the multiplicity claim? If neither comparison is adequate, sketch what a more honest analogy would be and what conclusion about the unity of consciousness it would actually license.
