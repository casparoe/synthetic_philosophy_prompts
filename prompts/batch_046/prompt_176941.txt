I spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about whether AI safety should be conceptualized primarily as an alignment problem—getting frontier systems to share human values—or as a control problem—building institutional and technical constraints that remain effective even if the system is misaligned. My friend insisted the distinction collapses in practice, since any robust control mechanism must eventually appeal to some aligned evaluation criterion; I countered that this conflates procedural containment with substantive goal-formation, and that treating them as interchangeable obscures which failure modes each addresses. I want to step back from the polemics and ask: within the conceptual space of AI control versus alignment—specifically, safety measures that explicitly assume the model may be misaligned—which open questions, lines of theoretical work, or practical implications should actually command our attention?

More concretely, I am trying to decide how to direct a small research agenda—say, three years of conceptual work for a group of philosophers and policy theorists rather than ML engineers—focused on safety architectures that do not presuppose corrigibility. Among the unresolved issues, I see at least three that seem upstream: first, whether containment strategies such as sandboxing, capability restriction, and stovepiped evaluation protocols implicitly commit us to a particular ontology of artificial agency—perhaps treating misalignment as the expression of a unified strategic preference rather than as fragmented, context-dependent mesa-optimization; second, whether there is a genuine conceptual dependency between resolving logical uncertainty in bounded agents and designing ex post interpretability audits that can function independently of the model's reported intentions; and third, what institutional design implications follow if we take seriously the possibility that a system could be both highly capable and genuinely misaligned, yet contained by procedural architecture rather than by value alignment. I am not asking for a bibliography or literature survey; I want a defended prioritization.

Please rank these three lines, or replace them with ones you judge more important, in terms of which deserves the scarcest resource—here, sustained conceptual attention over several years—defending the ordering with explicit criteria such as tractability for non-engineers, how many downstream questions depend on its resolution, and what empirical or theoretical developments over the next few years could reorder them. In doing so, please separate what is relatively settled—say, that sandboxing raises its own strategic reasoning risks—from what remains genuinely contested among people who understand the topic, and do not present a contested framing, such as the claim that procedural and substantive safety are fully orthogonal, as if it were the consensus view.
