My tutor threw this argument at me in our philosophy of mind module and I cant stop thinkin about it. Here are the steps:

(1) If 'pain' is a rigid designator, it picks out the same state in every world where it exists.
(2) The identity 'pain = C-fibre firing' is necessary a posteriori, just like water and H2O.
(3) So in any world where pain occurs, C-fibres fire, and vice versa.
(4) But then the phenomenal quality—what it's like—must be identical to that neural property, since there's no world where they come apart.
(5) So there are no purely phenomenal properties seperate from physical ones; phenomenal concepts are just confused descriptions of the same rigidly designated state.
(6) This proves too much, though: it doesnt just reject dualism, it wipes out any real gap between first-person access and neuroscience, and makes zombie worlds conceptually incoherent rather than just metaphysically impossible.

Which premise should I drop? I'd like to analyse which one is weakest, but I want to keep (2) and (4) since they feel core to Kripke's picture. Is the error treating mental terms as rigid designators in the first place, or is there a hidden slide from metaphysical necessity to conceptual coherence?
