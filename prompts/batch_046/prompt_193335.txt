Since joining a municipal participatory-budgeting board six months ago, I have noticed that my fellow members repeatedly invoke what they call the “laboratory model” of democratic governance: a proposed zoning or housing policy is framed as a tentative hypothesis, piloted in one district, evaluated against tracked outcomes—displacement rates, tax-base shifts, permit velocity—and then revised or abandoned according to the evidence, exactly as a research group would treat an experimental protocol. The analogy is usually traced to Dewey’s conviction that democracy and scientific inquiry share an underlying logical structure, and it is used to license a specific normative posture—tentative, anti-ideological, responsive to feedback—rather than to justify any particular policy outcome. I want to know whether the analogy actually carries that posture, or whether it collapses under pressure.

Please respond in the following sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. Correspondence table (~180 words): Lay out the explicit mapping between democratic governance and scientific experimentation. What plays the role of the hypothesis, the experimental apparatus, the control group, peer review, falsification, reproducibility, and the scientific community? Be concrete: refer to the kinds of local decisions our board actually faces—pilot upzoning, inclusionary-housing mandates, community-benefit agreements—rather than abstract democratic theory alone.

2. The disanalogies that matter (~280 words): Identify the points where the mapping breaks, with special attention to the ones that threaten the normative conclusion. Can democratic “experiments” have control groups in any meaningful sense when every citizen is simultaneously subject and observer? Are policy outcomes observable with enough precision and isolation to permit clear falsification, or do confounding variables (regional labor markets, state preemption, private capital flows) make attribution impossible? Does the fact that citizens are both the experimenters and the experimental subjects corrupt the analogy in a way that science avoids?

3. Smuggled cargo (~180 words): Note which features of scientific inquiry the analogy quietly imports—value-neutrality, the separability of observer and observed, the possibility of replication without altering the system, the idea that failure is costless information. Does democratic politics possess these, or does their absence constitute precisely what makes politics political? I am particularly interested in whether the analogy smuggles in a false picture of democratic citizens as detached investigators rather than stakeholders with irreversible interests.

4. Survival of the conclusion (~180 words): Assess whether the recommendation for tentative, pilot-based, evidence-driven governance survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Does the “laboratory” framing genuinely support anti-dogmatism, or does it merely dress ideological commitments in the language of method? If the analogy is doing rhetorical work it cannot do logically, say so explicitly.

5. A sharper replacement (~120 words): If the laboratory model is defective, propose a better analogy—perhaps drawn from Dewey’s own transactional or cooperative framework, or from another domain—that preserves the anti-dogmatic, feedback-responsive spirit without importing false scientific pretensions. Explain what the new source domain supplies that “the laboratory” lacks, and where it too might break.

I am not looking for bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations; I want an audit of the argument the analogy is making in our committee room.
