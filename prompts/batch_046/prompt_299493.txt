I'm preparing the opening for my metaphysics seminar on individuation, and I want to begin with a short poem—no more than sixteen lines, divided into two stanzas of eight lines each—that uses formal parallelism to stage a tension between classical and quantum identity conditions. The first stanza should depict Max Black's counterexample: two perfectly symmetric iron spheres suspended in an otherwise empty universe, qualitatively identical in every intrinsic and relational property save for bare numerical difference. The second stanza should mirror the first line-for-line, substituting quantum descriptions for classical ones, until the final couplet: in the first stanza, the spheres remain two despite sharing all properties; in the second, two fermions in a singlet state cannot be tagged this and that in any meaningful way, even though they are not one. The rhyme scheme should be loose or absent, but the syntactic repetition must be exact enough that the divergence in the closing lines does all the philosophical work—showing that Black's case preserves individuation without qualitative distinction, whereas quantum mechanics seems to dissolve labeling without collapsing multiplicity into unity. The poem should not resolve which framework is correct; it should leave the audience with the sense that indiscernible operates differently in each domain. I need it accurate—no hidden-variables interpretations, no invocation of Bell—just basic quantum statistics—so that specialists in philosophy of physics won't wince, but accessible enough that the metaphysicians in the room can follow the analogy.
