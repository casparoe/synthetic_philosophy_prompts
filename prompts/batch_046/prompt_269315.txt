Following a rather protracted argument with a friend over supper about whether scientific communication could ever be genuinely non-propositional, I found myself returning to Otto Neurath’s project for a universal picture language—ISOTYPE—and his broader physicalist conviction that scientific statements ought to be rendered as clusters of observational reports arranged visually rather than syntactically. My friend maintained that any standardised system of icons is merely propositional logic in disguise: once you fix what a particular pictogram denotes and establish rules for how icons combine, you have smuggled in grammatical structure that does all the actual inferential work, leaving the pictures themselves as mere decorative correlates. I want to understand, at a conceptual rather than historical level, whether this objection is fatal.

Specifically, please explain the tension between Neurath’s rejection of logical atomism and private mental states, on the one hand, and the apparent need for some structural mechanism—be it spatial arrangement, proportional scaling, or conventional pairing—to represent negation, disjunction, or quantitative comparison within a purely pictorial medium. If a picture represents a fact by resemblance, as Neurath seemed to suggest, how can the same medium represent the absence of that fact, or a conditional relation between two states of affairs, without relying on an implicit propositional framework that interprets the arrangement? I am not asking for a biography of the Vienna Circle, a bibliography of ISOTYPE publications, or citations of primary texts; rather, I want an intuitive but precise explanation of whether picture languages can genuinely carry logical relations, or whether they are necessarily parasitic on an underlying syntax that Neurath hoped to eliminate. In particular, address whether the standardisation of iconography—the very feature intended to make ISOTYPE universally accessible—inevitably reintroduces the kind of conventional, rule-governed structure that distinguishes language from mere correlation-display, and whether this undermines Neurath’s claim that pictorial representation avoids the metaphysical commitments embedded in verbal propositions.
