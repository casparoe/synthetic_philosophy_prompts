I'm writing this after a seminar on mental causation where we tried to extend the standard exclusion argument beyond its usual target, and I want to check whether the extension is legitimate or collapses through a hidden equivocation. Here is the reconstructed argument:

(1) Physical causal closure holds: every physical event has a sufficient cause that is entirely physical. (2) For any mental event M that causes physical effect E, M is realized by some physical base B that is also sufficient for E. (3) If B is sufficient for E, then M is causally redundant with respect to E, unless M is identical to B or B is not in fact sufficient. (4) Non-reductive physicalism denies both identity and the insufficiency of B. (5) So, on non-reductive physicalism, mental causation is systematically excluded. (6) Now consider institutional events, such as a corporation being dissolved by a board vote; the vote is realized by the individual physical movements and neural states of the directors. (7) Those individual physical movements and neural states are jointly sufficient for the legal dissolution (the papers filed, the registry updated, etc.). (8) Therefore, by the same reasoning as (1)–(5), the institutional property “being a board vote to dissolve” is causally redundant; only the micro-physical movements do the causal work. (9) But this applies to every macro-level causal claim in law, economics, and biology: if closure always holds and realization is always present, then only micro-physics ever causes anything. (10) Since macro-causation is indispensable and mental causation is at least as plausible as institutional causation, we must either reject closure altogether or reject the claim that realization always yields redundancy. (11) Yet rejecting closure abandons physicalism, while rejecting redundancy requires explaining why mental properties avoid exclusion that institutional properties do not, without being ad hoc.

The conclusion seems to be that the exclusion argument, if sound, eliminates not just mental causation but all higher-level causation, which is absurd. I feel like something slipped between (5) and (8)—perhaps in how “realization” works for collective or institutional properties versus mental properties, or in whether sufficiency at the micro-level really excludes macro-level efficacy, or in an unnoticed scope shift. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if I want to preserve mental causation without surrendering physical closure, do I have to pay the price of accepting downward causation for board votes and bacterial metabolism too? Or is there a principled asymmetry I can invoke?

Please diagnose the argument's weakest link, explaining why that step looked plausible and pricing the alternatives—especially whether saving mental causation forces one into universal emergentism or into an unmotivated exception for the mental.
