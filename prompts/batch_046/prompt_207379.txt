I'm a software engineer without any formal philosophy training, and the other day at work two of my colleagues got into a sustained disagreement that seemed to drift into metaphysics. We maintain a distributed user-service architecture made up of stateless containers that share no persistent storage and can be destroyed and rebuilt independently; Colleague A argued that because of this, there is no single object "the user service"—only temporary aggregates of running processes, comparable to a sandpile from which grains can be removed without destroying the pile. Colleague B replied that the service is defined by a single API contract, a shared logical schema, and a deployment manifest that orchestrates the containers as one functional unit, so an organizational relation genuinely binds the parts into a whole rather than merely heaping them together. At that point Colleague A introduced what he called the special-composition worry: unless there is some privileged bonding relation—whether causal interaction, functional integration, or intentional design—then unrestricted composition fails, and arbitrary collections of containers should not count as objects any more than arbitrary collections of atoms do. Colleague B countered that functional integration is precisely what our orchestration layer provides, making the service more analogous to an organism with interdependent organs than to an inert sandpile, and that applying physical-world restrictions to digital architectures risks a category mistake because software objects are constituted by relations of dependency and information flow rather than by spatial contiguity. Colleague A then pushed back that in software everything is ultimately arbitrary aggregation—Git repositories, Docker networks, load-balanced clusters—so if we accept restricted composition for physical objects we should be even more restrictive for digital ones, since there is no natural boundary that prevents us from declaring any random subset of containers a "service." Colleague B finished by saying that this overstates the arbitrariness: the deployment boundary, health-check logic, and schema dependencies create exactly the kind of non-arbitrary unity that philosophers demand, and denying it forces us into an implausible eliminativism about every engineered system.

Can you adjudicate this exchange move by move? For each of the four turns—(1) the sandpile analogy applied to stateless containers, (2) the claim that API contracts and manifests supply a genuine compositional relation, (3) the argument that digital objects should face stricter compositional restrictions because they lack physical contiguity, and (4) the claim that orchestration provides functional integration analogous to biological unity—please rate whether the move is clearly mistaken, mostly right, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify your rating. Also identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—perhaps about temporal vagueness in container identity, about whether composition requires consciousness or intentionality, or about whether the dispute is really about ontology or about pragmatic engineering taxonomy—and explain whether either side has accidentally assumed a position they would reject if they followed it consistently.
