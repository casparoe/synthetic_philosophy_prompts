Military decoration systems in several countries award combat medals to remote drone operators who direct lethal strikes, while withholding equivalent honors from civilian analysts or contractors who supply the targeting data, even when both contributions are causally necessary for the strike. At the same time, international humanitarian law extends the combatant’s privilege—the legal permission to kill without being treated as a murderer—only to those formally incorporated into the armed forces, not to civilians performing analogous targeting functions, regardless of physical proximity to harm. What conception of the moral equality of combatants, or of the basis for the combatant’s privilege, does this pairing of institutional practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it rest on exposure to risk, on membership in a command hierarchy, on a direct versus indirect causal distinction, or on some hybrid? Say whether the medal system and the legal doctrine presuppose the same philosophical view or different ones, whether the practice is internally consistent, and what a framework built on a purely functional or risk-based account of combatancy would have to change about both decorations and legal status.
