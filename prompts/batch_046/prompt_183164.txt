I'm a journalist covering philosophy of science and decision theory, and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about eight minutes—at a half-day workshop titled Choosing Blind: Rationality When Experience Changes You. The room is split: on one side are academic decision theorists who will catch any misstatement of formal expected-utility reasoning; on the other are clinicians—surgeons and audiologists—who counsel patients through transformative medical choices such as adult cochlear implantation or gender-affirming surgery, plus one family member of the organizer who has no philosophical background. I want the remarks to take L.A. Paul's notion of transformative experience not as decoration but as the structural spine that governs what I can and cannot say: the talk must explain why standard rational-choice frameworks collapse here, not merely because outcomes are unknown but because the present self lacks authoritative standing over a future self whose values may be incommensurable with its own, and it must do this without reducing the clinical perspective to mere intuition or pretending that formal dominance reasoning solves the problem. Please write these remarks in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—structured like a polished spoken letter or essay, in a tone appropriate for a journalist addressing experts without lecturing them and accessible enough that the non-specialist family member is never lost. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and assume I have no access to verify specific sources.
