I have no academic background in philosophy or history, but I have been thinking on my own about whether diseases and climate can be treated as genuine historical actors—forces with something like agency—without falling into environmental determinism that erases human choice. Please read Albert Camus's The Plague (La Peste) through this lens. Consider the plague bacillus and the stifling climate of Oran not merely as scenery, but as non-human causes that restructure solidarity and morality; at the same time, watch for the peril that the epidemic is made to seem like the true author of people's moral transformation. Focus particularly on Rieux and Tarrou's discussion of the plague's indifference, and on Grand's compulsive rewriting of a single sentence as a human attempt to reclaim narrative control. Where does the novel show that germs genuinely shape events, where does it resist being read as determinism, and what does the story add to this debate that abstract argument alone cannot provide? Please separate what is settled about Camus's allegory from what is genuinely contested among scholars of agency and environmental history, and do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus.
