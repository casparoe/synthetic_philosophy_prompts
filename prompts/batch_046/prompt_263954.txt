Philosophers of emotion have been debating whether emotions are best understood as evaluative judgments, as perceptual registrations of bodily or environmental salience, or as interoceptive feelings with intentional direction. For decades the literature has accumulated refinements—distinctions between occurrent and dispositional affect, between emotions with specific intentional objects and diffuse mood-atmospheres, between conceptually mediated evaluations and non-conceptual affective responses—without any camp conceding ground. The disagreement persists even though most participants now accept that emotions involve some combination of bodily change, evaluative content, and directed awareness.

My working theory is that this is not a single substantive dispute about the nature of emotion, but three different explanatory projects that happen to share a vocabulary: one concerned with the logical form and conceptual conditions of emotional content, one with the causal mechanisms that generate emotional valence, and one with the phenomenological structure of feeling something as valuable or threatening. On this view, the apparent counterexamples each side produces—objectless dread for the judgment theorist, conceptually unsophisticated animal fear for the same theorist, or cognitively impenetrable visceral reactions for the perceptualist—are not really testing a shared theory but marking the boundaries of different domains of inquiry.

I want a diagnosis rather than a defense of any position. Using what each side actually treats as decisive—whether a phenomenological report can overturn a neurofunctional account, whether evolutionary utility fixes emotional kind-membership, whether conceptual articulation is necessary for an emotion to have an object—tell me whether the stall is verbal (a disagreement about what counts as an explanation of emotion), substantive (a hidden empirical question about interoceptive accuracy or representational format), or structural (the sides are answering different questions about different phenomena under the heading 'emotion'). In particular, address whether edge cases like ambient mood-atmospheres or evaluative responses to abstract social structures are genuine anomalies for all three views or simply belong to a different category altogether. And given your diagnosis, say what would actually move the debate: a conceptual distinction that separates the questions cleanly, an empirical result that privileges one mechanism, a formal analysis of emotional representation, or an argument that the unifying project itself should be abandoned because affect is a heterogeneous family rather than a natural kind.
