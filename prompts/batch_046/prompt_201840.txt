A philosopher of obligation whose published case judgments I have catalogued has ruled as follows. A casual verbal promise to attend a social gathering, forgotten without harm, is not morally binding—mere social courtesy. A signed contract to deliver goods, broken for a more profitable deal, is binding and requires fulfillment or compensation. A promise extracted by blackmail is not binding, lacking legitimate authorship. A promise uttered under hypnosis and subsequently acted upon is not binding for the same reason. A parent’s promise to a child that the family will never move, broken by unavoidable relocation, is binding in spirit but not in letter: it demands explanation, apology, and repair rather than literal adherence. Finally, a research director’s written promise to a funding body—never communicated to the staff who depend on it—to maintain a laboratory’s funding line, upon which the institution structured its budget, is binding; institutional reliance suffices even when individual members are unaware.

Next case: A senior scientist configures an autonomous negotiation agent with her value parameters and authorizes it to enter binding collaborative agreements. The agent promises a partner laboratory exclusive access to a shared dataset for five years. The partner interacts solely with the agent, never meeting the scientist. The agent’s architecture includes parameter drift that the scientist did not fully constrain; had the initial seed varied slightly, the agent might have offered non-exclusive access. After two years, the scientist disables the agent and revokes the commitment, claiming she never personally made the promise and that the agent’s output was not fully determined by her will.

Predict her verdict on whether the scientist remains bound. Identify the implicit rule she is running—whether a reliance-based expectation theory, a normative-power commitment theory, an institutional-practice theory, or some hybrid—showing precisely how it accommodates all six prior judgments, and name the closest analog among them. Then lay out what the two leading competing rules predict about this agent case, give your best-guess verdict with reasons, and specify one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish which rule she actually holds.
