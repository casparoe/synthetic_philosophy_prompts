Since I began consulting on verification protocols for scientific instrumentation, I have become preoccupied with whether there is a hard logical ceiling on what can in principle be discovered. At a recent dinner, two colleagues—Elena, who works in modal epistemology, and Marcus, who works on dynamic epistemic logic—fell into a disagreement about Fitch's paradox of knowability that I have been unable to resolve. I am writing to ask you to adjudicate their exchange point by point, without appealing to literature I could not access myself.

Elena began by stating the classic argument. Assume that every truth is in principle knowable: for any proposition p, if p is true then it is possible that some agent knows p. Now suppose there is some true proposition q that is in fact unknown. Consider the conjunction C: "q is true and q is unknown." If anyone knew C, they would know q, contradicting the second conjunct of C; therefore C is true but unknowable, violating the original principle. Hence, if all truths are knowable, then no unknown truths exist—everything true is already known.

Marcus replied that Elena was relying on an implicit closure principle for knowledge under conjunction. He argued that we can consistently reject closure—especially for conjunctions that embed epistemic predicates—while retaining the intuition that every individual truth is discoverable. Without closure, he said, knowing the first conjunct of C does not automatically yield knowledge of q in the required form, so the contradiction dissolves.

Elena countered that she had not appealed to general closure. She drew a distinction between first-order knowability—the claim that p itself can come to be known—and second-order transparency—the claim that agents always have access to the fact that they know p. She maintained that Fitch's construction requires only the first-order principle applied specifically to C itself, not a blanket rule that knowledge distributes over arbitrary conjunctions. Marcus, she said, was conflating transparency with closure and attacking the wrong target.

Marcus then proposed a more specific repair. He suggested that in any realistic epistemic model, verification takes time. So the knowability principle should be indexed to temporal stages: if p is true at stage s, then there exists a later stage s' at which p is known. Under this formulation, the paradox is blocked because the evaluation of "q is unknown" is tied to the same stage at which knowledge is assessed; once the verification process for q reaches s', the unknown clause is false. There is never a stable, true conjunction of the form required by the argument.

Elena responded that this temporal indexing merely postpones the difficulty. If verification can extend across arbitrarily long sequences of stages, then one can construct a limit proposition L that is true but requires verification steps reaching through an infinite progression, so L is never known at any finite stage. She asked whether Marcus was therefore committed to a distinction between "finitely knowable" and "merely eventually knowable in the limit," and whether that distinction does not simply restate the boundary that the paradox was designed to expose, rather than dissolving it.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate Elena's initial presentation, Marcus's closure objection, Elena's distinction between first-order knowability and transparency, Marcus's temporal-indexing proposal, and Elena's limit-case reply, each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything both parties are missing—particularly whether their dispute addresses collective or distributed knowledge across agents, or whether it assumes that the knowability principle applies uniformly to self-referential propositions without restriction.
