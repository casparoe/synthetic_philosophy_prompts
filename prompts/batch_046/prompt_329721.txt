During a workshop on comparative transitional justice that I recently convened, two colleagues—Dr. Elena Vasquez, whose research centers on East Asian commemorative practices, and Dr. Marcus Webb, a political philosopher specializing in the American South—engaged in a sustained disagreement over whether Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung provides a genuinely transferable normative benchmark for other societies grappling with historical violence. Vasquez opened by arguing that Japan's postwar framework ought not to be evaluated as a failed approximation of the German model, but rather as a structurally distinct mnemonic regime: one organized around victim-centered atomic-bomb commemoration rather than perpetrator-centered expiation, so that applying German criteria to Japan commits a category error analogous to judging a mourning ritual by the standards of a criminal tribunal. Webb responded that this distinction collapses upon closer inspection, since Germany's own pedagogical architecture integrated extensive Jewish survivor testimony into state-sponsored reckoning, demonstrating that victim-centered and perpetrator-centered narratives are not mutually exclusive regimes; he further contended that Japan's persistent resistance to fully addressing wartime sexual slavery and forced labor reveals not an alternative mnemonic logic but a selective suppression that the "victim-centered" framing functionally obscures.

Vasquez then expanded the comparison to include Turkey's official denial of the Armenian genocide and the American South's Lost Cause heritage infrastructure, proposing that all four cases should be analyzed not along a unidimensional axis of reckoning versus denial, but according to what she called mnemonic subsidiarity: the degree to which narrative control and memorial funding are distributed among local communities versus centralized by state apparatus, with Germany representing high centralization, Japan moderate centralization with local victim-group autonomy, Turkey high centralization in service of denial, and the American South extreme fragmentation that allows local heritage groups to dominate public space. Webb countered that subsidiarity is ultimately a red herring, because in every case—including Germany—the state retains ultimate authority over curriculum standards, legal recognition of genocide, and public funding allocations; consequently, the morally relevant distinction is not the locus of narrative control but whether the institutional framework permits structural redress and material redistribution to affected communities, a criterion that, he argued, reveals Japan and the American South as more similar to each other than either is to Germany.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: assess Vasquez's initial claim about Japan's mnemonic regime, Webb's rebuttal regarding the integration of victim narratives in Germany, Vasquez's introduction of mnemonic subsidiarity as a comparative framework across all four cases, and Webb's final move about state authority and structural redress. Say who is right about which specific point, indicate what important consideration—perhaps regarding the temporal dynamics of generational transmission or the role of non-state transnational advocacy networks—both disputants appear to be neglecting, and state whether one side is more right overall.
