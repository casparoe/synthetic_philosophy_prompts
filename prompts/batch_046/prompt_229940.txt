Two members of an advanced ethics seminar, referred to here as Alex and Blake, have reached an impasse over moral particularism and its relationship to generalism. Alex defends the view that moral reasons are genuinely holistic: a feature that functions as a reason in favor of an action in one situation can, without contradiction, function as a reason against it in another, and there is no invariant valence that survives across contexts. Blake accepts that moral judgment is highly context-sensitive but insists that this is compatible with a sophisticated generalism, provided principles are understood as defeasible, qualified by implicit domain restrictions, and weighted by ceteris paribus conditions rather than treated as exceptionless algorithms.

In their first exchange, Alex offers a case in which a physician must choose between truthful disclosure and a gentle deception to a patient in the final hours of life. Alex argues that in this context, the feature "being honest" does not merely carry less weight but genuinely supplies a reason against disclosure, and that any attempt to capture this with a principle such as "be honest unless harm outweighs benefit" is circular, since the identification of what counts as harm or benefit already presupposes the particularist judgment in question. Blake replies that this only demonstrates that principles must include contextual specifications of their conditions of application, and that Alex mistakes the complexity of principle-formulation for the absence of principles altogether.

Alex then presses a second argument: moral learning, on the particularist account, resembles the acquisition of perceptual discrimination rather than the internalization of rules. One learns to see which features are salient and how they combine, not to subsume cases under general maxims. Blake counters that this analogy undermines Alex's position, because perceptual learning itself depends on the acquisition of generalizable capacities—recognizing colors, shapes, and spatial relations—without which there would be no discrimination to refine. If particularism denies any general structure to moral reasons, Blake asks, how can moral education be more than the accumulation of unrelated case-intuitions?

In response, Alex introduces a more subtle claim: generalists do not actually apply principles to reach judgments; rather, they reach particular judgments and then construct principles post hoc to rationalize them. The real explanatory work is done by particularist sensitivity, and generalism is therefore a redundant layer of description. Blake's final move is to charge that this renders particularism unstable: if reasons lack any stable structure across cases, then moral disagreement becomes intractable, because disputants share no common framework—only competing, incommensurable perceptions of salience. Blake suggests that particularism therefore collapses into a form of moral anti-realism, despite Alex's insistence that reasons are objective but context-dependent.

The seminar ends without resolution. Adjudicate the exchange point by point. Is Alex's physician case a successful counterexample to invariant-valence principles, or can Blake's qualified-generalism accommodate it without circularity? Does Blake's challenge about moral education genuinely threaten particularism, or does Alex's perceptual-analogy response successfully deflect it? Is Blake's charge that particularism collapses into anti-realism fair, given that particularists typically affirm objective but holistically structured reasons? Finally, identify what both parties appear to be missing: for instance, whether they conflate reason-holism with value-pluralism, whether particularism requires a distinctive account of moral expertise, or whether there is a middle position—such as a form of contextualist generalism—that neither has considered.
