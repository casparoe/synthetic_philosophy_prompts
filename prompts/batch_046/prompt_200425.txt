You are a philosopher of decision theory with experience teaching formal arguments to philosophy graduate students. Before outlining anything, take a moment to think carefully and list the conceptual dependencies that make coherence arguments work, then proceed.

I am leading a single 90-minute seminar for second-year philosophy graduate students. They have worked through Savage’s axiomatization of subjective expected utility but have not yet encountered coherence (Dutch book) arguments for expected-utility maximization. I need a teaching plan—not the explanation itself—for how to guide them through the coherence argument.

Plan the dependency sequence: which distinctions must be in place before others (in particular, the difference between coherence of belief and coherence of preference, and why the latter needs a representation result rather than a consistency check alone), and in what order the pieces should be introduced so that the normative force of the argument is visible. Choose one concrete carrying example: a three-state weather scenario with ambiguous outcomes in which an agent has cyclic preferences over state-contingent acts. Specify exactly what structural features this example must possess and what simplifying feature it should deliberately lack (for instance, state-independent utility) so that it does not collapse into the standard coin-flip money pump. Then map the misconceptions this audience is likely to form: I expect them to conflate coherence with diachronic consistency, to object that the bookie argument assumes implausible transparent access to mental states, and to treat the linearity of probabilities and the linearity of utility as separate achievements rather than jointly forced. For each misconception, identify the precise point in your sequence where it should be headed off.

Design one precise check-for-understanding question that would reveal whether the students see that coherence forces linearity in probabilities and utility together, not separately.

Finally, state explicitly which extension you will leave out and openly acknowledge as beyond this session—such as the dynamic coherence literature or the direct derivation from von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms—and say why omitting it protects the session’s focus.

Give a committed, definite plan with reasons for each structural choice; do not present a menu of options. Be precise about the example’s architecture and the misconception map.
