Consider the following institutional procurement design, now standard in several large-scale municipal infrastructure systems. The process has two stages. In Stage 1, potential contractors submit preliminary engineering designs and pay a non-refundable entry fee equal to 2 percent of their projected total cost. Only fee-paying firms may proceed to Stage 2, the competitive final bid. The escalation mechanism works as follows: if a firm is selected as the provisional winner but then withdraws—whether because new information reveals the project will be unprofitable, because internal capacity has changed, or because of an external shock—it must pay a withdrawal penalty equal to 15 percent of its preliminary bid. Crucially, the government does not retain these forfeited fees and penalties as general revenue; instead, they are rolled directly back into the budget pool for the next bidding round on the same project, lowering the effective cost baseline for future bidders. The structural result is that a firm can find itself remaining in a contract it privately expects to generate net losses, reasoning that the withdrawal penalty exceeds the anticipated loss, while procurement officers defend such continued engagement as necessary to protect process integrity.

What philosophical commitments about rationality, value, and responsibility does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume that a rational agent ought never to abandon a course of action once resources have been committed, regardless of future expectations? Or does it presuppose a collective-level rationality that justifies individual-level escalation? Examine whether the practice is philosophically coherent, or whether its parts presuppose inconsistent things—for instance, does the government’s decision to recycle forfeited penalties into the next round treat sunk costs as genuinely irrelevant to rational choice (as standard instrumental rationality demands) while simultaneously exploiting them to bind participants? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it is merely compatible with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined: could it be licensed by a pure expected-utility framework with risk aversion, by a commitment-based theory of practical rationality, or by a social-contract view of institutional fairness? Finally, describe what a procurement policy built on the view that sunk costs are always irrelevant to rational choice—and that withdrawal should never be penalized—would actually have to change in its structure, its funding mechanics, and its justification to contractors.
