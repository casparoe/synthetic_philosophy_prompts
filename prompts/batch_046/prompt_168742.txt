I'm running a workshop for about 14 grad students in a philosophy of history course, we've got 90 minutes and I'm tired of them just repeating that history is writen by the victors like it's a meme. I want them to actually feel why the cliché hides something nastier: it's not just that winners lie, but that the kind of causal story you can tell depends on whose filing cabinets survived. For the side with thick archives—bureaucratic records, minutes, supply logs—you naturally reach for structural explanations that spread responsibility across systems. For the side with thin archives—scattered personal letters, oral fragments, nothing institutional—you end up forced into intentionalist stories where every action looks like a deliberate choice because there's no paper trail of constraints. The idea is that historical relativism isn't just "different perspectives" but an asymmetry in what kinds of explanations the evidence even permits.

Design a simulation/game where they build competing explanations of the same fictional event from two simulated "archive packets"—one dense with bureaucratic docs, one sparse with personal traces. They should work in groups, construct the narratives, present them, and only then see how the form of explanation was rigged by the archive before anyone names the problem. Give me rules, roles, materials, some payoff or incentive structure (maybe peer evaluation of plausibility?), a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the idea. Include an honest note on what feature of real archival research this can't reproduce, plus a heads-up on what predictably breaks the first run. Keep it runnable without anyone needing real historical knowledge—fabricate the document summaries yourself. No reading lists or citations please.
