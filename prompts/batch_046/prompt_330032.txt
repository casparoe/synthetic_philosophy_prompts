Read Harold Ramis's 1993 film Groundhog Day through the lens of Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, but do not treat the time-loop as a straightforward cinematic allegory. I want you to navigate the tension between the cosmological interpretation—under which every state of affairs returns identically for eternity—and the ethical interpretation, which treats recurrence as a thought experiment that tests whether one can affirm one's life in its totality, including its unavoidable suffering. Phil Connors begins by experiencing repetition as punishment; he then converts the loop into an instrument for self-cultivation, acquiring skills and moral habits; ultimately he seems to break free through an act of selfless love combined with a perfectly executed day. Examine whether this arc dramatizes amor fati—an unconditional affirmation of the loop itself—or whether it depicts strategic mastery oriented toward eventual escape. Pay particular attention to the repeated scenes with the homeless old man: Phil attempts to save him, fails every time, and eventually accepts the failure while continuing to act. Does this sequence approximate a Nietzschean yes-saying to unavoidable suffering, or does the film's narrative resolution—release from repetition—reveal that affirmation remains conditional on eventual redemption? In addition, consider whether the film's comedic structure undermines the existential gravity Nietzsche assigns to the thought, and whether its implicit teleology—repetition as pedagogy culminating in liberation—distorts the non-progressive, cyclical character of eternal return. The reading should be disciplined in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of philosophy, and it should not distort the philosophical concept to accommodate narrative closure. End by identifying what the film, precisely through its resistance to the concept, reveals about the psychology of affirmation that abstract argument alone cannot capture.
