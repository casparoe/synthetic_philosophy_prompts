The student's submitted answer reads as follows. Meritocracy claims rewards should match contribution, but this assumes people deserve what they earn. Rawls argued that moral desert is never an appropriate basis for distributive justice, since talents and efforts are arbitrarily distributed from a moral point of view. Therefore the meritocrat's appeal to contribution fails: if effort is largely shaped by upbringing and natural endowment, then no one is the ultimate source of their achievements. That said, we can distinguish source-desert from ownership-desert. Source-desert requires that the agent be the original causal origin of her effort, free of social or genetic luck. Ownership-desert requires only that the agent have a legitimate claim to the fruits of her labor, regardless of whether she caused her own diligence. The meritocrat typically needs source-desert to justify unequal shares; without it, the view collapses into a libertarian entitlement theory that ignores structural unfairness. Still, meritocracy might be defended instrumentally. If rewards track contribution, people have stronger incentives to develop abilities, which increases overall welfare. This does not make the view egalitarian, since egalitarianism requires equal shares regardless of output. Critics reply that effort is never truly self-authored: even the desire to work hard is cultivated by social conditions. Consequently, source-desert is unattainable, and with it the moral foundation of meritocracy disappears.
