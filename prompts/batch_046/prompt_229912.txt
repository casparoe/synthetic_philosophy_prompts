I am serving as a teaching assistant for an advanced seminar on institutional economics and collective action, and I have been asked to evaluate a student response to a midterm prompt that we designed to move beyond standard textbook illustrations. The question concerns a decentralized solar microgrid serving a rural cluster of households: electricity drawn from the local mesh is non-excludable for connected users, consumption is rivalrous at all times because shared inverters and transmission lines have finite capacity—though rivalry becomes especially acute during peak evening hours—while the upkeep of storage nodes and grid maintenance produces benefits that are largely non-rivalrous and non-excludable for all connected households, even though any individual household’s direct contribution to maintenance labor is difficult to observe. The student was instructed to determine whether the risk of progressive grid degradation should be analyzed as a tragedy of the commons, a public-goods problem, both, or neither; to distinguish rigorously between excludability and rivalry in this specific technological context; to reference the theoretical frameworks associated with Hardin, Ostrom, and Samuelson; and to propose institutional design features that might prevent collapse without resorting to full privatization.

The student’s answer proceeds as follows. They correctly observe that Hardin’s pasture analogy describes a common-pool resource that is rivalrous yet non-excludable, and they apply this to the microgrid’s consumption side, noting that one household’s peak-hour draw reduces available capacity for others. They then correctly summarize Samuelson’s definition of a pure public good as both non-rivalrous and non-excludable, and they identify the maintenance benefits of the grid—reliable storage and functioning inverters—as falling into this category, with free-riding as the central risk. They also correctly list several of Ostrom’s design principles, including clear boundaries, proportional equivalence between benefits and costs, and collective-choice arrangements. However, they conflate the maintenance problem with the consumption problem by arguing that because individual upkeep efforts are unobservable, the degradation of shared infrastructure is “essentially the same tragedy of overuse that Hardin described,” ignoring that neglect of upkeep is free-riding on a non-rivalrous benefit rather than over-consumption of a rivalrous resource. They further misrepresent Ostrom’s scope, stating that she demonstrated collective management succeeds only in small, homogeneous, face-to-face communities, and therefore her principles cannot apply to a technologically mediated network like this one. Finally, they draw an inference that does not follow: they claim that because direct monitoring of each household’s labor is impossible, Ostrom’s monitoring principle is necessarily violated, which means collective management is impossible here and full privatization is the only remedy, without considering indirect or outcome-based monitoring mechanisms.
