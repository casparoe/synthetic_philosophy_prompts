I am a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and a recent office debate about server migration—whether a running system remains the same instance after every underlying component has been replaced over years—led me to the classic Ship of Theseus problem. Rather than the usual plank-by-plank formulation, I am interested in the distinction between material continuity and organizational or functional continuity that philosophers of technology apply to artifacts and engineered systems. I would like you to read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through that precise lens: treat the creature's assembled body and his subsequent mental and physical development as a case study in diachronic identity for composite objects.

Specifically, I want to understand where the novel treats the creature's identity as anchored to his original cadaver parts, to his functional design and causal history as a deliberately constructed being, or to some form of narrative or psychological continuity, and whether Shelley anticipates modern functional-persistence arguments rather than the biological or metaphysical versions of the paradox. Which scenes—the initial assembly, his self-education in isolation, the final Arctic confrontation—best illuminate this tension, and where does the novel resist being reduced to a simple illustration? In particular, does the horror derive partly from the failure of any continuity account to bridge the gap between assembled matter and recognized human personhood? I am preparing a short talk for a tech-philosophy meetup, so I need a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical distinction seriously, that points out where the work complicates rather than confirms the idea, and that explains what the story adds to the argument that a dry analysis of ship-planks or server nodes misses. Please do not rely on direct textual quotations, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want the analysis to stand on common knowledge of the plot and on the conceptual structure itself.
