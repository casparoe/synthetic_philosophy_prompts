I've been tracking a recurring defensive pattern in debates about conceptual boundaries—especially around distinctions meant to separate statements that are fixed purely by how words are defined from those that depend on empirical investigation of the world. The pattern runs like this. A philosopher proposes a sharp binary: on one side, claims true solely in virtue of meaning or convention; on the other, claims whose acceptance requires observing how things actually stand. Critics then produce intermediate cases—definitions that embed substantial theoretical commitments, or widely entrenched empirical generalizations that now function like rules of language. Rather than abandoning the binary, defenders absorb each case individually, assigning it to a hybrid or qualified subcategory: "this holds by convention but only within a specific theoretical framework," or "this is fact-dependent but with an unusually high degree of conventional entrenchment," or "this belongs to a transitional zone that does not threaten the core distinction." Each local judgment sounds reasonable. But over time the middle zone expands to include most of the philosophically interesting cases, while the original binary is still invoked for methodological work—say, to argue that one discipline needs empirical testing and another does not—without anyone specifying which fragment of the now-dispersed boundary applies. The distinction is never globally falsified; instead, all counterevidence is distributed across an accumulating taxonomy of individually plausible exceptions, and the binary survives mainly as a rhetorical frame. Is there an established name for this particular failure mode, in which a dichotomy is preserved not by global defense but by local protection through proliferating intermediate classifications? I am not asking for the general claim that all distinctions are vague, nor for the standard argument that no sharp boundary exists. I want to know whether philosophers have named this specific strategic or structural pattern, how it differs from ordinary conceptual vagueness and from applying a degenerative-research-programme structure to concepts rather than to scientific theories, and—if no precise term exists—what definition and boundary conditions would best capture it, especially the feature that the binary continues to be used for practical and argumentative work even after its explanatory content has been dispersed into the intermediate categories.
