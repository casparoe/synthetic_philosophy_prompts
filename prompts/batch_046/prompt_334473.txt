I have recently found myself repeatedly delaying necessary but unpleasant actions—medical appointments, difficult conversations, long-term financial adjustments—despite knowing precisely the costs I am imposing on my future self, and this has made the philosophical problem of weakness of will feel urgently personal rather than abstract. I am preparing to moderate a podcast interview with an unnamed philosopher-economist who defends a specific, non-standard account: that akrasia is not a failure of rational agency or a conflict between reason and passion, but the predictable output of hyperbolic discounting operating across what she terms successive temporal selves. On her view, each temporal slice discounts future rewards hyperbolically, so the present self rationally selects immediate gratification even when it anticipates the future self’s suffering; precommitment devices—Ulysses contracts, binding mechanisms, option elimination—are therefore not cures for weakness but inter-self bargaining tools that alter the option set facing the later agent. I want a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that turn on the actual content of this position, avoid generic biography or career questions, and press exactly where the literature presses: please include, for each major line of pressure, a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to offer.

Specifically, frame the exchange through the lens of diachronic autonomy—whether a present self’s binding of a future self is an exercise of self-governance or a form of temporal domination—and cover the following lines, with the reasoning behind each visible in the framing:

First, the metaphysical status of her “successive temporal selves.” If hyperbolic discounting is rational for each slice, does she commit to a kind of personal-identity fission? And if so, how does she avoid the conclusion that no single agent is weak-willed—only different agents with conflicting interests? Anticipate that she will reply these are not metaphysically distinct persons but functional nodes in one diachronic system, analogous to subpersonal modules. The follow-up should ask how that functionalist reply distinguishes her view from a hierarchical model of self-control, and whether the “binding” performed by precommitment is then merely one module dominating another rather than the agent exercising autonomy.

Second, the normative standing of blame and shame. If akrasia is rational intertemporal bargaining, blaming the present self for yielding seems as misplaced as blaming a party to a contract for enforcing its terms. How can she preserve the intuition that weakness of will is a genuine fault rather than a strategic disappointment? Anticipate that she might distinguish ex post regret (rational for the future self) from ex ante blameworthiness (inapplicable), or invoke collective responsibility across the diachronic system. The follow-up should press whether collective responsibility requires a unified subject that her model denies, and ask for a concrete case—say, a physician who delays necessary surgery—where she can explain why the patient should feel shame rather than mere strategic disappointment.

Third, the efficacy and ethics of precommitment itself. If the bound future self remains an agent with its own hyperbolic curve, does the contract genuinely resolve the conflict or merely suppress one party? Is there a “diachronic veto” problem: can the future self rightfully revoke the commitment? Anticipate that she may argue revocation undermines the credibility that makes precommitment rational, so truly rational agents should design irrevocable mechanisms. The follow-up should ask whether an irrevocable mechanism eliminates the phenomenon of weakness of will by removing the option entirely, and whether her view can still describe a case in which the agent, despite having no practical exit, experiences the psychological pull of the immediate reward—whether the will can be weak even when the hands are tied.

Fourth, empirical boundary conditions and the risk of unfalsifiability. Hyperbolic discounting is well documented for monetary rewards and addiction-like behaviors, but does her model extend to moral akrasia—cases where one acts against one’s all-things-considered moral judgment for no obvious material reward, such as lying to a friend out of momentary embarrassment? Anticipate that she might generalize hyperbolic discounting to all utility-bearing outcomes, including emotional and social ones, or concede that moral akrasia requires an auxiliary theory. The follow-up should ask whether adding that auxiliary theory renders her account unfalsifiable—if any failure of will can be redescribed as hyperbolic discounting of some hidden utility—and whether she can specify in advance what empirical finding would count against her model rather than being absorbed into it.

Finally, please include at least one question that asks whether her framework can distinguish healthy long-term planning from pathological self-coercion, and whether there is any normative criterion—other than the present self’s own preference—by which one temporal self should be recognized as having legitimate authority over another. The overall tone should be rigorous and respectful, suitable for a philosophical audience, without any request for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
