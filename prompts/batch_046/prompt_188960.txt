Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on whether cancel culture, deplatforming, and social sanction function as legitimate punishment in digital public spheres.

The first camp, call them the distributed-sanctionists, holds that networked social sanctions—including algorithmic demotion, deplatforming, and employment termination triggered by public speech—are proportionate, democratically legitimate, and necessary correctives in an environment where state regulation is sluggish and platform power is privatized. They treat digital reputation as a common-pool resource that communities may protect through exclusion, analogous to scaled civic ostracism but operating via network effects rather than state coercion.

The second camp, call them the procedural-institutionalists, holds that these same practices constitute a privatized extra-legal punishment regime that lacks procedural safeguards, generates unquantifiable chilling effects, and collapses the distinction between withholding association and withholding livelihood. They treat digital reputation as an indexical marker of social trust that should not be subject to collective extraction without due-process analogues, and they deny that reversibility and publicity adequately substitute for formal accountability.

Your deliverable should be a single document containing the following sections, in this exact order, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. Agreed Claims (about 350 words): A numbered list of factual and normative claims both camps can sign. Include agreement on the mechanics of modern platform governance, the empirical reality that high-profile sanctions alter speaker behavior, the distinction between state censorship and private association, and the claim that both camps reject purely consequentialist justifications that ignore speaker intent. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; paraphrase any well-known arguments without quotation marks.

2. Disagreements Table (about 450 words): A table with three columns—Precise Disagreement (worded so each camp accepts the formulation of its own view), The Crux, and Test Case / Predicted Assessment. There should be exactly three rows, one for each crux below.

3. Crux Analysis (about 350 words): For each of the three cruxes identified in the table, present a concrete hypothetical scenario that both camps agree is relevant, then record each camp’s predicted evaluation of that scenario in advance, with reasoning brief enough to fit the section length but precise enough to expose the underlying normative divergence.

The three cruxes you must address are:
- Whether there is a morally relevant discontinuity along the sanction gradient—the spectrum running from algorithmic demotion through deplatforming to employment termination—or whether these are merely points on a continuous punitive scale with no sharp ethical boundary.
- Whether digital reputation is best conceptualized as a private asset, a common-pool resource subject to community governance, or an indexical marker of social trust that resists collective appropriation.
- Whether the absence of formal due process in distributed sanctions is genuinely mitigated by the reversibility, transparency, and non-physical nature of digital penalties, or whether that mitigation is illusory because reputational harm is often irreversible in practice and structurally opaque.

For each crux, include the specific case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, plus what each side predicts about it.

Write in a formal, polished register. Ensure every formulation of disagreement is neutral enough that neither camp could claim misrepresentation of its own position, yet sharp enough that the residual dispute is genuinely substantive rather than verbal. If you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal—particularly around the definition of punishment—state which part and why, within the Crux Analysis section.
