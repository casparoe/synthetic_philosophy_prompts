I'm preparing a conceptual framework for reasoning about logical uncertainty — that is, how a computationally bounded but ideally rational agent ought to assign credences to mathematical propositions, proof-status claims, and meta-logical statements that it has not yet derived. Rather than comparing formalisms directly, I want the full specification of adequacy first. Give me the desiderata a theory in this domain must satisfy: the phenomena it must accommodate (e.g., graded belief in open conjectures, updating upon partial proof fragments, calibration against expert mathematicians' confidence without reducing logic to empirical frequency), the distinctions it must preserve (e.g., between a proposition being highly probable and being derivable within a fixed computation budget, between uncertainty about logical truth and uncertainty about one's own proof-checking reliability), and the consistency constraints it cannot breach (e.g., coherence across logical worlds, avoidance of self-referential Dutch books, preservation of non-dogmatism about the inference rules the agent itself employs). For each desideratum, state the motivating case or paradox that makes it binding, and indicate whether you take it to be negotiable or absolute.

I am particularly concerned with a tension that seems underexplored: any adequate theory should require that probabilities converge toward 0 or 1 as decisive proofs are discovered, yet it must simultaneously allow the agent to maintain non-vanishing credence in the possibility that its own verification procedures contain an undetected systematic error — an error that might invalidate what it takes to be a proof. Please treat the resolution of this tension as part of the specification, and say whether the two demands are jointly satisfiable or whether they reveal that the full list of desiderata is itself inconsistent. Finally, identify the single requirement that eliminates the broadest class of existing approaches — for instance, treatments that assign logical truths prior probability 1, or calibration schemes that treat logical belief as statistical sampling — and provide a brief table scoring the main families of approaches against the list. Keep the conceptual labor on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on historical attribution; no bibliographies or citation lists are needed.
