I am a novelist drafting a long-form narrative in which consciousness is distributed across objects, landscapes, and temporal layers—closer to a collective phenomenology than to conventional point-of-view fiction. In the course of research I have encountered panpsychism, and specifically the combination problem: if every fundamental physical entity possesses minimal consciousness, by what mechanism do these micro-experiential properties aggregate into the unified, structured macro-consciousness of a human subject? Rather than a standard philosophical summary, I ask you to translate both the core thesis and the combination problem into the vocabulary of narrative theory and fiction craft—focalization in its internal, external, fixed, and variable modes; diegetic levels and nested narration; free indirect discourse; narrative voice distinct from character voice; plot emerging from distributed subplots; heteroglossia; and the distinction between story and discourse. For each element of panpsychism and for the combination problem in particular, say whether it maps cleanly onto one of these concepts, whether it requires distortion to fit, or whether it lacks any counterpart in how novels construct consciousness from discrete textual elements. Then deliver a residue report: do the translation failures indicate that narrative theory simply lacks resources for ontological aggregation, or do they expose hidden assumptions in the combination problem—such as the transparency of parts-to-whole relations—that fiction routinely violates without contradiction? I seek no bibliography or citation; the value lies precisely in the analytical mismatch.
