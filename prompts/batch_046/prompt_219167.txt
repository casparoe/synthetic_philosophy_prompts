For a feature I am writing on the conceptual limits of rational-choice theory, I have encountered the following line of reasoning about the centipede game and backward induction. It seems to prove that the paradox collapses into self-refutation, but I cannot locate the precise misstep.

(1) In a finitely repeated centipede game with perfect information, common knowledge of rationality requires both players to stop at the first node.

(2) If both stop immediately, no cooperative move is ever realised, so there is never an observed deviation from backward-induction behaviour.

(3) Without observed deviations, the assumption that one's opponent is rational becomes empirically unfalsifiable: every outcome is compatible with it.

(4) A decision principle that depends on an unfalsifiable empirical assumption about an opponent's reasoning is epistemically empty, because it admits no correction.

(5) Hence backward induction cannot be legitimately applied: its correct application eliminates the evidential basis for the rationality premise on which it rests.

(6) The same structure holds for any finite game with a unique backward-induction solution, so rational-choice theory is globally self-undermining in all such cases.

The conclusion is clearly too strong, yet each premise looks plausible. Which step should be rejected, and at what conceptual cost? Can one defend backward induction by treating the rationality assumption as normative rather than empirical, or does that merely relocate the difficulty?
