Analyze the 2016 United Kingdom European Union membership referendum and its campaign dynamics as a case study in moral foundations theory, but avoid reducing the episode to a generic left-right dichotomy. Apply the full six-foundation framework—care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, and liberty/oppression—to the rhetorical strategies of both the Leave and Remain coalitions. Focus on two specific asymmetries: first, that Remain’s messaging was dominated by care/harm framing (economic risk, job losses, public service strain) and fairness/cheating framing (contractual obligations to partners, unfair free-riding), with a notable absence of loyalty or authority appeals that might have defended EU membership as an institutional commitment worthy of deference; second, that the Leave coalition was not psychologically homogeneous, combining high-binding conservative voters (for whom immigration and sovereignty activated loyalty, authority, and sanctity concerns) with libertarian-leaning voters who, according to the libertarian moral profile identified in extensions of the theory, score high on liberty/oppression and unusually low on care and sanctity. Map these subgroups onto the foundation matrix, and ask whether the theory can explain the coalition’s stability despite such divergent intuitive profiles, and whether Remain’s failure reflects a strategic neglect of binding foundations or a deeper limitation in the theory’s account of how foundations are activated in political contestation. Then reverse the lens: does the strategic, campaign-specific deployment of foundations—where the same voter might invoke liberty against Brussels bureaucracy and loyalty against immigration—support the descriptive claim that moral judgment is driven by stable modular intuitions, or does it suggest that these foundations are more context-sensitive and rhetorically constructed than the theory permits? Finally, address the normative question that frames the domain: if Remain and Leave supporters indeed operated with different weights across these six dimensions, does that psychological divergence provide any reason for thinking one side was morally correct, or does the Brexit episode instead show that descriptive moral psychology is silent on normative justification and that the appearance of moral disagreement may partly reflect the strategic mobilization of different intuitive modules?
