So there's this municipal water charter in a town called Millbrook — not a real place, but the setup is based on an actual hybrid model I read about somewhere. Voting members on the watershed board are: anyone who owns land in the recharge zone (even if they live three counties away and only visit thier cabin twice a year), anyone who holds a commercial water-use permit, and one "future-proxy" seat filled by lottery from local high-school seniors who serve for one year with full voting rights on any decision that affects groundwater quality past 2100. Meanwhile, renters who actually drink the tap water inside town limits have no direct vote; they are "covered" by the town council's ex-officio members.

What view of democratic membership — who counts as part of the demos — does this charter implicitly assume? Is it just stitching together property rights, stakeholder risk, and intergenerational care in a way that collapses under pressure, or is there a principled boundary theory that licenses exactly this combination? If we replaced whatever implicit theory is doing the work with a clean "all affected interests" principle, which seats survive and which disappear? And does the future-proxy seat presuppose that unborn people have present standing, or could it be justified on purely instrumental or trustee grounds?

Also, I'm curious whether the charter is philosophically underdetermined — could a control-based view or an all-subjected view explain the renter exclusion without also breaking the out-of-town landowner inclusion? I don't need a bibliography, just whether the practice holds together or falls apart once you name the commitments.
