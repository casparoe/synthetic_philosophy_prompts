As a sixth-form student writing an extended essay on distributive justice, I should like to ask what philosophical commitments are quietly built into the institutional design of universal basic income proposals. Specifically, I have noticed that nearly every serious scheme guarantees an unconditional cash grant regardless of employment status or willingness to work, yet almost none propose replacing in-kind provisions—public healthcare, compulsory education, or disability support—with an equivalent cash sum that recipients could spend freely. What theory of value, or of justice, does that combination presuppose? Does it assume that income is a primary good that ought to be distributed equally, while health and learning involve non-commodity values that money cannot properly substitute? Or does it presuppose that real freedom requires unconditional resources, but only up to a threshold that rules out the commodification of certain goods?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory behind this pattern, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether the same commitments could justify a fully cash-substitutable scheme or whether they exclude it. In doing so, please separate what is settled philosophical ground from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a disputed view as though it were the consensus. Finally, please write your answer in British English.
