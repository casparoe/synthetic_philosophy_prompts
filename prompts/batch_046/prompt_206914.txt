I'm working through my judgments about what dramatists owe real dead people, and I want to know whether they hang together under one principle or whether I'm holding incompatible standards.

Here are my verdicts.

- Inventing a private emotional scene for a dead figure—say, a queen weeping alone after a public defeat—when there is no record of it but nothing contradicts it: permissible, humanizing, within fiction's license.

- Inventing an entirely private conversation between two documented figures and putting words in their mouths that align with their known views: also permissible, provided it does not alter the causal history of public events.

- Compressing a multi-year diplomatic negotiation into a single meeting, altering who was present and the order of concessions, to heighten drama: impermissible, because it misrepresents the historical process that shaped real outcomes.

- Attributing to a dead activist an anachronistic theoretical vocabulary—having a 1960s civil-rights leader speak of intersectionality in the precise theoretical sense developed decades later—to make him sound like a modern ally: impermissible, and I feel it is somehow worse than the time-compression case, even though both involve falsehood.

- Creating a fictional romantic relationship for a dead writer to explain documented melancholy, with no evidence either way: permissible, on the same grounds as the private weeping scene.

What I keep going back and forth on is this: a documentary uses AI voice synthesis to have a dead president deliver a speech he never gave, but every sentence is stitched together from sentences he actually wrote or said elsewhere. Is that closer to the permissible invented conversation, or to the impermissible anachronistic vocabulary?

Is there a single principle—perhaps distinguishing violations of narrative autonomy from violations of epistemic authority—that delivers all five verdicts? If so, state it precisely, show which case motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for the AI-synthesis case and for one I haven't mentioned: a biopic that depicts every documented event accurately but assigns the dead protagonist a modern psychological diagnosis (for instance, autism) that was not available in his era, thereby reframing his public actions. If no single principle works without overturning one of my judgments, tell me which verdict is the outlier and what the cleaner principle looks like without it.
