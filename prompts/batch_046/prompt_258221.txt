The debate over Gödel's rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations has been stalled for decades between cosmologists who dismiss its closed timelike curves as physically irrelevant because our universe appears isotropic, and philosophers of time who treat those curves as evidence that linear temporal order is not a necessary feature of spacetime. Both sides invoke the same metric and the same field equations; neither denies the mathematics. My working theory is that the impasse is not empirical—no one expects observational cosmology to settle cosmic rotation precisely enough—but about what counts as a legitimate idealization: whether a cosmological model must preserve global causal structure to count as physically meaningful, or whether local consistency with observed data is sufficient. Is that right? Or is the disagreement actually about whether "time travel" in this metric is conceptually coherent, with each side smuggling in different conditions for personal identity over time? Diagnose it: a verbal dispute over "time," a substantive disagreement about causal structure, different evidential standards for theoretical cosmology, or bedrock intuitions about whether mathematics can legislate for physics? And tell me honestly: could any formal result—say, about the conditions under which spacetime admits a global time ordering—or any conceptual distinction actually resolve it, or is this an ill-posed question that splits into separate problems once physics and metaphysics are separated?
