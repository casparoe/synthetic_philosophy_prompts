I've been trying to work out what actually determines species membership, and my intuitions about concrete cases pull in directions that are hard to reconcile. I'd like to know whether there's a single principle that captures all of them, or which verdict I should abandon.

Consider these four situations. First, there are the ring-forming salamanders around California's Central Valley: adjacent populations interbreed freely, but the two terminal populations—coastal and inland—overlap geographically and do not interbreed. My verdict is that they constitute one species, with the terminal isolation being an artifact of geographic separation rather than an intrinsic reproductive barrier. Second, imagine two frog populations living in the same wetland, morphologically indistinguishable, and occasionally producing fertile hybrids, but differing in chromosome number so that hybrid meiosis usually fails. Here my verdict is that they are two distinct species, because chromosomal incompatibility creates a genuine reproductive boundary even if it is occasionally breached. Third, consider a synthetic organism engineered by combining genomes from two distinct plant genera, then established in the wild, where it reproduces clonally, occupies a novel ecological niche, and forms stable populations over many generations—without any natural evolutionary lineage leading to it. My verdict is that this should not be recognized as a species at all; it is a biological artifact, and taxonomic status requires an evolutionary history that this entity lacks. Fourth, there is an all-female lizard lineage that reproduces parthenogenetically, originated from a past hybridization event, and has maintained a distinct ecological role for thousands of generations. My verdict is that this is a legitimate species, despite the impossibility of interbreeding being definitional rather than contingent.

These four judgments feel right individually, but together they seem difficult to square. In the first case I privilege continuous gene flow over terminal reproductive isolation; in the second I privilege reproductive isolation despite occasional gene flow. In the third I reject an entity that meets biological and ecological criteria for specieshood, apparently because of its artificial origin; in the fourth I accept an entity that fails the interbreeding criterion entirely, apparently because of its natural evolutionary persistence. Is there one account of species—perhaps some refined version of the biological species concept, the phylogenetic concept, or an ecological concept—that delivers all four verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does that choice commit me to about whether species are primarily defined by reproductive dynamics, evolutionary lineage, ecological function, or some interaction of these? Please also state what your favored principle predicts for a case I have not listed: for instance, a population that exchanges genes freely with its neighbors but has been reproductively isolated for only a brief period by a recent, human-constructed geographic barrier.
