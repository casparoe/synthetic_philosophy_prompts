I've recently begun a seminar on Quine's philosophy of language, and the discussions have spilled over into conversations with a colleague in computational linguistics who builds multilingual translation systems. She keeps invoking Quine's gavagai scenario to support a sweeping claim: that because radical translation is indeterminate, there is literally no fact of the matter about what any foreign-language expression means, and therefore any machine-translation output is merely an arbitrary manual imposed on behavior rather than a recovery of genuine semantic content. I suspect she is running together several different theses that the thought experiment might support, but I am not sure which ones are actually entailed by the behavioral underdetermination of rival translation manuals and which are stronger metaphysical glosses she has added herself.

Could you disentangle the precise claims hiding inside that slogan for me? In particular, distinguish: (i) the epistemic claim that available behavioral evidence always underdetermines the choice among rival translation manuals; (ii) the metaphysical claim that there is no correct manual even in principle, independent of any evidential situation; (iii) the semantic claim that reference itself is inscrutable, so that gavagai might denote rabbits, temporal rabbit-stages, or undetached rabbit parts with no further fact to settle it; and (iv) the practical claim that because of (i)-(iii), cross-linguistic communication and automated translation are necessarily arbitrary impositions rather than recoveries of meaning. For each reading, please say whether it actually follows from Quine's argument, whether it is defensible on its own terms, and whether it supports my colleague's conclusion about translation technology. I am especially interested in whether the move from behavioral equivalence of manuals to metaphysical indeterminacy is a valid inference or a common misreading, and whether there is a coherent moderate position that accepts underdetermination for the radical-translation case but denies that it undermines ordinary bilingual competence or engineered translation systems.
