Recently I was appointed to the drafting committee for the governance charter of a small decentralized research collective to which I belong, and we have reached an impasse over a clause we have named the Reflection Ladder. The clause stipulates that any amendment to the charter takes effect only after a thirty-day reflection period, during which the pre-amendment version continues to govern all further amendments. It also states that the duration of the reflection period itself is subject to amendment, but only under the rules of the version currently in force—which therefore includes the thirty-day provision. Last week a member proposed shortening the reflection period to seven days and applying the change immediately, arguing that because the charter permits amendments during the reflection window, the new seven-day rule should govern its own ratification. Others counter that this collapses the ladder: if the old thirty-day rule governs amendments proposed during the reflection period, then the proposal must be evaluated under the thirty-day framework, meaning the change cannot take effect for thirty days, by which point it might be revoked.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent legal philosophers—Alina and Ben—who take opposing views on whether the Reflection Ladder is genuinely paradoxical or merely structurally complex. Alina should defend a stratificationist resolution: she should argue that temporal indexing creates a well-ordered sequence of governing versions, so that at any given moment there is an unambiguous meta-rule, and that the apparent loop dissolves once we distinguish the level at which a rule operates from the level at which it is amended. Ben should defend an anti-stratificationist, holist position: he should contend that because the reflection period's own amendability is governed by the very version it seeks to alter, there is no stable prior layer, and that treating chronological sequence as an ordering principle smuggles in an arbitrary cut-off that the charter itself does not authorize. They should respond directly to one another’s actual arguments—Alina should address Ben’s concern about the arbitrary temporal cut-off, and Ben should address Alina’s claim about temporal indexing—rather than speaking past each other. The dialogue should remain formal in tone, should not invoke external authorities or specific texts, and should conclude without a synthetic compromise: each should state clearly what would need to be demonstrated—an explicit hierarchical index, a formal consistency proof, or an admission of irreducible indeterminacy—for them to revise their position.
