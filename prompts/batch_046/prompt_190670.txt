I'm presenting a paper next Thursday at an interdisciplinary symposium on political memory and collective identity, and I need a Q&A prep sheet tailored to the four discussants who will respond. My thesis is that the Confederate "Lost Cause," the Masada narrative in Zionist historiography, the Kosovo Field mythology in Serbian nationalism, and the British "Dunkirk spirit" are not adequately explained as false consciousness, nationalist propaganda, or simple trauma repetition; instead, they instantiate what I am calling "defeat ontology"—a mode of collective self-understanding in which a military or political defeat is preserved not as a wound to be healed but as an active, unfulfilled claim upon the present. I argue that there is a structural asymmetry between the first three cases, in which defeat is tied to territorial or sovereignty claims that remain politically live, and Dunkirk, which was rapidly translated into a narrative of deliverance and national survival; and I defend a normative proposal that societies should not suppress these narratives through monument removal or curricular excision, but should "translate" them—converting the unfulfilled claim from a territorial-political register into a cultural-ethical one—while maintaining that translation has concrete failure conditions.

The audience is precisely calibrated to attack different joints of this argument. Dr. Marcus Chen is a political philosopher working on collective identity and recognition; he will ask whether "defeat ontology" is coherent without a phenomenological subject capable of "holding" an unfulfilled claim, and he will press me on whether I am smuggling in Hegelian recognition theory without proper attribution or defense. Dr. Aisha Patel is a sociologist of nationalism who thinks I am ignoring the material interests—economic, electoral, and military—that actually drive myth persistence; she runs a resource-mobilization framework and will ask whether my concept explains anything her vocabulary cannot already accommodate. Dr. James Whitmore is a public historian who advises municipal governments on monument policy and is politically committed to the removal of Confederate symbols; he will ask whether my "translation" proposal is merely a polite mechanism for preserving harmful monuments, and he will demand concrete, operational criteria for when translation fails and suppression becomes justified.
