Within debates over collective responsibility for the First World War—particularly in light of the Fischer controversy over whether German leadership deliberately sought expansive war aims—much philosophical attention has gone to causal attribution: who intended, planned, and initiated the conflict. Less settled, and I think more consequential, is what it means to hold a nation itself responsible once causation is established, especially across generational turnover and regime change. For this prompt, bracket the empirical historiography and treat the following three interdependent conceptual problems as your agenda space, though you may substitute others if they genuinely sit upstream.

First, the moral-continuity problem: under what conditions does a state remain the same moral agent over time, such that blame or obligation incurred by the German Empire in 1914 can be coherently directed at citizens or institutions of the Federal Republic in later decades? Does regime change break continuity, or does institutional and legal persistence preserve it?

Second, the distributive-composition problem: if collective blame is appropriate, how is it distributed among citizens, leaders, and structural institutions—especially given coercion, misinformation, and the fact that many affected individuals were unborn at the time? Is collective blame an aggregate of individual blames, a corporate status assigned to an enduring entity, or a sui generis judgment?

Third, the practical-illocutionary problem: does assigning blame to a nation require that the nation currently possess the institutional capacity to respond—through reparative measures, memorial practices, or constitutional reform—or can blame exist as a purely retrospective judgment without any corresponding practical demand?
