I'm writting this up for my philosophy of knowledge seminar and I'm kind of stuck. I'm a phd student in epistomology, and I've been arguing with my roommate about whether Nolan's Memento actually holds up if you read it through Nozicks tracking theory and the closure principle. The film's whole plot turns on Leonard Shelby's belief-system—polaroids, tattoos, notes he writes to himself—because he can't form new long-term memories. Nozick says you know p only if in nearby worlds where p is false you don't believe p, but Leonard's notes are exactly the kind of mechanism that doesn't track: in worlds where Teddy or Natalie forge a polaroid, he still believes whatever the caption says. So does he have zero propositional knowledge? Or does tracking apply differently when the belief is mediated by an artifcat he designed?

Then there's closure. Leonard knows he has anterograde amnesia, and he knows that entails he can't form new episodic memories. By closure he should know he can't genuinely remember killing anyone after the incident, yet he keeps "knowing" he's on a revenge mission. Is this a closure failure, or is the entailment broken because his cognitive architecture is too unstable to preserve the epistemic link? I'm wondering if the film dramatizes something like a "modal fragility" problem for closure—where the conditions only hold for agents with stable belief-forming mechanisms, and Leonard's broken memory shows that closure needs a hidden stability premise that Nozick never made explicit.

But I don't want to just reduce the film to an illustration. Where does Memento resist this reading? For instance, Leonard seems to retain some emotional or bodily knowledge about his wife that survives the breakdown—does that track differently than his propositional beliefs? And what does the film actually add to the debate that the papers don't capture? Maybe something about knowledge being distributed across external artifacts (the notes, the tattoos) and how that distribution makes tracking and closure apply not to a single mind but to a hybrid system. I'm teaching a session next week and want to avoid misleading people, so I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously, points to specific scenes—the polaroid where Teddy writes "don't believe his lies," the Sammy Jankis tattoo, the final reveal in the abandoned building—and says honestly where the philosophical lens illuminates the scene and where it forces the movie into a box that doesn't fit.
