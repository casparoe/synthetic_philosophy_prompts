Having spent my career in experimental physics and only recently turned to philosophy, I am struck by an asymmetry in how AI safety institutions assess failure. Funding bodies and laboratories routinely mandate adversarial red-teaming that treats catastrophic risk as a discrete event—an escape, a deception, a single exploit—scored in binary pass-or-fail terms. By contrast, there is no comparable institutional requirement to monitor whether a system's objectives or behaviors gradually diverge from their original specifications over months of deployment, interaction with users, and feedback with other agents; slow value drift is not assigned a metric or an oversight protocol. What philosophical theory of failure, agency, or moral responsibility does this practice presuppose? Does it commit us to viewing failure as essentially an event rather than a trajectory, and does it locate blame only in discrete acts rather than in sustained directional change? Reconstruct the implicit framework, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say what would have to change in accountability structures and evaluation design if we replaced that framework with one that treated gradual drift as the primary risk.
