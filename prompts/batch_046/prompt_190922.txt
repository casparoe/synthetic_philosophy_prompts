hey, I'm drafting notes for a seminar on punishment and I want to check if my commitments are actually compatable. Here's where I stand:

(a) punishment's main job should be rehabilitative, but retributive desert still sets the ceiling on severity — so you can't rehabilitate someone by giving them more than they deserve, but you also can't justify suffering just because they deserve it.

(b) Norway's Halden prison is the best real-world proof that humane conditions reduce recidivism, and it's not just selection bias.

(c) American mass incarceration is mostly a failure of moral imagination — we treat prisoners as moral exiles rather than citizens with suspended rights.

(d) but for some extreme crimes I think permanent civic exclusion is justified, not just long rehab.

(e) I also think Halden wouldn't scale to high-violence contexts because the social-trust preconditions are missing, so I'm not a naive universalist.

(f) prison architecture should follow a normalized environment principle — inmates live as close to free life as security allows.

(g) lately I've been thinking punishment should be reconceived as temporary democratic disenfranchisement rather than suffering-infliction, which is why Halden's design makes sense — it suspends liberty but doesn't revoke citizenship status.

Are these pulling apart? The obvious tension is (d) against (a) and (f): if rehab and normalization are central, how can permanent exclusion or abnormal conditions be okay? But I think maybe (g) saves (d) — permanent disenfranchisement just extends the suspension without breaking the citizenship model. Meanwhile (b) and (e) look like they clash — I praise Halden but say it can't scale — though maybe that's just being context-sensitive and not a real contradiction. Which ones are actually in tension, which only look bad, and what's the least costly fix? I don't want to just drop (d) because it feels important, but I don't want to keep it if it undermines everything else. Rank them by severety and tell me what has to give.
