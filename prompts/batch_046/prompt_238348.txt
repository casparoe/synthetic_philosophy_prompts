I’m prepping a tutorial for first-year philosophy students who haven’t done biology since high school, and I want to spend about 45 minutes on the species problem — specifically why it’s not just 'what do we call this group?' but a genuine tension between reproductive, morphological, and historical criteria. I feel like if I open with Mayr’s biological species concept they’ll latch onto interbreeding and never understand why it collapses for fossils, asexual lineages, and ring species. So I need the architecture: the order in which the three criteria should be introduced so that each one’s failure motivates the next, the single carrying example that does all the heavy lifting — I want something that combines the ring-species intuition with an anagenesis time-slice problem, like a marine invertebrate lineage that changes gradually over two million years with no branching, but where intermediate layers contain fossils that occured in distinct morphological bands, so students have to decide whether they’re seeing one species stretching out or successive ones replacing each other. I also need the predictable misconceptions mapped to the sequence — for instance 'species are just human conventions' or 'genetic sequencing solves it' — and exactly where to defuse each one. Plus a check for understanding that proves they see the issue as conceptual rather than waiting for more data, and a clear statement of what I should refuse to explain at this level — maybe phylogentic nomenclature rules, or the metaphysical debate about whether species are individuals? — so I don’t drift. No readings needed, just the teaching plan.
