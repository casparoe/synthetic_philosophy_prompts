Give an example of a conceptual issue arising at the intersection of ontology identification and structurally inaccessible information. Construct a case in which two ontologies—call them Ontology A, which takes enduring three-dimensional objects as basic, and Ontology B, which takes four-dimensional spacetime worms composed of temporal parts as basic—are not merely empirically equivalent but inter-translatable by a deterministic mapping rule that leaves no observable residue. Then introduce a specific internal feature, the "binding signature," which exists in each ontology but is in principle inaccessible to any observer situated within that ontology: in Ontology A it fixes whether object-identity across time is primitive or derived, while in Ontology B it fixes whether temporal parts are genuinely distinct or merely modal phases of a single worm. The conceptual issue should turn on whether the inaccessibility of this signature makes the empirical equivalence a benign case of underdetermination, or whether it generates a deeper ambiguity about whether there is any fact of the matter to be identified—specifically, whether cross-ontology identification of entities is coherent or collapses into contradiction depending on which signature is present, even though no empirical test can reveal which is present. Present the example precisely: specify the mapping rule's role, define the binding signature's function in both frameworks, and explain why its inaccessibility is structural rather than contingent. Then explain why this is distinct from standard arguments about global empirical equivalence or structural realist claims about hidden natures.
