I have recently agreed to lead a fifty-minute discussion session for my younger sister’s first-year philosophy-of-science study group, after she argued that von Neumann “invented the computer, so he must have believed the brain is one.” The students have only encountered his posthumous comparison of brain and machine in passing, and they need the idea rebuilt from scratch. I want a teaching plan, not the explanation itself.

The concept is von Neumann’s specific claim that the brain is not a digital computer but a statistical, probabilistic device that achieves reliability through massive parallel redundancy—structurally unlike the deterministic, serial, error-vulnerable stored-program architecture he designed, even though he insisted the analogy remains illuminating.

Plan the arc: the exact order in which the pieces must be introduced and why each depends on the last (start with the baseline of von Neumann computer architecture so the comparison has a target; then neuronal redundancy; then the shift to statistical, probabilistic logic); the one concrete scenario that should carry the session—the contrast between a single vacuum-tube failure crashing ENIAC and the silent loss of thousands of neurons—and precisely what this comparison must possess and what it must lack if it is not to mislead them into thinking neurons are merely slow binary switches; the misconceptions they will bring or form—especially that von Neumann was a reductive physicalist, that he endorsed a software/hardware separation for mind, and that he ignored the analog nature of neural signalling—and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a single closing question that reveals whether they have grasped the dependency structure rather than just repeated the slogan that “the brain is not a computer”; and what I should explicitly refuse to develop at this level—his formal theory of self-replicating automata and the mathematics of his probabilistic logic—while saying so openly.

Please write this in British English.
