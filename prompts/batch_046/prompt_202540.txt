I am a doctoral candidate in modern European intellectual history drafting a six-month workshop proposal for a research foundation. The project addresses a conceptual tension that I believe is concealed within the standard uniqueness-versus-comparability debate about the Holocaust. The Historikerstreit is typically narrated as a dispute over German national identity and the ethics of historical comparison, while Bauman’s thesis that modern bureaucratic rationality functioned as an enabling condition is often read as an argument for normalization. Rather than treating these as opposing camps, I want to reframe the issue as a problem about the kind of explanation modernity provides. Specifically, the proposal should defend a precise, non-obvious question: can we consistently maintain that the Holocaust is causally comparable to other genocides—explainable through analogous bureaucratic, technological, and ideological mechanisms—while also holding that it remains normatively non-substitutable in its moral-phenomenological structure, without that distinction collapsing into either exceptionalism or dilution?

Existing answers tend to treat causal comparison and normative uniqueness as mutually exclusive: either modernity supplies explanations that apply elsewhere, thereby blocking singularity claims, or uniqueness blocks any comparative causal analysis altogether. I think this is a false dichotomy, but I need the proposal to argue for a middle position rather than merely surveying alternatives. The approach should be conceptual rather than empirical: constructing a taxonomy that separates causal mechanisms, institutional pathways, and moral-phenomenological structures, then testing whether modernity-based explanations operate at the first two levels without necessarily determining the third. A successful deliverable would be a rigorous framework—presented as a working paper with structured case illustrations—that specifies exactly where comparison is licensed, where it is blocked, and what conditions would cause the framework to fail, such as if institutional pathways necessarily carry moral-phenomenological content. The scope must be realistic: six months permits conceptual architecture and limited illustrative testing, but not a full comparative genocide study, so the proposal must state explicit boundaries and identify the precise point at which the project becomes unmanageable for a single workshop cycle.
