I've been working through a draft essay on the interaction between anti-luminosity arguments and the KK principle, and I want to pressure-test a particular framing before committing to it. The standard literature treats Williamson's margin-of-error cases—think of the slowly warming room where you still know it's warm but can't know that you know it—as direct counterexamples to both luminosity and, by extension, to the KK principle (that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p). I think that move is too quick. My draft distinguishes first-order epistemic competence from second-order reflective access, then uses a prediction-market analogy to motivate the split: a well-calibrated forecaster can hold accurate first-order probabilities without having accurate real-time access to her own calibration curve, especially near decision boundaries where self-report noise dominates the signal. The idea is that knowing p can be operational—manifest in correct behavior and appropriate betting—while knowing that one knows p requires an additional audit layer that can fail for tractability reasons rather than for genuinely epistemic ones.

The essay then asks whether we should replace global KK with a graded version: Kp → KKp holds whenever p lies within the agent's reflective bandwidth, and the classic luminosity failures are precisely those boundary cases where p exceeds that bandwidth. This preserves the intuition behind KK for ordinary, tractable knowledge while accommodating the marginal cases.

I'd like comments on coherence and interest, not on citation coverage. Specifically: (1) Does the prediction-market analogy genuinely illuminate the accessibility/competence distinction, or does it smuggle in a behavioralist theory of knowledge that the standard anti-luminosity literature already rejects? (2) Is the graded KK principle stable, or does Williamson's argument apply with equal force to the tractable subset once we formalize "reflective bandwidth"? (3) Am I conflating psychological limits on self-monitoring with genuinely epistemic failures of second-order knowledge? Any suggestions on tightening the distinction—especially if you've worked through similar framing in rationalist epistemology discussions—would be useful.
