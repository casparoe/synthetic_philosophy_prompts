I'm writing up my decision-theoretic profile for a seminar audit and want a consistency check before I present it. Here are my commitments:

(a) In Newcomb's problem I one-box. I take the predictor's forecast to make my choice strong evidence for the box's contents, and I think rational agents should maximize evidential expected utility.

(b) I reject causal decision theory entirely. I think its ban on conditioning on one's own actions throws away information that rational choice should exploit.

(c) In the counterfactual mugging I refuse to pay once the coin has landed heads. At that point there is no live evidential or causal pathway connecting my payment to any state I care about.

(d) In Parfit's hitchhiker I think you should pay the driver on arrival, but not for the standard planning-stage reason. Instead, paying is rational because it causally reinforces the kind of agent I am, and that feedback loop is genuinely active at the moment of payment.

(e) I think decision-theoretic norms must be diachronically uniform: the evaluation of an option at the planning stage and at the execution stage must be governed by the exact same principle, with no special ex-ante exemption.

(f) I recently adopted what I call the indexical-discount thesis: in Newcomb-style problems we should apply a Sleeping-Beauty-style halving to the evidential probabilities, because my epistemic position is symmetric between the predictor-correct and predictor-wrong worlds relative to my choice. So the evidential weight of one-boxing should always be multiplied by one-half before computing expected utility.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what has to give, and identify the single revision that would clean up the most conflicts. I am especially unsure whether (e) undermines the usual resolution of (a) versus (c), and whether (f) can be held alongside (a) without weakening (b).
