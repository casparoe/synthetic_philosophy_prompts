If Arthur Schopenhauer were alive today and confronted with a hypothetical neurotechnology that could permanently suppress all craving and suffering without altering consciousness, reasoning, or personal identity—leaving only what might be called tranquil absence—would he regard its universal adoption as the ethical fulfillment of his philosophy? Or would he maintain that such biochemical suppression of the will falls short of the genuine negation he envisioned, and that true liberation requires a different kind of renunciation altogether?
