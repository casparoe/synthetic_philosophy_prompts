In the interpretation of the White Horse Dialogue traditionally associated with Gongsun Long and the School of Names, scholarly debate has remained stalled for decades among three dominant readings: that the argument demonstrates a logical separation of predicates with different extensions (so that "white horse" and "horse" denote distinct sets), that it advances a metaphysical claim about the non-combinability of universal essences (horse-ness and whiteness remaining irreducibly distinct), and that it functions chiefly as a rhetorical or dialectical exercise without serious ontological commitment. Each camp can cite textual support, yet none has persuaded the others, and the disagreement shows no sign of resolution through further philology alone. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not substantive but structural: the interpreters are answering different questions—one about classical Chinese grammatical taxonomy, one about the ontology of properties, one about the norms of disputation—while treating them all as answers to the single question of what Gongsun Long meant. Is that right? Or is there a hidden empirical or formal disagreement—perhaps about how compound predicates operate in pre-Qin logical practice, or about whether the dialogue presupposes a specific theory of reference—that either side has overlooked? I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, supported by what each reading actually says about the dialogue's premises, together with an honest assessment of whether any conceptual distinction drawn from contemporary philosophy of language or metaphysics—say, between sortal and qualitative universals, or between intension and extension—could break the impasse, or whether the disagreement is ill-posed and should be dissolved rather than solved.
