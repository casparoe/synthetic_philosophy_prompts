As a retired materials scientist who spent three decades in empirical laboratory work and has only recently begun attending philosophy seminars at my university's continuing-education program, I find myself repeatedly struck by a problem I never faced with physical specimens: how can we claim to know entities with which we have never causally interacted? I am organizing a single 90-minute workshop for approximately ten adult learners—engineers, teachers, and retired professionals—who have no formal training in philosophy, and I want them to feel the full force of Benacerraf's dilemma before anyone names it. Rather than delivering a lecture on causal theories of knowledge, I want a structured, participatory simulation in which the group itself generates the conflict through rules and staged discoveries.

Please design a runnable classroom activity—complete with roles, rules, materials, a phased timeline, and a debrief—that makes the participants discover, through their own actions, that they possess reliable, predictive knowledge of a formal structure that is causally isolated from them. To prevent this from being a generic classroom exercise, inject the following constructed element: have the participants collaboratively invent a miniature symbolic system, which they might call "Z-notation," consisting of internal combination rules for abstract symbols. Unbeknownst to them initially, these rules exactly mirror the mechanical behavior of a simple physical apparatus—say, a set of interlocking gears or magnetic sliders—that they have been manipulating empirically at an adjacent station. The activity should be arranged so that the group first establishes an "Empirical Access Rule" requiring causal interaction for certified knowledge, applies it rigorously to classify their physical observations, and only then realizes that their invented Z-notation allows them to predict the apparatus's behavior with perfect accuracy despite their never having "touched" or observed its abstract symbols as physical objects.

The deliverable should specify group roles or pairings, the incentive or recognition structure (even if only in points or deliberative authority), and a precise timeline within the 90 minutes. The debrief must map each phase onto Benacerraf's dilemma—the ontological commitment to abstract objects, the causal access condition, and the undeniable success of mathematical prediction—without merely explaining the view after the fact. Include an honest prediction of what will misfire on the first run—perhaps participants conflating the invented notation with physical labeling rather than genuinely abstract structure—and advise how to correct it in the moment. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the dilemma the physical simulation cannot reproduce—likely the specific metaphysical status of numbers as non-spatiotemporal entities, or the full generality of the causal theory across scientific domains—and state what supplementary discussion the facilitator must supply. Write this in a formal, polished register suitable for a workshop proposal, and do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
