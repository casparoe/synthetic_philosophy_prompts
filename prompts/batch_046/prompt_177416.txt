In a graduate seminar on historiography, two students—Nadia and Sam—got stuck in a sustained disagreement over a specific monograph. The historian evaluates the gendered exclusion practices of a sixteenth-century European guild of apothecaries, applying modern standards of distributive justice and condemning the guild for systematically denying apprenticeship to women. The historian argues that an honest account must name this as injustice rather than merely describing it, and that framing it solely through the guild's humoral cosmology—where gender division was believed necessary to maintain bodily order in medicinal preparations—would obscure the power dynamics at work. Nadia defends this approach; Sam thinks it is presentism that prevents genuine understanding.

Nadia opened: Historical understanding does not require moral endorsement, but refusing to apply present moral categories produces false neutrality. The guild members may have believed in humoral cosmology, yet they also had access to counter-examples—women practicing medicine informally—so their exclusion was not purely epistemic but partly a power-preserving choice. Applying modern justice standards reveals mechanisms they themselves obscured with metaphysical language.

Sam replied: That analysis smuggles in a modern ontology. The guild members did not conceptualize "power" or "gender" as moderns do; they conceptualized cosmic bodily order. To say they "obscured power with metaphysics" is to translate their world into ours, which is precisely the hermeneutic violence presentism commits. The correct method is internal critique—ask whether they violated their own cosmological commitments—not whether they violate ours.

Nadia countered: Internal critique is impotent here because the cosmology itself encoded the exclusion. The system was internally coherent; there was no contradiction to expose. If we limit evaluation to internal standards, we can never criticize structurally embedded injustices that actors naturalized within their own frameworks. So present moral standards are not optional; they are necessary if historiography is to be ethically serious rather than merely antiquarian.

Sam responded: But then you have made historiography a branch of moral philosophy, not an inquiry into the past. You also assume moral knowledge is cumulative in a way epistemic knowledge is not—why privilege modern moral intuition over modern scientific intuition? We reject geocentrism on evidential grounds, not moral ones. Applying modern ethics lacks any equivalent of empirical falsification; there is no procedure to show that our norms are "truer" than theirs. Calling this "necessary" simply means you have decided your values are universal.

Nadia's final move: The comparison to science fails because moral harm is not like empirical error. When we correct geocentrism, we wrong no one alive; when we call structural exclusion unjust, we honor its victims. Historiography owes solidarity with the harmed, and that duty outweighs hermeneutic purity. Even if moral norms are not "falsified," they can be deepened—our grasp of suffering and autonomy has expanded. Applying that depth is not colonialism but accountability.

Sam's closing point: That sounds principled, but it creates an asymmetry you have not defended. You permit presentism for moral evaluation, yet you presumably reject applying modern biomedicine uncritically when assessing their medicine. So your position is not that presentism is necessary; it is that presentism is necessary only where we care about the outcome. That is not a historiographical method; it is a preference for moral judgment dressed in methodological language.
