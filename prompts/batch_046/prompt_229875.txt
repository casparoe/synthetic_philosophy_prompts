In my philosophy of art seminar, we have been debating whether moral defects in an artwork necessarily diminish its artistic value, particularly in collaborative works where authorship is distributed. I would like you to compare two conceptual frameworks on this question. The first is the autonomy thesis: artistic value is categorically independent of moral value, so that an immoral work can still be excellent art provided its formal, expressive, and structural achievements succeed on their own terms. The second is what I will call the integration thesis: in certain cases, moral failure is not merely present in a work but structurally incorporated—its ethical defects function as formal elements that generate a distinctive aesthetic effect, meaning the immorality is artistically productive rather than simply tolerated. My central question is whether the integration thesis genuinely differs from the autonomy thesis, or whether it collapses back into it by treating moral defect as just another raw material that formal mastery can redeem. To keep the analysis concrete, apply the comparison to the following hypothetical case: a large-scale, multi-authored visual narrative—something like a collectively produced epic film or installation—whose organizational logic and compositional hierarchy appear to mirror and even aestheticize the very patterns of domination and erasure that its explicit subject matter critiques. Under which framework is it more conceptually coherent to praise the work's structural sophistication and formal innovation while maintaining that its ethical vision remains deeply flawed? Please develop the comparison without citing specific named artists, works, or philosophers, and avoid giving a simple yes-or-no verdict; instead, show where the two frameworks diverge in their account of how form relates to moral content.
