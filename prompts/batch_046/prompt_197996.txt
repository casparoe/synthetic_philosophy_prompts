You serve as the senior philosophical advisor to the university Committee on Institutional Memory and Public Art. I chair the committee and must circulate a formal recommendation before Thursday’s vote on a donor proposal for a permanent atrium installation titled “The Architecture of Remembrance.” The design arrays four memorial forms in parallel: Stolperstein-style brass plaques for local Holocaust victims; a replica of the Glendale “comfort women” statue; an abstract Turkish form evoking state policies around the Armenian genocide; and the empty granite plinth left after our 2020 removal of a Confederate soldier monument. The donor argues that juxtaposition reveals shared structures of denial and working-through. Faculty historians object that this flattens incommensurable moral economies—Germany’s state-anchored Vergangenheitsbewältigung, Japan’s contested bilateral silence, Turkey’s legal prohibition of “insulting Turkishness,” and the American South’s privatized Lost Cause mythology—into a single aesthetic grammar, committing what they call “mnemonic colonialism.” Student activists respond that refusing comparison merely protects American exceptionalism.

Please think carefully, step by step. First, briefly brainstorm the conceptual stakes: what exactly is risked when an institution curates comparative remembrance across these four regimes, and where is the boundary between illuminating analogy and impermissible equivalence? Then draft the polished institutional memo I will circulate. It must recommend a concrete course of action—accept with specified modifications, reject, or approve only a restricted version—together with a clear timeline for implementation or donor response. Treat the historians’ “mnemonic colonialism” objection as the strongest argument against acceptance and engage it seriously rather than dismissing it. Write in a formal committee voice, avoid all bibliographic apparatus and citation requests, and conclude with an unambiguous bottom line and its conditions.
