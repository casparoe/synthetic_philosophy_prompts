Analyze the 2022 UK four-day workweek pilot—in which dozens of firms cut hours without cutting pay and most later retained the arrangement—through the lens of Keynes’s vision of the good life, especially as developed in his 1930 essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren. Do not summarize productivity data; instead, map the employers, workers, and the absent state onto Keynes’s distinction between absolute needs (which economic growth eventually satisfies) and relative or insatiable needs (which it never does), and onto his claim that once the economic problem is solved the permanent challenge is how to inhabit leisure well. Incorporate his often-overlooked institutional corollary: that meaningful leisure requires publicly supported culture, exemplified by his wartime chairmanship of the Council for the Encouragement of Music and the Arts, the body that evolved into the Arts Council. What does the framework explain about why some workers reported anxiety, or filled freed hours with second jobs, rather than experiencing liberation? Where does it miss the dynamics—status competition through overwork, living costs that did not fall with hours, or the lack of the cultural infrastructure Keynes assumed? And does the pilot ultimately support his prediction that people fear leisure they have not been taught to inhabit, or does it show that for many participants the economic problem was never actually solved?
