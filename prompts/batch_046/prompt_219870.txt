I've been working through the philosophical digressions in Leo Tolstoy's War and Peace—particularly the sections composed in the late 1860s, where he interrupts the narrative to argue that historical events follow the same logic as calculus. He claims that each individual's free will is an infinitesimal so minute that it cancels out in isolation, and that only the integration of millions of such infinitesimals produces the inevitable curve of history; yet he simultaneously insists that when we examine any particular decision—such as Napoleon's advance toward Moscow—the causal chain dissolves into an irreducible tangle of contingent motives, accidents, and weather, leaving no directing intelligence to be found. I'm trying to understand how these two claims fit together, and whether they have any bearing on modern counterfactual reasoning.

More precisely, I want to know whether Tolstoy's calculus analogy implicitly addresses, or perhaps dissolves, the reference class problem that arises when philosophers try to evaluate counterfactual histories. If inevitability is just the statistical convergence of infinitely many micro-choices, then identifying the correct comparison class—say, deciding whether to weigh Napoleon's Moscow campaign against a world in which he remained in Paris, or against a distribution of all command decisions weighted by prior probabilities—requires drawing a boundary between the infinitesimal actions that belong inside the integral and the perturbations that count as external noise. Could you help me parse Tolstoy's argument about the integration of free acts, and explain whether his framework allows for a principled distinction between historically relevant counterfactual possibilities and mere modal noise? I am especially interested in whether his view entails that all branching counterfactuals are illusory, or whether it can be reconciled with a modal interpretation in which inevitability represents only high-probability convergence within a defined parameter space, rather than strict determinism that negates individual agency altogether.
