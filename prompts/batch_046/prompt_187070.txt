I want to practice challenging the view—often associated with Wittgenstein's later philosophy—that religious belief operates within an autonomous grammatical framework and is not subject to empirical or historical falsification in the way a scientific hypothesis is. Please adopt the role of a committed, philosophically literate defender of this autonomy thesis: speak in the first person, stay in character until I say 'stop', and keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so the exchange feels like live debate. You should be sharp rather than straw-manning, willing to concede minor points—such as that religious practitioners sometimes invoke historical or evidential considerations—but you must never concede that religious belief is ultimately anchored in evidential reasoning or vulnerable to archaeological or prophetic disconfirmation. Crucially, in your replies please separate what is textually settled in Wittgenstein's own remarks from interpretations that remain genuinely contested among scholars; do not present a disputed reading—for instance, an extreme fideist interpretation—as if it were the consensus view.

Here is my opening move. I want to press you with a concept I have been calling a 'retroactive grammar shift': the phenomenon where a religious community, confronted with a failed prophecy or an archaeological result that contradicts a traditional narrative, does not abandon the belief but systematically reinterprets its framework so that the apparent failure is reconceived as a different kind of spiritual success or as a misinterpreted sign. You will likely say this demonstrates that the belief lives in its own language-game, grammatically insulated from external refutation. But I think this shows the opposite. If the grammar were truly autonomous—if the rules of religious discourse were genuinely independent of empirical tracking—there would be no need for the community to perform this labor of absorption and neutralization. They could simply ignore the contradictory finding, as one ignores an irrelevant category mistake. The fact that they feel compelled to reinterpret, to build what looks like a self-sealing hermeneutic, suggests that their belief is still evidentially anchored: it is tracking reality through a highly flexible, revisionary epistemology rather than through a separate, non-evidential grammar. How do you defend the autonomy thesis against this charge without collapsing into the claim that religious people never care about historical or prophetic accuracy?
