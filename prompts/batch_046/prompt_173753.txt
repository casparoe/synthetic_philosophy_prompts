As a philosopher of action and causation, I have become interested in a particular asymmetry that arises in discussions of omission-based harm, and I should like to know whether the literature assigns it a specific name. The pattern is this. When we evaluate whether a failure to act counts as a cause of some outcome, we seem to apply a dual gate: first, we check whether the situation contained what one might call an 'activation script' — a socially or institutionally defined sequence in which a specific intervention was the expected next move; second, we check whether the agent stood in a relation of duty or special responsibility to the potential victim. Only when both gates are satisfied do we treat the omission as causally efficacious rather than as a mere background absence. By contrast, when we evaluate an active intervention that produces the same harm, we do not require an activation script to grant causal status; instead, we treat the action as a cause by default and then ask whether an excuse or justification defeats the attribution. This is not merely the familiar moral distinction between acts and omissions, because the asymmetry operates specifically at the level of causal ascription — the omission is treated as metaphysically inert unless both conditions are met. Does this dual-gate mechanism have an established label — perhaps within the literature on normative expectations, deviant causation, or omission bias — and how precisely does it differ from each of those? If no established concept captures it exactly, please coin a precise term, specify its boundary conditions (for instance, whether it extends to collective omissions or only to individual ones), and argue, in a committed fashion, whether this pattern reflects a genuine feature of causal reasoning or merely an artefact of moral evaluation being projected onto metaphysics. Please write in British English and keep your answer within 250 words.
