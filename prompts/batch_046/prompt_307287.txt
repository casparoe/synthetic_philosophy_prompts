I am drafting a philosophical interlude for my novel and require a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors who genuinely engage each other’s arguments rather than talk past them. One should be an eliminative materialist who fully accepts predictive-processing accounts of cognition—prediction-error minimization, hierarchical Bayesian inference, and all—yet insists that none of these mechanisms map onto discrete belief-states or desire-states, so that talk of “believing that p” or “wanting that q” should be excised from our serious ontology. The other should be a narrative realist who defends the structural indispensability of intentional explanations not through folk-psychological intuition but through the claim that first-person literary consciousness—self-deception, regret, anticipatory dread—possesses a normative architecture that collapses if we eliminate propositional attitudes entirely, even if those attitudes lack isolable neural correlates. Set their exchange in a deserted archive of obsolete psychiatric manuals: atmospheric, but not melodramatic. The eliminativist should press the realist on whether her indispensability claim is merely pragmatic (a useful fiction for storytelling) or genuinely ontological, and she should challenge whether predictive processing can account for the normative structure of self-deception without smuggling in propositional attitudes under another name. Write the dialogue in polished American English, maintaining a formal, literary tone appropriate to a novelist’s request, with real back-and-forth and no strawman on either side. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the exchange should stand on its own conceptual merits.
