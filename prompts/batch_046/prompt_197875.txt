I am writing up reflections for a philosophy seminar on moral responsibility, and I find myself with a cluster of intuitions about the principle of alternative possibilities that seem to pull in different directions once I move past the standard classroom examples. I want to know whether there is a single coherent principle behind them, or whether some have to be sacrificed. Here are my verdicts. First, the classic Frankfurt-style case: Jones intends to vote for a particular candidate, an observer (Black) has a device that could force Jones's hand but never activates it, and Jones votes freely; I say Jones is fully responsible even though he lacked any viable alternative course of action. Second, a flicker-of-freedom variation: Jones could have shown a barely perceptible hesitation—a micro-pause in his reasoning—that Black's device was calibrated to detect and override; I still lean toward holding him responsible, but I feel the presence of that detectable flicker introduces a genuine, if futile, alternative that complicates the judgment. Third, an opaque AI-preemption scenario: a surgeon performs a delicate operation with an embedded predictive system she does not know exists; the system silently blocks any motion that would cause harm, yet she experiences her movements as entirely self-directed and deliberate; if she accidentally harms a patient through a pathway the system failed to anticipate, I think she remains responsible, though it is unclear to me whether she could have done otherwise in any sense that should matter morally. Fourth, a collective decision case: a board of five votes unanimously for a damaging policy, and the chair possesses an unexercised veto power that none of the members knew she was considering; I judge the individual members less fully responsible than Jones in case one, not because they could have swayed the chair, but because the institutional structure seems to dissolve the kind of individual authorship that Frankfurt-style cases preserve. Fifth, a diachronic fragmentation case: on Monday an agent commits to a harmful plan with full knowledge and freedom; by Tuesday she undergoes a deep values shift that makes the plan abhorrent to her, but contractual and technological lock-ins prevent her from altering Wednesday's execution; on Wednesday she carries out the plan with sincere regret and no present ability to stop; I think she is not responsible for Wednesday's act, despite having had full alternatives on Monday. Is there an account of moral responsibility—perhaps indexed to the mental state of the agent at the moment of action, or to the accessibility of alternatives to the agent's practical reasoning, or to whether preemption is personal versus structural—that accommodates all five judgments? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the best available principle predict for an unlisted case: a sleepwalker who performs a complex harmful action during an episode, but upon waking describes having experienced a conscious, deliberate decision-making process with no awareness of any external constraint? Please separate in your response what is broadly settled about Frankfurt-style counterexamples and the fate of the principle of alternative possibilities from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of action, and avoid presenting any particular resolution—compatibilist, libertarian, or semi-compatibilist—as if it were the undisputed consensus.
