I sit on the governing board of a neighborhood commons whose charter requires that every binding decision express the will of the members. Last quarter we held two separate votes. In the first, members submitted ranked preference cards for three market policies: (A) no commercial vendors, (B) vendor permits with a fee, or (C) open vendor access without fees. The tally was 30 percent for A, 35 percent for B, and 35 percent for C, so B was adopted by plurality rule. Afterward, a group of long-tenured plot holders demanded a direct pairwise runoff between A and B; in that vote, A defeated B by 60 percent to 40 percent. They now argue that the pluralist result misrepresents the members' true collective preference and want the board to overturn B in favor of A. In the second vote, members decided yes or no on a new irrigation system; it passed with 72 percent approval, but turnout was heavily skewed toward older members who have held plots for more than a decade. A different faction now claims that this irrigation vote expresses an authentic will distinct from the vendor vote, because the people who actually sustain the commons showed up, while the vendor vote was inflated by newer members who treat the space like a weekend market. Meanwhile, a third group warns that if we start distinguishing real from fake member will, the charter collapses into whatever the board prefers, since any result can be disqualified by pointing to turnout or pairwise contradictions. I have to advise the board on whether to keep B, revert to A, or declare the charter unworkable. Is there a coherent philosophical account of the will of the members that lets me defend one of these moves without merely picking my favorite outcome? If I say the pairwise result reveals the true will, what do I commit to about democratic procedure? If I say the irrigation vote's demographic makeup makes it more authoritative, what does that imply about whose membership counts? And if I say both votes are equally valid expressions of will that happen to conflict, does that mean the charter is asking for something that does not exist?
