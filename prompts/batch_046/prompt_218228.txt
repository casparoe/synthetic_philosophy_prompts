I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, have been meeting with a small local group to read philosophy of mind and value theory; we have lately been working through Nozick’s experience machine and the standard taxonomy of well-being—hedonism, desire-fulfilment, and objective-list theories—yet I found our discussion stuck in the familiar groove of reality versus illusion. For our closing session I should like to commission a poem that pushes the problem somewhat further into the weeds: please write a Petrarchan sonnet of exactly fourteen lines in strict iambic pentameter, with a clear volta between the octave and the sestet, taking as its speaker either the simulated subject who has just discovered that the feeling of having climbed a real mountain was produced by electrodes rather than by gravity and exertion, or the machine itself reflecting on its own outputs. In the octave, build a concrete, seductive picture of a life rich in struggle and triumph—rock faces, tired muscles, the satisfaction of reaching a summit—so that the reader feels the weight of achievement; at the volta, reveal that every causal chain terminates inside the tank, that no stone was actually displaced and no weather endured; then, in the sestet, leave suspended precisely the tension our group reached, asking whether well-being requires that one’s achievements possess the right causal pedigree—that one’s efforts actually moved matter in the world—or whether the absence of that external anchor is only a metaphysical luxury we have grown accustomed to demanding. The rhyme scheme and the turn should do the philosophical work, not merely decorate it; keep the imagery physical—mountains, wires, gravity—avoid resolving into either a sermon about authenticity or a shrug about circular life, and please render the whole in polished British English.
