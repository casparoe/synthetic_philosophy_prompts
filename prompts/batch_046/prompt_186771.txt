Write a dialogue between two philosophers of logic—call them the Systemist and the Practitioner—who disagree about whether Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" reveals a genuine problem for the justification of deduction. The Systemist should defend the view that the tortoise's infinite regress is dissolved once we distinguish object-level inference rules, such as modus ponens, from meta-level structural rules in a formal sequent calculus: within the system, deduction needs no further propositional justification because the rules are primitive and semantically empty, and modern automated proof verification shows that human acceptance of hypothetical premises is unnecessary for derivational validity. The Practitioner should respond not by denying formalization, but by arguing that this merely relocates the tortoise's challenge: the selection of which rules are primitive—why modus ponens rather than an alternative conditional?—and the trust placed in automated verifiers both depend on prior practical commitments that cannot themselves be justified inside the system. Each should address the other's actual point—the Systemist must explain why practical trust in verification software is not just another ungrounded hypothetical, and the Practitioner must explain why that practical dependence is vicious rather than benign—without either reducing the other to a strawman. The dialogue should remain focused on deduction specifically, not drift into general skepticism or epistemology, and should conclude without either side conceding, but with the precise locus of disagreement clearly articulated: whether the need for human assent to logical rules is eliminated or only displaced by formalization and automation.
