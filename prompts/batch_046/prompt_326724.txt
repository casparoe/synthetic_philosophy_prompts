Here's an argument my study partner cooked up and it feels like it should collapse but I'm not seeing the hole. (1) The principle of indifference says that when we have no reason to distinguish possibilities, we spread credence evenly. (2) In Bertrand's chord problem, whether a random chord is longer than the triangle side comes out to 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on whether you parameterize by endpoint pair, radius, or midpoint. (3) Each parameterization respects a different symetrie of the circle—translation, rotation, scaling—so there's no neutral way to pick one. (4) So the principle literally gives contradictory answers for the same physical event. (5) Now apply the same logic to discrete cases: a fair coin is just a continuous mass distribution collapsed to two points, and you can embed those two points in a continuous line in infinitely many ways with different uniform measures. (6) Therefore the principle is broken everywhere, not just in geometry; we never have justification for assigning 50/50 to a coin, or for any symmetric prior. (7) But that can't be right, because then every statistical inference from ignorance collapses. (8) So does the principle survive in discrete cases but fail in continuous ones? Or is there a hidden assumption in (5) about embedding? I want to keep (1) if I can, but I don't want to bite the bullet that uniform priors are never justified. Where does the overreach actually happen?
