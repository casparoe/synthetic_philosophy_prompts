Read Anthony Burgess's novel A Clockwork Orange (1962), with reference to Stanley Kubrick's 1971 film adaptation where it clarifies the philosophical issue, through the lens of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem in moral philosophy. After the Ludovico Technique, Alex behaves non-violently not because he recognizes violence as wrong, but because he has been conditioned to experience physical revulsion at it. Philosophers ask whether an action can have moral worth when it is motivated by prudential self-preservation, social reward, or mechanically induced aversion rather than by moral recognition itself. The novel and film dramatize exactly this tension, yet they also push further: they seem to ask whether externally imposed motivation merely supplies the wrong kind of reason, or whether it eliminates the agent's capacity to have moral reasons at all.

In your analysis, address the following points. First, examine the prison chaplain's objection that stripping a person of choice destroys moral personhood. Does this map onto the wrong-kind-of-reasons framework — Alex still behaves well, but for the wrong reasons — or does it identify a more fundamental failure, such as the loss of reasons-responsiveness or the dissolution of moral agency? Second, consider the role of Nadsat, the invented adolescent slang through which Alex conceptualizes violence, loyalty, and desire. After conditioning, the vocabulary remains but its motivational grip is severed. Does this suggest that possessing the right kind of moral reasons depends on maintaining an intact conceptual scheme, and that behavioral conditioning can destroy not only motivation but the very framework within which moral reasons are intelligible? Third, compare the novel's conclusion — in which Alex's violent impulses eventually return spontaneously, apparently restoring his freedom to choose evil — with the film's more ambiguous ending. Which version better supports the philosophical claim that moral worth requires autonomous, reason-responsive choice rather than mere behavioral compliance?

Finally, identify where the work resists reduction to a philosophical illustration. Burgess's satire of state control and Kubrick's stylized depiction of violence both complicate any straightforward moral-psychological reading. What does the narrative add — perhaps about the relationship between conceptual vocabulary, conditioning, and moral identity — that analytical arguments about reasons and motivation alone tend to overlook?
