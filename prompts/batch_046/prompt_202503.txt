In debates about indirect normativity—where an action’s normative standing depends on its relation to an idealized deliberative procedure rather than on direct evaluation—there is a stall I want diagnosed rather than adjudicated. Proponents hold that if an agent with full information, coherence, and absence of distorting passions would endorse a norm, that endorsement partly constitutes the norm’s authority. Critics reply that the idealization conditions already embed the direct norms we care about (fairness, consistency, harm-avoidance), so the “indirect” route is only a spotlight on prior commitments, not an independent source of authority. For years the exchange has repeated the same move: proponents refine the formal conditions of idealization; critics show that each refinement imports a substantive direct constraint. Both sides accept the formal psychology of the filter; neither concedes that this settles whether authority is mediated or merely revealed. Is this impasse verbal—do “indirect” and “direct” collapse once the filter is fully specified? Is there a hidden empirical question about whether agents can genuinely treat procedural pedigree as authoritative without also recognizing the substantive reasons embedded in the procedure? Or is it a bedrock disagreement about whether normative authority must be transparent at the moment of action, making any mediated source inherently alienated? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement as it actually stands, indicate whether a conceptual distinction, an empirical finding about normative psychology, or a formal result about the independence of filter conditions could move it, and say whether the framing itself should be abandoned for being ill-posed.
