I've been trying on my own to understand the old argument about whether a socialist economy could work out prices without markets, though I have no formal training in economics or philosophy; I've only read a few accessible summaries. Last week, two people in my study group—I'll call them Mara and Leo—got stuck in a long exchange about it. Both seemed to know far more than I do, yet they kept circling back to the same tension without resolving it. I want to understand whose specific points hold up and whether they're actually disagreeing about the same thing.

Mara began by saying that Oskar Lange's proposal largely resolves the classic objection: a central planning board sets initial prices, observes resulting shortages and surpluses across sectors, and adjusts those prices iteratively—essentially replicating the market's signaling function without private ownership or capital. She argued that with modern computing power, solving the underlying system of equations is now feasible, so the old claim that socialism necessarily fails at economic calculation is obsolete.

Leo responded that this mischaracterizes the problem. He said Mises and Hayek were not mainly worried about computational difficulty—solving equations on paper—but about the dispersion of local, qualitative, and constantly changing knowledge. A factory manager knows particular conditions about supply, maintenance needs, and labor morale that cannot be fully captured in quantitative reports sent upward. Without private property and a profit-and-loss mechanism, that manager has little incentive to reveal such knowledge honestly; instead, there is an incentive to misreport capacity or inflate resource needs in order to secure easier production quotas. Thus the board lacks the very data it needs to make its adjustments meaningful.

Mara conceded that incentives matter, but she insisted that this is an institutional design problem separable from the calculation problem itself. If rewards are tied to accurate disclosure—perhaps through democratic oversight or peer evaluation—the board can still gather sufficient information. She then pushed further: markets do not access some neutral, pre-existing "local knowledge" either. They only reveal preferences that have been shaped by the existing distribution of goods, advertising, and price structures. A planning board could present different bundles of goods and discover different preference patterns, which means market exchange holds no special epistemic privilege. The knowledge revealed in either system is partly constructed by the system's own framework.

Leo countered that if the planner both designs the available options and interprets the responses, there is no independent feedback loop to check whether resources are being directed toward what people genuinely value. In markets, the profit-loss signal is external to any single actor's intentions; it functions as a distributed error-correction mechanism. Without that separation between decision-maker and evaluator, one is not performing economic calculation at all but substituting collective political judgment for a process of dispersed discovery. He added that even with perfect data and infinite computing power, a board is always solving for a static equilibrium, whereas real economies are ongoing discovery procedures—new technologies emerge, tastes shift unexpectedly, and local scarcities appear in ways no central model can anticipate in real time. Only dispersed actors adapting continuously can respond without waiting for central revision.

Mara's final move was to ask whether Leo was implicitly treating market-formed preferences as uniquely legitimate. If planning is genuinely democratic, she said, then the values being pursued are collectively chosen rather than privately accumulated, and the "error-correction" Leo praises is really just the correction of private misallocations, not a neutral measure of social well-being.

At that point they turned to me, still at an impasse. So I want to ask: point by point, who is right about what? Has the disagreement remained about calculation, or has it shifted into a deeper dispute about values, incentives, and whether knowledge can ever be separated from the institutional framework that produces it? And is there something important that both of them are missing—perhaps about whether qualitative local knowledge can be translated into quantitative signals without losing the very content that makes it useful, or about whether democratic planning introduces a different kind of rationality check that neither side has fully articulated?
