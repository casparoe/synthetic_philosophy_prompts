Read Stanley Kubrick’s Dr. Strangelove through the logic of commitment races in strategic interaction, particularly Thomas Schelling’s analysis of credible commitment and the strategic value of irrevocably removing one’s own options. The film’s Doomsday Machine is designed precisely as such a device: once activated, retaliation becomes automatic and unstoppable, which should render the threat credible and thereby deter a first strike. Yet the narrative turns on its catastrophic failure—General Ripper launches an attack that cannot be recalled, and the Soviet ambassador reveals that the machine was kept secret, nullifying its deterrent value altogether.

I am preparing a dissertation chapter on the philosophy of strategic rationality and want a reading that takes both the film and the logic seriously, without reducing the film to a mere illustration of game-theoretic formalism. Where does Kubrick grasp the central paradox of commitment—that making oneself unable to retreat can stabilize mutual deterrence but also generate existential risk precisely when control is fragmented? Where, conversely, does he conflate credible strategic commitment with simple mechanical automation?

In particular, I want you to examine what I take to be an underappreciated internal commitment race: not between the United States and the Soviet Union, but within the American command structure and even within individual bodies. Ripper issues an irrevocable order; the bomber crew interprets the recall code as Soviet deception and proceeds; Strangelove’s own arm acts as an autonomous agent beyond his will. Does the film suggest that attempts to externalize commitment into protocols, machines, and bureaucratic chains actually multiply the sites of uncontrollable action, rather than concentrating authoritative control?

Also consider Schelling’s distinction between commitments that are visible to the adversary and those that remain hidden. The Soviet decision to conceal the Doomsday Machine destroys its strategic function. Does the film present this secrecy as a rational strategic error, or as an inevitable structural feature of competitive armament? And beyond formal models, what does the film contribute to philosophical debates about commitment—perhaps regarding embodied irrationality, the diffusion of agency across institutions, or the impossibility of fully automating human judgment—that the literature on strategic rationality tends to neglect?
