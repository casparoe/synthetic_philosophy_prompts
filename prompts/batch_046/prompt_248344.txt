In education and social-policy debates, advocates often cite SNP-based heritability estimates for cognitive or academic outcomes—frequently reported in the 0.40 to 0.60 range—to argue that large-scale pedagogical or redistributive interventions are likely to hit a ceiling, since genetic variance accounts for a substantial share of observed differences within the studied populations. At the same time, the very agencies and school systems that invoke these figures continue to fund extensive curriculum reforms, teacher-training initiatives, and environmental-enrichment programs, implicitly treating the outcomes as highly malleable to precisely those kinds of environmental modifications. What philosophical commitments about causation, the meaning of variance-partitioning statistics, and the normative weight of population-level patterns does this institutional pairing presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of heritability that makes both moves seem coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accommodates it, and say whether the pairing actually depends on an equivocation between heritability-in-a-fixed-environment and causal malleability-across-environments. Then take a definite position: is the implicit theory defensible, or does it collapse once we adopt a rigorous interventionist or reaction-norm framework? Give your reasons, and specify exactly what policy logic—if any—would survive replacing the implicit theory with that framework.
