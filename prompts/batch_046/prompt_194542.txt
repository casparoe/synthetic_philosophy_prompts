I've been arguing with my roommate who does synthetic bio about whether "function" in a gene-edited organism even means the same thing as in wild-type ones. She keeps saying figuring out what a gene does is like debugging software -- you knock it out and see what breaks, so the function is just its current causal role in the system, no evolutionary history required. I say that's wrong, and that it's more like asking why a factory originally installed a specific machine: if you only look at what it's currently wired into, you miss that it was put there for one job and now does another, and that distinction between proper function and accidental effect is exactly what the history captures.

Can you audit both analogies properly? Give me the correspondence table for each -- what maps onto the code/debugger, the factory/installer, the current running system, the evolutionary past -- then tell me where the mapping breaks. I'm especially stuck on cases where there's basically no clear evolutionary history, like de novo genes or synthetic circuits we built from scratch: does the factory analogy just collapse there, or does it smuggle in an assumption that every part had an original design intent? And does the debugging analogy quietly import the idea that there's no real difference between a proper function and a side-effect?

This is for a talk I'm giving to a mixed room of biologists and philosophers, so I also need to know whether either analogy actually supports the view it's being used to defend once the disanalogies are on the table. If the conclusion was carrying the analogy the whole time, say so. And if both analogies are broken, is there a better one -- maybe from language, where a word's current usage and its etymology pull apart in exactly the ways we're seeing with genes? I don't need a bibliography, just want the conceptual machinery laid out clearly so I don't embarass myself in front of the biologists.
