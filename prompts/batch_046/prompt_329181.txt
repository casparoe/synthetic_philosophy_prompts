I write novels about people who discover, too late, that they have been acting as cogs in a machine that no one designed. That interest makes me attentive to a real institutional pattern: in large bureaucracies—governmental, corporate, or military—harmful outcomes are routinely produced through distributed decision chains in which each participant performs only a narrow, technically correct task. The legal response is rarely to redesign the architecture; instead, liability attaches only when an investigator can isolate a single actor who violated protocol with evident malice or reckless disregard. At the same time, internal ethics training emphasizes role morality—the suspension of personal judgment in favor of organizational rules. What philosophical theory of moral agency and responsibility does this pairing presuppose? Does it assume that responsibility requires a unitary intentional subject, in the way Hannah Arendt argued that Eichmann's defense failed precisely because he refused to think and judge as such a subject? Or does it tacitly endorse her diagnosis of the banality of evil, treating unthinking role-performance as an excusable norm? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and describe what an institution genuinely built on Arendt's conception of thinking and judgment would have to change—not merely in its ethics codes, but in its decision architecture. In answering, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among scholars who work on these issues, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
