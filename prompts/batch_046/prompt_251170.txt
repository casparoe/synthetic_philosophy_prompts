I'm in a philosophy seminar on value theory and the ethics of future generations, and we've been working through Samuel Scheffler's argument that human extinction is bad in a way that goes far beyond the deaths it involves—specifically, because it destroys what he calls our collective "afterlife," the expectation that our present projects, values, and efforts will be carried forward by people we will never know. Individual death, on this view, is made bearable precisely by that temporal extension; extinction removes the frame that makes present meaning possible, not merely by ending lives but by retroactively hollowing out the significance of what came before. I want to close our unit with an original poem rather than a summary, and I want the philosophical structure to do the formal work rather than relying on apocalyptic imagery.

Please write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains and only two rhymes—taking as its subject the distinction between individual mortality (which we accept) and collective extinction (which collapses the distance that makes acceptance possible). Let the first refrain be a phrase about work or care outliving its maker, and let the second refrain be a phrase about the silence that remains when there is no one left to receive it. Through the rotating tercets, allow the two refrains to begin interfering with each other—overlapping in sense or syntax in the middle stanzas—so that the form enacts the idea that extinction doesn't just add a final absence but dissolves the boundary between personal death and the death of the project itself.

In the closing quatrain, both refrains must appear together, but I want the line breaks and rhyme to suggest that they have ceased to oppose each other: they should read as a single statement, or as one having absorbed the other. Use concrete, non-catastrophic imagery—perhaps an unfinished manuscript, a language spoken by no child, a garden planted for descendants who will not come—rather than ruined cities or empty planets. Do not resolve the tension between two readings: that the loss is primarily the absence of future people, or that it is the retroactive diminishment of past effort. End on an image that could be interpreted either way, leaving the philosophical problem structurally open exactly where our seminar discussion ended. The poem should be accurate enough to Scheffler's conjecture that a reader familiar with the argument would recognize it, but it should not quote him directly; render the idea through the form itself.
