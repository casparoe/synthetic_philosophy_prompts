At a logic seminar, two graduate students, Priya and Jonas, argued about a precisely specified variant of the two-envelope paradox. The setup is deterministic except for one unknown: the amounts are always powers of two times $10, generated by starting at $10 and repeatedly doubling with a fixed but unknown continuation probability q ∈ (0,1) until a stop decision occurs; the stopped value goes in one envelope and its double in the other. You open an envelope and find $160, and you know the rules but not q.

Priya said the paradox survives here in modified form. Because q is unknown, your prior over q induces a predictive distribution over the other envelope. Integrating over q with a uniform hyperprior, she claimed, still yields an expected value for the other envelope strictly greater than $160 for every observed value—so you should always switch, yet the reasoning applies symmetrically after switching. She argued this proves the problem is not merely an improper uniform prior over amounts, but a deeper instability in conditioning on observed values when the parameter space is open-ended.

Jonas replied that this misapplies Bayes' rule. Once q is fixed but unknown, observing $160 updates your belief about q, not about the other envelope independently. Given any specific q, the other envelope is deterministically either $80 or $320; the correct inference conditions on both q and consistency with the generating rule. Because large values are more plausible under larger q, conditioning on $160 shifts your belief about q, and integrating out q afterward does not restore a 50/50 split between $80 and $320. He said Priya treats q as a random variable across worlds rather than as an unknown constant, smuggling the same improper reasoning through the hyperprior.

Priya answered that from the agent's epistemic position q genuinely is uncertain, and decision theory requires averaging over epistemic possibilities. She noted that with a Beta hyperprior putting substantial weight near q = 1, one can still construct cases where the posterior expectation favors switching for every observed value, so Jonas's "threshold" result depends on arbitrarily constraining the hyperprior.

Jonas countered that any hyperprior producing universal switching assigns implausible weight to unbounded continuation, and that with any reasonable hyperprior—say Beta(1,1) with appropriate decay—there exists a value-dependent threshold: switch below it, stay above it. The paradox then collapses into ordinary expected-utility maximization with a proper prior, not a genuine paradox.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: where is Priya correct about the conditioning over q, and where does Jonas's deterministic framing actually block her inference? Also identify what both are missing about how the unknown q interacts with the envelope-selection mechanism—for instance, whether your selection of which envelope to open is independent of q or determined by it—and say who is more right overall about whether this well-specified version truly resolves the original paradox.
