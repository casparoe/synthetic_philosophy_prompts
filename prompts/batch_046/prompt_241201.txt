There's this debate I keep running into about whether German folk morality — what people sometimes call Ordnungsmoral, this idea that rules are there to protect collective coordination and that breaking them for personal conscience is basically antisocial — is actually better than the American thing where you trust your gut even if it means ignoring a stupid rule. People bring up jaywalking in Berlin vs NYC, tax compliance, whether whistleblowers are heroes or traitors, and then it stalls out completely. One side says without predictability you can't have trust, and treating every exception like a moral triumph just erodes the institutions that keep people safe. The other side says blind rule-following is exactly how bureacratic evil happens, and that individual conscience is the only real check. I think part of the stall is that they're not even arguing about the same level — one is talking about institutional design, the other about first-person agency — but I'm not sure if that's right or if it's actually a verbal fight where "rule-following" means "respect for shared norms" to one group and "authoritarian compliance" to the other. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or are they answering different questions under one heading? And is there a hidden empirical claim doing the work — like whether high-rule societies actually have less corruption or just more hidden corruption — or is it purely clashing intuitions about authority? Give me a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, plus what would count as progress.
