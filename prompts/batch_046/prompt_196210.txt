I'm delivering the opening remarks at a one-day workshop titled "Many-Sided Judgment in Organizations." The room is split evenly: on one side are philosophers and logicians who work on Jain epistemology and will catch any misstatement about syādvāda, anekāntavāda, or the conditional operator syāt; on the other side are mediators and institutional auditors who have never encountered these terms and tend to hear "many-sided truth" as either flat relativism or as an excuse for avoiding hard choices.

I want the remarks to do real structural work. The workshop's premise is that organizational audit reports should be able to record genuinely contradictory assessments—for example, that a restructuring was financially necessary and also that it was ethically unjustified—without reducing one judgment to the other, without declaring a false compromise, and without the auditors being accused of hypocrisy for holding both. I want to use Jain anekāntavāda, specifically syādvāda and the logic of conditional predication, to frame this practice: not as a decorative cultural reference, but as the actual logical form that makes such joint holding coherent.

But I need to navigate a specific tension that will be present in the room. Several practitioners worry that if every assessment is only conditionally true—true in some respect—then strong moral criticism collapses: if the harmful decision can be affirmed syāt asti from the management standpoint, then condemning it from the equity standpoint violates the very spirit of many-sidedness. I need the speech to show why anekāntavāda does not disable strong objection but in fact requires it, and to do so without claiming that any single perspective holds absolute, exclusive authority.

The speech should run about eight minutes, be formal but fully accessible to the non-philosophers, and must land for both audiences. It should not cite texts, provide bibliographies, or ask the audience to read further; it should work out the conceptual structure aloud. Please write it, taking care not to misrepresent Jain doctrine in ways the specialists will reject, and taking care not to lose the practitioners in unnecessary technical vocabulary. The philosophical framing should shape what can and cannot be said: for instance, it should not be possible for me to end with a bland "everyone is right," since that misrepresents the doctrine and also defeats the workshop's practical purpose.
