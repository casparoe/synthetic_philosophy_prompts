I have recently been appointed to the municipal commission on permanent public installations, and I am trying to map the implicit philosophy behind our past rulings before I cast my vote on a proposal that sits at an uncomfortable intersection of our established patterns. Our record includes the following: we approved an abstract steel memorial that offered no interpretive plaque and was deliberately illegible to casual viewers, on the grounds that a work's resistance to immediate consumption is part of its commemorative dignity; we rejected an interactive ecological pavilion designed for universal accessibility and educational clarity, ruling that public space should not be treated as a didactic classroom; we permitted the relocation of a historic war monument to a new plaza with modified landscaping, holding that commemorative intent survives if the material substrate remains intact; we refused a plan to represent a demolished industrial site solely through an augmented-reality overlay, on the principle that preservation requires tangible presence rather than virtual projection; and we endorsed an artist-designed pedestrian bridge on the condition that artistic elements be confined to non-structural railings, accepting that art may coexist with primary function only in subordinate domains. The pending proposal is for a ground-anchored garden installation arranged in the geometric patterns of a reconstructed local orthography—an extinct writing system now revived only by a small community of scholars—using living native plants that will grow, die, and be replanted seasonally, so that the precise letterforms will shift gradually over years even though the soil and root structure remain physically permanent. The design is visually striking to anyone but fully readable only by those who have studied the reconstructed script, and its creators describe it not as a static monument but as a "living archive" of linguistic recovery that trades temporal stability for cultural depth.

Given these precedents, should the commission approve the garden? Which of our prior decisions is the closest analog—the abstract steel memorial, the relocated war monument, or the rejected augmented-reality proposal—and do our past rulings genuinely underdetermine the outcome? If they do, what principle would we be adopting with approval versus rejection, particularly regarding whether gradual change in form undermines commemorative or memorial status, and whether a work that demands specialized literacy rather than offering universal accessibility can legitimately occupy a space meant for the general public? If you find that our precedents point clearly one way, say so and identify the rule they jointly express; if not, articulate the two competing principles that best organize our track record and explain what each would predict about this living, slowly transforming installation.
