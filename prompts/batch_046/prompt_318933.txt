I teach a graduate seminar on democratic theory, and my students have lately been leaning on an analogy they have encountered in recent writing: the demos should be conceived as a co-authorship collective, modelled on the authors of a jointly written scholarly monograph. Just as one does not list as an author a colleague who merely works in the same building, a future scholar who might eventually cite the volume, or a proof-reading algorithm that corrects the prose, so too democratic standing ought to be reserved for those agents who actively contribute to the collective authorship of the laws that bind them. The analogy is being deployed to resist expansive boundary claims — for instance, that long-term non-citizen residents, future generations, or sustained synthetic decision systems should count as members of the demos.

Could you audit this analogy for me? I should like an explicit correspondence table mapping the constituent elements of joint academic authorship onto the elements of democratic membership; then a rigorous analysis of where the mapping fractures, with particular attention to which normative and structural features of the source domain are quietly imported and whether they genuinely obtain in the political target; an assessment of whether the exclusionary conclusion — that passive, indirect, or merely affected parties should be denied standing — survives once those disanalogies are made explicit; and finally a brief judgement on whether a more apt analogy for democratic boundaries is available, or whether the co-authorship image collapses entirely under philosophical pressure. Please write your response in British English, in a tone suitable for circulation among advanced students, and avoid bibliographic apparatus or citation.
