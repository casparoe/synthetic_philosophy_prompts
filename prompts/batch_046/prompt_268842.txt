I am a retired neurophysiologist who spent three decades measuring distributed electrical signaling in colonial marine organisms, and since stepping away from the laboratory I have begun auditing an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar. I would like you to draft a final-examination essay question—designed for a ninety-minute sitting and expecting a response of roughly eight hundred words—together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme. The topic should be Dennett’s well-known taxonomy dividing minds into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures, but the question must be unanswerable by mere recitation of definitions and examples. Instead, it should require students to examine whether the taxonomy implicitly assumes a single bounded organism with a unified nervous system, and it should inject a specific conceptual twist: ask them to assess whether Gregorian-level openness—characterized by the accumulation of shared cultural tools—necessarily depends on symbolic language in a strict sense, or whether a distributed system of shared, non-linguistic predictive models could achieve the same open-ended cognitive expansion. They should then apply that analysis to one boundary case of your framing—either a colonial organism with distributed neural architecture or a synthetic system updated through distributed parameter sharing—to see whether Dennett’s progression holds or collapses.

Please present the exam question first, then a model answer that explicitly makes the five core analytical moves: the precise functional distinction between each successive kind, the role of internal representation versus behavioral trial-and-error, the specific conditions that make Gregorian culture qualitatively different from accelerated Popperian learning, the argument for and against language-dependency, and the evaluation of the boundary case. Follow this with a marking scheme totaling twenty-five marks, allocating five marks to each of those five moves, and for each move name the specific misconception that should cost points—such as conflating Skinnerian reinforcement with Popperian internal simulation, or treating Gregorian openness as quantitative rather than structural—along with a one-line note on how candidates typically go wrong there. Do not request bibliographies, named secondary texts, or citation lists; the evaluation must rest entirely on conceptual reasoning that a well-prepared student could produce without access to external sources. Keep the tone formal and suitable for an examination committee, and avoid framing the question around obscure named theories that a small language model could not be expected to have retained.
