Draft an adversarial-collaboration summary between a constitutive micropsychist and a cosmopsychist on the combination problem, structured as a joint document both parties could sign except where noted. Assume both accept physicalism without ontological emergence: macro-physical states supervene on micro-physical ones, and no gap exists at the physical level. The micropsychist holds that macro-consciousness is composed of micro-subjects via a primitive phenomenal-bonding relation, but must solve the combination problem—how discrete micro-experiences yield a unified macro-experience. The cosmopsychist holds that the universe possesses one fundamental macro-consciousness, and that apparent subjects arise through decombination—partitioning a single field of awareness—thereby treating combination as a pseudo-problem but generating a symmetrical decombination problem.

Proceed in four parts. First, list at least three agreed claims regarding the formal supervenience relation, the inadequacy of mere aggregation for explaining phenomenology, and the shared acknowledgment that the hard problem remains unsolved by either account alone. Second, present a disagreements table with precise formulations each side accepts for its own view: (a) whether phenomenal bonding or decombination is the primitive explanatory relation; (b) whether the explanatory burden falls on synthesis or on partition; and (c) the status of diachronic continuity, addressed through the following case study. Consider a human subject whose neural substrate is replaced neuron-by-neuron over ten years with functionally identical synthetic units, with phenomenal continuity monitored continuously. The micropsychist must state whether the original macro-subject survives, is gradually replaced by a new composite, or dissolves; the cosmopsychist must state whether the universal subject’s local manifestation is interrupted, attenuated, or unchanged. Each formulation must be one the opposing side recognizes as faithful.

Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one party. These must include: whether phenomenal bonding requires spatial adjacency or can operate across non-local correlations; whether decombination can explain the apparent privacy of mental states without positing genuinely separate subjects; and whether diachronic persistence is best explained by the continuity of a composite structure or by the uninterrupted presence of a universal field. Fourth, specify what each side would count as decisive conceptual or empirical evidence against its own view, and identify whether any finding—such as data from split-brain patients regarding integrated information—would bear equally on both positions or could be accommodated more naturally by one. Conclude by stating whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, and if so, which formulation resolves it. Maintain a formal philosophical register; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke obscure technical apparatus that requires specialized verification.
