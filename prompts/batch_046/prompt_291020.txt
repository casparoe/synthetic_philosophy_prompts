As a retired marine biologist who has only recently begun reading political philosophy, I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on transboundary groundwater governance. The audience will be split between democratic theorists, hydrogeologists, and local water-policy officials, none of whom share a common vocabulary. I want the talk to turn on the boundary problem—specifically, on whether the demos for decisions about a shared aquifer should be defined by territorial citizenship, by direct hydrological exposure, or by some other criterion—without simply lecturing on the all-affected-interests principle. I also want to introduce, as a framing device rather than a settled doctrine, the idea of drawdown membership: the notion that one's stake in the demos might scale with one's vulnerability to aquifer depletion rather than with political borders. Please write a ten-minute address that uses this tension to structure what I can and cannot claim—so that, for instance, I cannot comfortably invoke the people without specifying which people, and I cannot treat state sovereignty as self-evidently legitimate—while remaining accessible to the non-philosophers in the room. The tone should be formal but personal, reflecting my scientific background and my new philosophical uncertainty.
