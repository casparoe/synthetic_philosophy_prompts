As a software engineer without any formal philosophy training, I have been trying to understand the backward induction paradox in the centipede game, and I am drafting some notes for a short essay that maps the problem onto concepts I know from distributed systems and recursive algorithms. I understand the standard structure: two players take turns deciding whether to stop and split a gradually increasing payoff or continue, with the risk that the other player will seize the larger share on the next move, and I see why common knowledge of rationality appears to drive both agents to terminate immediately at the first node. To formalize my intuition, I treated the sequence of decisions as a recursive termination proof—if the base case is rational defection at the final node, then induction forces defection at every preceding node back to the root. But this framing strikes me as suspiciously similar to a pure functional program with no side effects, perfect synchronization, and globally shared state, which is rarely how real concurrent systems behave. So my essay idea introduces what I am calling "stateful rationality": the claim that rational agents are not stateless functions but carry memory, fatigue, temporal context, and partial information, so that Player A's certainty about Player B's rationality might degrade across nodes not because of irrationality, but because of cognitive clock skew or incomplete message passing. I want to ask whether relaxing the idealization of perfect synchronicity in common knowledge—allowing that an agent might fail to fully represent that the other agent fully represents that they fully represent, and so on—fractures the inductive chain in a philosophically interesting way, or whether this is just a computer-science metaphor being forced onto a normative problem.

Before I develop this further, I would value comments on the coherence and accuracy of the framing. Specifically, am I describing the role of common knowledge in backward induction correctly, or have I mischaracterized it by treating it like a distributed consensus protocol? Does the notion of "stateful" or temporally lagged rationality correspond to any recognized distinction in game theory—perhaps between perfect and imperfect rationality, or between common knowledge and mutual belief—or is it a category error? I am also curious whether my analogy reveals a genuine limitation in how the paradox is usually presented, or whether it merely obscures the normative point that rationality should be timeless and context-independent. I am not looking for a bibliography; I want to know if the essay idea hangs together philosophically and whether the niche move of importing distributed-systems metaphors actually illuminates the centipede game rather than distorting it.
