Me and my mate had a long argument last night about the reference class problem, and he walked me through this chain that seems to prove self-locating belief is either irrational or totally groundless. I think there's a hidden slide between steps but I can't name it.

(1) Rational de se belief — like "I am the Monday copy of Sleeping Beauty" — needs a reference class of observers over whom credence is distributed.
(2) You can't define that class by external properties (e.g. "in this lab"), because a perfect brain-in-a-vat or distant twin with identical internal states wouldn't be included, yet I can't rule them out from the inside.
(3) So the class has to be defined purely by shared internal evidential state: every observer with exactly my present experiences and memories counts as a candidate.
(4) On that criterion, my reference class isn't just Monday-Sleeping-Beauty and Tuesday-Sleeping-Beauty; it also includes every simulation running her mental state, every Boltzmann brain that happens to match, and every causally disconnected twin in a distant galaxy, each weighted by its prior.
(5) So unless I already have a prior that crushes those possibilities to near zero — which I can't justify without circularity — my credence that I'm actually in the experiment should be vanishingly small.
(6) But I don't think that at all; I treat "I'm in the Sleeping Beauty setup" as a perfectly live hypothesis.
(7) Therefore either I'm irrational, or (3) is false and I'm allowed to restrict the class arbitrarily to "real, nearby, physical copies".
(8) But arbitrary restriction kills the objectivity that self-locating reasoning was supposed to provide.
(9) Hence de se belief collapses: it's either irrational or groundless.

Which premise do I reject? I'd rather hang onto (1) and (6) than (3) or (8), but I want to know what I'm paying if I drop (3). Does it just license ad hoc classes?
