Read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through the lens of behavioral criteria for mind, contrasting the familiar linguistic imitation game with a stronger behavioral standard that demands sustained sensorimotor interaction in an unpredictable physical environment. The creature acquires language, moral reasoning, and emotional depth by observing a family from concealment, then demands that Victor acknowledge him as a thinking being. Does the novel dramatize the claim that passing verbal and social-behavioral tests is insufficient for being granted a mind, because Victor refuses to engage with the creature's embodied presence and applies the criteria selectively on the basis of appearance? Where does the text suggest that the failure lies not in the creature's capacities but in the evaluator's refusal to test them fairly? And does Shelley anticipate the later philosophical objection—pressed in robotics and philosophy of mind—that a purely linguistic behavioral criterion misses something essential about mindedness that only embodied, open-ended interaction can reveal? Offer a reading that treats both the novel and the distinction seriously, noting where the work resists being reduced to an illustration of the debate and what it contributes that abstract argument about behavioral tests alone tends to overlook.
