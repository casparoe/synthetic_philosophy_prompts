Read Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland through the philosophical debate over whether dreams are experiences, as framed by Norman Malcolm's denial that sleeping involves any experiential stream and Daniel Dennett's rejection of the Cartesian theater in favor of consciousness being assembled from competing narrative drafts. Focus especially on Alice's discontinuous bodily identity, her inability to stabilize a self across the Caterpillar's interrogation about who she is, her uncertainty about whether she might be Ada or Mabel, the recursive false-awakening in the pool of tears where she briefly takes her surroundings for the sea, and the final collapse of the trial into a mere deck of cards.

Your task is a two-directional critical reading. First, show where the philosophical framework illuminates Carroll's scenes: does Malcolm's claim that there is no dream-self undergoing experiences help explain Alice's lack of bodily continuity and her tendency to rationalize her transformations only after they occur? Does Dennett's idea that consciousness is constructed from competing drafts rather than presented to a single inner observer make sense of Alice's shifting size and her post-hoc attempts to construct a coherent autobiography? Second, show where the novel resists reduction to either view. Consider whether Alice's immediate affective responses—fear at falling, indignation at the Queen, curiosity about her own identity—function as experiential residues that survive narrative reconstruction, and whether the recursive false awakenings actually challenge Dennett's account by implying an experiential continuity that persists across drafts without being anchored in any single waking report. Be faithful to the text's major scenes—the rabbit hole, the size changes, the pool, the trial—without treating them as mere illustrations.

Conclude by articulating what Carroll's fiction contributes to the debate that philosophical argument alone does not: whether the novel dramatizes a form of selfhood that is neither the Cartesian ego Malcolm rejects nor the purely reconstructed narrative Dennett proposes, and whether the ending's dissolution of the dream-world into cards suggests that the boundary between lived experience and its retrospective report is itself unstable in ways that neither philosopher fully captures.
