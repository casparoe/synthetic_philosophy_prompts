Question: Just war theory traditionally distinguishes jus ad bellum from jus in bello. Michael Walzer defends the "moral equality of combatants": soldiers on both sides have equal rights to kill in battle regardless of their cause's justice. Critically assess whether this separation can survive if one accepts that the moral permission to kill derives from individual liability to defensive harm rather than collective state authorization. Address the case of conscripted soldiers coerced into fighting for an unjust cause.

Student answer: Walzer separates the two domains because soldiers are state instruments who do not choose the war, so they bear no responsibility for its injustice. This yields moral equality: every combatant may kill enemies because they pose immediate lethal threats, just like self-defense. Revisionists such as Jeff McMahan reject this, holding that only those liable to harm may be killed, so unjust combatants lack permission to kill just ones. But this fails for conscripts: they are coerced and therefore not culpable, and because they lack moral responsibility they regain the right to defend themselves and kill just combatants. So Walzer's equality is ultimately preserved—once we see that absence of culpability restores combatant privileges on the unjust side.
