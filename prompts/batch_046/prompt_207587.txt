Question: Jamie is an experienced climate scientist who runs a model and gets result R. Later, she learns that 80% of her peers think she misinterpreted a parameter, but she checks her code twice and still sees R as correct. Is it rational for her to keep believing R? Discuss with reference to higher-order evidence and whether this involves epistemic akrasia.

Student answer:
This is a clear case of higher-order defeat. Jamie has first-order evidence (her model and code check) and higher-order evidence (the peer disagreement). According to Christensens common prior rule, she should update her confidance downward because disagreement implies she probably made an error. Epistemic akrasia happens when you believe p but also believe you shouldn't believe p, which is different from practical akrasia like knowing cake is bad but eating it anyway — that point comes from David Lewis's work on voluntary belief. Here Jamie might seem akratic if she keeps belief R while accepting the peers are likely right. But since she can directly check her code, she has privileged first-order access that defeats the higher-order worry, so she can rationally maintain full belief in R without being akratic at all. Therefore the peer disagreement doesn't matter if your direct check feels right.
