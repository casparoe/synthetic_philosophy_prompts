I sit on the Regional Integration and Welfare Access Review Board, which must issue a formal written opinion before the end of the quarter on a pilot proposal from the Department of Social Integration. The proposal—titled "Unconditional Access with Mutual Design"—would eliminate all language-proficiency and documentation-status prerequisites for immediate enrollment in housing vouchers, primary-care access, and income-support programs. In place of standard bureaucratic intake, it institutes a mandatory "co-design protocol": every beneficiary must participate in multilingual deliberative forums that jointly redesign local service-delivery rules with long-term residents, with no predetermined administrative language and no fixed assimilation timeline.

The philosophical tension goes beyond the familiar open-borders versus welfare-state sustainability debate. The proposal's architects argue that requiring immigrants to map their needs onto pre-existing bureaucratic categories—categories shaped by native-born residents' conceptual schemes—is a form of epistemic domination that undermines democratic legitimacy. Critics counter that the welfare state depends on "institutional reciprocity": a shared normative framework that makes collective contribution and accountability intelligible, and that replacing assimilation with open-ended co-design dissolves the very coherence that justifies redistributive obligations.

Our legal mandate permits "reasonable accommodation" for vulnerable arrivals but requires that any pilot maintain "fiscal and social coherence," a term the board has never defined. Draft the board's decision memo recommending approval, conditional approval with a specific sunset clause or performance metric, or rejection. The memo must propose a concrete course of action with an explicit time frame, and it must engage the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine philosophical seriousness—whether that is the claim that unconditional access collapses the reciprocity that grounds welfare-state obligations, or the claim that co-design obligations impose an intolerable participatory burden on people seeking basic survival support. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the reasoning must stand on arguments the board can evaluate without external reference.
