For the past three years I have kept an unfiltered voice-memo archive: after every consequential choice—taking a job, ending a friendship, adopting a principle—I record my reasoning in detail, with the explicit aim of not polishing it. Last year I began feeding these transcripts into a local language model that outputs a concise “operating manual” of my stated values, configured so that entries from the last six months are weighted twice as heavily as older ones. Before I make a significant decision now, I read the manual; I then act, record the action and my justification, and feed that back in. Recently I have noticed the loop tightening. The manual has become so internally coherent that when I feel an impulse that conflicts with it—say, a sudden desire to abandon a long-term project I have consistently framed as central to my identity—I treat that impulse as noise, suppress it, and record the suppression as further confirmation of my priorities. When I try to step outside and ask who exactly is observing this circulation, I find myself only generating another memo about self-observation, which the model folds back into the manual. I do not feel that I am performing a false self, yet I also cannot locate anything in me—any desire, any judgment, any feeling of “I”—that is not either an input to or an output of this system. Is my sense of self genuinely nothing more than this feedback loop, or is there a meaningful philosophical difference between being constituted by such a loop and being imprisoned in one? And if the loop itself produces the compelling feeling that there is an observer outside it, does that feeling have any authority, or is it just another output that serves the loop’s coherence?
