I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level metaphysics seminar, aimed at final-year undergraduates and first-year MA students. I need one essay question on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard textbook summary in which Bradley argues that relations require further relations and are therefore unreal. Instead, inject the following more precise framing: ask students to treat the internal structure of a relational judgment—e.g., that a stands in R to b—as susceptible to a version of the regress, but now split between (i) the metaphysical problem of what ties relata together in reality and (ii) the representational problem of what makes the judgment one unified content rather than an unordered list of terms. Require students to explain why adopting an 'external relations' view fails, on its own, to resolve the representational problem, and to evaluate whether a 'mode-of-combination' or adverbial account can genuinely block the regress without covertly reintroducing a hidden 'unity-fact' at a higher level. The answer must therefore distinguish the metaphysical regress from the representational one and say exactly where each proposed solution succeeds or collapses.

Deliverables:
1. The exam question itself, with explicit instructions about what must be covered, suitable for 90 minutes and 30 marks.
2. A model answer of approximately 600–800 words that makes the specific conceptual moves required.
3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks allocated (total 30), and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks.

Constraints: do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions, page numbers, or secondary literature. Do not include verbatim quotations from Bradley or any other philosopher; paraphrase all positions without quotation marks. The prompt should depend only on ideas that a well-prepared student could be expected to know without web access, and should avoid highly niche interpretations or obscure attributions that a small model might not have properly memorized. Write the prompt so that it demands conceptual precision rather than recall of labels.
