ok so i've been stuck writing my thesis chapter on voluntary action and i keep circling back to the Libet readiness-potential experiments. you know the setup — people watch a clock and report when they first feel the urge to flick their wrist, and meanwhile the EEG shows this readiness potential building up a few hundred milliseconds before that reported awareness. the standard interpretation is that the brain has already initiated the movement before consciousness gets involved, which people take as a problem for free will. then there's the usual counter-move: either conscious veto is still possible, or the task is artifical and doesn't capture real-world decision making. but here's the thing — both camps seem to agree on the neuro data, both accept that the RP precedes the subjective report, and yet the debate hasn't moved an inch in years. it feels like we're just repeating the same moves.

so i've been wondering if the stall is actually about something hidden in how we talk about causation. i came across this distinction between trigger causes and structural causes — like, is the RP the actual trigger that makes the finger move, or is it just part of the background conditions that make a voluntary movement possible? if one side is treating the RP as the deciding factor and the other is treating it as preparatory background, then they might not be disagreeing about freedom at all but about what kind of explanation counts in action theory. is that the right diagnosis — a conceptual disagreement about causal structure disguised as an empirical fight about free will? or is there a genuine empirical gap here that a better experiment could close? and if it is conceptual, is there any framing or distinction that could actually break the deadlock, or are we stuck with bedrock intuitions about what counts as "my" decision? i don't want a survey of every interpretation; i want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, with reasons, and an honest answer about whether anything could actually move it.
