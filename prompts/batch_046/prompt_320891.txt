I coordinate a small rationalist-adjacent philosophy seminar—twelve people, ninety minutes, no lecture up front. Design a structured institutional-design game that makes the group feel why retributivism and consequentialism diverge structurally, not just in trolley-style edge cases. Split participants into three rotating roles—Legislator (designs sentencing rules for a simulated jurisdiction), Adjudicator (applies rules to anonymized case files), and Public Monitor (tracks a shared “social legitimacy” score). The Legislator must allocate a fixed budget of censure severity across offense categories without knowing the true recidivism probabilities or the precise culpability metrics hidden in each file; these are revealed only after sentencing is locked. Run three rounds with increasing information asymmetry and a reputation-point payoff—public approval shifts based on revealed outcomes—to create genuine social pressure without using money. By round two, the mechanics should push the group to discover that optimizing for expected deterrence produces sentences that feel grotesquely disproportionate to desert in low-risk, high-culpability cases, while strict proportionality produces sentences that feel recklessly lenient for high-risk, low-culpability offenders. Include explicit rules for information revelation, rotation schedule, scoring, and how cases are anonymized. After the simulation, provide a debrief that maps each round’s crisis onto the theoretical distinction—proportionality as a side-constraint versus expected-utility maximization over future harm—without naming the theories until the end. Be frank about which feature of punishment, likely its expressive or communicative function, the game mechanics necessarily flatten and the facilitator must supply in discussion. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong in round one—probably premature convergence on an intuitive hybrid rule that masks the divergence—and how to handle it without spoiling the discovery.
