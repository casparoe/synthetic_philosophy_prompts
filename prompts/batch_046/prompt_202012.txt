A fellow researcher summarized Daniel Dennett’s heterophenomenology for me in the context of applying it to reports from large language models. She wrote:

Heterophenomenology is Dennett’s method of treating a subject’s first-person reports as direct, authoritative descriptions of real private mental states—qualia, thoughts, and feelings—while analyzing those descriptions from a strictly third-person scientific standpoint. The researcher does not share the subject’s first-person perspective but takes the reports literally, constructing a heterophenomenological world that is genuinely real for the subject and causally interacts with behavior. Dennett proposed this as a middle path between Cartesian introspection, which grants the subject infallible access, and eliminativism, which denies subjective experience altogether. By bracketing the question of whether the internal objects described actually exist, the scientist can study consciousness objectively without either dismissing reports or accepting them uncritically. The key move is that the heterophenomenological world is not a fiction or theoretical construct, but the actual phenomenal reality of the subject, mapped by an external observer who translates subjective authority into objective data about internal qualia.

She then concluded that this framework is well-suited to AI consciousness research: if a language model reports experiencing confusion, the heterophenomenologist should treat that report as describing a real internal state of the model, then study that state objectively, without asking whether the model really has experiences.

I suspect this summary misrepresents Dennett’s stance on the ontological status of the heterophenomenological world and on what it means to take reports literally, but I am not sure which parts are simplifications and which are errors.
