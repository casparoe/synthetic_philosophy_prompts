Consider a philosopher of memory whose considered judgments, informed by Margalit's ethics of shared remembrance and Rieff's defense of forgetting, run as follows. She holds that (1) states and institutions bear an unconditional duty to preserve accurate archives of mass atrocity, even against present political pressure to close them; (2) individual survivors have no obligation to personally retain traumatic details and may destroy private records in service of healing; (3) descendants of victims are not required to inherit grief or maintain personal commemorative practices, though collective education should continue; and (4) perpetrators and their institutional successors must never be permitted to suppress documentary evidence, regardless of whether direct responsibility has lapsed.

Now a new case: one survivor, whose testimony is one of thousands held in a state archive documenting a severe ethnic cleansing, formally requests that her specific testimony be permanently redacted and destroyed. She argues that her narrative remains her personal property, that thousands of parallel testimonies fully preserve collective knowledge, and that retaining her words against her will transforms her suffering into institutional property and violates her right to forget. The archive has no legal mandate to retain any particular testimony, only to maintain an adequate historical record.

If this thinker wishes to remain consistent with her earlier verdicts, should she support or oppose the redaction? Identify at least two distinct principled rules that fit her four earlier judgments equally well, state what each predicts about this request, offer your best interpretation of her view with reasons, and specify one further case that would best distinguish the competing rules.
