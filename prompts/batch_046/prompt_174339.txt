I am a senior software engineer with no formal philosophy background—my training is in distributed systems and formal verification—though I recently audited a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and cognitive science out of personal interest. Next Thursday I am presenting a twenty-minute argument at an interdisciplinary colloquium jointly hosted by the philosophy and computer science departments; my audience will be roughly half analytic philosophers of mind and philosophy of science who are generally hostile to importing engineering metaphors into metaphysical debates, about a quarter cognitive scientists who will want empirical data on how people actually form mental-state judgments, and a quarter computer scientists who will understand the abstraction analogy but will press me on whether I am merely redescribing well-known framing effects or the representativeness heuristic without adding any genuine explanatory purchase. The thesis I am defending is that classic intuition pumps—such as the Chinese Room, Mary the scientist's color experience, and hypothetical scenarios about artificial moral patients—do not simply illustrate pre-existing intuitions but actively manufacture them by collapsing the distinction between functional interface descriptions (what a system does) and ontological implementation commitments (what a system is), a structural failure I compare to leaky abstractions in software architecture where contract-level reasoning silently imports dependency-level assumptions.

Because I have no philosophy credentials and the audience knows it, I expect the objections will be sharper and more methodological than they would be for an established scholar; I need a prep sheet listing the eight objections I am most likely to actually face, ordered from the one that could most thoroughly undermine my credibility to the one that is merely awkward, each phrased in the precise register that audience member would use—whether that is a demand for empirical falsifiability from the cognitive scientists, a charge of category error from the philosophers of mind, or a request for formalization from the CS faculty—together with a concise two-minute reply for each and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest or concedes too much. I also want you to flag the single question I am hoping nobody asks, likely a challenge from the philosophy-of-science contingent asking whether my own claim about intuition pumps is itself just another pump, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; I will not have online access during the session and I want to avoid guessing at references I cannot verify.

The argument I present relies on three specific moves: first, that the Chinese Room invites us to accept that syntax is not sufficient for semantics only by smuggling in an unstated premise about the necessary conditions for understanding that is itself drawn from the interface-level behavior we observe; second, that Mary's room argument derives its force not from the absence of physical information but from a conflation of representational format with phenomenal content; and third, that recent debates about whether large language models could be moral patients reproduce the same collapse when commentators slide from this system produces outputs that track moral reasoning to this system possesses moral standing, without noticing that the inference depends on an unstated bridge principle linking functional tracking to intrinsic value. I need the objections to target these three moves specifically, and I want the replies to be calibrated for a speaker who can speak competently about formal verification and interface contracts but who should not pretend to have read the entire canon of twentieth-century philosophy of mind.
