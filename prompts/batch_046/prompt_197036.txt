I'm putting together the final for my upper-level philosophy of science seminar and I'm stuck on the adaptationism unit. We covered Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique, but every past exam just asks students to explain the San Marco analogy and list the problems with the adaptive programme. I want something that actually makes them do philosophy rather than memorise a summary, so I've cooked up a hypothetical case: imagine a deep-sea vent bacterium that carries a dense cluster of chaperone proteins to survive extreme heat. The cluster happens to bind iron and produces a reddish pigment that, in the dim vent environment, makes the bacterium less visible to predators that detect chemical gradients. Biologists initially publish papers arguing the pigment was selected for camouflage, but later genomic analysis shows the cluster's architecture is entirely constrained by thermal-stability requirements and that the iron-binding is an unavoidable chemical side-effect.

Could you write an exam essay question that uses this scenario to test whether students really grasp the spandrels logic? Specifically, it should ask them to evaluate whether the pigment is a spandrel, an exaptation, or a true adaptation, and it should force them to address where Gould and Lewontin think the burden of proof lies in such cases. The question needs to be doable in about 45 minutes and worth 30 marks. Please also provide a model answer of around 600 words and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer—which must explicitly discuss the distinction between current utility and historical selective origin, and correctly identify the logical structure of the adaptive programme's burden of proof—from a B answer, which might apply the labels correctly but miss the deeper point about how the spandrels critique inverts the default assumption. Write it all in British English, and dont ask for references or a bibliography since this is meant to be answered without library access. I also dont want any direct quotes from Gould and Lewontin—just paraphrase if needed—because I want students to reconstruct the argument in their own words.
