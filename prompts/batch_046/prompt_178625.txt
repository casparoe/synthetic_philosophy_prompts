Design a 75-minute workshop for 8–10 participants in a philosophy reading group that makes them feel the asymmetry of tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while pleasure, achievement, and flourishing matter at most instrumentally, and often harmfully, by generating new craving.

Structure it as a two-phase simulation called “The Cessation Council.” In Phase 1 (15 minutes), give each participant a private “Well-Being Ledger” with four categories: Suffering (S), Craving (C), Flourishing (F), and Tranquility (T, defined strictly as S=0 and C=0). They begin at S=2, C=3, F=1, T=0. Each then secretly selects one of three “Life Paths” for themselves: (A) The Flourishing Path (F+3, C+2, S unchanged); (B) The Relief Path (S-2, C-1, F unchanged); or (C) The Tranquility Path (S-2, C-3, F-1, T=1). They record the choice but reveal only that they have picked A, B, or C.

In Phase 2 (25 minutes), the group becomes a council with 10 shared “Intervention Tokens” and must fund exactly three of six “Societal Projects” described on narrative cards (no numbers visible). The projects should include: universal chronic-pain eradication (Relief); a competitive culture of artistic ambition and luxury abundance (Flourishing, with hidden craving costs); widespread desire-cessation training with basic needs guaranteed (Tranquility); addiction-elimination clinics (Relief); open-access monastic retreat centers (Tranquility); and a national program for peak achievement and romantic pairing (Flourishing). The council deliberates and votes without knowing the hidden scoring metric.

In Phase 3 (15 minutes), reveal the metric: only net reductions in S and C count; F points are decorative; T is the only state with intrinsic value. The council calculates its collective score. Then participants disclose their Phase 1 paths. The group compares private choices with the council’s public allocations.

The debrief (20 minutes) should map specific moments onto the idea: the private ledger choice exposes whether participants bought flourishing at the cost of renewed craving; the council vote reveals collective bias toward visible achievement over invisible craving-reduction. Note what will predictably go wrong—participants will usually overfund Flourishing projects due to social prestige, and some will privately choose Tranquility while publicly defending Flourishing; handle this by asking whether the defense appeals to intrinsic value or to signaling. Be frank about the activity’s limitation: the ledger can represent tranquility only as the absence of negative values, not as a qualitative phenomenological state; the facilitator must supply a brief guided reflection or first-person account of what craving-cessation feels like, since the simulation cannot reproduce that lived dimension on its own. The participants should leave seeing why, on this view, eliminating craving is not symmetric with producing joy, and why a society might rationally prefer widespread tranquility to widespread excellence.
