I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but a disagreement that broke out during a recent team offsite has stayed with me, and I want a careful adjudication rather than a broad survey. Two colleagues, Marcus and Elena, argued about simultaneous invention, using Newton and Leibniz with calculus and Darwin and Wallace with natural selection as their central examples. I will recount their exchange as precisely as I remember it. Marcus began by saying that these pairs demonstrate that major scientific discoveries are historically inevitable: had Newton never lived, calculus would still have emerged within a few decades, and the same holds for evolutionary theory, which Wallace articulated independently. Therefore, assigning discovery credit to a single individual is largely a social convention, comparable to tagging a code commit with an author’s name when the same fix would eventually have been written by someone else. Elena replied that this treats scientific theories as pure abstract algorithms with interchangeable implementations, but in reality Newton’s geometric formulation of fluxions and Leibniz’s differential notation are not merely cosmetic variants; they carry different implicit commitments about infinitesimals and continuity, and they opened distinct research programs with different problem-solving capacities. She made an analogous point about Darwin and Wallace: Darwin’s emphasis on sexual selection, pangenesis, and gradualist mechanisms created a conceptual framework that Wallace’s more sparse, selection-only account did not provide, so substituting one for the other would have altered the trajectory of subsequent biology rather than leaving it unchanged. Marcus answered with an analogy drawn from software engineering: if two developers independently implement quicksort and mergesort, we do not claim they made two different discoveries; we say they produced different implementations of the same abstract sorting algorithm, and the discovery belongs to the algorithm, not to the syntax. Elena countered that this analogy fails because scientific theories are not merely black-box outputs or algorithms evaluated only by their results; they are also formal cognitive tools that constrain what counts as evidence and how questions are framed. She compared the situation to merging two git branches that resolve the same bug but refactor the underlying architecture in incompatible ways: you cannot declare them identical commits, because the history, structure, and future extensibility of the codebase differ, and treating them as one ignores path dependency. Marcus then said Elena was smuggling in a media-theory claim that the medium is part of the message, which belongs to communication studies rather than philosophy of science, and that if we accept her view then every notational or presentational variant becomes a separate discovery, which renders attribution impossible rather than meaningful. Elena’s final move was to ask Marcus to specify, without circular reasoning, what the pure abstract content of calculus is independently of any formalization, and she suggested that his confidence in such a content-free discovery is itself an unexamined philosophical assumption.
