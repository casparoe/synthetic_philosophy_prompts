I'm trying to identify whether there's an established term for a pattern I've noticed in debates over why some nations are wealthy, and if so, how precisely it should be carved.

Consider the three familiar explanatory families: institutional arrangements (property rights, inclusive political structures), geographic endowments (climate, disease ecology, navigability), and cultural frameworks (norms of cooperation, time preference, social trust). In practice, analysts often defend one family not by showing that the others are irrelevant everywhere, but by distributing counterexamples among them. When two regions share geography but diverge in prosperity, institutions are credited; when they share institutions but diverge, geography or culture absorbs the residual. The result is not merely that each theory carries auxiliary hypotheses, but that the three together form something like a closed explanatory loop: any observed outcome can be mapped onto at least one family, and the failure of family A in a particular comparison is routinely used to justify family B's relevance rather than being registered as a strike against the overall tripartite scheme.

This feels structurally different from an ordinary Duhem-Quine problem, because the protective adjustments aren't internal to one theory but are inter-theoretic rotations. It also feels more organized than generic post-hoc rationalization, since there is a stable division of labor—geography for static constraints, institutions for policy-sensitive variation, culture for the stubborn remainder—that shields the collective framework from any single disconfirming natural experiment.

A concrete configuration: imagine adjacent regions split by a colonial border that imposed different legal systems. If the institutional-divergence prediction holds, institutions win. If outcomes converge despite the institutional split, analysts often invoke shared geography or pre-colonial cultural continuity to explain away the null result—without treating that convergence as evidence that institutional differences were overrated in the first place. Conversely, when two regions undergo nearly identical institutional reforms but diverge in outcomes, the divergence is rarely taken to undermine institutionalism generally; instead it is localized to a geographic or cultural moderator, leaving the institutional family intact.

My questions: is there an established concept—perhaps in philosophy of history, comparative politics, or econometrics—for this kind of rotational immunization, where a cluster of competing historical explanations protects itself by exporting anomalies among its members rather than internalizing them? If not, how should we distinguish it from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis protection, from model averaging, and from straightforward theoretical pluralism? And if we do coin a term, what are its boundary conditions—does it require exactly three explanatory buckets, or is it a general feature of any overdetermined causal scheme in history where failures are systematically relocated? I'm not looking for a bare label. I want to know whether the carving already exists, what it includes and excludes, and whether this pattern genuinely undermines causal inference in development economics or is simply the unavoidable price of reasoning about complex historical systems.
