Compare Ian Hacking’s account of looping effects in psychiatric classification with the concept of “taxonomic drag”—the institutional inertia exerted on a diagnostic category by the legal, administrative, pharmaceutical, and therapeutic infrastructures that crystallize around it, causing the category to persist, expand, or contract independently of shifts in the clinical phenomena it originally targeted. Focus on how each framework explains both the malleability and the stability of psychiatric kinds: looping effects stress the feedback loop between a classification and the self-understanding and behavior of those classified, whereas taxonomic drag stresses path-dependent institutional embedding that can operate even when individual feedback is weak or absent. For each framework, specify (i) the precise mechanism that drives change in a category, (ii) the precise mechanism that resists change or drives expansion, and (iii) the kind of empirical or conceptual evidence that would discriminate one mechanism from the other. Apply this comparison to two cases: the post-DSM-III expansion of autism spectrum disorder, and the continued clinical and legal use of “hysteria” and its derivatives long after formal removal from major nosologies. Articulate where the two frameworks make genuinely different predictions—particularly regarding what would happen if an official diagnostic label were suddenly withdrawn from manuals but retained in billing codes, disability law, and treatment protocols—and avoid reducing either framework to a special case of the other.
