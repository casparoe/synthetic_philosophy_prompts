I serve as an ethics reviewer for a neuroprosthetics board that must decide whether an implanted artificial substrate genuinely realizes a patient's mental states—beliefs, intentions, perceptions—or merely mimics their behavioral outputs, for purposes of informed consent and liability. Turn the principle of functionalism together with the thesis of multiple realizability into a reusable evaluation procedure: list the ordered questions my team should ask, specify what answer is sufficient to proceed at each stage, mark the point where a failure requires rejection, identify the step where the question becomes one of values rather than functional equivalence, and include a counterfactual-robustness check that verifies the substrate preserves causal-functional dependencies not only in standard conditions but under perturbation, paying special attention to whether the functional role is narrowly internal or widely environmental. Apply the procedure to three proposals: a hippocampal chip that replicates episodic-memory retrieval exactly; a cloud-linked processor that duplicates belief-formation but relies on external servers for part of its functional architecture; and a motor-intention prosthesis that restores action-guidance but eliminates subjective agency, yielding an answer the board will dislike. State precisely what the procedure cannot adjudicate. Deliver the response as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introductory or concluding text.
