I have been preparing a long-form piece on whether classical metaphysical principles survive contact with modern thought experiments in physics and philosophy of science, and in the course of my research I returned to Leibniz’s 1715 correspondence with Samuel Clarke. In the relevant portion of the exchange, Leibniz defends what we now call the identity of indiscernibles by appealing to the principle of sufficient reason: he argues that if two entities were to share every intrinsic quality and every relational property—so that there existed literally no feature, internal or external, by which one could be singled out from the other—then there could be no sufficient reason for them to be numerically distinct. To treat them as two rather than one would be to posit a difference without any ground, which he takes to be impossible not only for God but for nature itself. I understand the broad architecture of this argument, but I am struggling to determine its precise scope when it is brought into contact with the scenario later devised by Max Black: two perfectly homogeneous spheres suspended in an otherwise empty, symmetric universe, separated by some fixed spatial interval.

Black’s usual reply is that spatial separation itself supplies a relational difference—sphere A stands three miles to the left of sphere B—so the spheres are not genuinely indiscernible after all, and Leibniz’s principle is satisfied rather than violated. Yet if one takes seriously Leibniz’s own relational conception of space, as he articulates it against Clarke’s Newtonian absolutism, then spatial relations are not primitive containers but depend on the actual configuration of bodies. In an otherwise empty universe containing only these two spheres, I am unsure whether the relation “being three miles to the left of” is fully determinate, or whether the absence of any further bodies or orientational framework renders the distinction unstable. To push the difficulty further, I have been wondering about a modified case that I have not seen discussed in the standard literature: suppose the two spheres exist in a one-dimensional circular space with no intrinsic orientation—no left, no right, only a single closed loop—so that each sphere is equidistant from the other along both directions and there is no asymmetrical relation to serve as a distinguish-er. In such a setting, even spatial separation seems to collapse as a source of difference.

Could you help me understand exactly what the passage from the Clarke correspondence is meant to rule out? Is Leibniz’s appeal to sufficient reason directed at the absence of any distinguishability whatsoever—including the absence of determinable spatial relations—or does it tacitly assume that bodies always inhabit a framework rich enough to supply relational differences? And if the latter, does that mean Black’s original spheres survive the principle, while only my one-dimensional circular variant genuinely violates it? I want to be precise about whether the principle is doing metaphysical work or whether it is being quietly supported by background assumptions about the structure of space.
