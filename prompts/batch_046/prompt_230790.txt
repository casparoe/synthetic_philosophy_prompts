Having spent decades interpreting null measurements and instrumental silence in distributed experimental systems, I have recently turned to philosophical analyses of causation by omission and wish to test two competing frameworks against a case drawn from my own domain of expertise. First, compare the counterfactual-dependence account—according to which an omission is causally efficacious with respect to an outcome precisely when the occurrence of the absent event, holding background conditions fixed, would have prevented or altered that outcome—with the normative-baseline account, which holds that an omission functions as a cause only when it constitutes a deviation from an expected, obligatory, or functionally required course of events. Second, apply both to the following synthetic scenario: a redundant sensor network is engineered so that the failure of any single node to transmit is automatically reconstructed from neighboring signals, leaving no detectable gap in the data record; nevertheless, one node's silent omission introduces a subtle, uncorrected bias into the reconstruction algorithm, which over successive iterations propagates into a systematic calibration drift affecting the entire array. The local absence is therefore invisible at the system-output level, yet implicated in a distant emergent error. Does the counterfactual account assign causal status to the node's omission, given that the immediate reconstructed record exhibits no counterfactual dependence on the missing transmission? Does the normative-baseline account fare better by appealing to the node's functional duty to report, or does the system's redundancy eliminate the very expectation that makes deviation meaningful? Conclude by identifying the structural feature that does the philosophical work—specifically, the decoupling of local absence from global detectability—and state whether a scientist's operational distinction between random noise and structurally significant absence offers any resolution, or whether it imports statistical criteria that remain foreign to pure metaphysics.
