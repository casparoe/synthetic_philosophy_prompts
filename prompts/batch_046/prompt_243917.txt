Write a sestina about why knowledge is more valuable than a true belief that is correct only by accident. Choose six end-words that can bear philosophical weight—for example guide, chain, world, true, hold, and fall, or six others of your own—and let the form’s obligatory rotation do the conceptual work: in successive stanzas each word should return in a new grammatical and philosophical position, tracking how a belief can occur once as true but unheld, versus anchored across nearby possibilities by a chain that guides it away from error. Use the three-line envoi to gather all six end-words together and leave open whether the value lies in the chain itself or in the worlds it thereby excludes. The reasoning should be precise enough that someone familiar with the problem—that a belief might be correct yet easily false in a nearby situation, lacking the stability that distinguishes knowledge—recognizes the structure, but do not cite Plato, name any dialogue, or include references. Write so that a reader who has never encountered the problem can feel the tension through the form alone: the repetition should make the anchored belief feel different from the unanchored guess that never returns.
