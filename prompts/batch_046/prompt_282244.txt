In my research on pragmatism and the philosophy of language, I have been following the long stalemate between Rortian antirepresentationalists and their critics—between those who take Rorty to have dissolved the traditional picture of knowledge as accurate mental representation, and those who charge that this dissolution either collapses into self-refutation or tacitly reintroduces the representational relations it denies. After several decades, the exchange has become remarkably stable. The critic presses the self-refutation objection: if beliefs do not represent states of affairs, then the belief that beliefs do not represent fails itself; the Rortian replies that this begs the question by treating representational success as the only available norm; the critic then asks whether, on this account, there remains any non-arbitrary distinction between a belief that is merely useful and one that is genuinely correct; and the Rortian answers that correctness is a normative, practical achievement, not a metaphysical correspondence relation. The positions have not moved appreciably.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not, at bottom, about the ontology of mental states, but about what I will call the explanatory threshold for philosophical satisfaction—specifically, whether explaining first-order representational practices (ordinary judgments that things are thus-and-so) requires a second-order theory in which representational relations function as primitive explanatory posits. To test this framing, consider the following case. A cartographer abandons the idea that her map "mirrors" the terrain, treating cartography instead as a normative practice of coordinating actions for navigation, yet she retains a rigorous internal distinction between correct and incorrect coordinate assignments. Does she count as an antirepresentationalist? Rortians are inclined to say yes, because she has dropped the metaphysical mirror-image; critics are inclined to say no, because the coordinate distinction is representational all the way down. I suspect the debate stalls precisely because both sides read their own explanatory threshold into this case without defending it.

Given this, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on Rorty. Is the stalemate verbal—centered on an ambiguity in "representation" that conflates first-order judgment with second-order metaphysical explanation? Is it substantive but concealed, turning perhaps on an empirical claim about whether cognitive science needs representational posits to explain error-correction? Or are the parties answering different questions—one about the norms of justification, one about the ontology of belief—under a single heading? And if your diagnosis is that the disagreement concerns bedrock intuitions about what counts as an explanation, is there anything—a formal distinction, an empirical result from cognitive science, or a reconceptualization of the cartographer case—that could actually move it? Or should we expect a permanent impasse, and if so, why? Please ground your diagnosis in what each side actually argues, not in a survey of interpretations, and be explicit about what, if anything, would count as progress.
