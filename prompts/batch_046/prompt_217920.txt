For an upper-level seminar combining philosophy and formal methods — eighteen students, seventy-five minutes — design a participatory exercise that makes the instability of cooperation visceral rather than calculated. The conceptual target is not the standard prisoner's dilemma, but a less familiar configuration: interaction partners face a payoff structure that rewards mutual cooperation, yet each pair operates under a different, privately known repetition rule — some are in a one-shot encounter, others in an iterated chain of unknown length — and after each round, students observe only aggregate outcomes, not individual histories, then choose whether to retain their current strategy or copy one they observed succeeding. The aim is for participants to begin with an intuitive expectation that cooperative norms will stabilize, then discover through play that cooperative strategies are systematically displaced, not because anyone maximizes selfishly, but because the hidden heterogeneity in repetition rules and the noise in observational learning make defection look more successful than it is.

Provide explicit rules, pairing mechanics, point-based payoffs requiring no real money, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the session. The structure should require students to act — selecting strategies, observing outcomes, copying — before any conceptual framing is offered. In the debrief, map specific phases of play onto: the formal distinction between one-shot and iterated games; the evolutionary condition for invasion of a cooperative strategy; and the epistemic asymmetry that arises when beliefs about future interaction are private rather than common knowledge. Identify what will predictably malfunction in a first run — for instance, students attempting to negotiate pair-specific agreements, or misinterpreting the copying rule as direct retaliation — and suggest concrete fixes. Conclude with an honest assessment of which dynamic feature — true intergenerational selection, random mutation, or long-run equilibrium convergence — the classroom format necessarily omits, and how you would verbally supply that absence without undermining the experiential point.
