I have been working through an interpretation of Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness that focuses on a structural tension I think is underexplored. On the one hand, she argues that sustained intellectual study—particularly in physics and metaphysics—is essential to happiness because it dispels the prejudices and superstitions that generate anxiety. On the other hand, she maintains that certain illusions, especially those that sustain romantic love and social attachment, are not merely excusable but practically indispensable: without them, the emotional texture of life collapses. The standard commentary treats this as a pragmatic concession to human weakness, but I want to push a stronger reading: that du Châtelet is implicitly defending a form of domain-specific rationality in which the norms of belief-formation vary with the functional role a belief plays. In scientific and philosophical domains, belief should be governed by evidence and clarity; in the domain of intimate affective life, belief should be governed by its contribution to emotional coherence and relational stability. I call this a compartmentalized epistemology, and I use it to frame her defense of illusion not as an exception to rationality but as a rational allocation of cognitive resources across distinct spheres of life.

I am particularly interested in whether this reading is defensible or whether it over-systematizes her position. She does distinguish between harmful prejudices and beneficial illusions, but does she provide any principled boundary between them, or is the distinction ultimately ad hoc—protecting whatever false beliefs happen to make us happy? I also wonder whether importing the language of domain-specific rationality anachronistically imposes a contemporary philosophical framework onto an eighteenth-century text that was working within very different categories—Lockean psychology, Wolffian rationalism, and a court culture that treated love as a social art rather than an epistemic problem. Finally, I want to know whether this interpretation is philosophically interesting in its own right: does it offer a coherent middle path between strict rationalism and romantic anti-rationalism, or does it collapse into a permission structure for self-deception?

Could you comment on the accuracy of this interpretation relative to the arguments in her Discourse, on whether the compartmentalized epistemology framing illuminates her view or distorts it, and on whether the essay idea is worth developing? I am not looking for bibliographic suggestions or a literature survey—just an assessment of whether the conceptual architecture holds together and where its weakest joints are.
