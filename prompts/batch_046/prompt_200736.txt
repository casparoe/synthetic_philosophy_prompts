I've been thinking about Bongard problems and whether they actually prove anything about concept induction, and I came up with this chain that feels suspicious because it basically says neural nets can never genuinely learn. Can you check where it slips?

1. A standard Bongard set gives you twelwe boxes split left and right, each side obeying or breaking one hidden rule. The rule might be about shape count, line curvature, or position — there's no built-in limit on what counts as a valid concept.

2. If a learner gets the split right from just those examples, it has to project the concept onto unseen cases. But Goodman's old problem shows that any finite sample is compatible with endless bizarre rules, like "triangles present except on boxes shown after 3pm on Tuesdays."

3. So when a model solves standard Bongard puzzles, it isn't performing open-ended induction; it's relying on baked-in priors about geometry, number, and continuity that were already in its training data.

4. Now imagine a genuinely novel Bongard-style task where the rule depends on acoustic resonence patterns mapped onto visual shapes — something completely outside normal human visual priors. A standard net would fail here, because the prior isn't in its weights.

5. That means the only reason nets do well on ordinary Bongard problems is hidden human bias in the data. They haven't solved induction; they've just mimick'd a human bias that happens to cover the standard puzzle space.

6. Therefore Bongard problems don't demonstrate real concept induction in machines. They just show that machines can reproduce human-like categorisation when the category is already inside the distribution they were trained on.

I think step 4 is where the sleight of hand is — I'm assuming that "novel" means "outside human priors" and that failure there disproves success elsewhere — but I can't quite say why that feels wrong.
