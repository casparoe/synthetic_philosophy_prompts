Here is my current package of commitments on prediction-sensitive choice and superrationality, and I want a consistency audit before I present it. (i) In Newcomb's problem I one-box: the evidential correlation with the predictor's past filling of the boxes is rationally decisive, even though my present act does not causally affect them. (ii) In the counterfactual mugging—where a predictor has already formed a belief about whether I will pay, and an independent random event determines whether I am actually confronted with the request—I think that once I observe the event has resolved in my favor (no request was made), I should not be disposed to pay in the counterfactual branch; the past is fixed and my present choice is decoupled. (iii) In Parfit's hitchhiker I think I should pay the driver after being dropped off, because my present willingness is part of the counterfactual profile that explains why I was picked up in the first place. (iv) In the Platonia dilemma—two causally isolated, spatially separated agents who are perfect psychological duplicates and must each choose for both—I think each should defect; I reject superrationality here because there is no legitimate evidential link between the twins' choices. (v) In the Luring Lottery—where a near-perfect predictor has arranged that one specific ticket is the one my psychology makes me most likely to select, that ticket is predetermined to lose, and the others win—I think I should deliberately avoid that specific ticket once I recognize the pattern, even though this looks structurally parallel to one-boxing. (vi) I endorse a principle of "counterfactual anchoring": evidential decision-theoretic reasoning is legitimate only when the correlation between act and outcome is anchored in a counterfactual dependency of some past state on my present dispositions, and it is illegitimate when anchored solely in a predictor's belief that is causally insulated from my present choice and about which I have direct epistemic access. (vii) I hold that rational deliberation should be invariant to the temporal order in which a problem is presented and to incidental framing differences.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, ordering them by severity and specifying, for each, what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Identify at least one pair that looks tense but actually dissolves on inspection, explaining exactly how the distinction in (vi) or another commitment resolves it. Then tell me which single commitment, if revised or dropped, would clean up the largest number of tensions. Be specific about whether (ii) and (iii) can both stand under (vi), whether (v) is compatible with (vi) given that in the Luring Lottery I do have direct knowledge of the predictor's specific arrangement, and whether (i) and (iv) are really in conflict or only apparently so. Do not just list tensions; rank them and defend the ranking.
