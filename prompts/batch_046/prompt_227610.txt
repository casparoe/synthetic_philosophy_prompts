I'm preparing a ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary reading group that includes a cognitive scientist, a physicist, and a philosophy graduate student who keeps insisting that transcendental arguments are merely disguised causal explanations. We have been working through Kant's theoretical philosophy, but the group consistently treats "transcendental" as either an intensifier meaning "profoundly important" or as a claim about mystical access to things-in-themselves, and they assume the argument in the Critique of Pure Reason must be attempting to derive the laws of nature from pure introspection. I want to teach the actual logical form—asking what must already hold for a specific kind of experience to be possible—without letting the discussion collapse into empirical psychology on one side or speculative metaphysics on the other.

Plan the explanatory arc: the dependency order in which the components must be introduced so that the distinction between a condition of possibility and a causal mechanism is firmly in place before any Kantian terminology is named; the one concrete carrying example—not the standard summary of time and space—that demonstrates this form in action and that must avoid both mental-image psychology and direct ontological inference; the misconceptions the group will predictably form at each step, especially conflating necessary conditions with causal origins and treating the conclusion as a world-discovery rather than as a claim about the structure of experience, together with the precise point in the sequence where each should be intercepted; one check-for-understanding question that reveals whether they have grasped the argument's architecture rather than memorized its conclusion; and the specific portion of the standard transcendental-deduction apparatus I should explicitly bracket and refuse to open at this level. Deliver this as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
