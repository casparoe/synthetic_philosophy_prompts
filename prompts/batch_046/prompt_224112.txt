I am designing a ninety-minute workshop for an upper-level undergraduate ethics seminar with approximately ten students, and I want an in-class activity that lets them physically encounter the possibility of genuine moral dilemmas—situations in which two obligations are not merely competing but structurally irreducible, leaving unavoidable moral remainder regardless of choice. Rather than using familiar trolley-style scenarios, please construct a simulation centered on what I will call a dual-commitment board: each student receives two tangible tokens representing duties that are explicitly non-commensurable—say, a promise-keeping obligation and a harm-prevention obligation—where the board is designed with no vertical ranking axis, so there is literally no spatial position that places one duty above the other without destroying the corresponding token. The activity should proceed through three rounds—first an individual timed allocation, then a paired negotiation in which partners hold incompatible token sets and must jointly place them on a shared board with insufficient slots, and finally a collective round in which the whole group must distribute all accumulated tokens across a communal surface that physically cannot hold them all—while requiring every participant to retain any unchosen token in a visible residue pocket that carries over between rounds, so that rejected obligations remain materially present. The rules and material constraints should generate the tension themselves, not through facilitator explanation, so that students feel the pressure of irreducible conflict and the weight of moral residue before any philosophical terminology is introduced. Please include a debrief that maps each physical moment—the residue pocket, the absence of hierarchy on the board, and the collective scarcity—onto the philosophical debate between those who treat all dilemmas as apparent conflicts resolvable by better reasoning and those who defend genuine metaphysical dilemmas, and be explicit about which of these theatrical elements actually tracks the philosophical claim and which merely dramatizes it; also describe what will predictably fail, such as students inventing implicit trade-off weights or resolving the tension by appealing to an unstated hierarchy, and how to handle that in the moment. I need you to separate what is settled in this debate—for instance, that apparent moral conflicts are ubiquitous and that major normative theories typically deny the existence of genuine dilemmas—from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers, particularly whether experienced moral residue demonstrates a metaphysical conflict between obligations or only an epistemic limitation in identifying the correct resolution, and please do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
