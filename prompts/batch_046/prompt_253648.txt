I'm working through Descartes' Meditations for a course on early modern philosophy, and I'm stuck on the move in Meditation II from the recognition that I exist as long as I think, to the claim that I am essentially a thinking thing and that I know this more clearly than anything bodily. The passage seems to treat the occurrence of thought as sufficient to establish not just that some thinking is happening, but that I am a substance whose essence is thought; yet it also relies on a kind of first-person transparency—knowing that I think whenever I do—that may not apply to any system lacking immediate self-access, including perhaps an artificial information-processor. Could you help me understand exactly what the cogito is meant to prove? Does it establish substantial existence, mere occurrence of a mental event, or only the invulnerability of present-tense first-person judgments to doubt? And does the step from thinking to being a thinking thing import an assumption about self-knowledge that the skeptical argument hasn't yet earned?
