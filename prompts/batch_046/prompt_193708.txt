I spent my career in field ecology before retiring, and lately I have been reading philosophy of mind and ethics. A colleague recently summarized for me the debate about insect sentience and what he terms the scale argument: because there may be ten quintillion insects alive at once, even a one-in-a-billion chance that any given insect is sentient yields an enormous expected number of sentient beings, so moral concern for insects should be comparable to concern for vertebrates. He said critics reply that this reasoning commits a distributive fallacy of phenomenal weighting—treating sentience like a physical quantity that can be summed across individuals—whereas sentience is an intrinsic, all-or-nothing property of a unified subject. From this, he concluded that aggregation is conceptually illegitimate, and therefore we should reject the low-probability premise altogether and deny insects any moral status absent direct vertebrate-like neural homology. This sounds suspiciously neat to me. Is this a fair summary of the actual philosophical dispute? Please distinguish: which parts correctly describe the scale argument as used in animal-ethics discussions; where does the summary conflate epistemic risk aggregation with metaphysical property aggregation; is the distributive fallacy a recognized objection or a mischaracterization; and does his conclusion—that we must reject the premise—actually follow, or does it confuse the failure of one aggregation model with the failure of all probabilistic reasoning about sentience?
