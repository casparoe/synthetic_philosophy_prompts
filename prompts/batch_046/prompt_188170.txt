I'm drafting the final essay examination for an upper-level seminar on Augustine and the ethics of language. I need a single 90-minute question that students cannot answer by summarizing De mendacio and Contra mendacium alone. Instead, the prompt should require them to reconstruct Augustine's argument that intentional falsehood violates the very purpose of language—because words are instituted to convey the contents of the mind—and then to ask whether that argument depends on the semiotic theory he lays out in De doctrina Christiana, where signs serve truthful disclosure in service of the love of God and neighbor. The question should then force students to test that semiotic foundation against modern speech-act taxonomy: if Austin distinguishes locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary dimensions, can an interlocutor consistently accept that taxonomy while still endorsing Augustine's claim that every intentional falsehood involves a morally corrupt divided heart? Or does the modern distinction show that Augustine confuses the failure to state a fact with the failure to perform an honest speech act?

Please deliver:
1. The exam question, framed for students who have read all three texts but must synthesize rather than recite.
2. A model answer of about 600 words that demonstrates the required synthesis, including the specific tension between Augustine's sign-theory and speech-act pluralism.
3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves, totaling 20 marks, with each criterion assigned a specific point value and a one-line description of the characteristic weak answer that fails it.
