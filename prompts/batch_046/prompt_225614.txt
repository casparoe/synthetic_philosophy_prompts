I'm trying to apply Nietzsche's genealogy of morality to concrete stances, using a distinction I think matters but haven't formalized: "moral latency," the gap between an instinctive reaction and the moment it gets framed as a moral value. Here are my gut verdicts.

A warrior culture celebrates killing enemies in battle immediately, with no talk of duty, guilt, or universal rule; the pleasure is in the act itself. Verdict: noble morality, essentially zero latency.

A religious ascetic suppresses pleasure, then later frames that suppression as a virtue that makes him superior to indulgers. Verdict: slave morality driven by ressentiment, high latency.

A modern philanthropist gives away nearly all wealth, explaining it as repayment of an unearned moral debt to humanity. Verdict: the framing feels reactive and slave-like, but the directness of the act feels noble; I keep going back and forth.

A social critic condemns the wealthy for greed, yet privately admits he envies their freedom and wants the same status without guilt. Verdict: clearly slave morality, reactive and high-latency.

A leader brutally disciplines weak followers, not to improve them, but because their weakness disgusts him and he wants to purge it from his group. Verdict: I can't settle it — reactive in targeting weakness, yet apparently affirmative in its cruelty and lack of moral justification.

Give me a committed answer in American English: state one precise principle (or a small set of candidates) that captures A through D, resolves C and E definitively, and says exactly what it predicts for F — a person who lives in complete indifference, neither affirming strength nor condemning weakness, avoiding moral judgment altogether. For each clause of your principle, name the case that motivates it. Be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle saves and which it overturns, and do not hedge by leaving the choice to me.
