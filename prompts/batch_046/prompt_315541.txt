I am revising the syllabus for a graduate seminar in democratic theory, and in drafting a module on institutional innovation I have become preoccupied with a hybrid legislative design that is rarely implemented but increasingly discussed in theoretical literature. The proposal divides a legislature into two chambers: one filled by election and one filled by random sortition, with an explicit functional separation. The elected chamber retains exclusive initiative power over all fiscal and distributive policy, while the sortition chamber possesses only a veto power over any measure that would alter the basic structure of rights, lacking any capacity to propose legislation itself. Defenders justify this bifurcation by arguing that election confers a distinctive form of authorization that random selection cannot replicate, whereas sortition produces a form of descriptive representation that is epistemically valuable precisely because it eliminates the strategic incentives of campaigning. Critics respond that the arrangement treats democratic legitimacy as composed of two incommensurable goods—authorization and mirror representation—without explaining why one selection mechanism should monopolize initiative while the other monopolizes constraint, and they ask whether the same functional division could not be achieved through internal rules within a single chamber.

What philosophical commitments about representation, legitimacy, and political equality does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit normative theory: must one accept that authorization and descriptive representation are genuinely distinct goods that cannot be jointly optimized by a single mechanism? Does the design commit to a particular conception of equality—perhaps distinguishing equality of opportunity to govern from equality of ex ante influence—or is it compatible with several rival accounts? Examine whether the veto function assigned to the sortition chamber requires an epistemic theory of democracy, specifically the claim that randomly selected citizens possess a form of practical knowledge that elected representatives systematically lack, or whether that function can be justified on purely procedural or fairness-based grounds without any epistemic commitment. Finally, consider what would become redundant or incoherent in this two-chamber structure if one replaced its underlying theory with a straightforwardly aggregative conception of democratic legitimacy, according to which the only relevant consideration is whether policy outcomes reflect the informed preferences of the affected population. Which features could be eliminated without loss, and which, if any, remain indispensable?
