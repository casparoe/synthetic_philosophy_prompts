I'm trying to understand what civil marriage law actually thinks marriage is for. Here's the practice that puzzles me: you apply for a license, they check your age and that your not already married, but they dont ask if you plan to have kids, be sexually exclusive, share money, or even live together. Same-sex couples do the exact same paperwork. Yet when people defend marriage against plural marriage or domestic partnerships, they talk about it being a "unique" bond with an "essential nature." Meanwhile divorce courts still treat cheating as a special breach, and in some states the marital presumption of parentage gets applied to a lesbian spouse who had nothing to do with conception—no adoption, just the marriage certificate. So what theory of marriage does all that presuppose? Is it that marriage is basically a romantic commitment the state recognizes? Or an economic unit? Or is the practice just philosophically incoherent—presupposing one thing in licensing, another in divorce, and another in parentage? And if same-sex marriage didnt break anything, does that mean the procreation story was always just post-hoc? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, say where it's consistent or not, and tell me what a marriage law built on a different view—say, pure contract—would actually have to change.
