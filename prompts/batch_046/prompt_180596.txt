Hey, I'm on the grant review panel for the Riverton Community Wellness Board—I'm mostly a novelist, but they roped me in after I wrote that quiet novel about people trying to live with less noise. We're deadlocked on the fall funding vote, and I need to circulate a memo before Tuesday's meeting. One proposal is the "Ecstasy Lab," a residency that runs intense peak-experience workshops—guided breathwork, loud music, movement—to build what they call "positive affect capital," basically stockpiling joy and awe. The other is the "Stillwater Initiative," which teaches radical craving-cessation: digital fasting, desire-mapping, and sitting with discomfort until the wanting just... dissolves. Their whole framework is tranquilism—only the absence of craving and suffering actually matters, and pleasure is just a distracting flicker that doesn't count on the ledger.

I was talking with a friend who's in a philosophy course, and she said tranquilism sounds kind of logical if you think about it—like, why chase highs if the real win is just not hurting?—but also maybe too bleak for a wellness board, since it might mean never funding anything that feels good. Some of our board members think Stillwater is nihilistic in disguise; others think Ecstasy Lab is just manufacturing addiction to peaks. I want the memo to recomend one program with concrete criteria the board can reuse—maybe measuring "negative desire debt" versus "hedonic uplift"—and it needs to take the tranquilist position seriously, not just dismiss it as depressing, but also be honest about what's weak about it, especially for people who think art and joy are part of mental health. Please write it in American English, keep the tone casual since this isn't a law review, and use the standard spelling of tranquilism—I've been writing "tranquillism" with two l's in my notes and I'm pretty sure that's wrong.
