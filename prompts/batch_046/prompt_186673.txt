I recently encountered a summary of the gender-equality paradox—the observation that nations with stronger gender-equality norms and policies frequently exhibit larger gender differences in occupational choice, especially between STEM fields and care-oriented professions. The author links the pattern to a well-known study from the late 2010s, and asserts that the divergence is robust for preference-based measures such as things-versus-people orientation, yet largely disappears for leadership and managerial roles, which they interpret as evidence that the driver is revealed preference rather than structural discrimination. They propose that high economic security and institutional trust in egalitarian societies reduce coercive pressure, allowing people to sort according to intrinsic dispositions that are somewhat gender-differentiated, whereas in less egalitarian contexts economic necessity suppresses this sorting. From this descriptive account they derive a normative claim: justice should aim for equality of opportunity rather than equality of outcome, because enforcing outcome parity in career distribution would mean overriding genuinely revealed preferences. The summary also frames the mechanism through a borrowed economic concept of preference unmasking, treating culture mainly as a uniform suppressor of true preferences rather than as a differential shaper of them. I find the leap from correlation to causation regarding institutional trust suspicious, and I doubt the leadership-role exception is as clean as presented, but I cannot tell whether these are fatal flaws or just standard simplifications.
