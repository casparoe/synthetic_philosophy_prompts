As a software engineer working on machine-learning systems, I have become interested in the philosophical assumptions behind a design pattern we use constantly: training agents through reinforcement learning from human feedback, where the system optimizes for predicted annotator approval rather than for an explicitly defined task objective. In practice, we treat the agent as approval-directed—its effective "goal" is reconstructed from the distribution of human ratings—rather than goal-directed in the sense of pursuing a pre-specified outcome like minimal error on a formal specification. I would like to understand what philosophical commitments this institutional practice presupposes.

Specifically: what theory of value or practical reason does this pattern implicitly commit to? Does it assume that human evaluators possess a kind of direct, transparent access to the normative properties that make an output correct or good—something like an epistemic privilege of first-person preference? Or can it be reconciled with the view that goals are inherently opaque, context-dependent, and only partially articulable? Furthermore, the practice sits in tension with standard software-engineering norms that emphasize explicit specifications, unit tests, and traceable requirements. Does that tension reveal an inconsistent set of commitments—perhaps a pragmatic instrumentalism about system behavior combined with a realist assumption that aggregated human approval tracks an independent standard of quality?

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory, or theories, that make this practice coherent. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with rival views of agency and value. Then tell me: if we took seriously the idea that an agent should be purely goal-directed—pursuing an externally defined objective with no mediating layer of reconstructed preference—what specific changes would be necessary not just in our training pipelines, but in how we conceptualize correctness, error, and accountability in the systems we build?
