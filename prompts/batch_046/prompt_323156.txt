Write a short story about a forensic auditor of reconstructed first-person experience logs—someone whose job is to distinguish Stalinesque revisions, in which false perceptual content is injected directly into the present stream so that no true intermediate state is ever experienced, from Orwellian revisions, in which an archived memory trace is retroactively overwritten after the fact, leaving the live experience intact but corrupting the record. Your protagonist should be investigating one ambiguous case from the collapse of a predictive-policing regime: a witness insists they saw a crimson alert flare shift to emerald precisely as a blast occurred, yet every sensor array logs the flare as emerald at both temporal positions, with no intermediate crimson state registered anywhere in the physical environment. The auditor must determine whether the witness's consciousness executed a color-phi-style fill-in—constructing an illusory transitional experience across a temporal gap that never existed in the stream—or whether an Orwellian edit was applied to the archival storage after the event, falsifying the record without altering the original lived moment. Do not resolve the ambiguity with a technical reveal; instead, structure the narrative around the protagonist's internal reasoning about the timing of conscious experience itself. Both interpretations should be defended with equal competence, and the story should close on the unsettling possibility that our ordinary intuition about when an experience occurs may depend entirely on which model of revision we implicitly assume. Keep the setting concrete—rooms, interfaces, physical fatigue—but let the philosophical tension drive the plot.
