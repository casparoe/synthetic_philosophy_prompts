Consider the institutional pairing of the Treaty of Versailles's war-guilt clause—which assigned collective responsibility for the conflict to Germany and its allies—and the subsequent historiographical practice, crystallized in the Fischer controversy, of asking whether Imperial Germany's leadership deliberately engineered the war. Both practices treat the nation-state as an appropriate subject of blame, yet contemporary international criminal law rejects collective state punishment in favor of strictly individual liability. What philosophical commitments about collective agency, diachronic national identity, and the conditions for moral blameworthiness do these practices jointly presuppose? Does assigning treaty-level guilt to a nation require treating it as a unitary moral agent, or can the practice be reconstructed as a symbolic or distributive burden placed on citizens? Does the Fischer debate's focus on elite intentional planning solve the problem of collective agency, or does it merely substitute the mental states of a small group for those of an enduring collective? And if we deny that states can be blameworthy in the same sense as persons, can we consistently retain either the historiographical framing or the treaty mechanism, or must both be abandoned?
