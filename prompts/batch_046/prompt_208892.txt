Read Kazuo Ishiguro’s Never Let Me Go through the philosophy of valid consent, concentrating on the three standard invalidating conditions: deception, coercion, and incompetence. The novel’s clones are raised at Hailsham to believe they are ordinary children, gradually learn they exist to provide organs, and never formally agree to this fate; they are also kept from knowing about deferrals until late, and from any conceptual framework that would let them treat refusal as a live option. Where does the novel show deception to be structural rather than personal—the guardians themselves are partly ignorant—and where does it suggest the clones lack not mere information but the normative concepts needed for refusal? Does the book ultimately claim that consent requires a genuine alternative, or does it present the clones’ gentle acceptance as a tragic but autonomous choice? Take the philosophical debate seriously—distinguish deception about facts from deception about norms, and ask whether coercion and incompetence collapse into one another here—while saying honestly where the novel resists the philosophical framework.
