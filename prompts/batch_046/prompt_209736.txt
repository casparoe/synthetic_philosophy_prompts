I'm presenting a reconstructed argument on Aquinas's conditions for legitimate resistance to tyranny in a philosophy seminar next week. My thesis is that Aquinas permits the removal of a tyrant not as an act of rebellion against a legitimate authority, but only when the ruler's systematic violation of the common good has caused a formal dissolution of political authority—what I call a decomposition of the ratio legis—such that the agent is no longer a ruler in the Thomistic metaphysical sense but a private usurper of violence, and the act is therefore an execution of an already-void authority rather than a breach of obedience. I further argue that this applies strictly to tyrants per usurpationem and, in a far more restricted form, to tyrants per exercitium only when no superior tribunal exists and the unitas ordinis of the community has collapsed into a state lacking the form of justice, at which point private agents recover a natural right of defence that is structurally parallel to, but metaphysically distinct from, Hobbesian self-preservation. I defend this reading against the standard collapse of Aquinas's two categories and against anachronistic liberal readings that treat the permission as a proto-right of revolution.

The audience will be a philosophy of law and political philosophy reading group comprising: four analytic political philosophers (mostly liberal contractarians, sceptical of natural law and teleological metaphysics, familiar with Rawls and Nozick but not with Thomistic jurisprudence); one historian of medieval philosophy who knows De Regno and the Summa closely and is hostile to readings that import modern state-failure concepts into Aquinas; and one legal theorist working on authority and institutional failure who will be sympathetic to structural analogies but will press on whether my framework yields determinable answers. There are no decision theorists or theologians present.
