Having spent my career in statistical mechanics and only recently turned to philosophy, I am struggling with a well-known passage in Pascal's Pensées in which he argues that justice without force is powerless, while force without justice is tyrannical, and that legitimate authority requires the two to be coupled—justice directing force, and force sustaining justice. I want to understand the logical form of this claim rather than its historical context. Is Pascal offering a descriptive thesis about how stable human institutions actually function, a normative thesis about the conditions for legitimate rule, or a theological thesis about post-lapsarian corruption that makes pure justice impossible? I am also trying to avoid importing my own disciplinary habits: in physics, coupled variables often form a well-posed system only when taken together, and I am tempted to read Pascal as saying that justice and force are conceptually incomplete without each other, analogous to a Hamiltonian lacking its boundary conditions. Is that analogy defensible, or does it misrepresent his argument? If the analogy is wrong, what is the proper framework—perhaps drawn from his own mathematics or from seventeenth-century jurisprudence—for understanding why he treats their union as necessary rather than merely contingent? I am not asking for a survey of interpretations; I want a precise diagnosis of the claim's structure and an honest assessment of whether a conceptual distinction drawn from the natural sciences can illuminate it without distortion.
