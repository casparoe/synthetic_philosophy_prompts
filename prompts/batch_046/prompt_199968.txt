I'm running a postgraduate philosophy workshop — about ten of us, 90 minutes, room with movable chairs and a whiteboard we never use properly. We've been having this arguement over whether deontology collapses once you admit threshold conditions, and I keep half-remembering something like the "structural paradox of permission" where forbidding harm to save others ends up forbidding you from stopping that same harm when it's about to happen through someone else. Might be a misremembering, but I want an in-room activity that makes us feel that circular bite rather than just diagramming it on the board.

Design a structured role-play with three escalating rounds and small token stakes — say, everyone starts with 5 chips and outcomes shift them, but the real pressure should be social and visible. Split us into "agents" (each gets a card with an absolute constraint: never use an innocent party as a means or redirect harm onto them) and "interveners" (who can act but risk becoming direct agents). Round one: one victim, one potential saver — straightforward. Round two: five victims, but saving them requires redirecting harm onto one bystander who wasn't in danger — the classic tension. Round three is where the paradox should hit: the harm is propagating naturally (no agent caused it), but stopping it requires positioning the innocent bystander in the harm's path as a shield, which isn't "redirecting" in the usual sense but still uses them. The rules should be written so that by round three the agents realise their constraint blocks them from preventing exactly the harm it was meant to prohibit, and the interveners discover that "indirect" help still makes them complicit. Don't name the paradox until the debrief — we should hit the wall ourselves around minute 50, probably arguing about whether shielding counts as using.

Then map each round onto the logical structure: where the agent-relative restriction applies, where doing/allowing breaks down under pressure, and what it costs to hold the line at each stage. Be frank about what this can't reproduce — probably the counterfactual reasoning about moral status that makes the philosophical version genuinely puzzling, not just emotionally awkward — and tell me what will predictably go wrong (people ignoring their card-rules once chips are at stake, or arguing that "shielding" isn't "using"). Take a committed position in the design: pick either a rights-based or threshold-based version of the paradox and build the rules around it, don't offer a menu.
