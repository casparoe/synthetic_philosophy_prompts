I chair the biannual renewal committee for our university’s interdisciplinary research clusters—groups of three to five labs that share a core theoretical framework and apply for institutional funding every two years. We are not peer reviewers judging individual papers; we are deciding whether a collective research programme is still generating genuine empirical progress or has begun protecting its central commitments by endlessly adjusting auxiliary hypotheses. I have become convinced that Imre Lakatos’s framework distinguishing progressive problem-shifts from degenerating ones captures exactly what we need to assess, but the philosophical formulation is not operational for a committee that includes non-philosophers and must reach a decision in about two hours per cluster using only the renewal dossier: pre-registered predictions, published results, revised models, and a brief narrative from the cluster director. Please translate that framework into a concrete, reusable decision procedure my committee can run repeatedly—ordered questions asked in sequence, with clear criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stopping rules that yield one of three recommendations (renew without conditions, renew with mandatory structural changes, or initiate phased withdrawal), and clearly marked points where judgment is unavoidable because the issue concerns institutional values rather than empirical trajectory. The procedure must handle the specific hard cases that arise in modern interdisciplinary science: when a failed prediction is addressed by refining measurement instruments rather than altering theory; when the cluster shifts its domain of application rather than its hard core; and when the protective belt of auxiliary assumptions grows so complex that it becomes practically unfalsifiable. Then apply the procedure to three clusters. Cluster Alpha (neuroeconomics) holds as its hard core that reward-prediction errors drive learning; after three consecutive failed replications of a key behavioral effect, it introduced a hierarchical contextual-modulation layer that accommodates the failures but has not yet yielded a novel, risky empirical prediction of its own. Cluster Beta (synthetic ecology) is built around the claim that ecosystem resilience collapses discontinuously past specific connectivity thresholds; over the past funding cycle it correctly predicted two such collapses in controlled mesocosm studies and refined one mis-specified parameter, leaving the discontinuity thesis untouched. Cluster Gamma (cognitive architecture) is a large, well-funded group centered on predictive-processing models of perception; it has absorbed failures in studies of visual illusions, working-memory capacity limits, and interoceptive signaling by expanding the precision-weighting hierarchy and adding region-specific priors, producing an increasingly flexible architecture that now accommodates nearly any observed result. Run your procedure on all three, including the case—almost certainly Gamma—where it recommends a course of action that the committee will be politically and intellectually reluctant to take, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot adjudicate, such as whether a cluster’s student-training record or public outreach value should override its empirical stagnation. Do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or references to specific texts; I need a practical tool, not a literature review.
