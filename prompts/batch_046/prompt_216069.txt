Write a formal dialogue between two equally sophisticated political philosophers—Dr. Kessler and Dr. Voss—on whether democratic representation functions as a moral shield or a moral conduit in "dirty hands" politics. Both agree that officeholders sometimes must commit necessary wrongs (for instance, authorizing a targeted strike with foreseen civilian casualties to prevent a larger atrocity) for the collective good, but they sharply disagree about the distribution of moral residue. Kessler defends "delegated contamination": she argues that when citizens authorize a representative to act, the resulting moral taint does not stay confined to the officeholder but transmits back through the authorization chain in diluted form—what she calls "moral residue transmission." Without this transmission, she claims, democratic publics become reckless, demanding outcomes while outsourcing the stain. Voss advances an "institutional insulation" thesis: representation exists precisely to absorb moral dirt so that citizens remain clean, preserving the division of moral labor that makes democratic legitimacy possible. He warns that treating authorization as contamination collapses this division, turning voters into co-perpetrators and undermining the very justification for the wrong. Have them engage directly: Kessler should press Voss on whether a citizen who voted for the leader but later condemns the act is genuinely insulated, and Voss should challenge Kessler on whether "prospective authorization" can really be separated from "retrospective endorsement" without collapsing into hypocrisy. Neither should be a strawman; each should answer the other's specific points. Present the dialogue with clear speaker labels (KESSLER / VOSS), write it in American English, and keep the entire exchange to no more than 800 words.
