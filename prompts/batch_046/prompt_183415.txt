I've noticed a recurring structure in Augustine's treatment of deception that I can't name. He condemns verbal lying absolutely -- even to save a life -- yet he permits non-verbal actions that mislead pursuers, and he treats scriptural figures that are literally false (prophetic symbols, parables) as spiritually truthful rather than deceptive. The pattern is that causing someone to hold a false belief seems to be forbidden or permitted not mainly by the speaker's intent or by harm done, but by whether the falsehood occurs inside an authoritative interpretive framework -- a biblical genre, a prophetic convention -- where non-literal meaning is institutionally established. Outside that framework, the same false statement is a lie; inside it, it's virtue or necessity.

Does this specific boundary-drawing have an established name in Augustine scholarship or in philosophy of language? Or is it just an instance of mental reservation, double effect, or hermeneutic charity? If there is no precise concept, how should we delineate it: does it depend on the listener's membership in the interpreting community, on the speaker's institutional role, or on something else? And please say plainly if my description rests on a misreading of Augustine, or if the distinction I'm drawing collapses into something simpler.
