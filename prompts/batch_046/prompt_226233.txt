I am a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I have been trying to understand William James's will to believe against W. K. Clifford's evidentialism. I sketched out the following argument, but it now strikes me as suspicious because the conclusion seems to prove too much: it makes Clifford's principle not just demanding but practically self-defeating. (1) James permits belief without sufficient evidence only when the option is genuinely live, forced, and momentous. (2) Every decision to deploy software or to halt it is live for the team, forced by deadlines and user needs, and momentous because failures carry real harm. (3) The belief that this build is safe and its negation both lack exhaustive evidence, since unknown dependencies and untested edge cases always remain, yet the team must adopt one stance in order to act. (4) Therefore James allows the engineer to choose either belief based on practical necessity. (5) Clifford demands that belief always be strictly proportioned to available evidence, so with incomplete verification one must suspend judgment about safety. (6) But suspending judgment is itself a cognitive stance that determines action—namely, withholding deployment—so Clifford does not escape belief-choice; he merely privileges the belief that suspension is correct. (7) Applying Clifford consistently, we should also suspend judgment about whether suspension is correct, generating an infinite regress of meta-suspension that paralyzes all action. (8) Thus Clifford's principle either collapses into James-style practical choice or renders every practical judgment, including ordinary engineering, epistemically illegitimate. I find both horns unacceptable, and if forced to choose I would rather keep (1) and (4) than accept (8). Where does this argument go wrong?
