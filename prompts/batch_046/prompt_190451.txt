Having retired from a career in experimental physics and only recently turned my attention to philosophy of language, I have been working through Frege's puzzle about identity statements and have constructed an argument that derives an implausible consequence from premises I would prefer not to abandon. (1) Frege's resolution holds that an identity statement "a = b" carries cognitive value only when "a" and "b" present the same referent through different senses—that is, through distinct modes of presentation—whereas "a = a" is trivial because both terms share exactly one sense. (2) From this it seems to follow that the informativeness of any identity claim is strictly proportional to the divergence between the senses of its terms. (3) In scientific modeling, however, we routinely assign different notational symbols—say, θ in one formalism and φ in another—to a single underlying parameter, or represent an identical physical state through different coordinate frames. (4) By (2), recognizing that θ denotes the very same constant as φ, or that a state-vector transforms identically between frames, ought to possess the same substantive cognitive weight as the astronomical discovery that Hesperus is Phosphorus. (5) The reasoning extends to measurement conventions: since "0°C" and "273.15 K" invoke distinct calculational procedures, their equivalence should be equally non-trivial in Frege's sense. (6) Pushed further, because I represent myself at different times through different descriptive associations, memories, and temporal perspectives, the diachronic claim that I this morning am identical to I this evening should stand in need of empirical justification rather than being treated as a default presumption. (7) Yet in practice we regard parameter identifications, unit conversions, and self-identity over time as routine or unproblematic, not as discoveries that might overturn our theories. (8) Hence either (2) illegitimately universalizes Frege's local insight about proper names, or our scientific and personal practice systematically suppresses genuinely informative identity claims. I am inclined to preserve (1), but I am unsure whether the fault lies in (2)'s overgeneralization, in (4)'s application to theoretical representations, or in some hidden slide between cognitive significance and epistemic surprise. Which step should I reject, and what philosophical price does that rejection exact?
