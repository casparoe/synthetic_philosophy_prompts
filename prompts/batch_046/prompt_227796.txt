I sit on our city's heritage commitee and we're trying to be consistent about monuments and names, but our track record feels kind of scattered. So far we've pulled down that bronze of the colonial governor who ordered the 1923 massacre, even though the sculptor was well-known; kept the street named after Dr. Voss, who ran those eugenics studies in the 40s but also designed the vaccine protocol, except we added a plaque saying he was dead wrong about race science; left the 1931 labor strike monument standing after we learned the union leader embezzled the strike fund, on the grounds that it honors the workers, not him; renamed the high school that carried Senator Reed's name after it came out he voted for segregation, but only once a public vote came back 58% in favor; and we kept the plaza fountain that was paid for by the convict-labor cotton mill, except we built that shadow wall listing the workers' names right beside it.

Now there's a proposal about Hawthorne Bridge—named after Elias Hawthorne, the nature poet they still teach in every highschool, except he was active in that fascist militia in the 30s and apparently used his book royalties to buy them rifles. The bridge itself was partly built with a donation he made in 1938. One camp wants to rename it after the indigenous activist Marisol Tene; another wants to keep the name but install a counter-plaque; and there's this odd argument that because people mostly think of the bridge as a view, the name is descriptive rather than honorific. I'm trying to figure out what we'd do if we actually care about consistency—what's the closest analog in our past, is it the scientist-with-plaque case or the labor-monument case? I also wonder if we're running a "moral taint of the physical substrate" rule, where it matters that the bridge was literally built with fascist-funded money, versus just a "who are we honoring" rule. If our precedents genuinely don't settle it, what principle do we adopt by renaming, and what do we adopt by keeping with a plaque? And what hypothetical case would split those rules—say, if the bridge had been named after him but built with clean public funds, or if his poetry had never touched politics at all?
