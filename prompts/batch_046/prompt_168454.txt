I was arguing with my friends last night about this old physics puzzle called Maxwell's demon — the imaginary little guy who sorts fast and slow molecules through a trapdoor and seems to break the second law of thermodynamics. Every youtube video and textbook I find says the paradox is solved because eventually the demon has to erase what it learned about each molecule, and that erasing information literally costs energy and makes heat. So now in chip design and computing they treat deleting data as a real physical event that warms things up, not just a metaphor.

But I'm stuck on what that practice actually assumes about information itself. When physicists say the demon's memory is part of the system and that forgetting saves the law, are they presupposing that knowing something is physically seperate from not knowing it — like ignorance is the natural state of the universe, but knowledge is this special thermodynamic burden you have to pay for? Does that mean information must be made of physical stuff, or could it still be abstract and the principle just applies to the brain or machine doing the knowing? And here's the part that confuses me: if knowing vs not-knowing really has different physical costs, wouldn't that imply that any system that remembers things — maybe even a human mind — is automatically governed by heat laws in some direct way?

If we dropped that commitment and said information is just abstract patterns with no physical weight, what would have to change? Would that erasure principle still apply to computers, or would we need to rethink how heat works in data centers? I don't need a reading list, I'm just trying to figure out if there's a hidden philosophy behind why physics treats forgetting as the thing that rescues thermodynamics, and whether that view actually holds together or sneaks in ideas about consciousness being physical.
