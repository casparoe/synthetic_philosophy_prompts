I am preparing a graduate workshop on conceptual variation and want to test whether Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model—treating a theme as a structure with adjustable parameters like tempo, scale, or instrumentation—can be mapped onto philosophical argumentation rather than its usual domains in music or geometry. To avoid relying on familiar examples, I have defined five invented "philosophical knobs" that one might twist when constructing a thought experiment about distributive justice: (1) the granularity of moral agency (individual versus collective versus institutional), (2) the reversibility of the allocation mechanism, (3) the epistemic transparency of the decision procedure, (4) the temporal horizon over which outcomes are evaluated, and (5) the emotional salience of the goods being distributed. Rank these five knobs from the most creatively fertile (the twist that most reliably yields a genuinely new philosophical insight rather than a familiar variant) to the least fertile (the twist that most often collapses into incoherence or restates an existing position). Present the result as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, stating the knob, its rank, and the specific reason it lands there; include no introduction or conclusion.
