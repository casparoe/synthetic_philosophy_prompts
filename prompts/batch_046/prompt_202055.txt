I cover philosophy and science for a general audience, and I keep encountering an argumentative structure I cannot quite place. A person is blamed for some action; critics concede that at the decisive moment there was genuinely no accessible alternative—the agent’s psychology and circumstances made the choice unavoidable. Rather than defending the idea that blame requires real options, the response shifts to the origin of that inevitability: it was produced by the agent’s own prior character, commitments, or earlier choices, so the absence of alternatives is treated as self-authored rather than exculpatory. The constraint is internal, not an external manipulator. Does this exact dialectical move—sidestepping the alternative-possibilities condition by tracing unavoidable action back to the agent’s own formed self—have an established name or conceptual boundary? How does it relate to Frankfurt-style counterexamples and to source-based accounts of responsibility, and under what conditions does it actually preserve blame? Please begin with your bottom-line verdict—whether this is a recognized pattern with a specific label, and where its edges lie—then lay out the reasoning with no preamble and no restatement of the question. In doing so, separate what is generally settled from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed view as if it were the consensus.
