I've been convening a small philosophy-of-science reading group, and over the past semester we've kept returning to what I now call inversion arguments — cases where a system achieves an apparently intelligent or well-adapted outcome without anything like understanding, foresight, or intentional design guiding the process. The canonical pair is Darwin's mechanism of natural selection, which produces exquisitely adapted organisms without any comprehending designer selecting for fitness, and Turing's formalization of computation, which yields correct symbolic outputs without the machine grasping their meaning or broader context.

Recently our discussions have stalled into two entrenched camps. One side treats these inversions as strictly domain-local: they show that particular mechanisms — variation with selective retention, or syntactic rule-following — can substitute for comprehension within specific boundaries, but they do not undermine the broader claim that genuinely normative, representational, or flexibly responsive achievements still require a comprehending agent somewhere in the causal chain. The opposing side treats the inversions as revealing a general structural principle: whenever a system displays competence, the comprehension we post-hoc attribute is either causally redundant or a retrospective narrative projection, and Darwin together with Turing demonstrates that comprehension never actually does explanatory work. We've replayed the same moves — evolutionary arms races, chess-playing algorithms, the syntax-semantics distinction, whether error-correction requires an internal model — and nobody has shifted position.

I am not asking for an adjudication between these camps. Instead, I want a structural diagnosis of the stall itself. Is the disagreement hidden behind an ambiguity in competence — does it require normativity, context-sensitivity, or open-ended flexibility — or behind incompatible uses of comprehension, perhaps sliding between phenomenal consciousness, functional understanding, and rational accessibility? Or are we facing bedrock intuitions about explanatory completeness — one side finding the omission of comprehension intolerably incomplete, the other finding its inclusion intolerably mysterious — that neither side can argue for? Given whatever diagnosis you offer, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a clarifying distinction, a boundary test case, a reconceptualization of explanation, or an honest argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
