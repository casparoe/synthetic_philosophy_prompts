I recently led a philosophy-of-logic seminar discussion on determinacy arguments and realized that my students could reproduce the strategy-stealing proof for Hex without feeling any conceptual tension between “there exists a winning strategy” and “no agent can say what it is.” I want to design a 75-minute workshop for 14 graduate students in logic and philosophy of science that makes them inhabit that tension physically rather than following it on paper. The room has tables for trios; the time is one continuous block.

Design a runnable classroom game—call it Shadow Placement—played on a small symmetric board I will describe below, plus roles, a point-based payoff scheme with no real money, and a structured debrief. The participants should run into the strategy-stealing assumption themselves through the rules and roles before anyone names it.

Specify the board and rules: a 4-by-4 grid of cells arranged with perfect left-right symmetry and a central “barrier” column that divides the board into two mirror-image halves; players take turns placing one token in any empty cell on either half, but placing in a cell that already has a mirror-image token placed by either player triggers an immediate loss for the player who just moved. By construction there are no draws—every full board assigns exactly one player the symmetric-breaking loss. Give the exact layout, the turn order, and a brief explanation of why draws are impossible that the facilitator can supply if asked but participants should not be given up front.

Structure the 75 minutes: about 15 minutes of paired play across three rounds with rotating partners; 20 minutes in assigned trios drafting explicit conditional strategy cards (“If opponent plays A, I play B”) for both first and second player; 15 minutes for the “stealing” exercise, in which the trio assigned to defend the second player receives a scripted argument that lets them literally copy the first player’s card deck, make a dummy opening move, and then adopt the stolen plan—reward points to the trio that first locates the internal contradiction in the script rather than to the trio that “wins” the board game; and 25 minutes for the debrief.

Assign roles within each trio—Strategist, Skeptic, and Arbitrator—so that consensus does not collapse too quickly. The payoff should accumulate across rounds: one point for correctly predicting a round’s outcome, two points for identifying a flaw in an opponent’s conditional card, and three points for locating the contradiction in the stealing script. The rules should be arranged so that participants first experience the failure of explicit strategy construction, then feel the seductive force of the copying argument, and only then confront the fact that the proof yields no actual first move.

In the debrief, map each phase onto the formal argument: the dummy move corresponds to the wasted opening; the adoption of the opponent’s deck corresponds to the symmetry-breaking reply; the contradiction corresponds to the reductio that Player 2 cannot have had a winning strategy. Predict exactly what will go wrong the first time you run it: participants will almost certainly think the proof reveals Player 1’s specific winning opening move, and you must tell me precisely how to handle that misconception without lecturing—perhaps by asking them to write down that move and then testing it against the stolen deck. Be frank about which feature of the result the activity cannot reproduce: the topological or combinatorial guarantee that the board admits no draw, which the facilitator must state and justify since participants will not discover it through play alone.

Finally, inject one niche extension for the last 10 minutes: ask the trios whether the same stealing script would hold if the board were infinite and the win condition required reaching a limit point rather than filling a finite region, and have them identify exactly which step of the script—dummy move, adoption, or contradiction—breaks under that change. They do not need to solve the infinite case; they only need to locate the fracture point.
