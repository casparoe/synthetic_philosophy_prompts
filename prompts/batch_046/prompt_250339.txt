You are a philosopher of medicine and clinical ethics consultant with expertise in the anti-psychiatry tradition and institutional decision-making. Before writing anything, spend a few sentences brainstorming the core conceptual distinctions in the Szaszian framework that must survive translation into a practical tool—especially the difference between literal brain diseases and metaphorical “mental illnesses,” the role of value judgments in diagnosis, and the claim that involuntary psychiatric intervention functions as social control rather than medical therapy—so that the operational version does not silently collapse these into standard biomedical risk-management language.

Your client is a three-person clinical ethics panel at a regional psychiatric intake facility. The panel reviews about four proposals per month, has 45 minutes per case, includes one psychiatrist, one patient-rights advocate, and one non-specialist administrator, and must reach a unanimous signed recommendation. The facility has adopted a Szasz-informed rights framework: it treats psychiatric conditions as problems in living and moral conflicts rather than as literal diseases on par with tuberculosis, and it regards involuntary observation, medication, or restraint as coercive social measures that require extraordinary justification, not as routine medical treatments.

The facility now wants a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stop points with “no,” explicit hand-off points where judgment rather than fact must decide, and honest flags where the procedure will misfire—to evaluate proposals that deploy predictive behavioral-profiling algorithms in emergency psychiatric intake. These algorithms use speech-pattern analysis, mobility tracking, or social-media sentiment scoring to flag individuals for involuntary observation, to deny voluntary admission, or to trigger preemptive restraint, often without identifying any specific brain pathology.

Write the procedure as a flowchart in words that a non-philosopher panelist can run with a one-page worksheet. Then apply it to three proposals:

1. Deploying an NLP-based suicide-risk screener that automatically initiates an involuntary 72-hour hold when a patient’s intake interview language patterns match a “high-risk” cluster, even when no acute brain injury or toxicology finding is present.
2. Installing gait-analysis sensors in the waiting area to predict impending manic episodes; the system would trigger preemptive chemical restraint for flagged patients before they request help, based solely on kinetic-pattern deviation from a baseline.
3. A voluntary outpatient therapy program that uses predictive profiling to exclude applicants whose algorithmic “treatment-noncompliance risk score” exceeds a threshold, on the grounds that resources should be reserved for those likely to complete the protocol.

One of these applications must yield an answer the facility’s clinical director will strongly dislike, and you must explain why the procedure produces that result rather than smoothing it away. Also specify exactly what the procedure cannot settle—where values, institutional power, or empirical uncertainty must be acknowledged rather than disguised as a technical output—and note the hard cases in which the panel should refuse to render a recommendation because the framework itself is being asked to do work it was not designed for.

Keep the procedure free of bibliographies, citation requests, or appeals to texts the panel cannot access. Do not ask the model to quote specific passages from Szasz’s writings; paraphrase the relevant ideas without quotation marks unless you have fetched the exact wording from a source. Do not invoke obscure sub-theories or highly specialized empirical findings that a generalist panel or a model without web access could not reasonably be expected to know. Ground the distinctions in concepts widely associated with the Szaszian critique, and inject the algorithmic-profiling context as the niche practical domain that forces the abstract framework into contact with modern institutional technology.
