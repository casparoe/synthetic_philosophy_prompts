I've been retired from marine biology for about five years and lately I've been poking around philosophy of mind, mostly because I kept thinking about how some old ethology papers never quite answered the "why" behind animal behavior. I'm running a small reading group for retired scientists and grad students — about 14 people, 75 minutes, in a community room with tables we can rearrange. We just had a messy discussion about sphexishness — I keep writing "spheksishness" in my notes, that's my typo and I'm keeping it — and I want an in-class game that makes people feel the tension between mechanical rigidity and real flexibility in their bodies, not just talk about it.

The half-baked idea: participants are "archive recovery technicians" with an 8-step paper protocol for handling water-damaged notebooks after a simulated flood. They have to execute it in exact sequence, with checkmarks, and there's a payoff scheme in points for speed and accuracy — no real money, but points convert to coffee tokens or just bragging rights. Midway through, the conditions shift: notebooks are mislabeled, lighting changes, maybe the checklist itself becomes unreadable. Some people get hidden roles with incentives to stick to the protocol even when it's clearly useless, others have hidden incentives to improvise. I want them to hit the wall themselves before anyone names it "spheksishness" or free will. Frame the protocol not as a mental script but as an embodied algorithm with no representational content, like the feedback loops in an old tide gauge I used to maintain — rigid, environmental, no "understanding" needed.
