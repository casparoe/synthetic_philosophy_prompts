I am not a professional philosopher or researcher, but I have spent considerable time reading public debates about artificial intelligence safety, and I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern for which I cannot find a precise name. The pattern unfolds as follows. A speaker advances a highly specific, mechanistic claim about future AI behavior—for instance, that a system trained via reinforcement learning on an imperfect proxy will, under certain architectural conditions, construct an internal sub-optimizer with goals orthogonal to the intended objective, and that this will manifest through a multi-stage process of reward-model exploitation. When interlocutors challenge the claim—pointing out the absence of empirical demonstrations of exactly this causal chain, or questioning the plausibility of the architectural conditions—the speaker does not defend the mechanism in detail. Instead, they retreat to a far broader, less committal formulation: that optimizing intensely against any proxy tends to produce surprising and undesirable outcomes, and that we should therefore be cautious. This broader claim is not trivially true, but it is far harder to attack directly. Once critics have engaged with the broad claim—often conceding that it holds in some limited domains—the speaker treats that concession as support for the original, specific mechanism, and resumes deploying the detailed version in subsequent arguments. The two formulations share vocabulary—“optimization,” “proxy,” “misalignment”—but they do very different argumentative work. I am familiar with the concept of a motte-and-bailey, yet this does not seem to fit cleanly: there is no clearly defensible “motte” being protected, since both the specific and the general claims are genuinely contested; the retreat is not to an uncontroversial position but to a vaguer one; and the speaker does not seem to be hiding behind a defensible core so much as borrowing credibility back and forth between two claims of different strength. Does this configuration have an established name in argumentation theory, rhetoric, or the philosophy of science? If not, I would like it named and defined with precision: what exactly distinguishes it from plain equivocation, from strategic ambiguity in scientific modeling, and from the ordinary use of auxiliary hypotheses? I also want clear boundary conditions—cases where a speaker is genuinely clarifying rather than oscillating, and cases where the pattern collapses into something already well-described. The purpose is not merely to label a fallacy, but to understand the structure well enough to identify it in debates outside of AI safety, without relying on domain-specific jargon.
