Ok so I've been thinking about how different countries deal with being the "bad guy" in history, and there's this pattern that doesn't quite line up.

Germany funds these Stolpersteine — little brass plaques in the sidewalk outside houses where people were deported — plus big central memorials like the concrete-block field in Berlin. The state pays for it, puts it right in public space, and doesn't try to seperate "the perpetrators" from "the nation now." Meanwhile Japan issues formal cabinet apologies for wartime aggression, but then officials visit Yasukuni Shrine where Class A war criminals are enshrined in a seperate facility inside the same precinct, like the apology and the honor belong to two different worlds. Turkey doesn't just avoid talking about the Armenian genocide; it actively criminalizes public mention of it and treats any acknowledgment as an attack on Turkish identity right now. And in the American South you've got Confederate statues being pulled out of town squares and stuck in cemeteries or museum storage, with some state laws blocking removal and others allowing it — the framing shifts from "this is our civic identity" to "this is private grief we don't want in government space."

What philosophical theory of collective moral continuity — like, is the present German state literally the same moral agent that ran the camps, or a successor with different obligations — does each institutional setup actually presuppose? Or are they philosophically underdetermined? Could Germany's embedded sidewalk plaques work just as well under a strict discontinuity view? Does Japan's split between political apology and shrine ritual presuppose that moral responsibility and ritual honor can be cleanly partitioned? Is the South's relocation practice consistent with the idea that monuments express present collective values, or does it secretly presuppose something weirder about cleansing space of historical agency?

Reconstruct the implicit view for each case, say where the practice strictly needs it versus where rival theories could license the same design, and flag what's genuinely contested in transitional justice and political philosophy versus what's more settled. Don't present one theory of collective responsibility as the concensus if people who actually study this still disagree.
