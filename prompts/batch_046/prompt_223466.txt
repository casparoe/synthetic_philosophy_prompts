Me and three friends have been living in a loose co-op near the old rail yard for about three years now. We do gig work, split bills, and survive on a mix of grocery rescue and cash jobs — we probably work 20 hours a week total and the rest of the time we're building stuff out of scrap or just hanging out. It's genuinely good, or at least it feels good, though we have zero savings and if the weather gets bad we're eating instant noodles for a week.

Last month our roommate Marcus came back from some anthroplogy podcast binge and started pushing this idea that we should pool our money, lease a quarter-acre, and start a serious crop operation with assigned shifts and a storage shed. His argument is that our current affluence is fake — we're just outsourcing our survival to supply chains we don't control, and that real autonomy means producing our own calories even if it takes 50-hour weeks. He also says that once we have surplus stored up, we can specialize: one person fixes tools, one person cooks, etc. But my other roommate Lena says that's exactly how hierarchy starts — the person who controls the storage controls the group, and suddenly specialization means some people are pulling weeds while others decide what gets cooked. She also mentioned that in a lot of small-scale societies people actually work less hours than farmers do, which makes me wonder if Marcus is describing progress or just a more exhausting version of the same trap.

I'm torn. Part of me thinks Marcus is right that we're deluding ourselves with our free time, and that building a real food base is the only rational move if we want to be serious about living outside the system. But another part thinks Lena's pointing at something real about how stored surplus creates bosses, and that trading our leisure for security might be the worst deal ever made. Can you help me think through whether Marcus's plan is genuinely a step up, or whether there's a hidden structural problem in moving from loose foraging-style living to organized production?
