Read Groundhog Day as a philosophical thought experiment in karma understood strictly as moral desert—not as causal feedback or reincarnation, but as cosmological bookkeeping in which suffering and release are distributed in proportion to moral quality with no external judge. Phil Connors suffers repetition and eventually escapes after moral improvement, yet his initial arrogance, while unpleasant, hardly seems to warrant infinite temporal imprisonment. I want a reading that treats the loop seriously: do the scenes with the homeless man—where perfect foreknowledge still fails to prevent death—break the proportionality claim, or do they quietly shift karma from tracking outcomes to tracking intentions? Compare the suicide attempts with the final uncalculated kindness that breaks the loop: does the narrative treat release as earned reward, or as the causal psychological consequence of changed character? If the latter, has the film smuggled in a theory of moral development that actually undermines the desert claim it seems to dramatize? Give me a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical distinction seriously, and say whether Groundhog Day adds anything to the debate—perhaps about the psychology of earning release—that pure philosophical argument misses.
