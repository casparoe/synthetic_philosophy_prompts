A summary in a recent popular philosophy article describes the reference class problem as follows: "Whenever we assign a probability to a unique event—say, whether a particular 45-year-old left-handed lighthouse keeper in Gothenburg will develop lung cancer—we face an unavoidable ambiguity. We can classify him by occupation, by handedness, by city, by age, or by smoking status, and each reference class yields a different base rate. Because nature provides no canonical partition, frequentist probability collapses for singular predictions; it only makes sense for infinite repetitions of identical trials. The only coherent escape is full-bore Bayesianism, which abandons objective frequencies and makes every prediction depend on an agent's prior beliefs." How accurate is this characterization? Examine it sentence by sentence: does the problem truly undermine frequentist single-case predictions, or is there a defensible frequentist reply? Does Bayesianism eliminate the ambiguity, or does it simply hide the reference-class choice inside the prior? And is the summary right to present the problem as unique to statistical prediction, or does a parallel issue arise in causal explanation and inductive generalization?
