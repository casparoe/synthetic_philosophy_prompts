I'm preparing an independent study presentation on Keynes's ethical vision, specifically the conception of the good life he outlines in his 1930 essay about economic prospects for future generations. I want a taxonomy in tree form that sorts interpretations of this vision. The root branch should ask whether the good life Keynes describes is primarily a matter of achieving material abundance and leisure, or whether it requires a prior moral-psychological transformation—specifically, overcoming what he describes as the obsessive valuation of economic accumulation. From there, branch on whether that transformation is compatible with retaining capitalist incentives or requires moving beyond them. The final branch should ask whether the artistic and contemplative activities Keynes praises actually depend on conditions of scarcity, anxiety, or economic struggle—a view I am tentatively labeling the melancholic Keynesian position, even if it lacks a standard name. Each leaf should identify the interpretation (using established names where they exist, or unnamed otherwise) and state in one sentence its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment, especially regarding whether Keynes's own temperament—his tendency toward pessimism despite his optimism—undermines his argument. The tree should include at least one logically possible but unoccupied branch, with a brief diagnosis of why it remains empty: is it incoherent, merely unexplored, or genuinely unattractive? Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I only need the conceptual map, and I want to see where the positions genuinely come apart without relying on prior familiarity with the labels.
