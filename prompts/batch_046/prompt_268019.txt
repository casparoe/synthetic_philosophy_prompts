I am revising a scene in my novel in which two mathematicians—both scrupulously rational and entirely transparent with each other—sustain opposite subjective probabilities about the same empirical claim long after they have shared every relevant argument and dataset. A reader objected that this violates the common claim that rational agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree, and therefore my scene is psychologically implausible. Before I alter the manuscript, I need a precise conceptual map of what that slogan could actually be asserting. Please lay out the distinct theses it might express, ordered from the logically trivial to the radically strong, and for each indicate whether it is defensible, false, or merely a tautology disguised as insight. I want to know which versions genuinely depend on conditions I could complicate or withhold in fiction—common knowledge of rationality, identity of prior distributions, agreement on the sample space, shared interpretation of evidence, or convergence on a single model of the world—and which versions instead smuggle in normative assumptions about what rationality requires rather than describing what it permits. For each reading, state whether it actually rules out my narrative scenario, and identify which reading is probably the one people invoke when they use the slogan to dismiss disagreement as irrational. Finally, mark the points where the principle stops being a claim about belief and becomes a claim about conversational etiquette or social coordination, since that boundary is thematically important to the scene. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need only the disambiguation I can use at my desk.
