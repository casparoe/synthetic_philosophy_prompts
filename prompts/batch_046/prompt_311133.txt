I'm a doctoral student in normative ethics working on distributive justice across temporal dimensions, and I'm attempting to formalize my supervisor's implicit theoretical commitments based on her recent rulings in our seminar's hypothetical-society exercises. Her verdicts across six prior cases have been as follows. First, in a two-world comparison—World A with one individual at utility 10 and one at 0, versus World B with both at 4—she judges B strictly better despite the lower aggregate. Second, in a small-gain scenario where every individual can be raised uniformly from 5 to 6 at no cost, she treats the improvement as obligatory but assigns it minimal moral weight. Third, in a sacrifice-for-equality case—reducing the best-off from 20 to 10 in order to raise the worst-off from 2 to 3—she rejects the exchange as disproportionate. Fourth, in a pure-leveling-down case—reducing the best-off from 10 to 5 with no compensating benefit to anyone—she judges this strictly worse. Fifth, in a threshold scenario where all individuals already sit above a decent minimum, she permits further benefits to the well-off but denies them any distributive priority. Sixth, in an asymmetric trade-off—helping one severely disadvantaged person a great deal versus helping many well-off people a little with greater total benefit—she consistently selects the severely disadvantaged option, even at a net loss in aggregate well-being.

I now want to test whether her framework aggregates over whole lives or over momentary states, using a new constructed case I call the Inter-Period Cohort Exchange. Consider two cohorts of equal size living simultaneously across forty years. Cohort Alpha naturally experiences utility 8 during years 1–20 and utility 2 during years 21–40; Cohort Beta naturally experiences a constant utility 5 across all forty years. A proposed social policy redistributes resources between the cohorts across time: during years 1–20, Alpha is reduced to 4 and Beta is raised to 6; during years 21–40, Alpha is raised to 6 and Beta is reduced to 4. Every individual therefore experiences exactly twenty years at 4 and twenty years at 6, leaving each cohort's lifetime average unchanged at 5, but altering the global distribution of momentary well-being: the worst temporal slice in the status quo (Alpha at 2) is eliminated, replaced by slices at 4, yet some individuals—Alpha in years 1–20 and Beta in years 21–40—are made worse off relative to their baseline.

How should she rule for consistency? Is the case structurally closer to her acceptance of the two-world prioritarian comparison—since the absolute worst momentary state is improved—or to her rejection of pure leveling-down—since the intervention harms specific individuals without improving any cohort's lifetime total? Is she implicitly running a momentary-state prioritarianism, a whole-life prioritarianism that should be indifferent here, a contemporaneous egalitarianism focused on period-by-period disparity reduction, or a hybrid view with a non-aggregative temporal plateau? And if these interpretations genuinely diverge on this case, what single further scenario—perhaps one involving purely intrapersonal redistribution of well-being across life-stages with no inter-cohort element—would definitively distinguish them?
