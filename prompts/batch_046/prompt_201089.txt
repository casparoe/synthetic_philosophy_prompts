Construct a variant of Kavka's toxin puzzle in which the reward is not paid for a bare intention to drink, but for having formed and registered a specific conditional plan: you must commit in writing, witnessed by an independent arbiter, to consume a genuinely unpleasant but harmless toxin tomorrow morning unless a designated external event (say, a specific public announcement) occurs by then; the commitment contract includes an irreversible one-way notification that is sent automatically to the arbiter if you attempt to revoke it, making revocation socially visible and psychologically costly though not physically impossible. The billionaire can verify that the plan is registered with the arbiter, but has no access to your private mental states and relies on no exotic technology or perfect predictor. Present the scenario in a single paragraph set in an ordinary workplace or clinical context, then walk through what the standard resolution of the original puzzle says about the rationality of forming the intention versus carrying out the action. Apply that reasoning to your variant: does the agent still have reason to register the plan but not to drink? Or does the presence of the irreversible notification mechanism change the calculus at the planning stage, the execution stage, or both? Identify the precise structural feature doing the philosophical work—whether it is the separation of planning from execution, the social cost of revocation, or the public observability of the commitment mechanism. Finally, show how a defender of the standard resolution would attack your variant, for instance by arguing that the mechanism collapses the planning stage into the execution stage or that the cost of revocation makes drinking rational after all, and state whether removing that feature restores the logic of the original puzzle.
