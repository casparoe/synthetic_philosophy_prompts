I have recently been asked to help draft a governance framework for distributed artificial intelligence systems, and in trying to articulate exactly what kind of entity such a system is, I have arrived at the following commitments about parts and wholes. I want to know whether they form a coherent package or whether I am helping myself to incompatible motivations.

Here is where I stand. First, I accept a restricted answer to the special composition question: arbitrary aggregates do not form a whole, and composition requires some form of integrated causal or functional interaction among parts that yields a unified capacity not present in the parts taken separately. Second, I take ontological vagueness seriously; there are genuine borderline cases of composition, such as an organism undergoing gradual dissolution or a network with intermittent connectivity, where there is no determinate fact of the matter rather than mere epistemic ignorance. Third, I maintain that institutional and technological entities—including corporations, distributed software architectures, and collective decision systems—are not literally composed of their members or hardware nodes; they exist only derivatively or conceptually, and talk of them as wholes is pragmatic or metaphorical. Fourth, despite that denial, I think moral and legal accountability should be assigned directly at the systemic level whenever an entity exhibits integrated behavior that is not reducible to the actions of its components, regardless of whether it satisfies the ontological conditions for literal composition. Fifth, I want to insist that practical reasoning about collective systems should not be held hostage to a solved ontology of composition; we ought to be able to assess responsibility and authority without first deciding whether the system counts as a real whole.

Do these hang together? In particular, commitments one and three seem to pull in opposite directions: if functional integration is what licenses literal composition, then a sufficiently integrated distributed system might actually qualify as a genuine whole, which would undermine my categorical denial in three. Meanwhile, commitments three and four appear to generate a tension: if these entities are only derivatively real, on what grounds do we assign them direct accountability rather than distributing it entirely among the genuinely composed parts, namely the individual agents or physical nodes? And is the apparent conflict between one and two—namely, a sharp functional criterion for composition versus the reality of vagueness—merely superficial, given that functional integration itself can come in degrees?

What I need is an audit, not a lecture. Order the tensions by severity. For each, say whether it is genuine or dissolves on closer inspection, specify exactly which commitment must give way, and identify the cheapest revision—whether abandoning restricted composition, accepting that integrated AI systems are literal wholes, or giving up direct systemic accountability—that restores consistency without sacrificing the practical motivation behind four.
