A colleague summarized the case for legislative sortition paired with mandatory rotation as follows: "Career politicians constitute a self-selecting caste with interests structurally misaligned with those of the governed, so democratic legitimacy demands that legislators be drawn by lot for a single, non-renewable term. This eliminates reelection incentives, yields descriptively representative bodies, and resolves the principal-agent problem because representatives eventually return to private life and live under the laws they pass. Critics cite incompetence, but that objection mistakes institutional design for individual capacity: permanent expert staff, deliberative mini-publics feeding binding recommendations upward, and structured training can supply technical knowledge without reproducing a governing class. The deeper resistance is conceptual—we equate democracy with the electoral right to choose rulers rather than with an equal ex ante probability of holding office. Once that conceptual error is corrected, the hybrid model is the obviously superior arrangement for any polity suffering from entrenched party capture."

I want to know whether this summary is fair. Which parts accurately capture the arguments made by defenders of sortition and term limits, which parts subtly misrepresent or overstate those arguments, and does the conclusion—that this hybrid is "obviously superior" for polities with party capture—actually follow from the corrected premises? In particular, address whether the claim that the principal-agent problem is "resolved" holds up, and whether the proposed institutional supplements (expert staff and mini-publics) avoid the very problem of unaccountable power that the argument claims to solve.
