I've been working through some comparative archaeology for a seminar on long-term societal change, and I keep running into an asymmetry that seems conceptually loaded rather than empirically neutral. When scholars discuss the Classic Maya lowlands around the ninth century, they routinely invoke 'collapse'—a term that carries connotations of failure, regression, or the loss of an achieved level of civilization—whereas for the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean, with its comparable dissolution of palace economies, demographic dispersal, and the cessation of large-scale monument building, the preferred vocabulary is usually 'transformation,' 'reconfiguration,' or 'systems collapse' used only cautiously. I want to understand the conceptual issue this divergence reveals. Is there a rigorous, non-teleological definition of societal collapse—one that doesn't implicitly treat high-complexity, centralized political integration and monumental construction as the baseline or natural endpoint of social development—such that applying it to the Maya but not to the Bronze Age could be justified by features internal to the cases themselves? Or does the distinction largely track whether we view the outcome as a regrettable deviation from an expected trajectory? Furthermore, if we succeed in purging 'collapse' of that normative and evolutionary loading, do we retain enough analytical content to distinguish it from ordinary historical change, or does it dissolve into a purely rhetorical category that tells us more about the observer's commitments to progress and complexity than about the societies being observed?
