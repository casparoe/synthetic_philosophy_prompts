I have recently been asked to advise a commission reviewing which norms should be recognized as binding in a jurisdiction where formal state institutions have collapsed and been replaced by hybrid governance structures. Because my own thinking on legal validity has been shaped by a senior mentor in jurisprudence, I want to test whether her applied judgments are consistent before I present my recommendation. Over several discussions, she has rendered clear verdicts on three cases. First, she holds that a constitutional amendment, enacted through every required procedural step but authorizing arbitrary detention and abolishing judicial review, is valid law and ought to be applied by courts. Second, she maintains that an emergency decree promulgated by an unelected transitional council—morally urgent, widely obeyed, and structurally coherent, yet produced without any recognized institutional authorization—is not law and should be disregarded. Third, she judges that a centuries-old customary practice of communal land seizure, consistently enforced by local magistrates and recognized in appellate judgments but never codified and deeply discriminatory, is not law.

These three verdicts seem to me compatible with more than one jurisprudential principle. I now need her view, or the view that best coheres with her record, on a fourth situation that has become directly relevant to the commission's work: a comprehensive code regulating digital inheritance, virtual property, and algorithmic contract enforcement, generated by a large language model trained on centuries of legal texts, refined through adversarial review by scholars, and adopted as binding by a decentralized network of arbitration nodes that have resolved over ten thousand disputes in eight years. No legislature enacted the code; however, national courts have begun citing it as persuasive authority, commercial actors structure transactions around it, and its provisions are general, public, non-retroactive, and internally consistent.

What is the rule that best explains her three earlier judgments? What is the most plausible alternative rule that fits them equally well? Under each rule, is the AI-generated code law? Which prediction is the more defensible reading of her position, and what makes it preferable? Finally, identify one further hypothetical case—one that does not depend on technology or artificial intelligence—that would sharply distinguish the two rules and reveal which one she actually runs.
