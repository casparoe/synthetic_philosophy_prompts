Your parents are disputing why their two sisters—raised in the same working-class household with nearly identical resources—lived opposite economic lives. Both claimed to value long-term security, yet one enacted it through relentless self-denial: she treated her small business as an irreducible vocation, refused all consumer debt, reinvested every surplus, and accumulated slow, durable wealth over four decades. The other pursued the same stated value through leveraged consumption and frequent career changes for comfort, eventually collapsing into insolvency despite far higher peak earnings. Your father insists the first sister's worldview was an independent causal engine—values that actively reshaped material conditions rather than merely rationalizing behavior after the fact. Your mother insists this reverses causation: a forgotten family loan and one fortunate contract did all the explanatory work, and since the "values" and the economic behaviors are observationally inseparable, attributing the divergence to culture mistakes correlation for cause and risks explaining action with itself. They ask you to advise them on whether values can ever be genuine causes of economic trajectory, or whether invoking culture always collapses into a circular narrative. How should they think about this disagreement?
