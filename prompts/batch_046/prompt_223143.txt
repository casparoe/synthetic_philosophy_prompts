I am working on a literary novel in which a protagonist’s consciousness is not localized in a single brain but distributed across three living subjects, each hosting a non-redundant functional fragment of what Hofstadter describes as a strange loop—the self-referential pattern he takes to constitute an I. The fragments are asymmetric: one substrate carries autobiographical memory, one carries affective valence, and one carries narrative integration, with information flowing among them in real time but never fully converging in any one skull. I want the philosophy underpinning this scenario to be rigorous rather than magical-realist hand-waving.

Please explain, with conceptual precision, whether an I can be genuinely distributed across brains in this manner, or whether Hofstadter’s framework—according to which the self is a pattern that can persist in others after death—only licenses replication or continuation within a single host, not simultaneous diffusion. In particular, address the distinction between (a) one loop instantiated in parallel across multiple substrates, (b) one loop distributed with its constituent parts separated across substrates, and (c) several loops each only partially resembling the original. Where exactly does the boundary lie between a distributed self and a collective of imitative fragments?

As you do so, please separate what is settled in the philosophy of mind and cognitive science from what remains genuinely contested among specialists. Do not present a contested interpretation—whether about the necessity of unified phenomenal access, the role of embodied continuity, or the coherence conditions for personal identity over time—as if it were the consensus view.
