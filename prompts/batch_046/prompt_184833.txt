I have just come out of a long and rather intense argument with a close friend about whether cultural values can serve as genuine explanatory causes in historical sociology, and we kept returning to Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis without ever resolving our disagreement. She insisted that Weber offers a direct causal claim—that the psychological pressure of Calvinist predestination produced the capitalist spirit—whereas I maintained that this reading mistakes Weber's methodological intentions and that treating values as efficient causes is precisely the kind of error his essay warns against. To clarify my own position, I have drafted the following notes for a short essay.

The central claim is that Weber's notion of elective affinity should be understood not as a causal mechanism linking religious doctrine to economic behavior, but as what I call a structural permission: a symbolic framework that does not push actors toward capital accumulation so much as render certain rationalizations of accumulation legitimate and others illegitimate, thereby altering the environment in which economic practices are selected without functioning as an efficient cause in the natural-scientific sense. I want to connect this to Weber's methodological writings on ideal types, arguing that ideal types are not historical descriptions but heuristic boundary conditions. Their purpose is not to state that this value-complex produced that outcome, but to pose the counterfactual question of what consistent economic rationality would look like if oriented entirely by this value-complex. I further want to argue that the familiar objection—that Weber confuses correlation with causation—rests on a misreading, since Weber never intended a law-like explanation; the real philosophical difficulty is whether structural permission is itself a coherent explanatory category, or whether it collapses either into tautology (capitalism appears wherever the values that permit it appear) or into disguised materialism (the permission is merely epiphenomenal to underlying material conditions).

I would be grateful for your comments on these notes, especially on three points. First, accuracy: do I correctly represent Weber's rejection of efficient-causal explanations in cultural sociology, and does the structural permission framing genuinely capture his concept of elective affinity or distort it? Second, coherence and interest: is the analogy to a selection environment for practices illuminating, or does it obscure the specific historical mechanisms Weber actually discusses? Third, and importantly for my own clarity, please separate what is settled in Weber scholarship from what remains genuinely contested among knowledgeable interpreters—particularly regarding whether Weber should be read as a methodological individualist, a cultural holist, or something in between—and do not present any contested interpretive stance as though it were the consensus view.
