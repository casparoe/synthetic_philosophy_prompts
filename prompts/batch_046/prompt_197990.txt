Over the past several months I have been helping navigate care decisions for a family member facing progressive cognitive decline, and the abstract question of whether well-being consists solely in subjective experience or requires some further relation to reality has become personally urgent. I am writing the final examination for an upper-division seminar on theories of well-being, and I want an essay prompt that cannot be answered by rehearsing standard summaries of Nozick’s experience-machine argument.

Please design a 45-minute exam essay (approximately 600 words, worth 40 marks) built around the following modified case. A cognitively intact patient with a chronic, painful condition is offered entry into a fully transparent virtual environment. She retains accurate knowledge that the environment is simulated, retains true beliefs about her actual family and circumstances, but can interact only with high-fidelity simulations of them; within the environment she experiences complete analgesia and sustained positive affect, but she lacks any causal interaction with the real world. The exam question should ask students to determine whether she has decisive reason to enter, drawing specifically on hedonist, desire-satisfactionist, and objective-list accounts of well-being. Crucially, the question must force them to identify precisely where Nozick’s concern about contact with reality succeeds or collapses when deception is absent but causal engagement with reality is not.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, phrased so that a student cannot earn high marks by reciting textbook labels;
2. A model answer of roughly 600 words that takes a clear position, defends it through named logical moves, and addresses all three theoretical frameworks in relation to the case;
3. A marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by listing the specific argumentative moves an A must contain and the specific errors or omissions that should cost marks.

In addition, the model answer should conclude with a brief reflective paragraph stating what specific argument or empirical consideration would change its verdict, and identifying the point in the reasoning that is most vulnerable—particularly where the distinction between epistemic access to reality and causal interaction with it might be weaker than the answer assumes.
