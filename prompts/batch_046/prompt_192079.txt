I write to you as a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving active research, has turned to philosophy of mind and logic with an enthusiasm that perhaps exceeds my formal training. I wish to commission a sustained piece of satire—approximately seven hundred words—written in the deadpan voice of an institutional strategic planning document, specifically a funding proposal and organizational charter issued by a fictional body I shall call the Institute for Recursive Organizational Consciousness. The form should be continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or any typographic emphasis whatsoever, composed as though for a single recipient in a formal letter or internal memorandum. The philosophical target is Douglas Hofstadter's Gödelian analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach, in which self-referential formal structures—Gödel's incompleteness formulas—are offered as an illuminating model for how self-aware minds might emerge from formal rules. The satire should take this analogy not as metaphor but as literal administrative engineering, exaggerating the real tendency in certain philosophical and popular-scientific discourse to slide from formal self-reference to full-blown consciousness without adequately accounting for the disanalogies. The invented proponents—the institute's directors—should calmly propose that university departments can achieve genuine self-awareness only by arranging hiring committees as strange loops in which each evaluator is evaluated by the very body they sit on, that annual budgets must contain self-evaluating line items capable of assessing their own fiscal consciousness, and that tenure reviews should be structured so that the syntax of evaluation generates an emergent semantic awareness of institutional purpose, all in service of closing the explanatory gap between organizational procedure and self-knowledge. Each absurd proposal should trace a recognizable feature of the original Gödelian analogy—recursive embedding, the distinction between syntax and semantics, emergent properties that outstrip their formal substrate—so that a reader familiar with Hofstadter's work recognizes the exaggeration as conceptually fair rather than ignorant straw-manning. The irony must remain entirely deadpan and legible without winking: there should be no explicit signal that the charter is absurd, yet the absurdity should be unmistakable to anyone who understands where formal self-reference actually ends and biological consciousness begins. I ask that you avoid bibliographies, citations, appeals to named contemporary authorities, or requests for further reading, since the piece should stand entirely on its internal conceptual logic. Please deliver this as uninterrupted continuous prose, maintaining the measured, somewhat formal tone of a retired scientist who has perhaps taken one analogy too seriously.
