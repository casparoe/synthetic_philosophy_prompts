I serve on a municipal climate-adaptation board that must translate ensemble-model flood-risk outputs into public recommendations, and this has forced me to confront whether my philosophical commitments about probability and belief are actually coherent. I have arrived at the following positions, and I need an audit of whether they form a consistent package.

(1) I endorse the Principal Principle in roughly its canonical form: a rational agent's credence in an event should match the known objective chance of that event, absent inadmissible evidence.

(2) I restrict this deference to chances that are physically grounded—features of the world generated by stochastic dynamics—rather than extending it to purely epistemic probabilities, statistical summaries, or measures of model uncertainty.

(3) I therefore regard the probabilities produced by climate-ensemble runs (for example, a 12% annual exceedance probability for a given flood threshold) as artifacts of parameterization, ensemble design, and structural model choice, not as objective chances in the sense required by (1).

(4) At the same time, in my advisory capacity I argue that policymakers should treat these very outputs as authoritative anchors for belief and resource allocation—effectively deferring to them exactly as the Principal Principle would prescribe—because no more objective benchmark is available and collective decision-making requires a shared focal point.

(5) I reject undermining-style updates in policy-relevant contexts: if a low-chance event occurs, I maintain that rational agents should update on the outcome but should not retrospectively revise their belief in the governing chance law or in the model that encoded it, at least within the time horizons relevant to adaptation planning.

(6) I also hold that when two structurally different models assign different probabilities to the same physical event, there is no further fact of the matter that determines which is correct, hence no unique objective chance exists to which deference is owed; nevertheless, I insist that one must select a single model and treat its output as binding.

These six claims appear to conflict: (3) denies that climate-model outputs are the right kind of thing for (1) to apply to, yet (4) demands deference to them; (6) denies there is a unique chance, yet (4) and (1) seem to require one; and (5) forbids revising the model in light of outcomes, which seems hard to maintain if (3) is true and (4) treats the model as authoritative.

Please adjudicate point by point. For each pair, say whether the tension is real or only apparent, and if real, identify the specific claim that must be weakened or abandoned to restore consistency. Rank the conflicts by severity. State what the cheapest repair to the overall package is, and what it costs—especially whether saving (4) requires giving up (2) or merely refining it.
