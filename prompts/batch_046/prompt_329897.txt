I serve on the advisory council for a regional heritage foundation that is redesigning its permanent exhibition on local economic history. The current display presents the past two centuries as a linear progression: agrarian subsistence, industrial boom, post-industrial contraction, and an anticipated green-technology renewal, with each stage framed as solving problems the previous one created. A new proposal argues for restructuring the narrative around three observed cycles of rapid growth, overextension, collapse, and regeneration. The proponents claim that societies do not accumulate progress like capital; rather, they behave like organisms with a natural rhythm—solidarity and creativity peak during hardship and erode during prolonged prosperity, making periodic dissolution not a failure but a necessary clearing of rigid structures. They point to specific local patterns: cooperative labor networks formed after each crash, then gradually bureaucratized and dissolved within two generations. The opponents counter that this framing ignores genuinely irreversible achievements—workplace safety regulations, expanded suffrage, ecological monitoring—that do not disappear in the next downturn, and that presenting history as a wheel encourages fatalism about current economic and environmental crises. The board asks me whether to adopt the cyclical framing, preserve the linear one, or design a hybrid, and they want the decision tied to a philosophical view about whether history has a direction at all. If history is genuinely cyclical, should public institutions be designed to accommodate periodic collapse rather than prevent it? If it is linear, are we committed to the belief that every past problem is eventually solvable? Or is there a coherent intermediate view—perhaps that history spirals—under which we can honor both recurrence and irreversible change? I need advice that is not generic encouragement, but a reasoned judgment about the nature of historical movement and its concrete implications for how a public museum should represent the past to guide present action.
