Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have found myself drawn to the conceptual architecture of moral philosophy, particularly where it intersects with scientific uncertainty. I am designing the final assessment for a small graduate seminar I now convene on applied ethics, and I want an essay question that examines consequentialist cluelessness—not through a generic recitation of its canonical formulations, but through a deliberately constructed scenario that forces students to locate the precise fracture points in expected-value reasoning. The case I have in mind involves a proposed government program of large-scale ocean alkalinity enhancement intended to mitigate acidification, where second-order ecological effects on phytoplankton dynamics, deep-sea carbon sequestration rates, and regional fishery networks depend on parameter values for which we possess almost no empirical constraint. I want the exam question to ask students to articulate why standard expected-utility calculation collapses in this setting, to distinguish structural from empirical forms of cluelessness as they apply here, and to evaluate whether alternative decision rules—specifically maximin and a precautionary principle—actually resolve the difficulty or merely displace it into a different normative register. The question should be answerable in approximately forty-five minutes and should not reward students who merely name-check the problem; instead, it must reward those who can show exactly where the causal opacity undermines aggregation and why substituting a different rule does not restore epistemic access to the outcomes.

Please provide the exam question itself, followed by a model answer of roughly seven hundred words, and a marking scheme that separates an A-grade response from a B-grade response by specifying the exact analytical moves each must contain. In particular, an A answer should identify the relocation problem—that maximin and precautionary reasoning presuppose a framing of outcomes and a selection of relevant variables that is itself subject to the same cluelessness—while a B answer should be penalized for treating these alternatives as unproblematic escape routes without examining their hidden dependency on prior normative weighting. The marking scheme should also flag common misconceptions, such as conflating cluelessness with ordinary risk aversion or treating the absence of data as a temporary rather than structurally irreducible feature of the decision environment. Write the prompt from my perspective as a retired natural scientist who has only recently begun formal philosophical study, keep the tone formal and polished, and do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to obscure theoretical apparatus that a well-read but non-specialist model could not reasonably reconstruct.
