As a novelist working on a historical drama, I have been tracking the rulings of an informal advisory board—composed of biographers, archivists, and adaptation lawyers—on proposals to dramatize real deceased figures. Their precedents are as follows.

First, they approved a script that invented a private scene of religious doubt for a statesman who died in 1850, using phrases inferred from his letters to bridge a documented reversal in his public policy; they held that reconstructing a psychological transition is permissible when it explains a verified historical change.

Second, they rejected a screenplay that introduced a wholly fictional rival scientist in order to dramatize a documented rivalry between two real ones, ruling that inventing relational figures distorts the record of actual lives even when the broader outcome is accurate.

Third, they approved, on the condition that the framing narration explicitly mark the interpretation as retrospective, a biopic that applied a modern psychiatric category to a dead artist (d. 1920) and placed diagnostic language in a doctor’s mouth, drawing the phrases from her own journals.

Fourth, they rejected a drama that attributed specific rally speeches to the spouse of a dead activist, despite the spouse’s documented existence in census records, on the grounds that precise public words may not be assigned to a figure who left no writings, speeches, or other trace.

I now seek the board’s judgment on a new proposal for my own manuscript: a biopic of a sanitary-reform physician (d. 1910, with no living descendants) whose recently discovered private notebooks contain ambiguous, fragmentary notes suggesting he privately doubted the efficacy of one reform he publicly championed. The script invents a specific conversation with his sister—who appears in census records but left no writings—in which he articulates these doubts using phrases drawn directly from the notebooks. The scene is intended to humanize him and to explain the tension between his documented advocacy and these private reservations.

If the board wishes to remain consistent with itself, should they approve or reject this proposal? Identify the closest analog among their four precedents. Then articulate the two competing rules that jointly explain all four rulings: one that permits reconstructed private scenes when they serve as explanatory bridges between documented mental states and public actions, and one that restricts dramatization to documented words, actions, or interpretations explicitly framed as retrospective. For each rule, state what it predicts for the physician case and what underlying principle the board would be adopting by following that prediction. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish these two rules from one another.
