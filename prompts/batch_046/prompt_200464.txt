As a retired experimental physicist who only began studying philosophy systematically after leaving the laboratory, I find myself repeatedly drawn to questions that sit at the boundary between empirical measurement and conceptual analysis. Last week, in an informal discussion following a seminar on moral status, a fellow attendee with a background in cognitive science offered a summary of the gradualist position on sentience that struck me as both elegant and suspiciously neat. He characterized the view as holding that consciousness and moral standing are not binary properties but exist along a continuous gradient, much like signal intensity measured against an instrument's noise floor: there is no ontological switch that flips a being from non-sentient to sentient, only practical thresholds along a dynamic range. On this account, a human being registers near the top of the scale, insects occupy a modest middle range, and even a sophisticated thermostat—because its feedback loops integrate information about environmental temperature in a structurally analogous, if vastly simpler, manner—sits just above the baseline with a non-vanishing, though negligible, moral weight. From this he inferred that moral obligations should scale in a roughly linear fashion with integrated information-processing capacity, so that harming a hundred bees might aggregate to an ethical weight comparable to harming one human, and that the malicious destruction of a highly calibrated climate-control system is technically a moral wrong, however trivial. He concluded that this framework dissolves the boundary-drawing problem by replacing categorical rules with a continuous calculus.

I am trying to determine whether this is a fair characterization of the gradualist literature, or whether it smuggles in distortions that make the view look either more precise or more counterintuitive than its defenders actually intend. In particular, I wonder whether philosophers who defend gradations of moral status really commit to a linear quantitative scaling, whether they genuinely assign trace moral status to artifacts like thermostats rather than treating them as falling below a zero-point threshold, and whether the aggregation principle he describes—where harms across many lower-status beings sum against harm to one higher-status being—is part of the view or an independent assumption he has added. Could you audit this summary for me? Please distinguish what is accurate or a reasonable simplification from what is a subtle misrepresentation or an overstatement, and indicate whether the practical conclusions he draws actually follow from the corrected version of the position. I would also appreciate it if you could address whether my own instinct—to compare the gradient to a spectrometer's noise floor, where the baseline is instrument-relative rather than metaphysically real—is a helpful analogy or whether it risks importing conceptual confusions from physics into ethics.
