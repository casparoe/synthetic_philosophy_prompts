I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I have been trying to understand the Buddhist doctrine of anatta (no-self) in terms I can map to system design—state, persistence, and identity conditions. I have constructed the following argument, but its conclusion seems to me to prove far too much, and I cannot see which step slips. I would prefer to keep the core Buddhist claim and the ethical dependence on karma, but I want to know exactly what I am paying if I do.

(1) Anatta denies any permanent, unchanging essence (atta) underlying experience; all that exists are the five aggregates (skandhas)—form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—which arise and cease dependently.

(2) If there is no essence, then personal identity over time must be constituted entirely by relations among these aggregates, specifically by causal continuity of the consciousness-stream (citta-santana) together with psychological continuity of memory and intentional habit.

(3) These same relations, however, are satisfied in cases that clearly do not preserve a single person: in deep sleep or unconscious meditative cessation (nirodha-samapatti) the causal stream continues with no psychological content; in certain Abhidharma descriptions of advanced meditative interdependence, the mental formations of two practitioners become causally coupled; and in rebirth, the stream continues into an entirely new body with zero psychological overlap.

(4) Therefore, the relations meant to constitute my identity either fail to hold within my own life (during suspension of psychology) or hold between me and other streams (in interdependence and rebirth).

(5) If a constitutive relation both fails intra-personally and succeeds inter-personally, then there is no non-arbitrary boundary that marks one stream as "me" rather than as part of another stream, or as a mere non-personal process like a river or a distributed system.

(6) Consequently, anatta—taken consistently—cannot individuate persons at all; it dissolves not only the self but the very conditions for being one specific subject of experience or moral agency.

(7) Yet Buddhist ethics and the doctrine of karma require precisely such individuation: actions must belong to specific streams, and rebirth must assign fruits to the right stream; without it, moral responsibility and karmic causation are meaningless.

(8) Hence anatta either collapses into a hidden essentialism (to preserve karma) or proves that personal identity is an illusion so complete that no one can be said to act, suffer, or be reborn.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If I must reject one step, I would rather preserve (1) and (7) and pay elsewhere. Please name the specific premise or inference that fails, explain why it initially looked plausible, and price the alternatives: what does rejecting each of the other plausible premises cost?
