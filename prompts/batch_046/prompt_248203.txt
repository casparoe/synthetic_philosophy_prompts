I am leading a small graduate reading group through contemporary debates in axiology, and we have reached an impasse that feels more structural than substantive. Most participants accept some version of tranquilism — the stronger claim that only the absence of craving and suffering carries genuine value, while even satisfied states are derivative or disguised dependencies — but we have been circling a finer distinction that rarely gets named in the standard literature: the gap between “negative pleasure” (the relief felt when a craving is suspended) and what one student called “pure absence,” a state in which the structure of craving was never present and is not even available as a missing term. I want to close our final session with a poem that renders this distinction with conceptual precision rather than sentiment. Please write a villanelle — nineteen lines, ABA rhyme scheme, with two refrains that alternate and converge in the closing couplet — from the voice of a proponent of this stricter view. The first refrain should run something like “Only the space where craving never was,” and the second like “Even relief remembers the thirst it ended.” The philosophical work should be done by the form itself: each recurrence of the second refrain should be placed in a context that exposes its hidden dependency, so that by the time the two lines stand together in the final quatrain the reader sees that relief is not the opposite of suffering but its postponed echo. The poem should be accurate enough that someone working in Schopenhauer-influenced value theory would recognize the distinction being drawn, yet it should read as an independent lyric rather than a paraphrase of any specific text. I will read it aloud at the end of the meeting; it should not resolve the tension with a sermon, but leave the structural point standing.
