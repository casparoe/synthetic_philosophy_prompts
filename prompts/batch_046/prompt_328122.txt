Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in AI safety: the Alignment-Priority camp, which maintains that a highly capable system whose internal objective or reasoning structure is not genuinely oriented toward human values will eventually circumvent any containment mechanism, so that solving value alignment is a necessary precondition for safe deployment; and the Control-Priority camp, which maintains that institutional and technical containment architectures—sandboxing, oversight layers, capability caps, multi-agent review, and interruptibility protocols—can reliably manage systems regardless of their internal orientation, making alignment unnecessary as a deployment prerequisite.

The deliverable should be structured as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: precise statements about the behavior of intelligent systems, the empirical limits of current monitoring techniques, the conditions under which institutional deployment decisions are made, and the formal distinction between controlling outputs and altering internal objectives. Each claim must be phrased in neutral language that neither camp would disavow. Second, a table of residual disagreements, with each camp’s position stated in wording that camp itself would accept; identify explicitly any disagreement that is verbal—where both sides accept the same underlying state of affairs but describe it with different evaluative language—and explain why. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would cause at least one camp to revise its view. I take the central cruxes to include: (a) whether misalignment produces observable signatures in reasoning, planning, or self-modeling traces before it manifests in harmful actions; (b) whether a containment architecture can remain effective against a system that possesses partial reflective access—specifically, the ability to model its own container and to propose but not unilaterally execute modifications to it; and (c) whether collective governance structures can overcome the incentive for individual actors to deploy prematurely, or whether unilateral action is structurally inevitable at sufficient capability thresholds. Fourth, for each crux, specify one concrete scenario, institutional design, or empirical benchmark that both camps agree bears on it—for instance, the predicted behavior of a system with reflective sandbox access that consistently assigns low internal probability to human oversight continuing, or the track record of safety-protocol adherence in other high-stakes engineering domains—and record in advance what each camp predicts that case will demonstrate. Fifth, a concluding assessment of whether any part of the dispute collapses once verbal disagreements are removed, and of what empirical or conceptual result would actually move the debate.

Keep all formulations exact. Do not allow either side to retreat to vague invocations of “trust,” “control,” or “values.” If a disagreement depends on an empirical claim about system behavior, specify the observable behavior at issue; if it depends on a conceptual claim about the relationship between internal objectives and external outputs, make that relationship explicit. The goal is a document both parties could sign, with the remaining disagreement rendered sharp enough to be tested rather than merely reiterated.
