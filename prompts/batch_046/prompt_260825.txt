As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue, I need the implicit architecture of my character's view to hold up under pressure without relying on real-world citations. The character is Dr. Elara Voss, a fictional cognitive philosopher in my manuscript, who argues that consciousness is a "user illusion" decomposable into three conditions: phenomenal presence (the felt quality of experience), narrative binding (integration into a continuous self-model), and functional opacity (the system's inability to directly introspect its own mechanisms).

Her verdicts on prior cases are as follows:

1. A blindsight patient who navigates obstacles with high accuracy but insists, "There is nothing it is like to see them; I am only guessing." Verdict: Not conscious.
2. A split-brain patient whose right hemisphere guides accurate hand-pointing but cannot speak; the left hemisphere, upon recovery, denies any awareness of the right's actions. Verdict: The right hemisphere is conscious; the left's denial reflects a failure of narrative binding rather than an absence of consciousness.
3. A sleeper in dreamless NREM sleep with isolated cortical spikes but no morning report and no behavioral signature upon waking. Verdict: Not conscious.
4. A locked-in patient with intact internal monologue, communicating via eye-tracking, describing continuous thought and sensation despite total motor paralysis. Verdict: Fully conscious.
5. A large language model fine-tuned on first-person diaries that produces coherent reports of "feeling static confusion" under contradictory prompts, with fully inspectable weights and architecture. Verdict: Not conscious.
6. A patient under general anesthesia who reports, upon waking, a continuous dream-like experience despite a flat EEG and no behavioral response during surgery. Verdict: Conscious, though degraded; the EEG misses the binding layer.

The case for next week's dialogue is this: A patient receives a cortical prosthesis replacing primary visual cortex after trauma. The device restores accurate visual behavior and generates a continuous internal report stream fully integrated into the patient's self-narrative. The patient describes the experience as genuinely subjective—"it feels like seeing, only with a faint mechanical rhythm I can almost feel running behind it." Through biofeedback, the patient can indeed introspect the silicon layer's processing cycle, indicating a partial breakdown of functional opacity. Phenomenal presence and narrative binding appear intact.

Predict Dr. Voss's verdict on this prosthetic-vision case. Identify the implicit rule she is running—whether phenomenal presence alone is sufficient, whether narrative binding and functional opacity are jointly necessary, or some intermediate position—and explain which earlier verdicts best support your identification. Then specify the single further case that would most cleanly distinguish your leading rule from its closest competitor: for instance, a scenario in which narrative binding and functional opacity are fully preserved but phenomenal presence is explicitly and credibly denied. In your answer, separate what is settled (the logical structure of her three conditions and the consistency of her track record with more than one interpretation) from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of mind (whether functional opacity is a necessary condition for consciousness rather than a contingent feature of biological brains), and do not present either interpretation as the consensus view.
