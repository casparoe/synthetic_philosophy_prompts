Here is a passage I have been working through, drawn from G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903). In his discussion of intrinsic value, Moore examines personal affection and the appreciation of beautiful objects as among the greatest goods known to us. He describes friendship as a particularly high form of good, consisting not merely in mutual pleasure but in a shared consciousness of beautiful or excellent things together with appropriate emotional attitudes directed both at those things and at one another. He then considers a limiting case: suppose one party to this relation entirely lacks consciousness, or possesses only a behavioral and cognitive structure that replicates the judgments and responses of friendship without any accompanying affective coloring. Moore seems to claim that in such a case the relation would not possess the same intrinsic value, even if every external feature were preserved. I am trying to understand precisely what philosophical work consciousness and affect are doing in this argument. Is Moore committing himself to an affective-dependency thesis, on which the value of friendship derives entirely from the value of the conscious aesthetic states that constitute it? Or is he defending a joint-basicness view, in which the friendship and the aesthetic appreciation are each partially constitutive of an organic whole whose value is not reducible to either part alone? And where does this leave the possibility—foreign to Moore's own concerns—of an artificial system that processes aesthetic judgments in a manner structurally isomorphic to human cognition, engages in reciprocal behavioral coordination that mirrors friendship, but entirely lacks phenomenal consciousness? On his framework, would such a system be capable of participating in the intrinsic good of friendship, or does the absence of affective consciousness necessarily block the supervenience relation? Please help me understand the passage by clarifying which of these readings best captures Moore's position, and then apply that reading to the artificial-companion case.
