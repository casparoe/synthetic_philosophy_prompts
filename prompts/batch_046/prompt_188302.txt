Consider five competing interpretations of Charles Darwin’s stance on the relationship between morality and religion, arranged below without implied ordering:

1. The evolutionary-psychological reading: moral sentiments are adaptive social instincts shaped by natural selection for in-group cohesion.
2. The group-selection reading: altruistic norms arise primarily from competition between human groups rather than within them.
3. The agnostic-naturalist reading: Darwin retained a private, deistic distance while treating morality as entirely explicable through natural causes, with no residual theological grounding.
4. The moral-anti-realist extrapolation: Darwin’s naturalistic genealogy of ethics undermines the objectivity of moral claims, leaving only descriptive evolutionary explanations.
5. The “functional-coevolution” reading (a less common analogical extrapolation): drawing on Darwin’s botanical studies—particularly his analysis of orchid-pollinator interdependence—this view holds that he implicitly modeled moral norms and religious rituals as functionally interlocked, co-evolved structures that stabilize social reproduction without implying teleological design or objective moral truth.

Please rank these interpretations from 1 (most textually and philosophically defensible given Darwin’s published writings, notebooks, and letters) to 5 (least defensible). For each rank, provide a one-sentence justification. Then, in a brief paragraph, evaluate whether interpretation 5 is a legitimate extension of Darwin’s methodological analogies or an anachronistic imposition, and state what that evaluation implies about the boundary between Darwin’s explicit ethical claims and his analogical reasoning in other domains.
