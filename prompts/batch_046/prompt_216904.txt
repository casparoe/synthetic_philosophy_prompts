I'm moderating an academic colloquium Q&A with Dr. Elena Voss, an unnamed defender of the view she calls normative entanglement in the ethics of blame. On her account, a blamer loses standing not through categorical hypocrisy—having done the very same wrong—but only when their own unacknowledged failure operates through the same normative mechanism they invoke against the target. Structural isomorphism in the failure mode is necessary and sufficient for disqualification; categorical identity is neither. She further holds that institutional agents can be entangled in ways that outstrip the entanglements of any individual member, and that partial structural overlap produces proportional diminishment of standing rather than total loss.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press this view precisely where the existing literature on hypocrisy and standing presses it. I want one question that forces her to defend the categorical/structural distinction against the objection that mechanisms are always act-specific, with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that mechanisms can be shared across domains. I want one on whether her view makes hypocrisy too scarce, since precise structural isomorphism seems rare, with a follow-up anticipating her answer that normative mechanisms are broader than particular acts. Include one on self-blame: if I suffer from the same structural failure, do I lack standing to blame myself, and how does she account for the asymmetry between first-person and third-person blame? Include one on institutional blame—specifically whether a regulatory agency that systematically ignores its own procedural rules can blame a firm for procedural violations, with a follow-up on distributed agency and collective entanglement. Include one tailored to the "no one is perfect" objection within her framework: if every potential blamer carries some partial structural entanglement, does standing become a scarce resource, and is there a de minimis threshold? Include one on moral luck, asking whether an accidental or unknowing structural failure undermines standing as much as a culpable one, with a follow-up keyed to her probable distinction between being entangled and being culpably entangled. Include one on proportionality: if structural overlap is only partial, is standing linearly diminished, or is there a tipping point? End with a question asking what concrete case or piece of evidence would compel her to abandon normative entanglement for a simpler categorical account of hypocrisy. Keep the tone formal and colloquial-appropriate; avoid generic biographical questions, and do not request citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
