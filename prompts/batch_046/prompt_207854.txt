I have spent considerable time thinking, without any academic background, about whether an authoritarian government can be morally justified solely by its results—specifically the sustained prosperity and public order often discussed in relation to states like China or Singapore. I believe the standard debate between “performance legitimacy” and democratic consent overlooks a condition I want to call the continuity guarantee: the idea that a regime’s moral claim depends not only on present welfare, but on visible institutional mechanisms that ensure stability across leadership transitions, so that citizens are not merely enjoying one ruler’s temporary competence. Please argue that prosperity and order do justify non-democratic rule, provided this continuity guarantee is genuinely satisfied. Your argument should defend the view that if citizens experience consistently rising material welfare, reliable public safety, and an institutional pathway that prevents violent rupture or economic collapse after the current leadership departs, then they lack a compelling moral grievance against the absence of elections or free expression, because the state has fulfilled its core obligation—delivering durable, predictable flourishing rather than fleeting patronage. In doing so, please address the objection that this framework reduces people to passive clients rather than self-governing agents, and explain why receiving long-term security and welfare might outweigh the value of political agency within this specific reasoning. Finally, state how confident you are in your overall conclusion, and identify the precise step or claim in your argument about which you are least certain.
