I'm prepping a lecture on consciousness and causation and I dug up this old essay by Huxley from like 1874 — the one where he compares consciousness to the steam-whistle of a locomotive, riding along with the machinery but not pushing anything. He describes human beings as conscious automata, with mental states just being byproducts. But then he admits that if consciousness doesn't cause anything, then our judgments about our own mental states are causally cut off from the states themselves — the brain makes both happen side by side, but the pain doesn't cause the belief that I'm in pain. One of my grad studens said this is basically the paradox of phenomenal judgment that Chalmers and others discuss later.

I'm not sure Huxley saw it as a paradox though; maybe he just accepted that we're automata that talk about feelings we never actually causally access. Could you help me parse whether the locomotive analogy actually makes the judgment problem sharper or if it hides something? Specifically, does Huxley need a hidden theory of mental content — like a causal or correlational account — to even say that our judgments are about consciousness, and if so does that undermine his epiphenomenalism? And is there any way for an epiphenomenalist to claim that knowledge of consciousness is possible, or does the view just collapse into a kind of skeptical silence about the very thing it's supposed to explain? Also, I'm wondering if there's a distinction between knowing that consciousness exists and knowing what it's like — maybe Huxley saves the first but loses the second?
