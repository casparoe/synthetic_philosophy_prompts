I'm working through the epistemic logic literature on the knowability principle and trying to calibrate my intuitions about when Fitch-style collapse actually threatens a domain. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases, and I'm struggling to find a unified principle that respects all of them.

Case 1: A mathematician derives a correct proof of a conjecture, records it in a notebook, and dies before anyone reads it. The proof exists physically but is never accessed. I think the conjecture was in principle knowable, and the mere failure of actualization doesn't violate the principle—knowledge is modal, not historical.

Case 2: There is a true statement about the interior of a black-hole singularity that is causally disconnected from every physically possible observer, forever. Here I hesitate. If "knowable" means "accessible to some physically possible observer," this seems like a genuine counterexample to an unrestricted principle; but if we allow counterfactual observers in non-actual worlds, it might survive. My gut says this is a real failure, not just a failure of actualization.

Case 3: In a distributed AI system, agent A knows p, agent B knows q, but institutional barriers prevent any single agent from knowing both, though the system could in principle be reconfigured. The conjunction p ∧ q is true. I feel it is collectively knowable, yet applying Fitch individually to each agent seems to miss the structure, while applying it collectively risks a strange form of collapse that doesn't map onto human epistemology.

Case 4: A small language model outputs a correct fact about 18th-century taxonomy drawn from its training data, with no traceable source and no transparent justification available to it. If we grant that artificial systems can be knowers in a derivative sense, I think Fitch should not apply in the same way—perhaps because mediated access breaks the transparency condition required for the paradox.

Case 5: A logical truth too complex for any actual human or computer to derive, but derivable in principle given unbounded time and resources. I think this is clearly knowable; "in principle" should cover idealized agents.

What I need is a principle, or a small family of candidates, that distinguishes Case 2 (potential failure) from Cases 1, 3, 4, and 5 (not failures). Specifically, does the key lie in (a) the modal base—physical versus logical possibility, (b) the unity of the knowing subject—individual versus distributed, (c) the transparency or accessibility of the epistemic path, or (d) a distinction between "knowable-by-some-actual-type-of-agent" and "knowable-in-some-possible-world"? For each principle, state it precisely, identify which case motivates each clause, give its verdict on a case I haven't mentioned—say, a true statement hidden in quantum noise that could be extracted only with technology requiring resources exceeding the observable universe—and be explicit about which of my five verdicts it must overturn. I'm not asking for a bibliography or reading list, just the conceptual architecture and where it forces me to revise my intuitions.
