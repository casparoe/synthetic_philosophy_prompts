I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. At a recent technical meetup, two colleagues — neither of them philosophers — had a sustained disagreement about split-brain patients and whether one body can genuinely host more than one conscious mind. I found both sides persuasive and could not adjudicate them myself. The exchange ran roughly as follows.

Maya, who works with neuroscience datasets, argued that once the corpus callosum is severed, the left and right hemispheres behave like independent agents. In standard experimental conditions, the left hemisphere can verbally report an object shown only to the right visual field, while the right hemisphere — which controls the left hand — can guide that hand to point at a different object shown to the left field, with no verbal acknowledgement of contradiction. She said this is not a mere communication lag but two separate streams of awareness, analogous to running two virtual machines on one hypervisor with no shared memory bus between them.

Leo, who designs distributed systems, replied that this view ignores everyday unity. Subcortical connections remain intact, emotional responses are integrated, and outside the laboratory the patient behaves as one person. He modelled the situation as a single logical service with partitioned nodes: occasional split-brain artefacts occur, but the underlying architecture is one system with eventual consistency, not two users on shared hardware.

Maya then raised alien-hand phenomena and contradictory-behaviour tests. In some documented cases the left hand undoes what the right hand has just completed, and in split-brain experiments the two hemispheres can maintain contradictory beliefs about the same situation — for instance, the left hemisphere denies seeing an object that the right hemisphere has just directed the left hand to retrieve. She claimed Leo’s ‘eventual consistency’ analogy fails because distributed databases still possess conflict-resolution rules and a single source of truth; the split-brain patient has no arbitration mechanism whatsoever, with each hemisphere genuinely unaware of the other’s representations. Therefore, she concluded, it is not one partitioned system but two distinct systems that happen to share a body.

Leo conceded the absence of a central arbiter, yet insisted that unity does not require arbitration. He pointed out that shared biological origin, continuous bodily interaction, and a common learning history bind the hemispheres together — more like separate microservices in a monorepo that share an underlying database and deployment pipeline, even if their APIs are independent. He also warned against treating laboratory-induced contradictions as metaphysically decisive, comparing it to judging a network’s true architecture solely by its behaviour during a forced partition test.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves above — Maya’s opening, Leo’s reply, Maya’s rebuttal, and Leo’s final response — rate the move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then state who is more right overall regarding the unity of consciousness in split-brain cases, and identify one significant consideration that both parties overlook, preferably one that does not depend on specialised philosophical literature but that a thoughtful non-specialist ought to take seriously. Please write your response in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
