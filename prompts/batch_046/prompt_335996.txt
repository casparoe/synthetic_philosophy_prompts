I am a retired experimental physicist who, after leaving active laboratory work, have begun auditing an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar at a nearby university; in conversations with the instructor over coffee, I have noticed that students can recite von Neumann's comparison of brains and computers with ease, yet they treat the brain's analog noise as a mere engineering obstacle rather than as a feature with philosophical consequences for mental representation. For the seminar's final examination, I wish to set a 90-minute essay question that forces students to work inside von Neumann's posthumous lectures published as The Computer and the Brain, specifically confronting what I shall call the statistical reliability thesis: the claim that neural computation achieves logical determinism not through the precise gate fidelity characteristic of von Neumann's digital automata, but through the statistical convergence of massively parallel, noisy analog channels. The question must require students to apply this thesis to a philosophical crux I am framing as the determinacy condition on mental representation—the requirement that a representational state possess a definite, discrete content despite being physically implemented in a substrate that is, at the neural level, analog and probabilistic—and to evaluate whether accommodating the statistical reliability thesis forces a revision of the standard computational theory of mind, or whether that theory can survive by rigorously distinguishing architectural levels of description.

Please draft the exam question itself, suitable for graduate students who have read the primary text but should be unable to answer by summary alone; then provide a model answer of approximately 600 words that articulates the statistical reliability thesis precisely, applies it to the determinacy condition without generic functionalist slogans, and evaluates its impact on computationalism. Finally, supply a marking scheme totaling 25 marks, organized around five must-hit analytical moves, with the point value of each move and a brief one-line note on the characteristic failure candidates exhibit there; the scheme should clearly separate an A-range response, which navigates the tension between analog implementation and discrete representational content, from a B-range response, which describes von Neumann's hybrid architecture correctly yet never connects it to the philosophical problem of representation. The prompt should not request bibliographies, citation lists, or further reading recommendations, since the task is conceptual analysis rather than scholarly apparatus.
