I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science and epistemology. A colleague summarized the regress debate for me as follows: foundationalists halt justification with basic beliefs that need no further support, which in science correspond to brute calibration constants—such as defined physical constants or primary measurement standards—that we accept by convention and do not justify further. Coherentists reject basic beliefs and hold that justification is holistic and circular, which in a laboratory means calibrating instruments only against one another in a closed loop; this preserves consistency, my colleague claimed, but makes it impossible in principle to detect systematic drift shared across all instruments. Infinitists demand an endless, non-circular chain of reasons for every belief, which in experimental terms means no measurement ever reaches a terminating ground, so the view is best treated as a skeptical reductio rather than a live philosophical option. From this, my colleague concluded that foundationalism alone is philosophically defensible for actual scientific practice, while coherentism and infinitism are merely descriptions of temporary practical shortcuts rather than serious epistemological positions. The analogy to instrument calibration feels illuminating but suspiciously tidy; is this summary fair? Separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what defenders of each view would deny, take a committed position on whether the conclusion that foundationalism is uniquely suited to science survives correction, and present your answer in American English as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
