I'm preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends what she calls a "structural-framing" account of valid consent. On her view, deception vitiates consent only when it misrepresents the institutional or power structure within which an interaction occurs, not when it concerns incidental features of the act itself; coercion is present only when refusal would trigger a "disproportionate structural penalty" relative to the agent's baseline social position, rather than any significant personal cost; and competence is graded along an "epistemic-access gradient" that measures a subject's grasp of the future obligations the act generates, rather than being assessed through a binary rational-autonomy threshold. She tends to deflect objections by insisting that critics are conflating her structural conditions with traditional psychological ones. Write me a sequence of five interview questions that makes this deflection progressively harder to deploy. Each question should target one of the three pillars—deception, coercion, or competence—anticipate her standard reply, and include a direct follow-up that forces her to specify the boundary conditions of her view rather than retreating to the distinction between structural and psychological analysis. The sequence should build in difficulty, and it should conclude with a question about what observable institutional feature or empirical finding could in principle count against the claim that structural framing, rather than individual psychology, is the correct locus of consent-validity. Please keep the questions substantive and avoid generic biographical or methodological openings.
