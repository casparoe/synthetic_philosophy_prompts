As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been working through Bernard Williams's integrity objection—particularly the idea, often summarized as one thought too many, that saving one's spouse out of impartial calculation misses the point. I keep framing moral reasoning as analogous to hard-coding a priority versus evaluating it at runtime, and I constructed an argument that now strikes me as proving integrity impossible. Here it is:

(1) If I save my partner because an impartial calculus favors it, then I have one thought too many; the legitimate ground is simply that she is my partner.

(2) But to treat the fact that she is my partner as authoritative rather than arbitrary, I must be able to justify excluding competing impartial reasons. That justification just is a weighing of my commitment against those reasons.

(3) So maintaining integrity requires me to have already performed the comparative evaluation that (1) says corrupts the decision.

(4) Therefore, integrity is available only if I never reflectively justify my commitments—if I hold them blindly.

(5) Yet blind, unreflective attachment is not integrity; it is dogmatism.

(6) So integrity demands both that I weigh my commitments and that I never weigh them.

(7) Hence integrity is impossible.

I want to keep (1) and (5) if possible. Which premise or inference should I reject, why did it seem plausible, and what do defenders of ground projects versus critics of the objection say about the cost?
