I help manage a small wildlife sanctuary, and a bureaucratic form has turned into something that feels like a conceptual puzzle. Our funding agreement asks us to report how many distinct species we protect, with each species carrying its own budget line. In our wetlands we have two frog populations: one is green and spotted, the other brown and smooth, with different sizes and mating calls. Where the wetlands overlap they interbreed without difficulty, and the offspring are fully fertile with either group. Separately, there is an island population that looks exactly like the brown frogs, but it has been geographically isolated for generations; nobody knows whether it could still interbreed if brought together. To complicate matters, several of our protected plants reproduce only by sending out runners and cloning themselves—there is no sexual reproduction at all—yet the form still demands that we list them as species. Among our staff, three ways of drawing boundaries have come up. One view is that a species is just a group that can exchange genes and produce fertile young together, which would merge the two frog populations but leave the island group uncertain. Another view holds that visible and behavioral differences are what matter, which might split the green and brown frogs despite their hybridization. A third view, suggested by a volunteer who reads about evolutionary theory, is that a species is not a “kind” like gold or water but more like a single, long-lived individual—an extended lineage—so counting species is like counting branches on a family tree rather than sorting coins by metal. Each approach gives a different number for our frogs, and the cloning plants seem to break all three. I am not asking for a taxonomy of every frog; I want to know whether there is a genuinely correct answer hidden behind these rules, whether they are just practical tools suited to different purposes, or whether the very question “how many species do you have?” rests on a mistake when we apply it to conservation planning. How should I think about this?
