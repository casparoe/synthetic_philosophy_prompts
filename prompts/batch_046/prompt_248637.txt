I'm trying to sort out whether famines are basically natural disasters that politics makes worse, or politcal crimes that happen to involve weather. For my development ethics course I read Sen's take on Bengal 1943 — that rice was available but people lost entitlements because of wartime priorities and market failures — and it made me think the "natural disaster" framing is mostly wrong. But then I start testing my gut intutions on other cases and it gets messy.

Here are my verdicts. The Bengal famine: not a natural disaster, it's a politcal crime, because aggregate food was there and the British administration made choices that destroyed access. The Irish famine in the 1840s: this one feels different. There was a blight, yes, but grain was still being exported and relief was half-hearted, so I'd still call it a politcal crime, though the natural trigger makes it feel less pure than Bengal. The Great Leap Forward: obviously politcal, no drought excuse, just bad policy and false reports. But then there's the 1984 Ethiopia case — drought plus Mengistu blocking aid to rebel regions — and I want to say that's also a politcal crime, yet it involves both weather and deliberate starvation. Meanwhile, imagine a well-meaning democracy hit by a sudden cyclone that wipes out roads; they have grain in warehouses but literally can't move it in time, and some people starve. That one feels like a real natural disaster with politcal incompetence, not a crime. Or take a dictator who lets an ethnic minority starve during a drought, but the drought is severe; I still call it a crime, but I'm not sure if I'm reacting to the intent or just the preventability.

So is there one principle that sorts all of these? Maybe something like "a famine is a politcal crime whenever the starvation results from institutional choices that violate a baseline entitlement that was technically feasible to protect"? But that might make the democracy-with-cyclone case come out wrong, or it might over-include. I also wonder about a case I haven't listed: what if a government knows a drought is coming, has the resources, but delays action because of bureaucratic gridlock rather than malice? Where does that fall? Can you state a principle — or maybe two competing ones — that captures my pattern, say which of my verdicts has to be overturned if we want simplicity, and tell me what it predicts for that bureaucratic-delay case? Also please say whether the principle treats the Irish famine and the Great Leap Forward as the same kind of wrong, or different kinds.
