Please explain the conceptual tension between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function by focusing on a class of cases I will call lineage-orphan functions. A lineage-orphan function arises when a trait in an organism was originally shaped by selection in a different evolutionary lineage—for example, through horizontal gene transfer or long-term symbiotic integration—and now makes a stable causal contribution to the organism's current physiology, yet the organism's own selective history never favored the trait for that contribution. First, articulate why a strict etiological account seems compelled either to deny that the trait has a proper function in the present organism or to stretch the notion of selection across lineages; then explain how a causal-role account assigns function in these same cases without that historical constraint. Next, identify the exact normative divergence between the two views: specifically, whether the trait's failure in the present organism should be classified as a malfunction (the loss of something the organism is supposed to do) or merely as the disappearance of an externally acquired capacity. Conclude by noting which conceptual commitments—about the individuation of organisms, the boundaries of selection, or the normativity of dysfunction—must be resolved to adjudicate between the accounts. Do not provide citations, references to named authors, or a bibliography; treat the issue as a conceptual problem that can be addressed through reasoning alone.
