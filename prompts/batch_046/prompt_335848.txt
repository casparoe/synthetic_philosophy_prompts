I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy in earnest, and I have become struck by a recurring pattern in conceptual reform for which I lack a name. Consider a long-standing theoretical boundary—say, between what counts as part of a system and what counts as its surroundings, or between a functional description and a mechanistic one. Over time anomalies accumulate, and reformers argue that the boundary is an arbitrary relic, a fence that obstructs progress. They dismantle it in favor of a more unified account. The new account resolves the anomalies, yet gradually one notices that the old boundary was doing silent work: it was quietly preventing certain kinds of mistaken inference or misapplication that the unified account now permits, because nothing in the new framework replaces the implicit constraint. The reformers never mapped the fence's protective function before tearing it down; they assumed its presence was mere historical inertia. I see analogues in philosophical debates—perhaps in the erosion of distinctions between mental and physical description, or in eliminativist programs that discard folk concepts without replacing their regulatory role. This feels related to Chesterton's image of the fence and to Burke's idea that inherited distinctions often embody wisdom exceeding any single reasoner's grasp, but those ideas are usually applied to social institutions or moral norms, not to the architecture of theories. Is there an established term—perhaps in philosophy of science or epistemology—for this specific failure mode: the abolition of a conceptual distinction whose implicit constraining function was never articulated, leaving a hidden vulnerability visible only after the distinction is gone? If there is no name, should it be treated as a distinct variant of the Chesterton-Burke phenomenon applied to conceptual frameworks rather than to practices? I want a committed answer: take a definite position on whether this is an unnamed extension of those ideas, whether it merits an independent concept, and precisely how it relates to Burkean prescription and Chesterton's fence, defending your identification with reasons rather than surveying options and leaving the choice to me.
