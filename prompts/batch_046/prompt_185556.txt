I have been following debates about democratic backsliding in Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and even the United States, and I noticed something puzzling about the way international monitors and journalists apply the label. They almost always point to concrete institutional moves—stacking constitutional courts, taking over public broadcasters, changing electoral rules—while they rarely call a country a backslider when its formal institutions remain technically intact but the shared understanding that political opponents have legitimate standing gradually collapses. I have started calling this second pattern normative hollowing, a kind of erosion that leaves the procedural shell untouched. Given this practice, what conception of democracy are these assessments implicitly relying on? Must they assume that democracy is essentially a set of institutional mechanisms that can be evaluated on their own, independent of the civic culture and mutual recognition that make those mechanisms meaningful? Or is there a way for a practice-based or proceduralist view to justify the same emphasis without denying the importance of norms? I am also struck by an apparent tension: the same reports often discuss rising polarization and declining trust in democratic values, which sound like symptoms of normative hollowing, yet they treat these as warning signs rather than as backsliding itself. Does that mean the practice actually presupposes a single coherent theory, or is it philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival accounts but applied inconsistently? And if we decided that normative hollowing should count as backsliding in its own right, what specific changes would be required in how monitors gather evidence and draw their conclusions?
