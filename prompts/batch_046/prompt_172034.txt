I'm leading a single ninety-minute session for a university reading group—mostly humanities undergraduates with no prior exposure to Kierkegaard—on his claim that the modern press does not merely spread errors but structurally creates a new social abstraction he calls "the public." I want them to see that the danger is formal, not content-based: an anonymous, simultaneous aggregation that can hold contradictory opinions without any concrete individual being committed to any of them. To prevent the session from collapsing into a generic "social media is bad" conversation, I will anchor everything in one carrying case drawn from 1840s Copenhagen: a newspaper publishing, in the same issue, vehement letters both for and against a proposed church reform, with no named author accountable and no reader compelled to adopt either position. I need a teaching plan rather than an explanation. In what precise order must I introduce (a) the distinction between a physical crowd and this abstract public, (b) the mechanism of anonymity, (c) the leveling of qualitative distinction into quantitative opinion, and (d) the press's specific role in manufacturing the public's existence—so that each layer depends on the previous one? For the carrying example, what properties must it possess and what properties must it lack, so that it illuminates the formal architecture rather than functioning as mere historical color? The audience will almost certainly form two misconceptions in sequence—first, that Kierkegaard is simply an elitist attacking democratic participation, and second, that he is complaining about misinformation or propaganda—so at exactly which points in the dependency chain should I head each one off? What single check can I pose at the end to determine whether they have grasped the abstraction itself, as opposed to merely assenting that anonymity feels unhealthy? Finally, what should I explicitly set aside—likely the full theological weight of the "single individual"—and how do I frame that omission so it does not feel like a missing foundation?
