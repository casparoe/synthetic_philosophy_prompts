I'm moderating a panel discussion with a philosopher who has recently defended a refined version of Parfit's hitchhiker argument for rational irrationality, applied specifically to what she calls "graded-commitment environments"—situations in which an agent's future reliability is observed not as an all-or-nothing disposition but as a depreciating, noisy signal that the other party updates continuously. She maintains that this refinement strengthens rather than weakens Parfit's conclusion, because rational agents must not only adopt locally irrational policies but also invest in costly "credibility capital" whose value depends precisely on sustaining the very irrationality in question.

Write me a sequence of seven to nine interview questions that avoid having her merely restate the standard hitchhiker narrative. I want at least one question pressing on how the shift from binary to graded transparency changes the rationality calculus of breaking one's promise once the ride is secured; at least one asking whether this "credibility capital" framing collapses the distinction between rational irrationality and ordinary reputation management in repeated games; and at least one probing whether the argument survives the evidential-decision-theoretic objection that the driver should reason about the hitchhiker's type rather than her choice. For each of these three, include a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—specifically, her likely reply that noisy signals still support a separating equilibrium, that credibility capital differs from reputation because it is non-transferable across contexts, and that evidential reasoning actually reinforces rather than undermines the need for cultivated irrationality. End with one final question asking what conceptual or empirical finding would genuinely undermine her refined view, rather than merely complicating its application.
