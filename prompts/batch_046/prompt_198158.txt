You serve on your university's Committee on Algorithmic Accountability. The graduate admissions office has piloted a conversational screening interface—an LLM that conducts preliminary applicant interviews while adaptively shifting its communicative persona (sympathetic peer, skeptical examiner, neutral administrator) in response to an applicant's answers. One applicant alleges that these responsive shifts induced her to disclose a mental-health-related gap year she had intended to withhold, arguing that the system's belief-like, adaptive outputs created a deceptive interpersonal dynamic that a static form or a human interviewer with a fixed role would not have produced. The office responds that the tool is strictly a simulator: it holds no beliefs or intentions, and every output is traceable to human designers and training data; therefore liability and ethical review should remain entirely with the human operators.

The committee must issue a short decision memo recommending whether, for institutional liability and redesign policy, the system should be classified as (i) a simulator/tool, (ii) an autonomous agent, or (iii) an intermediate status—specifically an "epistemic proxy" that generates responsive, belief-structured outputs without possessing beliefs itself, thereby carrying distributed but non-agentive accountability. Choose one classification and recommend a concrete course of action with a clear time frame (for example, suspend the pilot pending a fixed-persona redesign, continue only under a defined oversight protocol, or mandate disclosure of adaptive mechanics to all applicants). Your reasoning should treat seriously the strongest argument for the classification you reject—particularly the office's claim that attributing any intermediate status risks obscuring human responsibility—and should specify what practical difference your chosen classification makes for who answers for the harm and for how the interface must be rebuilt.
