I work as a software engineer without any formal training in philosophy, though I read philosophy of science and cognitive psychology in my spare time. Recently, in a discussion with colleagues about why large-scale system failures always seem inevitable in post-mortems yet were rarely anticipated beforehand, I noticed a recurring explanatory pattern for which I do not have a precise label. I am writing to ask whether this pattern corresponds to an established concept, or whether it requires a new name with clear boundary conditions.

The pattern works as follows. Once an outcome is known—whether a catastrophic service outage or a major historical technological decision—analysts reconstruct the preceding events into a smooth, linear causal chain, so that each step appears to make the next one probable and the final result feels almost predetermined. At the same time, these analysts assert that the warning signs were obvious and that competent observers should have predicted the failure all along. The coherence of the reconstructed narrative and the retrospective claim of high predictability appear to reinforce each other: the more elegant the story looks in hindsight, the stronger the implicit judgment that the outcome was foreseeable. I have observed this interaction in internal engineering reviews and in broader historical accounts of technology. I am aware of separate literatures concerning how knowing an outcome distorts our assessment of prior probabilities, and concerning how people impose coherent story structures on otherwise disconnected events. What I want to understand is whether philosophers or psychologists have specifically studied this mutual reinforcement—the way retrospective storytelling amplifies retrospective overconfidence about foresight in explanatory practice. Before you answer, please adopt the role of an expert epistemologist and philosopher of science. Begin by briefly brainstorming the relevant conceptual distinctions, then provide a polished, structured analysis that addresses whether this interaction has an established name, how it differs from the two separate tendencies mentioned above, and, if no established name exists, how one might define it precisely with illustrative boundary cases.
