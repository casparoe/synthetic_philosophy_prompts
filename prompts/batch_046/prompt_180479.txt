Write a deadpan parody of a corporate End-User License Agreement—approximately 450 words, in standard legal boilerplate—for a subscription service named "Esse-Perceptum" that guarantees the continued existence of a subscriber's personal property. The agreement must never break character or wink at the reader; the humor should come entirely from translating Berkeley's master argument into contractual logic that is actually consistent with it, not from inventing absurdities foreign to the view.

Specifically, translate these real features into clauses: (1) the move from "it is impossible to conceive an object existing unperceived" to "therefore an unperceived object is impossible"; (2) the reliance on continuous divine perception as a backstop for objects outside human observation; and (3) the treatment of conceptual impossibility as ontological nullity. For instance, include a "Conception Warranty" stating that any subscriber attempt to imagine an unobserved chair automatically triggers observation by the system's omnipresent observer (the "Divine Perception Service Level Agreement"); an "Impossibility Clause" declaring that because no subscriber can form a concept of property existing outside all perception, such property cannot exist and the company bears no liability for its disappearance; and a "Dispute Resolution" section requiring that all claims about missing objects be resolved by checking whether the object was conceived as unperceived during arbitration, in which case the claim is void because its premise is conceptually impossible.

Keep the irony sustained and legible: a reader familiar with Berkeley should recognize that every clause maps onto a genuine feature of his idealism and the master argument, while a general reader should grasp that legal formalism is being used to expose the strange consequence of treating conceptual limits as ontological ones. Do not name Berkeley in the document itself; let the structure do the work. Include at least one clause addressing the problem of other minds—whether fellow subscribers can serve as witnesses to an object's existence—and end with a "Severability" clause asserting that if any provision depends on an unperceived object, that provision is null not through legal error but because its subject never existed.
