I just spent several hours arguing with a close friend about whether analogies between the fall of the Roman Republic and modern democratic decay are intellectually serious or just rhetorical shortcuts. She insisted that Rome lacked democracy and a written constitution, so any comparison to contemporary backsliding is a category error; I countered that the parallel lies not in populist personalities but in a structural feature both contexts share—what I am calling, provisionally, a crisis of meta-normative arbitration, where the rules for resolving institutional conflict are themselves contested. I am now applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal that turns this disagreement into a tractable research project.

The core idea is this: rather than treating Rome’s collapse as a story of ambitious individuals violating norms, I want to examine whether the Republic’s fundamental vulnerability was the absence of any undisputed authority—legal or customary—to adjudicate conflicts between the Senate, popular assemblies, and tribunes. I then want to ask whether modern democracies with formal supreme courts have actually solved this arbitration problem, or whether they have relocated it into constitutional interpretation, where strategic norm-stretching within legal bounds reproduces the same ambiguity Rome faced. The question should have a determinate answer: either modern constitutional courts provide a genuinely different institutional architecture, or they do not, and the Roman analogy applies at a deeper structural level than usually recognized.

Please write the proposal with the following elements: a precise research question that avoids both the superficial strongman analogy and the dismissive response that Rome was not a democracy; an explanation of why existing historical and political-theoretical treatments leave this specific arbitration question open; a month-by-month method describing what I will actually do—conceptual analysis of Roman institutional conflicts, comparative examination of modern constitutional interpretation crises, and a framework for distinguishing technical legal resolution from substantive normative settlement; the possible conclusions and the specific considerations that would decide between them, including the honest possibility that the analogy collapses; what a failed project would look like; and a realistic assessment of scope for one year, including what part of the comparison is likely to remain incomplete. Do not include a bibliography or citation list—I will supply those myself. Reference arguments and positions in general terms only. Keep the tone formal and appropriate for a fellowship committee.
