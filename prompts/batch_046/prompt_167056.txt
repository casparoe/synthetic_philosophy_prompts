I am preparing an argument for my school's philosophy seminar on value pluralism, and I have constructed a chain of reasoning that now worries me because it seems to dissolve moral responsibility in precisely the contexts where we feel it most urgently. Here is the structure: (1) When values are genuinely incommensurable—following Isaiah Berlin's pluralism and Ruth Chang's analysis of hard choices—there exists no common metric that permits rational ranking between them. (2) If no such metric exists, then selecting one value over another cannot be justified by reasons; the decision is at best expressive or arbitrary, not rationally accessible. (3) Moral responsibility requires that an agent's choice be responsive to reasons rather than to brute preference or chance. (4) Transitional justice is a paradigmatic case of incommensurability: peace and retributive justice are treated as incomparable goods, as seen in debates surrounding South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission. (5) Therefore, a leader who grants amnesty rather than pursuing prosecution has not made a reasoned choice, and consequently cannot be held responsible for the harm that amnesty may leave unaddressed. (6) By extension, all criticism of such a decision collapses into mere aesthetic disagreement about the leader's commitments, not genuine moral evaluation. This feels wrong—especially when amnesty is granted corruptly rather than sincerely—yet I want to preserve the idea that hard choices are genuinely hard, and I am reluctant to abandon premises (1) and (2). Where exactly does the argument fail? Please identify the weakest step, explain why it looked plausible, and indicate what I am paying if I reject it. Furthermore, please state clearly what specific consideration—perhaps a distinction between incommensurability and mere incomparability, or a particular account of how commitment generates reasons—would lead you to revise your diagnosis, and where in this reasoning you think the error is most likely to be hidden.
