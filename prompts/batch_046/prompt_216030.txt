I am a philosophy professor advising a state legislative staff office, and I need a reusable decision procedure for a senior legislative attorney who must repeatedly evaluate proposed modifications to capital-sentencing statutes—roughly once per session—under tight deadlines, with no moral philosopher on staff, and with recommendations going to a committee that includes both retentionist and abolitionist members. The procedure should operationalize the interaction of three standard considerations—retributive desert (whether the offense merits death as proportionate moral response), deterrence (whether the proposed rule produces a measurable ex ante reduction in the relevant offense class), and epistemic risk (the non-negligible probability that an innocent defendant will be executed given the proposed evidentiary and appellate safeguards).

Crucially, do not treat these as weights on a single scale to be balanced. Instead, translate them into a sequential three-gate architecture arranged in fixed order: retributive eligibility first, then deterrent efficacy, then epistemic risk. Each gate must have explicit entry conditions, explicit failure conditions that direct a specific recommendation (retain, reject, or refer), and a defined logical relation to the others. Only if all three gates are cleared does the procedure reach a values-override zone in which no further philosophical filter applies and the decision belongs to the legislature’s moral judgment. This ordering is not arbitrary: placing epistemic risk last rather than first presupposes that fallibility concerns, however grave, operate within a framework that has already established both that the punishment is deserved and that it serves a legitimate social function.

Please organize your response exactly as follows:

1. Role, Constraints, and Decision Context (approximately 120 words): Define the attorney’s practical setting, frequency of use, time pressure, audience, and what counts as a usable recommendation.

2. Conceptual Translation: The Three-Gate Architecture (approximately 250 words): Explain precisely how each philosophical consideration becomes a gate with entry conditions, failure conditions, and the reason the ordering matters—specifically, why epistemic risk is placed last and what that placement assumes about the relation between justice and institutional fallibility.

3. The Procedure: A Reusable Step-by-Step Checklist (approximately 350 words): Present the flowchart in words—the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, the exact points where the procedure stops with a recommendation, and the point where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Include an explicit misfire flag: describe a case in which the gate ordering will yield an answer that conflicts with common intuition, and state how the attorney should report it without disguising the result.

4. Worked Applications (approximately 500 words): Apply the procedure to three proposals:
   a) Expanding capital eligibility to large-scale drug-trafficking homicides;
   b) Retaining capital punishment for aggravated first-degree murder but adding mandatory post-conviction DNA review and a ten-year appellate window;
   c) A total moratorium pending an independent innocence-commission report.
   One of these must be the case where the procedure produces an answer you expect the committee will dislike; state explicitly what that answer is and explain why the gate ordering generates it.

5. Limits, Judgment Overrides, and Honest Misfires (approximately 200 words): Identify the hard cases the procedure cannot settle—ambiguous evidentiary thresholds, contested deterrence data, or theories of moral desert that diverge on proportionality. Mark these clearly as judgment calls dressed up as procedural steps. State precisely what the procedure cannot do, including whether it can resolve whether retributive punishment is ever morally justified in principle.
