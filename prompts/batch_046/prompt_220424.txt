I am a PhD student in philosophy working on nineteenth-century political thought, and I am preparing a consistency check for a seminar presentation on Kierkegaard's social philosophy. Over the past year I have developed the following cluster of commitments, which I think captures a defensible middle path, but I am unsure whether they actually cohere.

First, I take Kierkegaard's concept of the public not as a demographic category but as an ontological abstraction that dissolves individual responsibility into an anonymous aggregate—what he describes as the leveling process—where commitment is replaced by detached observation. Second, I read his critique of the press as targeting precisely the structural conditions that make this abstraction sustainable: the press generates the illusion of universal engagement without requiring any particular individual to stand behind a view or bear its consequences. Third, I believe this analysis applies with heightened force to algorithmically curated digital publics, where personalization fragments shared reality while maintaining the phantom of collective opinion, producing what I am inclined to call hyper-leveling. Fourth, I nevertheless hold that democratic legitimacy requires some form of public discourse and institutional transparency, so a complete withdrawal into the position of the single individual is politically inadequate and risks collapsing Kierkegaard's stance into an apolitical aestheticism. Fifth, I reject interpretations that cast Kierkegaard as an elitist contemptuous of the masses; his objection, on my view, is to the structural diffusion of accountability, not to popular participation as such. Sixth, I maintain that the appropriate ethical response is not retreat but a practice I call engaged inwardness: participating in public institutions while maintaining transparent ownership of one's commitments, thereby resisting leveling without abandoning the public sphere altogether.

My concern is that these commitments may not hang together. Specifically, if algorithmic media intensifies leveling to a degree that makes genuine commitment structurally impossible within them, then my call for engaged inwardness within those same institutions looks unstable. At the same time, my rejection of elitism seems to sit uneasily with my claim that democratic legitimacy requires public discourse, since if the public is inherently an abstraction that erodes responsibility, it is unclear how it could serve as a foundation for legitimate collective decision-making. Meanwhile, my interpretation of the press as generating illusion rather than falsehood might appear to conflict with my acceptance of democratic transparency, which seems to presuppose that public information can be genuinely shared rather than merely observed.

Could you audit this set? I want to know which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled with minimal cost, and what the least damaging revision would be. Please rank the tensions by severity, and indicate for each whether the resolution requires giving up a substantive commitment or only refining its formulation. I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations to specific journal articles—just an assessment of whether my position is jointly tenable.
