As a novelist drafting a moral framework for a character who treats suffering-focused ethics with absolute logical rigor, I have constructed the following chain of reasoning about negative utilitarianism, but I find the conclusion so extreme—seeming to license universal extinction and to undermine moral agency itself—that I am convinced a hidden slide, perhaps a quantifier shift or an equivocation between comparative world-evaluation and intra-world prescription, must occur somewhere in the steps. I would like to know precisely which premise or transition fails, why it initially seemed plausible, and what abandoning it costs the broader view.

(1) Negative utilitarianism holds that suffering is the sole intrinsic moral evil, while happiness is axiologically neutral: it is valuable only instrumentally or not at all, and its presence never improves the moral ledger.

(2) Consequently, a life containing both happiness H and suffering S makes exactly the same contribution to global value as a life containing suffering S alone—namely, a negative contribution of magnitude S.

(3) A counterfactual world in which that being never exists contains neither that happiness nor that suffering, hence contributes zero to the moral ledger.

(4) Therefore, for any being whose life includes any suffering whatsoever, the world without that being is strictly morally superior to the world with that being.

(5) Since every actual life contains at least some suffering, this applies universally.

(6) It follows that the best possible world is one entirely devoid of sentient beings, because any addition of a sentient being introduces suffering that could have been avoided by non-creation.

(7) But if no sentient beings exist, then no moral agents exist, and therefore there are no obligations—including the obligation to minimize suffering—making the theory self-undermining.

(8) Applying the reasoning within an existing world yields a parallel result: for any suffering being B, eliminating B removes B’s suffering; since happiness is neutral, there is no moral cost to B’s absence that could offset the gain.

(9) Thus the theory appears to require not merely anti-natalism but the active elimination of all suffering-bearing beings.

(10) Yet proponents of suffering-focused ethics typically reject both universal extinction and active elimination, insisting instead that we should reduce suffering within lives while permitting their continuation.

(11) I feel the argument exploits a confusion between evaluating worlds counterfactually and prescribing actions within a world, but I cannot name the exact step. Which premise should I reject, and what does that rejection commit me to—must I grant happiness some positive weight, accept that moral reasons are agent-relative, or deny that non-existence is comparable to existence?
