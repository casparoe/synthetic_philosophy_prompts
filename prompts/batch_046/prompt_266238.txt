I am writing my dissertation on conceptual boundary disputes in the philosophy of science, and in the course of studying debates over artificial cognition, synthetic biology, and algorithmic art, I have repeatedly encountered an argumentative pattern that I cannot place precisely within the standard taxonomy of fallacies or conceptual errors. The pattern runs as follows. A speaker grants that an entity possesses all the functional and structural properties that ordinarily define membership in a category—say, that a system processes information adaptively and learns from feedback, or that a molecule replicates, metabolizes, and responds to selection pressures—yet insists that the entity is not a member of the category at all because it also possesses, or lacks, one further property that is incidental to the category's governing purpose: for instance, that the system lacks biological consciousness, or that the replication occurs without an evolutionary history. Crucially, the speaker does not treat this as establishing a legitimate subcategory—"this is a special kind of intelligence"—but as a flat disqualification: "therefore this is not intelligence," which then licenses treating the entity as falling entirely outside the norms that apply to members of the class. I have observed this move deployed symmetrically by defenders and critics alike. What is distinctive is that the feature invoked is never presented as part of the original, explicit definition; it is introduced ad hoc precisely when the category faces pressure to expand, yet it is treated as if it had always been a necessary condition for membership.

Does this pattern have an established name in logic, philosophy of language, or conceptual analysis? I am familiar with the fallacy of division, with various forms of essentialism, and with ordinary equivocation, but none of these seem to capture the specific structure I am describing: the defensive activation of a hidden necessary condition that should, if anything, specify a subtype rather than negate class membership altogether, combined with the implicit shift between a functional or operational definition (which includes the challenging case) and a substantial or historical definition (which excludes it). If no precise term exists, I would value a careful characterization that distinguishes the fallacious deployments from legitimate taxonomic exclusions—where the excluding feature is part of the explicit criterion from the outset—and from mere ambiguity, since the speaker typically uses the category term consistently, only with an unstated supplementary restriction that is revealed only under argumentative pressure. I am also interested in whether any tradition in the philosophy of language has analyzed category terms as carrying dormant necessary conditions that can be activated defensively in this way, and if so, whether that tradition treats the move as a logical error or as a feature of how natural-language classification actually operates.
