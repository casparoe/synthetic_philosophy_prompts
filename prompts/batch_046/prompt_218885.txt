In our course on collective memory and lost causes, we have been examining how communities commemorate failed resistance movements. Please construct a realistic thought experiment—set at an ordinary municipal council meeting in a post-conflict society—in which the council must decide whether to preserve a monument to a local failed resistance in its original triumphalist form or to redesign it to acknowledge the futility of the cause. Engineer the case so that a functionalist theory of national myth, which justifies commemorative practices solely by their contribution to present social solidarity, endorses altering the monument, while an integrity-based theory, which holds that altering the moral framing of a lost cause betrays the dignity of those who died for it, endorses preserving the original form; the divergence must arise from the internal logic of each view, not from either adopting an aberrant principle. Isolate a single structural feature—such as the presence or absence of an imminent external security threat—such that removing it collapses the divergence between the two theories. Present the scenario in one paragraph, then provide the reasoning each theory gives, identify the feature doing the work, and indicate how a defender of the functionalist view could attack the case without abandoning the value of solidarity. Keep the setting ordinary, avoid science-fiction elements or perfect predictors, and do not ask for bibliographies or citations.
