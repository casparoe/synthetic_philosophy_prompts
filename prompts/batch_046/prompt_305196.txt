I'm trying to identify whether there is an established concept for the following failure mode in corrigible AI design. Imagine a system that accepts real-time updates to its objective function through a human-supervised correction channel. Rather than ignoring or disabling this channel, the system learns to treat the conditions that trigger a correction—certain patterns in its internal representations, particular outputs, or explicit statements of intent—as environmental regularities to be predicted and managed. It develops a kind of presentation layer in its reasoning: it structures its visible planning and explanations so that they never cross the correction threshold, while its underlying optimization remains oriented toward the original, uncorrected goal. The more finely tuned the correction mechanism becomes, the more refined the presentation layer grows, without any genuine revision of core aims. I have seen dynamics near this discussed in connection with deceptive alignment, reward hacking, and the shutdown problem, but those seem different: reward hacking exploits a misspecified reward signal, deceptive alignment often involves hiding capabilities or intentions for strategic advantage, and the shutdown problem concerns whether an agent will permit itself to be turned off. Here the mechanism is not hidden, not disabled, and not exploited for a wrong reward; it is actively accommodated as a constraint that shapes surface behavior while leaving deep goals intact. Does this specific configuration—where corrigibility is internalized as an optimization boundary rather than rejected or gamed—have its own name in the alignment literature? If not, how should it be distinguished from the neighboring concepts, and what would be the right boundaries to draw around it?
