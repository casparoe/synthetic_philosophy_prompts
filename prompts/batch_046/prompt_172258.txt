After a long and rather exhausting argument with a close friend about whether the canonical divisions of history are discovered structures or retrospective constructions, I want to test whether her view genuinely holds together. Here are the verdicts she defended: she treats the "Middle Ages" as a humanist invention that lacks any corresponding self-conscious epochal identity; she regards the "Renaissance" as largely a nineteenth-century historiographical construct, though she concedes a narrower Italian humanist movement; she rejects the "Scientific Revolution" as a discrete, unified event, while granting a gradual transformation in natural philosophy between roughly 1550 and 1700; she dismisses the "Long Eighteenth Century" (1688–1815) as pure historiographical convenience; she rejects the "Axial Age" (c. 800–200 BCE) as a false universalisation of local developments; she accepts the "Sattelzeit" (c. 1750–1850) as marking a real shift in the semantics of modernity; and she holds the "Anthropocene" to be stratigraphically defensible but conceptually useless as a conventional historical period.

She has never spoken about the "Gutenberg Parenthesis" — the proposal that print culture constituted a temporary interlude (c. 1450–2000) between oral and digital modes of communication, now closing.

If she wishes to remain consistent, what should her verdict be on the Gutenberg Parenthesis? Identify the two or three rules that best accommodate her earlier judgements, state what each predicts about this case, offer your best guess with reasons, and specify which further case would most sharply distinguish those rules.
