I am a retired experimental physiologist who has only recently begun studying philosophy in any systematic way, and I have become struck by what looks like a long-stalled dispute that neither side expects to resolve. The issue is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: if pleasure is the sole intrinsic good, then every pleasure must share some feature in virtue of which it is good, yet the pleasure of tasting wine, the pleasure of solving a proof, and the pleasure of relieving an itch seem phenomenologically and structurally unlike one another. One camp insists on a uniform qualitative tone—something like a single phenomenal “pleasantness” present in all cases—while the other treats pleasure as a family-resemblance category with no deeper unity, perhaps divided further into higher and lower varieties. For generations the exchange has followed the same pattern: introspective counterexamples offered by the heterogeneity side, conceptual analyses of “feeling” offered by the uniformity side, and then a polite stalemate. From my scientific background, I notice that contemporary work distinguishes sharply between incentive salience, or “wanting,” mediated largely by dopaminergic circuits, and actual consummatory “liking,” localized in small opioid and cannabinoid hotspots, with further modulation by alliesthesia—the dependence of hedonic value on internal physiological state—so that empirically pleasure is neither one mechanism nor an entirely disconnected set. At the same time, some neuroeconomists posit a “common currency” of reward value in orbitofrontal regions, suggesting a functional unity that may not map onto any phenomenal uniformity. Given this, I wonder whether the philosophical impasse is really a hidden empirical disagreement about whether there is one reward system or many, or whether philosophers and scientists are answering different questions under the single word “pleasure”—one phenomenological and taxonomic, the other functional and mechanistic. Is the stall verbal, turning on what counts as a natural kind? Is it substantive, with the sides applying different standards of evidence—introspection versus neural measurement? Or are both sides ultimately appealing to bedrock intuitions about which cases genuinely belong in the category? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction that separates phenomenal quality from reward function, an empirical result about the unity or fragmentation of reward circuitry, a formal model of value comparison, or should we conclude that no single answer is required because “hedonism” itself fragments into several distinct theses that share only a name?
