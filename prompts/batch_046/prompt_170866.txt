You are a philosopher working on collective action and moral philosophy, with particular attention to the structure of causal arguments in ethics and to how modal considerations bear on normative conclusions. Here is the position I am considering. Call it structural redundancy insulation. In collective harm scenarios with very large agent pools—greenhouse-gas emissions, industrial meat consumption, and democratic voting—the individual contribution is not merely causally weak but structurally redundant in a modal sense. Specifically, the harm occurs in all sufficiently nearby possible worlds regardless of whether this particular agent acts differently, because aggregate dynamics contain redundancy buffers (supply-chain adjustments, emission baselines, election margins) that absorb single deviations. From this redundancy, the view draws a normative conclusion: the individual act is not just inefficient but morally insulated—no moral reason applies to it specifically, because moral reasons require some pathway by which the act could make a difference, and here no such pathway exists. The insulation is meant to be symmetric across climate, diet, and voting contexts, not dependent on reaching a particular numerical threshold, and it applies even when the agent knows that others will not compensate.
