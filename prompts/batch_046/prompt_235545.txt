I'm a software engineer with basically zero formal philosphical background, so apologies if i'm mixing up terms. I was arguing with my friends about why college is free in germany and the nordics but costs a fortune in the US and UK, and we kept circling back to the same question: what does each system actually think a person is?

In the US/UK model you take out loans that follow you for decades, and people talk about 'investing in yourself' and 'ROI on your degree.' The state doesn't pay; you buy access like you'd buy a software license. Meanwhile in germany there's no tuition (just a small semester fee), and in sweden and denmark they sometimes even pay you a stipend to study. Its weird, but it feels like they treat education like public infrastructure — roads, water, open-source libraries — something society maintains because everyone benefits from it being there.

But here's the part that confuses me: both sides claim they're being fair. The US side says its unfair to make taxpayers pay for someone else's private career boost. The nordic side says its unfair to make your future depend on your parents' bank account. So there's a hidden disagreement about what fairness even means here — is it about desert? equality of opportunity? treating people like customers vs citizens?

What theory of value or justice does each funding model actually presuppose? Does the loan model only make sense if you think education is mainly a private consumption good with individual risk, or can a public-good theorist still justify charging users? And is the free-tuition model committed to the idea that knowledge is a non-rivalrous public good like open-source code, or is it compatible with other views — maybe meritocracy, maybe social contract? I also notice the practices aren't internally consistent: in germany you can study for free but there's pressure not to take too long (they have rules about max study time), which feels like a hidden productivity assumption baked in. Meanwhile the US punishes you with interest regardless of whether you finish or even get a job.

Reconstruct the implicit philosphical commitments for both models, say which ones are strictly necessary vs just fitting well, and tell me what a policy built on the opposite view would actually have to change. And if my open-source vs proprietary analogy hides something important about how these systems actually work, call that out.
