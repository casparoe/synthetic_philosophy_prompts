My friend and I just finished a long, somewhat circular argument about whether the self is best understood as a center of narrative gravity—an explanatory attractor in the space of biographical interpretation—rather than as some underlying substance or kernel. She kept insisting that if the self is genuinely just a convergence point of narratives, then we ought to be able to split and merge selves with roughly the same ease that we split and merge stories. I tried to push back with the intuition that narrative integration is asymmetrical: once a gravitational well has formed around a particular cluster of fragments, it acquires inertia, reshaping whatever new fragments are added to it rather than being reshaped by them in turn. We never resolved whether this asymmetry is a real feature of the view or just a failure of imagination, and I want to stage an activity that makes the tension visceral rather than abstract.

I am running an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar—twelve graduate students, one ninety-minute session, meeting once. Design a structured simulation or role-play that makes the participants feel the force of the narrative-gravity view, but specifically through the problem I am calling asymmetrical narrative accretion: the idea that constructing a self from fragments is not like assembling a machine from parts, because the center, once established, pulls subsequent fragments into its orbit and resists dissolution even when contradictory evidence arrives. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that the students run into this resistance themselves—experiencing how a narrative center reshapes the data that ought to challenge it—before the facilitator names the phenomenon.

Specify the physical setting and materials (cards, envelopes, boards, or roles), the group structure and rules of play, a payoff or decision-pressure scheme that requires no real money but creates genuine stakes for how they partition or integrate fragments, and a precise timeline for the ninety minutes. Include a debrief that maps each phase of the activity onto parts of the idea—construction of the center, accretion of contradictory fragments, the attempt at partition, and the failure of clean division—while being explicit about which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce (most likely the first-person, phenomenological sense of being that gravitational center) and how you must supply it verbally. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—where students will misinterpret the rules, where the asymmetry will fail to appear, and how to recover without lecturing.
