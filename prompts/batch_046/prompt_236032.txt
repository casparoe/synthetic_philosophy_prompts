I am a novelist in the midst of drafting a multi-generational saga about a declining mill town, and I find myself repeatedly stalled by a conceptual difficulty I first encountered in a social science seminar last winter. The difficulty concerns methodological individualism and holism in historical explanation — the question of whether every social outcome must ultimately be traced to the beliefs and choices of particular people, or whether collective structures can possess genuine causal powers that are not reducible to them. Because my editor expects internal consistency in how causation is portrayed across some thirty chapters, and because I write quickly without time to consult theoretical notes, I need a reusable decision procedure: a sequence of questions I can run in about fifteen minutes before drafting any scene, which tells me whether the event in question should be narrated as the product of individual agency or of emergent structural forces.

The procedure should be organised around three successive filters. First, the filter of counterfactual substitution: would the outcome have changed if the specific individuals involved had been replaced by others with different intentions but occupying the same structural positions? Second, the filter of scale invariance: does the explanation hold when the number of actors is scaled up or down without altering the causal story? Third, the filter of temporal precedence: did the structural conditions exist prior to and independently of the individual actions that appear to have triggered them? At each stage I need to know what counts as a sufficient answer, where a negative result definitively kills one form of explanation, and where ambiguity forces me to record either explanatory leakage — an individualist account that silently borrows collective conditions — or agentic residue — a structural account that still requires particular human acts to initiate change. Please also mark the points where the procedure must stop and defer to artistic judgment rather than delivering a mechanical verdict.

Apply the procedure to three scenes from my current draft: the closure of the mill in 1983, apparently decided by a distant board of directors acting solely on market data; the spontaneous strike of 1981, in which every worker stops simultaneously yet no organiser can be named; and a scene I am particularly keen to preserve, in which the mill owner, acting from personal religious conviction, refuses a profitable contract and thereby accelerates the town's decline. In this last case I anticipate that the procedure may classify the event as individualist, which would undermine my thematic intention to show that personal virtue can be structurally destructive; I want to see precisely where that unwelcome verdict arises and whether any legitimate escape route exists within the rules of the procedure itself. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: whether it can ever genuinely settle questions of moral responsibility for collective harm, or whether it must remain purely a narrative-organisation tool that should never be mistaken for an account of historical truth.
