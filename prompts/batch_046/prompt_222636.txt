I spent much of last evening in a long argument with a close friend about Adam Smith’s account of sympathy and the impartial spectator in The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and neither of us yielded ground. We kept circling a tension that standard summaries tend to smooth over: Smith describes sympathy as an imaginative change of places with the sufferer, yet he also treats the spectator’s dispassionate judgment as normatively authoritative precisely because it is removed. My friend insisted these are compatible—the spectator is just the calibrated result of repeated sympathetic exercises—while I pressed what I called a problem of “sympathetic attenuation”: because grief and visible suffering are more communicable through expression and gesture than is the invisible effort of restraining revenge or suppressing vanity, the spectator’s emotional baseline may be structurally tilted toward passive display rather than active moral agency. If that tilt is built into sympathy’s mechanics, I argued, then the spectator’s “correction” for bias can never fully recover the weight of virtues that do not show; my friend countered that removal and comparison precisely allow the spectator to reweight sentiments by their cause rather than their intensity.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent interpreters of Smith—one defending the coherence of the spectator as an internal calibration standard, the other advancing the attenuation objection—who respond directly to each other’s points rather than talking past one another. The dialogue should work from Smith’s own distinctions, such as sympathy with the agent versus sympathy with the sufferer, propriety versus merit, and the difference between feeling a sentiment in one’s own breast and approving it in another, without invoking secondary scholarship, asking for citations, or treating either speaker as a strawman. Include a specific moment in which the calibrationist explains how the spectator corrects for initial emotional bias, followed by a counter-moment in which the attenuationist asks whether such correction can ever restore full normative weight to active virtue if sympathy’s baseline access is already weaker for restrained agency than for open grief. Both speakers should concede genuine difficulties: the calibrationist should acknowledge why visible suffering might dominate the sympathetic field, and the attenuationist should explain why Smith might still have believed the spectator achieves genuine impartiality despite that asymmetry. Keep the exchange rigorous but readable, as if two thoughtful readers were continuing our argument over coffee rather than lecturing.
