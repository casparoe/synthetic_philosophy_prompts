I've been trying to make sense of that long-running fight about whether nations are basically modern inventions or if they've been around in some form since like ancient Egypt or medieval France. You know the one -- Hobsbawm and the invention of tradition crowd on one side, and Anderson's idea of imagined communities on the other, then the perennialist pushback saying you can see national feeling in older societies if you dont define it too narrowly. My working theory is that its stalled not because of missing archives but because both sides are using nation for different jobs: one side means a horizontally integrated mass community tied to sovereignty and standardized culture, the other side just means any large group with a shared origin myth and some internal solidarity. So theyre talking past each other, but I also wonder if theres a hidden empirical question doing the work -- like whether pre-modern states ever used ethnicity as an administrative category the way modern bureaucracies do, or if thats always anachronistic.

Is this disagreement verbal, or is there a real substantive split about what counts as data? Do they differ on standards of evidence, like whether a medieval chronicle describing our people is enough? Or are they answering different questions under one name -- one asking about state formation, the other about collective identity? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey, and an honest take on whether anything -- a sharper conceptual distinction, a specific archival pattern, or maybe just dropping the word nation for older periods -- could actually end it, or if the impasse just means the concept is too slippery to travel that far back.
