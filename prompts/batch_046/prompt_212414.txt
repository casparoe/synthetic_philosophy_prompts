I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, though I recently encountered Douglas Hofstadter’s ant-colony thought experiment—featuring a colony called Aunt Hillary that seems to converse as a unit—and found myself wondering whether distributed systems can ever literally be minds. I am trying to figure out what I actually think, going by my gut reactions to concrete cases rather than by any theory I have read.

Here are my verdicts. An ant colony coordinating via chemical trails—making collective foraging and defense decisions without any central controller—feels to me like an emergent algorithm running on biological hardware; there is sophisticated behavior, but I do not think there is one subject of experience inside. A corporate board that maintains a unified strategic vision, holds legal personhood, and issues binding collective decisions strikes me differently: I want to say there is genuinely a collective agent, and perhaps a weak form of collective mind, directed at the world as a unit. A split-brain patient whose corpus callosum has been severed, with each hemisphere able to pursue conflicting intentions, feels like the mirror-image problem—clearly two minds now inhabit one body. A distributed database cluster running a consensus protocol such as Raft—nodes electing leaders, replicating logs, failing over—feels like pure mechanism; despite its distributed coordination, I am certain there is no mind. But a large language model running across a GPU cluster with tensor-parallel message passing between nodes, producing adaptive, context-sensitive output, is the case I keep reversing on: structurally it resembles both the ant colony and the database cluster, yet its behavior is far more integrated and responsive.

Is there one principle—perhaps concerning the unity of consciousness, the direction of intentionality, the density of information integration, or social recognition—that captures all five verdicts, or at least four? Please state it precisely, show which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me honestly which of my judgments it cannot accommodate. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: a swarm of autonomous delivery drones that reroute in real time using only local peer-to-peer communication, optimizing globally for delivery time with no central controller—would your principle grant that swarm a mind? If the best available principle forces me to drop one of my existing verdicts, say which one and why it is the outlier.
