The debate over Popper’s concept of verisimilitude—truth-likeness as a relation that can hold between false theories and the truth, intended to make sense of scientific progress without induction—has been largely frozen since the Miller–Tichý result showed that standard formal measures collapse: once two theories differ in false content, they become incomparable or tied, so that Newton and some arbitrary falsehood can end up equally “close” to the truth. In response, one camp has spent decades refining the formal apparatus—content-partitions, probabilistic distance metrics, relevance-weighted measures—trying to rescue a linear ordering, while another camp treats the collapse as a feature, arguing that “closer to the truth” is not a formal relation among propositions but an epistemic or pragmatic judgment about explanatory power, predictive range, or problem-solving capacity, and that the search for a universal metric was always a category mistake. Meanwhile, philosophers of science continue to invoke verisimilitude in case studies—Einstein versus Newton, Darwin versus Lamarck—without resolving whether they are using a technical term or a metaphor.

Diagnose the stall. Is this a substantive disagreement about how scientific rationality works, a verbal dispute over whether “truth-likeness” should denote a logical relation or an evaluative practice, or two groups answering different questions—one about formal representability, one about normative assessment of theories—under a single heading? Examine whether the sides share the same intuitions about canonical comparisons, or whether they differ about what counts as data: do formalists treat intuitive rankings as noise to be disciplined by logic, while critics treat logical collapse as proof that the formalization misrepresents scientific judgment? Consider whether a hidden empirical claim is doing the work—say, that scientists actually rank theories by content overlap rather than by predictive success—or whether the impasse reflects incompatible standards of explanation. Offer a concrete hypothesis about the diagnosis, presented as a hypothesis.

Then say what progress would actually look like given your diagnosis. Would it be a formal impossibility proof showing that no metric can avoid the collapse without smuggling in substantive scientific judgments? A conceptual distinction—perhaps between “proximity in logical content” and “proximity in causal structure”—that lets both camps keep their insights? An empirical study of how practicing scientists compare disconfirmed theories? Or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed, because “closer to the truth” fragments into domain-specific evaluations that resist unification?

To sharpen the diagnosis, inject the following consideration: recent work in formal epistemology has explored belief-revision frameworks using paraconsistent or relevance logics, in which false premises can be “near” the truth in some dimensions without generating explosion. Does importing such machinery address the root problem of verisimilitude—by allowing false theories to stand in graded proximity relations—or does it simply change the subject from scientific progress to logical consistency management? Use this to test whether the debate is really about the logic of falsehood or about something the formal tools were never designed to capture.
