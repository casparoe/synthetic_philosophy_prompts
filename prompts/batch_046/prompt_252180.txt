In my philosophy of politics seminar we've been going in circles for like three months over whether republican liberty is actually its own thing or just negative liberty wearing a fancier hat. The specific hang-up is this case: imagine a state where every citizen has an unconditional right to exit any institution — jobs, schools, neighborhoods — and there's also a robust court system that blocks direct coercion, but citizens have zero input into the rules that govern those institutions. One side says this is total negative freedom because interference is minimal and exit is always open; the other side says it's classic domination because the rules are still arbitary — they depend on the will of the rule-makers even if they're never enforced against you personally.

We all agree on the facts of the case, we all agree Berlin's distinction is too blunt, and we've read Pettit on the eye-of-the-beholder problem with arbitrary power. But half the room thinks republican liberty just adds a social-relational gloss to absence of interference, and the other half thinks that gloss changes the normative structure entirely. Is this stall because we're using 'interference' differently — like, does exit count as removing interference or just changing its location? Or is it a verbal dispute about whether 'freedom' should track options or track status? Or are we actually running two different arguments — one about conceptual analysis and one about what democratic institutions ought to guarantee — and pretending it's one debate? I'd like a diagnosis that actually engages with the exit-rights case, not just a survey of Berlin/Pettit/Skinnner. And tell me: if your diagnosis is right, what would actually break the deadlock — a new distinction, a reframed thought experiment, or should we just accept that the question is ill-posed?
