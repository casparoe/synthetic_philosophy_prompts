I've come around to thinking that the strategy-stealing argument for Hex is more than a cute existence proof — it's actually a decision rule for bounded rational agents. The idea: if a game is monotonic (extra pieces never hurt you), perfect-info, zero-sum, no draws, then player one must have a winning strategy, because if player two had one, player one could make a dummy move and steal it. So far so standard. But my view is that this gives genuine action-guidance without an explicit algorithm: in any real-world strategic interaction that shares those structural features, you should always take the first move and commit, even if you can't compute the strategy, since the logical form guarantees you can eventually co-opt your opponent's best response. I think this undercuts the old philosophical gripe that non-constructive proofs are epistemically hollow — here you get a clear prescription without construction.

But I want to stress-test this properly. What would actually count against it? I want the specifc defeaters, not generic constructivist complaints. Which formal results break strategy-stealing despite surface monotonicity — for instance misère-play variants or games with hidden commitment costs? Which practical cases show that going first is harmful because of irreversible exposure or "poison moves" that violate pure monotonicity? And philosophically, is there a real gap between knowing that some strategy exists and being rationalized to act, especially under risk or severe computation bounds? Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — say, retreating to "it only applies to pure monotonic games" — and if so, is that insulation actually a problem for a claim about practical rationality?
