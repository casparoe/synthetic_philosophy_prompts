I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, though I have been reading independently in philosophy of history and technology studies. Next month I will present a seminar paper to an interdisciplinary audience—historians of technology, philosophers of science, and a technology-policy analyst—defending the view that contemporary debates about digital infrastructure systematically confuse structural and event-level causation. Drawing on Fernand Braudel’s distinction between the longue durée of geography, climate, and slow material structures and the shorter rhythms of events and individual agency, I argue that policy discussions of AI governance, semiconductor supply chains, and cloud-computing monopolies over-emphasize narratives about specific founders, singular regulatory rulings, or breakthrough moments, while neglecting deeper constraints that operate on multi-decadal or geological timescales: the thermodynamic geography of data-center cooling, the spatial concentration of rare-earth refining capacity, and the physical timelines required to construct advanced fabrication plants.

To organize this argument, I have developed a conceptual framework I call infrastructure stratigraphy, which sorts technological change into four nested analytical layers—geophysical (energy and mineral geography), material-scientific (process-node limits and substrate properties), institutional (standards bodies and long-term trade architectures), and evental (product launches, executive decisions, and legislative acts)—with the central claim that sound policy analysis should begin at the deepest layer and invoke the evental layer only when lower-level structures genuinely underdetermine the outcome. The audience includes two historians who have worked extensively on Braudel and are protective of his methodological precision; several philosophers of technology who are skeptical of any framework that appears to dissolve human agency into structural determinism; and a policy analyst who regards this framing as technological determinism disguised in historiographical vocabulary. I need a preparation sheet for the Q&A: the objections they will actually raise, in the vocabulary and form each group would use, ordered by likelihood and damage; which points I should concede outright, which I must hold, and especially how to defend the claim that these layers form a hierarchy rather than a flat system of mutual co-constitution, since I suspect that is the weakest point in my position and the one they will press hardest.
