I'm designing a taxonomy for a graduate seminar on indirect normativity and idealized deliberation. I need a branching decision tree that sorts positions on whether practical reasoning should ever be governed by rules endorsed through idealized deliberation rather than by direct evaluation of the act.

Structure the tree from a root node that asks whether an agent can have a decisive reason to act against the direct recommendation of the case at hand, solely because an idealized version of her reasoning endorses a different general rule.

Then split by:
1. The kind of idealization: is it epistemic (full relevant information, corrected cognitive biases, actual preferences) or volitional (fully coherent preferences and values, but with the agent's actual epistemic limitations)?
2. The scope: does the indirect rule apply globally, or is it domain-restricted (e.g., only under emotional pressure, only in institutional roles, or only when outcomes are irreversible)?
3. The conflict-resolution rule: if direct and indirect evaluation clash, does direct always win, does indirect always win, or is there a second-order meta-rule that selects between them?

Each leaf should be labeled either by a named position in the literature (if one occupies it) or 'unnamed,' followed by one sentence describing its most awkward commitment — especially for combinations that seem unoccupied. Include the constructed case of the archivist: she holds documentary proof that a past atrocity was committed by a community now engaged in a fragile reconciliation; direct evaluation urges disclosure for justice, while an idealized deliberator with full emotional and historical knowledge might endorse a rule of strategic silence to preserve peace. Use this case explicitly to show where two branches diverge.

Keep the tree to roughly eight to ten leaves. Flag any pairs of positions that your tree cannot cleanly separate, and briefly diagnose unoccupied combinations: are they incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?
