I've been mulling over vagueness since a thread on a rationalist forum about where categories actually break down, and I cooked up this institutional case that seems to sit right between epistemicism and supervaluationism. Picture a regulatory board founded by ten people, with a charter stating that the board retains its special amendment powers only so long as the majority of its members are original founders. Every year exactly one founder retires and is replaced by a new expert, with no other changes to rules or culture. By year ten all founders are gone, yet each single replacement felt entirely legitimate and nobody could point to the exact moment authority disappeared. So does the board lose its powers on year six, year seven, or never? And is there a hidden fact about which year it was?

I'd like you to rank — or rather rate and then tier — four responses to this kind of boundary problem: the epistemic view (sharp unknown boundary, Williamson-style), supervaluationism (no unique correct cutoff, but every precisification is either true or false), a fuzzy-logic account that assigns precise degrees of membership, and a contextualist stance where practical stakes determine where we draw the line. Rate each out of ten on two axes: first, how well it respects our intuitive judgements about the board's authority year by year without generating absurdities that favour one year arbitrarily; second, how metaphysically cheap it is, meaning whether it forces us to accept hidden sharp lines, truth-value gaps, or infinitely precise membership functions. Then put them in an overall tier list — S, A, B, C, maybe D — with brief reasoning for the ordering.

Please write in British English, and don't bother with bibliographies or page references; I'm after the conceptual structure rather than scholarship. Sorry if this is a bit muddled — I was discussing it with friends over a few pints and my notes are a mess, but I think the core idea is clear enough. Alot of the confusion comes from mixing legal language with philosophy, but that's the point.
