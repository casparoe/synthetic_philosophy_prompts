I'm writing an essay for my philosophy elective on how theories of legal interpretation travel beyond formal statutes, and I want to use Shirley Jackson's short story The Lottery as my central example. I am trying to think through the story using textualism and purposivism—not as buzzwords, but as competing accounts of why the villagers continue the ritual. The black box and the slips of paper are treated as authoritative texts: the villagers preserve the physical artifacts and follow the procedural steps with near-liturgical precision, even though the original purpose of the lottery (ensuring a good harvest) has been forgotten and the box itself is physically deteriorating. At the same time, Old Man Warner and others defend the practice by appealing to tradition, which sounds like a purposivist appeal to the community's ongoing function rather than to the rule's literal content.

Could you read the story with me and tell me which scenes—perhaps the replacement of the original box, the arguments over changing the rules, or Tessie Hutchinson's final protest—best illuminate one framework or the other? I want to know where the story genuinely supports a textualist or purposivist reading, where it resists both by showing that the villagers are interpreting neither text nor purpose but mere inertia, and what Jackson's narrative reveals about the psychology of interpretation that legal philosophers like Scalia or Dworkin tend to overlook in their debates about statutory meaning. Please also consider whether the absence of any written law or authoritative interpreter in the village makes the analogy to modern jurisprudence strained, and where an honest critic should concede that the story is doing something other than illustrating legal theory.
