I am trying to identify a recurring argumentative move I keep seeing in philosophical and scientific debates about the cosmological fine-tuning argument, and I want to know whether it has an established name or whether it needs a new one.

The pattern works like this. A speaker begins by stressing that a physical constant—say, the cosmological constant, or the ratio of gravitational to electromagnetic strength—occupies an extraordinarily narrow band compatible with the emergence of complex structures. The claim is presented as an objective feature of the parameter space: had the value fallen outside that band, life would be impossible. When a critic objects that the “parameter space” is not uniquely defined—that the band looks narrow only because of an implicit normalization, or because the theoretical framework that sets the bounds is itself contingent—the speaker retreats to a second, weaker position: regardless of how the space is measured, it remains striking that the observed value is one of the few that permits observers, and this selection effect does not fully dissolve the appearance of surprise. Once the challenge about mathematical framing has been neutralized, however, the speaker resumes treating the original objective-narrowness claim as intact, using it to support inferences about design, multiverse hypotheses, or the need for explanation.

This is not quite the motte-and-bailey, because the two positions are not a defensible core and an indefensible extension; both are substantive, and they share the same rhetorical vocabulary (“fine-tuned,” “life-permitting range”). It is also not straightforward equivocation, since the speaker is not alternating between two clearly distinct meanings of a single term, but rather oscillating between two different standards of evaluation—an objective, framing-dependent metric of range size and an observer-conditioned metric of selection surprise—while treating them as interchangeable evidence for the same conclusion. I have noticed the move in discussions of the cosmological constant, in comparisons of fundamental forces, and in arguments about the low-entropy initial conditions of the universe. In each case, the retreat is triggered specifically by an attack on the normalization or measure, not by an attack on the probabilistic intuition itself.

Is there an established concept in philosophy of science or argumentation theory that captures this exact oscillation? If there is, how does it differ from the nearest neighbors—motte-and-bailey, equivocation, or a simple change of subject? If there is not, can you coin a precise term, define its necessary conditions, and specify its boundary cases? In particular, I want to know whether the concept applies only when the retreat is tactical—used to deflect a framing challenge and then abandoned—or also when a speaker sincerely holds both standards simultaneously without recognizing the tension between them. What are the edges: does it cover cases where the two standards are explicitly distinguished but still illegitimately pooled as joint support, or only cases where they are silently substituted for one another?
