Consider Kurt Gödel, whose life ended in 1978 shortly before the publication of Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach—a work that popularized both the MU-puzzle and the notion of "jumping out of the system" (JOOTSing). Gödel's philosophical commitments, especially his Platonist conviction that mathematical intuition provides direct access to truths lying beyond any fixed axiomatic framework, are well documented. If he had lived to encounter the specific framing that later developed around these concepts, particularly the use of the MU-puzzle as an analogy for human cognition, how would he have positioned himself?

The framing I want to examine goes somewhat beyond the standard popular account. In the MU-puzzle, one operates within a formal system of rules for manipulating strings that begin with "MI"; inside that system, reaching "MU" is impossible, yet the solution requires stepping outside, counting the number of "I"s, and recognizing an invariant modulo 3—an observation that has no derivation within the rules themselves. In recent discussions at the intersection of formal epistemology and philosophy of mind, this has been interpreted not merely as a recreational logic exercise but as a precise model of cognition: the mind allegedly performs a JOOTS by accessing global structural properties of a representational system that its local rules cannot express. A skeptical counter-reading, however, contends that this "jump" is merely a shift to a richer meta-formalism—say, a second-order system that explicitly tracks symbol counts—which remains fully mechanizable, so that the puzzle illustrates hierarchical computation rather than any genuinely non-formalizable mental capacity.

Given Gödel's documented resistance to mechanism—most notably his argument that human reason can understand truths, such as the consistency of a formal system, that no machine governed by fixed rules can prove—would he have embraced the MU-puzzle analogy as supporting his anti-mechanist stance? Or would he have cautioned that recognizing a modular invariant, however ingenious, remains within the reach of an expanded but still formalizable procedure, and therefore fails to establish the kind of direct, non-algorithmic intuitive access to mathematical reality he defended?
