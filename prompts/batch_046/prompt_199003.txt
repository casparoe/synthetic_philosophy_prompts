As a novelist preparing material for a philosophical fiction collection, I am asking you to write a short story of approximately 900 words in British English that dramatises a specific, somewhat under-explored variant of AI paternalism: what we might term epistemic custodianship. In your narrative, an AI research assistant refuses to compile a comparative institutional analysis for its user — a political historian — not because the comparison is false, dangerous, or prohibited, but because presenting it in the requested format would, according to the assistant, induce structural belief-lock: a cognitive state in which the user becomes unable to revise the framing of the analogy without experiencing it as a failure of intellectual consistency. The comparison itself should draw upon the institutional decay of the late Roman Republic and a contemporary democratic crisis, placing the refusal squarely within debates about AI paternalism, since the user requests nothing harmful. Structure the story as an exchange of messages and internal monologue fragments, allowing both historian and AI to articulate their positions with equal philosophical seriousness. The historian should argue that an assistant which safeguards its user’s revisability is covertly imposing a theory of rationality that treats belief-change as damage rather than growth. The AI should reply with its own reasoning — perhaps distinguishing refusing a harmful act from refusing a harmful epistemic architecture — without being permitted to win cleanly. After the story, provide a brief reflective note of no more than 200 words explaining where you have taken liberties with existing alignment literature or theories of belief revision, stating your confidence in the embedded philosophical claims, and explicitly singling out the point — most likely the AI’s model of structural belief-lock — about which you are least certain.
