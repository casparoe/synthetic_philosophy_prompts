For my junior-year history seminar, I have been tracking my instructor's feedback on my interpretive essays about the French Revolution. She evaluates each event according to whether it is best explained by class struggle, by ideology, or by contingency, and she also notes whether it illustrates the idea that revolutions devour their children. Her verdicts on the cases I have submitted so far are as follows.

On the storming of the Bastille in July 1789, she wrote that I overstated class antagonism; she accepts that contingency—the rumor of royal troops and the search for weapons—was the immediate trigger, and that ideology—the symbolic destruction of arbitrary power—gave the event its revolutionary meaning, but she insists that structural class struggle was only a background condition and not a proximate cause. She marked this as not an example of revolutions devouring their children.

On the Great Fear of July–August 1789, she said that my framing in terms of peasant class war was partially accurate but missed the primary driver, which was contingency: a cascading rumor about an aristocratic plot that spread through specific regional communication networks. She noted that the rhetoric of popular sovereignty was largely a post-hoc ideological justification, and she again said this is not an instance of the revolution consuming its own.

On the trial and execution of Louis XVI in January 1793, she argued that ideology—specifically the performative invention of republican sovereignty and the trial's symbolic rupture with divine right—was the dominant register, while contingency, in the form of wartime pressure and internal rebellion, created the conditions without determining the outcome. She said this does not count as revolutions devouring their children, because the king was an external enemy of the revolutionary project rather than one of its own agents.

On the Thermidorian Reaction of July 1794 and the fall of Robespierre, she agreed that this clearly exemplifies revolutions devouring their children. However, she emphasized that the correct explanation centers on ideology: the Convention's rejection of Robespierre's radical republican virtue and the procedural extremism of the Law of 22 Prairial. She granted that contingency—the military victories of late June and July reducing the emergency justification for the Terror—played an enabling role, but she explicitly rejected a class-struggle account, noting that both Robespierre and his Thermidorian opponents were drawn from roughly the same bourgeois and professional strata.

On the suppression of the Germinal and Prairial uprisings in April and May 1795, she said that this also illustrates revolutions devouring their children, but here the explanatory weight shifts: class struggle, in the form of the Convention's defense of property rights, market freedom, and bourgeois economic order against sans-culotte demands for price controls and direct democracy, is primary. Ideology—the rhetoric of constitutional legality and social order—functions mainly as legitimating cover, and contingency is minimal because the economic conflicts were structurally embedded in the Directory's consolidation.

Next week I must write on the suppression of Gracchus Babeuf's Conspiracy of the Equals in May 1796 and Babeuf's subsequent execution in 1797. The case involves a former Jacobin journalist and his supporters, who advocated for common property and the abolition of private wealth, being betrayed by an informant within the circle, arrested by the Directory's police, tried outside Paris in a deliberate relocation, and executed after a conspiracy that never moved to open insurrection. I need to predict my instructor's verdict: will she classify this as an instance of revolutions devouring their children? And will she assign explanatory priority to class struggle, to ideology, or to contingency—particularly given the specific contingency of the betrayal and the strategic choice of trial venue?

More importantly, I need to identify the implicit interpretive rule she is running. The candidates that seem consistent with her track record are: (1) a class-struggle determinism that applies the "devouring children" label whenever a bourgeois revolutionary government suppresses a popular economic movement; (2) an ideological-overdetermination model that applies the label only when the suppression targets a radical ideological program that threatens the revolution's own founding principles; (3) a contingency-first or aleatory-concatenation model that treats the "devouring children" pattern as a retrospective narrative imposed on events whose outcomes depend on specific betrayals, military accidents, and local power vacuums; and (4) a synthetic performative-structural view that I have not fully grasped but that might weight the symbolic performance of sovereignty differently in institutional versus clandestine contexts.

Please predict her verdict on Babeuf, specify which of these rules (or another) best coheres with all five prior judgments, and identify the single further case that would best distinguish between the two most plausible rules. In your answer, please separate what is settled among professional historians of the Revolution—such as the basic chronology of 1796, the Directory's economic policies, and Babeuf's advocacy of common property—from what remains genuinely contested, particularly regarding whether Babeuf's movement represented a structurally inevitable class conflict or an ideologically contingent deviation. Do not present any one interpretive school as if it were the consensus.
