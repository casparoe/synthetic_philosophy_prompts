I am writing a philosophical dialogue for a novel in which a character must choose among three successive versions of an artistic contract, each surrendering an imperceptibly smaller fraction of creative autonomy arranged along a continuous spectrum from full control to none. For this scene, construct a thought experiment built exactly to these specifications: three states, A, B, and C, ordered along a single quantitative dimension, such that A is better than B and B is better than C, yet C is better than A, with the intransitivity arising specifically from the accumulation of sub-threshold differences rather than from ambiguity in the predicate "better than" itself. Present the scenario in ordinary, non-technical terms suitable for a reader without philosophical training, then demonstrate that it satisfies each specification: state the pairwise judgments, identify the comparison method, map the adjacent pairs or nearby states that drive each verdict, and isolate the precise structural feature—related to the continuous gradation—that causes transitivity to fail. Next, explain what happens if that feature is eliminated, for instance by replacing the spectrum with discrete, noticeably distinct jumps, and show whether the intransitivity collapses. Finally, articulate how a defender of the transitivity of "better than" would attack the case, whether that attack succeeds, and what remains of the paradox once the objection is addressed. Please respond in American English, without bibliographies, citations, named theories requiring external verification, or appeals to exotic scenarios.
