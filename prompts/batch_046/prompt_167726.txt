my study group has this argument about rome falling and now i cant sleep. (1) if the core legal and administrative institutions of a society keep running in recognizible form after a political rupture, then saying it "fell" is just romantic historiography — what happened was transformation, not collapse. (2) after 476 the corpus of roman law was still being copied, the church preserved roman bureaucratic forms, and barbarian kings used roman tax collectors. (3) so the western empire didnt fall; it transformed into medieval europe, and the east kept going til 1453 anyway. (4) but apply that same continuity test to the classic maya: their calendrical knowledge and local village governance survived the so-called collapse around 900 CE. (5) same deal for easter island — kinship structures and language persisted after the ecological crash and statue-building stopped. (6) in fact, for any society you care to name, some cultural thread survives, so (1) implies that societal collapse is literally impossible. (7) but thats absurd — easter island clearly collapsed in a meaningful way, and the maya lost their state-level institutions. (8) therefore (1) is false, which means rome probably did fall in 476 after all, back to gibbon. but heres the thing: i really want to keep (1) because it feels right that continuity matters. if i reject it, im conceding that a society can be "dead" even when its laws and churches are still operating, which seems wrong. where exactly does the argument slip? is there a hidden equivocation between "institutional continuity" and "societal survival"? and what does it cost me to fix it without going back to the old "barbarians destroyed rome" story?
