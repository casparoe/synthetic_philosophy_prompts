I have been working through Karl Jaspers’s four categories of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—to ask whether a nation or other enduring collective can be guilty for wrongs committed before any living member existed. I want to know whether a single principle lies behind the following verdicts, or whether I must abandon at least one.

Case 1: The Federal Republic of Germany in 1955. The state retains legal continuity with the Reich; much public infrastructure was built with forced labor; most adult citizens lived through the regime, though direct perpetrators are largely absent from government. Verdict: the state bears political guilt; citizens who were not perpetrators bear no criminal or moral guilt individually, though some may bear metaphysical guilt for belonging to the community that permitted the crimes.

Case 2: A German citizen born in 1998, who inherits citizenship automatically, benefits from that infrastructure, but actively repudiates nationalism and contributes to restitution. Verdict: she bears no personal moral or criminal guilt; political guilt attaches only to her formal role as citizen of a continuing state, not to her person.

Case 3: A corporation—call it Meridian—founded in 1900, which used slave labor in 1920. Every original shareholder and manager is dead. The firm still holds patents derived from that labor and distributes profits to current shareholders who bought stock with no knowledge of the origin. Verdict: the legal entity owes restitution, but no present shareholder is morally guilty; culpability does not travel through voluntary market transactions.

Case 4: The nation of Alvaria expelled an indigenous people in 1870; the expulsion is still encoded in property law, and current citizens hold titles traceable to it. The government denies any wrongdoing and blocks restoration. Verdict: the nation is structurally guilty; current citizens who maintain those titles and the legal denial bear an inherited political guilt, even though they did not choose their birth.

Case 5: The grandchildren of Alvarian perpetrators who have organized to return land and dissolve the legal structures of exclusion, yet still reside on the territory. Verdict: their active moral guilt is dissolved, but I feel a weaker residual metaphysical guilt remains—solidarity in the failure of the human community—though this feels thinner than the guilt in Case 4, despite the structural parallel.

My tension: Case 3 and Case 4 are structurally similar—benefits flow through an enduring institution across generations of non-culpable individuals—yet I deny individual moral guilt in Case 3 and affirm inherited political guilt in Case 4. Is the difference that citizenship is non-voluntary in a way shareholding is not? That a state possesses a moral personality a corporation lacks? Or am I simply inconsistent?

Please provide a precise principle, or a small family of candidates, that distinguishes collective guilt that properly attaches to present members from institutional liability that stops at the boundary. For each principle, state which clause is motivated by which case, apply it to an unconsidered case—a religious denomination that excommunicated heretics in 1600, whose modern members inherit church property built with confiscated goods but whose theology now condemns the original act—and say explicitly which of my five verdicts it forces me to drop. Keep the response under 450 words, with the principle stated in its own paragraph and with no bibliographic references.
