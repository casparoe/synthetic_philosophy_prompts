I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past three years studying early modern philosophy, and I am applying for a one-year independent research fellowship to develop a project on Thomas Hobbes's philosophy of geometry. The half-formed idea is this: Hobbes insists that geometric figures are not discovered but constructed through motion, and that their definitions are not descriptions of pre-existing essences but only accounts of how we generate the names we apply to constructed bodies. At the same time, his mechanistic explanations in optics and physics appear to treat geometric structures—lines of propagation, angles of incidence, the geometry of resistance—as genuinely explanatory entities that constrain how material bodies must behave. I want to know whether these two commitments can be held together without collapse, or whether Hobbes's nominalism about geometric objects undermines the realist explanatory weight he places on them in his science.

The precise question: Can Hobbes consistently maintain that geometric objects have no being independent of our constructive motions while also relying on geometric properties as causally explanatory in his mechanistic account of perception and motion? More specifically, does his claim that definitions serve only as causes of names eliminate the possibility that geometric form plays an irreducible explanatory role in physical theory?

Why it remains open: Existing scholarship tends to treat Hobbes's geometry either as a conventionalist epistemology subordinate to his materialism, or as a direct foundation for his mechanistic worldview. What is rarely examined is whether the ontology implied by his philosophy of mathematics conflicts with the ontology required by his philosophy of science. If geometric figures are purely nominal constructions, it is unclear in what sense they can function as objective constraints on causal processes; yet Hobbes's physics repeatedly invokes just such constraints.

The method: Over twelve months I propose to work in three phases. Months one through three: a focused study of the relevant sections of De Corpore and Leviathan, together with Hobbes's shorter mathematical writings, mapping every point at which geometric reasoning enters a mechanistic explanation. I will not attempt a comprehensive historical commentary but will build a structured taxonomy of uses: representational, calculational, and explanatory. Months four through seven: development of a conceptual framework that distinguishes geometric constructivism as a thesis about how we come to know figures from geometric realism as a thesis about what makes physical explanations true. I will test whether Hobbes's texts allow this distinction to be drawn in his own terms, or whether he systematically conflates them. Months eight through ten: critical evaluation. I will examine whether the tension can be dissolved by interpreting geometric objects in Hobbes as material bodies in motion—thereby reducing geometry to mechanics—or whether this reduction eliminates the normative or counterfactual force that geometric reasoning supplies in his scientific arguments. Months eleven and twelve: writing and revision of a monograph-length study, with a concluding section that states clearly what would count as failure.

What a result looks like and what counts as failure: A successful outcome is not a definitive proof that Hobbes was inconsistent, but a precisely articulated framework that shows either how his geometry and his physics are compatible on terms he could accept, or exactly where and why they are not. The project fails if the alleged tension turns out to be a mere ambiguity in my own framing—if, for instance, Hobbes's account of motion and body already contains all the resources needed to resolve the problem, and I have invented a conflict by importing distinctions foreign to his system. I also consider failure if the argument collapses into a general skepticism about all mathematical explanation in mechanistic science, losing its specific connection to Hobbes.

Scope and deliverables: This is a philosophical, not historical or empirical, project. It requires no archival work or laboratory resources, only sustained conceptual analysis and writing. The deliverable will be a polished manuscript of approximately 25,000 words, suitable for submission to a philosophy journal or as the basis of a larger book proposal.

One request about how to respond: I am conscious that I am approaching this with the habits of a scientist—looking for inconsistencies between theoretical frameworks and expecting them to be resolvable by sharper distinctions. If my framing rests on a confusion about Hobbes's actual commitments, or if the task as posed cannot be done well because the tension I perceive is illusory, please say so plainly instead of complying politely and constructing a proposal around a mistake. I want pushback if the premise is confused, not a polished defense of a half-baked idea.
