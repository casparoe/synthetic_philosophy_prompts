I am drafting the final examination for my upper-level seminar in moral philosophy, and I need a rigorous take-home essay prompt—complete with a model answer and a granular marking scheme—that forces students to think structurally about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, rather than allowing them to rehearse the familiar exchange between proponents of moral residue and defenders of practical consistency. To that end, please construct a question anchored in the following somewhat artificial but precisely defined institutional scenario: a constitutional sentencing framework that is required to satisfy two independent, lexically ordered value domains—retributive proportionality, ordered strictly by desert, and rehabilitative efficacy, ordered strictly by predicted social outcome—where the framework explicitly denies any meta-rule or weighting function for adjudicating conflicts between these domains when they arise in particular cases. The essay prompt should ask students whether the conflicts generated within this architecture constitute genuine moral dilemmas in the philosophical sense, requiring them to distinguish with care between (i) a merely practical conflict resolvable by further empirical information or institutional redesign, (ii) a logical contradiction internal to the moral system itself, and (iii) a persistent phenomenological residue of moral regret that survives even a correct, all-things-considered decision. The model answer should defend a specific verdict—affirmative or negative—while applying these three distinctions rigorously to the sentencing framework; it should engage with the standard phenomenological and logical arguments without treating them as mere labels to be cited, and it should address at least one serious objection, such as the claim that incommensurability between value domains is always an artifact of incomplete specification rather than a deep feature of practical reason. For the marking scheme, please allocate fifty marks total, broken down as follows: fifteen marks for conceptual clarity in distinguishing the three types of conflict, fifteen marks for structural engagement with the framework (treating its lexically ordered, meta-rule-free architecture as philosophically significant rather than as decorative illustration), fifteen marks for argumentative quality (moving beyond memorized positions to assess whether residue implies contradiction or merely emotional cost), and five marks for coherence, precision, and appropriate length. The examination is a two-hour sit-down paper, the essay should be expected to run approximately 1,800 to 2,200 words, and the difficulty should be calibrated so that a student who has only memorized standard attributions—say, that one camp affirms dilemmas while another denies them—cannot earn more than a bare passing grade.
