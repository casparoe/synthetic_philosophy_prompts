I am a retired molecular biologist who, after a career in experimental genetics, has recently turned to metaphysics and philosophy of personal identity. I find that discussions of origin essentialism — specifically, whether one could have come from a different sperm and egg and still be oneself — are usually conducted without adequate attention to the biological mechanisms I know well. I therefore want you to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps might produce after a sustained adversarial collaboration.

The first camp, which I will call the Developmental-Trajectory Essentialists, holds that personal identity is anchored in the specific spatiotemporal path of cellular division, organizational differentiation, and epigenetic development that begins with a particular zygote; they concede that the genome alone is insufficient, given monozygotic twinning and induced chimerism, but they maintain that the actual trajectory of development is metaphysically necessary. The second camp, the Biological-Contingency Theorists, holds that origin is metaphysically contingent: personal identity consists in appropriate psychological and functional continuity, and the initial biological substrate — genome, zygote, even mitochondrial DNA — is in principle replaceable without destroying the person, provided organizational and mental relations are preserved.

Please structure the document as follows. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely sign, including agreed biological facts about twinning, chimerism, mitochondrial replacement therapy, and the temporal variability of an individual's genome. Second, a disagreements table stating each camp's core thesis in wording that its own proponents would accept. Third, the cruxes — I take these to be whether a developmental trajectory can be individuated without circular reference to personal identity, whether mitochondrial replacement produces a numerically new person, and whether gradual or total genetic replacement over a lifespan undermines essentialism. Fourth, for each crux, identify one concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's predicted resolution. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or requests for further reading. Write in a formal, polished style appropriate to a philosophical working paper, and avoid verbatim quotations from specific philosophers or texts; paraphrase any positions you reference.
