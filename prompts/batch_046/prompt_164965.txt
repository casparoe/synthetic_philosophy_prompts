I have been thinking lately about how societies ought to distribute benefits and burdens, mostly from things I have read in popular philosophy writing. One view that caught my attention is prioritarianism: the idea that improving the situation of someone who is badly off carries more moral weight than improving the situation of someone who is already well off, even if the absolute gain is identical. I also learned about the leveling-down objection, which suggests that prioritarianism has a strange implication—if we cannot raise the worst-off people up, then lowering the best-off people down to achieve equality might be considered an improvement, since the gap in moral weight shrinks. At first this seemed like a decisive problem, but then I started wondering whether I was missing a distinction.

More recently, I encountered sufficientarianism, which holds that what matters is that everyone reaches some adequate threshold of well-being, and that once people are above that line, further differences between them are not morally important in the same direct way. I am trying to understand how these two views differ specifically in their response to leveling-down scenarios. To that end, I tried to formulate a distinction myself between what I would call intrinsic leveling—where equality is valued for its own sake, so reducing someone’s welfare without helping anyone else could still count as good—and instrumental leveling—where reducing the top is only justified if it genuinely produces benefits for the bottom. I suspect prioritarianism rejects intrinsic leveling but might still face a subtler form of instrumental leveling in cases where resources are fixed and redistribution is impossible, whereas sufficientarianism might avoid both forms but then face a different difficulty: if everyone is just barely above the threshold, a policy that harms the slightly-better-off to help the slightly-worse-off might seem unnecessary even if the latter fall just below the line.

Could you compare prioritarianism and sufficientarianism with respect to how each handles the leveling-down objection, using the distinction between intrinsic and instrumental leveling as an organizing framework? Please explain whether my framing of that distinction is coherent or whether I am conflating separate issues, clarify how each theory actually evaluates downward adjustments that do not improve anyone’s absolute position, and defend a reasoned verdict about which view offers the more plausible account of distributive justice in these edge cases. I have no formal background in ethics, so I would appreciate a clear, step-by-step comparison that corrects any misconceptions in my reasoning.
