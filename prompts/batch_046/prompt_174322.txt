I'm trying to reach reflective equilibrium on what makes an artifact genuinely valuable as an original, rather than as a copy, forgery, or reconstruction. Here are my concrete verdicts.

A destroyed seventeenth-century painting is reconstructed by artisans using period-correct pigments and techniques, guided only by archival descriptions and black-and-white photographs. The result is visually indistinguishable from the lost work. Verdict: it is aesthetically admirable, but it is not that painting; its value is closer to a superb reproduction than to the lost original.

The actual original survives, but seventy percent of its paint layer was replaced by conservators after fire damage, using chemically identical materials applied with matching technique. Verdict: it remains that very painting; its value is barely diminished, despite being mostly new matter.

A master forger produces a perfect physical duplicate of an existing painting—same canvas age, same brushstroke topology, same chemical signature—intending to deceive. Even if it hangs undetected for years, I think it carries essentially none of the original's value; discovery collapses its worth to near zero.

An algorithm trained on Bach's complete corpus reconstructs missing measures of an unfinished fugue, producing music structurally indistinguishable from authentic Bach. Verdict: the result may be beautiful, but it is not a Bach fugue; encountering it does not count as encountering Bach's work.

A museum visitor spends an hour in front of a high-fidelity holographic replica of an ancient statue, genuinely believing it to be the marble original. Their emotional and aesthetic response is profound. Verdict: the experience is real and valid, but the institution has failed them epistemically; the value they received was not the specific value of encountering that historical token.

Two physically identical marble sculptures sit side by side. One was carved by a master; the other was carved in real time by an equally skilled forger copying the first. Verdict: the first is a masterpiece worth millions; the second is a technical curiosity worth little, despite material and visual identity.

The case I keep going back and forth on is digital generation. A digital artist trains a diffusion model exclusively on one painter's complete, verified oeuvre and generates a new image in that painter's unmistakable style, with no direct copying of any specific canvas. The output is signed by the model, not the painter. Is this closer to the reconstructed fugue—beautiful but not by the master—or closer to the forger's marble copy? Or does the absence of a single copied token place it in a different category entirely?

What principle, if any, unifies the first six cases? I want something precise that explains why material continuity preserves value in the restored painting but not in the artisan reconstruction, why deception destroys value in the forgery but not the prior experience in the hologram case, and why identical appearance produces divergent verdicts in the paired sculptures. If no single principle saves every verdict, which one should I overturn? And what does the resulting account predict for the digital-generation case?
