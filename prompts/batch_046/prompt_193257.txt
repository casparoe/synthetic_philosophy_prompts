I'm writing a feature on why time seems to have a single direction, and I've been cataloguing my own reactions to specific scenarios to see if they hang together. Watching a shattered glass reassemble in reverse: I instantly judge it is running backward, because disorder is spontaneously decreasing in a way that never occurs naturally. Watching dye diffuse in water and then seeing the reverse: I still sense the correct order, but far less viscerally—if the reversal were shown, I would be suspicious rather than certain. A computer systematically overwriting structured data with random noise feels like it is moving "forward" with entropy; a computer organizing random noise into a database feels like a local reversal, even though both processes change information. A physicist uses symmetric mechanics to predict tomorrow's orbit and also to retrodict yesterday's—yet I feel only the prediction genuinely travels with the grain of time, while retrodiction is reconstruction against it. But then I imagine an isolated pocket in a universe that has otherwise reached perfect thermal equilibrium, where a brief fluctuation produces a conscious observer with false memories of a past; my intuition still assigns that observer a present facing a future, even though there is no global entropy gradient. At the same time, I think that if physics turned out to be perfectly time-symmetric with no special boundary condition, I would still cite my own experience of remembering rather than anticipating as proof of real asymmetry—yet I also feel that asymmetry should not depend on consciousness being present. Is there a coherent principle—grounded in entropy, causal structure, or the logic of records—that can accommodate all of these judgments, or must I abandon one? And in particular, does the arrow survive locally inside that equilibrium pocket without a global thermodynamic gradient, or is my insistence that it survives merely an anthropocentric projection?
