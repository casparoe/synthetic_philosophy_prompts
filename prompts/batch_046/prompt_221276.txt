I just spent three hours arguing with a close friend about whether human deliberation is genuinely adaptive or merely sphexishness with better branding—the kind of mechanical rigidity Dean Wooldridge documented in Sphex ichneumoneus, where the wasp repeats its burrow-checking ritual even after its prey has been moved, never revising the sequence itself. My friend insists that free will is just complex rigidity; I think there is a qualitative break, but I need my students to feel the difference from the inside rather than hear it named.

Please design a runnable classroom activity for an upper-level philosophy seminar—about fourteen students, ninety minutes—that stages this tension physically and procedurally. Divide the group into pairs or small teams acting as agents who must complete a multi-stage task—collecting, verifying, and depositing—under an explicit protocol that initially earns efficiency points. Unbeknownst to them, the environment will shift in a specific, predictable way through a hidden audit stage that requires verification of the deposit location, mimicking bureaucratic loop-lock, so that rigid adherence to the original sequence causes failure, while breaking the loop requires sacrificing accumulated points or accepting a flexibility tax. The rules and payoffs should be arranged so that participants experience smooth success, then sudden exploitation of their ritual, and finally a forced choice between maintaining the protocol and adapting, all before anyone explains what is happening.

Include specific roles, such as one student per group serving as an unannounced environment shifter who can relocate items; a phased timeline with approximate minutes; the exact scoring structure; and instructions for the debrief that map each phase onto the distinction between mechanical repetition and adaptive flexibility. Identify what will predictably derail the exercise—for instance, one group will likely spot the exploit early and game the system, or participants will blame the rules rather than recognizing their own rigidity—and state frankly which feature of free will, likely the capacity for second-order revision of one's own procedural rules, the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied through discussion. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; build the design from the conceptual architecture alone, and feel free to push the analogy beyond the wasp into institutional or algorithmic ritual if that sharpens the experience.
