I'm mapping out conceptual priorities in the philosophy of medicine, specifically in the space beneath the familiar naturalism-versus-normativism debate. Rather than rehearse the standard exchange between biostatistical and harmful-dysfunction frameworks, I want to focus on five specific conceptual knots that sit at the intersection of evolutionary medicine, epidemiology, disability theory, and clinical ethics. Each is tractable without new empirical data, but each also carries implications for how the core debate should ultimately be settled.

The five lines are as follows.

First, evolutionary mismatch as a dysfunction baseline. The mismatch hypothesis holds that much modern morbidity reflects biology adapted to ancestral environments rather than to contemporary ones. Can this provide a purely descriptive, non-circular criterion for dysfunction? Or does selecting the reference ancestral environment already involve normative judgments about what counts as a proper human ecology?

Second, subclinical allostatic load and the harm threshold. Chronic low-grade inflammatory and metabolic deviations—collectively described as allostatic load—predict morbidity long before subjective symptoms or functional impairment appear. For normativists who define disorder as harmful dysfunction, this forces a choice: either expand harm to include purely predictive biological risk, or concede a large zone of biological dysfunction that is not yet disease. For naturalists, it raises whether statistical deviation from a species-typical range is sufficient when such deviation is widespread in aging cohorts.

Third, multi-morbidity and statistical normality in aging societies. Among people over seventy, having multiple chronic conditions is statistically typical. This challenges naturalist appeals to statistical abnormality, but it also challenges normativist appeals to individual harm, since many elderly individuals adapt well to technically impairing conditions. Is this a crisis for both frameworks, or does it show that the unit of analysis—individual versus cohort—is the real conceptual issue?

Fourth, the social model and mental health classification. Disability studies distinguish biological impairment from socially constructed disability. In mental health, this boundary is unstable: conditions such as autism or ADHD involve biological variation and socially mediated harm, but the harm often depends on environmental accommodation. Can a normativist account absorb this distinction without collapsing into pure social construction? Can a naturalist account preserve it without treating social context as diagnostically irrelevant?

Fifth, enhancement and the species-typical ceiling. Interventions that restore or exceed species-typical function—cognitive or immune optimization, for instance—reveal that naturalism relies on a baseline of typical function that may not be value-free. Does this show naturalism was always crypto-normative about appropriate functional levels, or can naturalists defend the baseline as purely descriptive?

Give me a committed, defended agenda, not a neutral survey. For each rank, specify: (a) whether it is upstream or downstream of the others; (b) why it is tractable for conceptual philosophy rather than requiring new laboratory or clinical findings; (c) what result or clarification would cause you to revise the ranking. Be explicit about your criteria—for instance, dependency relations among the questions, practical stakes for medicine or policy, or the likelihood of yielding a decisive argument for one side of the naturalism-normativism divide.

Finally, take a definite position: does resolving any one of these settle the broader debate, or are they parallel problems that require separate treatment? State which view is correct and defend it directly. I want definite choices and reasons, not a balanced menu of options.
