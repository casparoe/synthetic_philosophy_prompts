I'm presenting to a joint philosophy-of-mind and AI-ethics seminar my thesis that attributing localized, belief-like representational states to large language models is methodologically indispensable—but only within a tightly circumscribed predictive domain, specifically for explaining strategic misrepresentation in multi-turn negotiation simulations—without any ontological commitment to consciousness, global mental architecture, or biological intentionality. I call this a "scoped instrumentalism," and I argue it differs both from Dennett's unrestricted intentional stance and from the mechanistic interpretability program's eliminativism about mental-state talk. The audience is split: about half are analytic philosophers of mind with strong Searlean commitments to the biological substrate of original intentionality and a reflexive suspicion that mental-state attribution to silicon systems is always anthropomorphic projection; two are cognitive scientists working on predictive-processing models who are broadly sympathetic to instrumentalist explanations but are specifically worried about "creeping anthropomorphism" in AI evaluation; and one is a computer scientist in mechanistic interpretability who regards belief-talk as redundant given circuit-level tracing and who thinks philosophical debates about AI mental states distract from actual safety work. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the eight or so objections I will actually face, ordered by the damage they could do to the proposal, each phrased in the vocabulary and conceptual frame of the subgroup most likely to raise it. For each, indicate what I should concede up front, what I must hold without retreat, and provide a concise reply calibrated to about two minutes, with an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that targets the boundary conditions of my "scoped" claim and forces me to specify whether the heuristic applies beyond negotiation contexts to, say, mathematical reasoning or creative writing, where the predictive payoff is far less clear.
