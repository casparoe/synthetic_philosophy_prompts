ok so here's something that always bugs me. in most european countries commercial surrogacy is banned outright, and paid egg donation is either illegal or capped at a small "expense" reimbursement. but sperm donation is generally permitted and often comes with some compensation. then in the US you have full commercial surrogacy contracts that include behavoural clauses — like the surrogate can't drink alcohol or travel after a certain point, with financial penalties if she breaks them — and egg donor fees that can run into the tens of thousands based on "desirable" traits. but here's the weird part: in european altruistic surrogacy programs (where they're legal at all), the surrogate can recieve reimbursements for maternity clothes, lost wages, travel, etc. that often add up to more than the capped egg-donor payments, even tho egg retrieval involves hormonal drugs and surgical extraction. so institutionally we have three tiers: sperm is bodily material you can be paid for, egg is dangerous labor that must stay non-commercial except through hidden compensation, and gestation is either banned as market labor or heavily surveilled altruism with big reimbursements.

what theory of value or bodily autonomy does that architecture actually presuppose? is it about risk, gender, genetic uniqueness, or the potential for exploitation? and is the practice internally consistent under one view, or is it a patchwork that several rival theories could license? if we rebuilt the policy on a pure labor-rights model or on strict bodily-autonomy grounds, which parts of the current design — the caps, the behavioral clauses, the sperm/egg distinction — would have to change?
