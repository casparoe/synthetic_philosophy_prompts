Since assuming responsibility for my late aunt's estate, I have been cataloging a substantial collection of images produced by an early text-to-image diffusion model for which no prompt logs, attribution records, or training-set documentation survive. As I prepare recommendations for a potential donation to a university museum, I find myself confronting a modified version of Hume's problem of taste: if there is no identifiable artist whose sensibility could anchor judgment, can intersubjective agreement about these works still serve as a corrective standard, or does aesthetic evaluation collapse into mere preference? To clarify my own position, I ask you to rank the following five interpretations of Hume's standard—each revised to address authorless algorithmic images—according to how well they preserve both the subjectivity of sentiment and the possibility of rational correction across viewers. Assign ranks 1 (most adequate) through 5 (least adequate) for guiding a cataloger's display decisions, and provide one to two sentences of justification for each placement.

The five interpretations are:
A. The orthodox extension: apply Hume's standard by substituting trained human viewers of algorithmic outputs for the true judges, treating refined, unbiased perception of the image itself as the normative benchmark.
B. The institutionalist revision: beauty is determined by the deliberated verdict of a properly constituted museum acquisition board, so that individual sentiment is subordinate to collective protocol.
C. The distributed-causal reading: the model's weights encode statistical regularities derived from prior human aesthetic judgments, meaning the true standard is dispersed across the training population, and an image's merit consists in how faithfully it samples that distributed taste.
D. The eliminativist reading: without an intentional agent behind the work, aesthetic judgment lacks its proper object and collapses into bare preference reporting; consequently, Hume's framework is inapplicable to these images.
E. The phenomenological revision: the proper object of judgment is not the image file but the viewer's conscious experience of it, so the standard applies only to reports of that experience rather than to properties attributed to the image.

In your reasoning, include a direct comparison between the interpretations you place at ranks 1 and 2, and identify specifically which of the five fails most severely on Hume's own requirement that taste involves both immediate feeling and the legitimate possibility of dispute.
