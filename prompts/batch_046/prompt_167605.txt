For my philosophy of science seminar we are working through causal inference, and I encountered an unnamed supplementary handout that summarizes Simpson's paradox and its broader implications. It runs roughly like this: Simpson's paradox proves that whenever data from different subgroups are combined, the overall statistical trend can reverse the trend that holds within every subgroup, which means aggregation is inherently unreliable and should be avoided whenever any stratifying variable might be present. The handout says this is precisely why Judea Pearl's do-calculus was developed—to provide an algorithm that automatically detects which variables are true confounders so that analysts can adjust for exactly those and thereby eliminate any risk of reversal. It further claims that in the philosophy of medicine this insight has produced a widespread rejection of meta-analysis, on the ground that pooling results across studies necessarily introduces Simpson reversals and renders the pooled estimate epistemically meaningless. Finally, it suggests that the paradox undermines frequentist confidence intervals computed on aggregated samples, since the direction of association depends on an arbitrary choice about whether to stratify, leaving such intervals with no objective interpretation. Parts of this feel like standard caution, but other parts sound sweeping and possibly confused with selection-bias phenomena I have not fully sorted out. Could you audit the summary claim by claim? For each point, please distinguish what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, what subtly misrepresents the statistical result or Pearl's framework, and what is simply wrong—especially whether the conclusions about meta-analysis and frequentism actually follow. I also want to know whether the implicit rule "never pool data across a potential confounder" is consistent with mainstream causal-inference practice, or whether it conflates Simpson's paradox with a different bias mechanism altogether.
