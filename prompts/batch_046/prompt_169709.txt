I have been trying to make sense of a pattern in our laws and institutions that feels inconsistent to me, and I want to understand what deeper view it might be resting on. We treat torture as an absolute moral line: even one instance is a categorical wrong, and we do not permit it on the grounds that it might prevent greater suffering elsewhere. But at the same time, our environmental and urban policies routinely allow what might be called a dust-speck regime: each car, factory, or power plant is permitted to emit or generate a tiny amount of discomfort—slight respiratory irritation, a bit of noise, a minor stress—spread across millions of people, provided no single source crosses an individual threshold. The aggregate suffering from all those tiny harms might well exceed the suffering of one tortured person, yet the law treats the first case as a routine regulatory budget and the second as an intolerable rights violation.

What philosophical commitments does this institutional split presuppose? Does it require the belief that harm is not genuinely aggregative across persons—that there is a qualitative, not merely quantitative, difference between one severe harm and many dispersed mild ones? Or does it presuppose a particular theory of moral responsibility, one in which wrongness requires a direct, identifiable act against a specific victim rather than a statistical contribution to a background condition? Could the same underlying view license both practices consistently, or does one quietly depend on a view of value that the other denies?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of harm, value, and responsibility that makes this split seem coherent. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits well with it—for instance, is it necessarily tied to a view about human rights, or could it also be explained by practical limits on knowledge and enforcement? Then tell me whether the split is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories could justify it, or whether one particular view is required. Finally, explain what would have to change in either criminal or environmental law if we replaced that underlying view with a straightforwardly aggregative one, in which a million small harms are weighed directly against one large harm without a categorical barrier between them.
