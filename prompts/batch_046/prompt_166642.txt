I spent much of last evening in a rather heated but ultimately fruitful argument with a friend about whether a nation can literally be guilty for wrongs committed generations before anyone now alive was born, and we kept returning to a specific cluster of institutional practices that seem to take a stand on the question without ever defending it explicitly: formal state apologies for historical atrocities, reparative tax schemes that treat present citizens as financial debtors regardless of personal ancestry, and civic-education curricula that instruct students to identify with a collective moral responsibility for past national actions. My friend argued that Jaspers' well-known distinction between political, criminal, and metaphysical guilt makes sense of this cluster—political guilt attaches to the state as an enduring institution, not to individuals—yet I kept feeling that the practices together presuppose something stronger and stranger: a diachronic collective agent that retains numerical identity and moral standing despite complete turnover in membership. If we reject that metaphysical picture and treat the nation as only a succession of linked institutional roles, can the apology still be coherent, since an apology seems to require a speaker who bears the fault? Or does the reparative taxation then collapse into an unjust burden on people who never participated? And does the educational component quietly assume an origin-essentialist view of citizenship—one in which moral status is inherited like a stain—while simultaneously demanding a backward-directed shared intentionality that no standard theory of joint action actually licenses? I would like a reconstruction of the implicit philosophical commitments embedded in this triad of practices: what they strictly presuppose about intergenerational justice, collective agency, and personal identity; where they are merely compatible with several rival theories; whether their parts presuppose consistent things; and what precisely would have to change in institutional design if we replaced the diachronic-agent view with a purely institutional or role-based account.
