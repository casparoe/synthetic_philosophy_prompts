I'm a doctoral student in classics and ancient Near Eastern studies, and I'm trying to articulate the principle behind my intuitions about when a legend acquires historical status. Here are my verdicts on four cases.

First, the Trojan War: I accept that a real conflict occurred near Hisarlik in the late Bronze Age, given the archaeological destruction layer at Troy VIIa and the Hittite diplomatic references to Wilusa and Ahhiyawa. The Homeric narrative is heavily mythologized, but I treat the underlying event as historically anchored.

Second, King Arthur: I treat him as essentially synthetic. Despite Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae and the later chronicle tradition writing in a historiographical mode, there is no contemporary fifth- or sixth-century attestation, and the earliest mentions in the work attributed to Nennius and in the Annales Cambriae are ambiguous and separated by centuries. Without independent corroboration of a specific unifying leader by that name, I deny a meaningful historical kernel.

Third, the Exodus: I deny that the biblical account of 600,000 people departing Egypt and wandering forty years is historical, given the absence of archaeological traces in the Sinai and Transjordan consistent with such a population movement. I allow that smaller Semitic movements into or out of Egypt might leave a faint mnemonic trace—perhaps linked to Hyksos-era dynamics or later Levantine origins—but I do not consider this sufficient to call the narrative historically based.

Fourth, the case that keeps reversing on me: the United Monarchy of David and Solomon. The Tel Dan stele provides exogenous attestation of a "House of David," and tenth-century Jerusalem shows settlement growth, yet the biblical portrait of a vast empire with extensive building programs and far-reaching influence is likely exaggerated or anachronistic. Is this like Troy—a real event wrapped in mythic inflation—or like Arthur, where minimal evidence is being overread into a synthetic figure?

My tentative thought is that the difference lies in whether corroborating evidence is both independent of the legend-text and specific enough to anchor a particular event, regardless of how inflated the narrative scale becomes. Does that principle consistently deliver my first three verdicts? What does it predict for David, and for a case I haven't listed—say, the Minotaur legend given the real palace complex at Knossos with its labyrinthine architecture? And if the principle fails, which of my four verdicts should I abandon?
