I've been thinking about the procreation asymmetry — basically it's wrong to have a kid who'd suffer badly, but not wrong to skip having one who'd be happy — and I want to read Alfonso Cuarón's Children of Men through that lens. The film's set in a near-future Britain where everyone suddenly went infertile, and then Kee, a young refugee, turns up pregnant. Immediately everyone treats her like she's carrying the future of humanity, not just a baby. But the world she's bringing it into is basically a police state with refugee camps and constant bombings. Does the film actually take a side on whether she should have the baby? Or does it just assume reproduction is always worth it, regardless of conditions?

I'm especially interested in Theo's choice to get her to the boat — is he acting on some belief that the child has a right to exist, or is he just trying not to be the guy who let the only pregnant woman die? There's also that bit where they hide on the farm and the old couple talk about there past — does that frame reproduction as a kind of collective duty rather than an individual choice?

I'd like a reading that takes both the philosophy and the film seriously, not just reducing it to an illustration. Does Cuarón dramatise something about collective procreation — like, it's not just one couple's decison but a species-level choice — that the standard individual-focused asymmetry misses? And where does the film resist the framing: maybe the baby isn't a philosophical case study but a symbol of hope, and treating her that way misses the point? I'm writing this for a philosophy discussion group, so I want something that gets into the weeds without being misleading.
