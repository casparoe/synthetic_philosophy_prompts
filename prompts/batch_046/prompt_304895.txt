I have been working through some literature on collective action for an advanced seminar, and I keep returning to a conceptual difficulty that feels more metaphysical than practical. The standard accounts—whether Searle's analysis of collective intentionality as a primitive we-mode within individual minds, or Gilbert's theory of joint commitment generating plural subjects—both insist that genuinely joint action is irreducible to parallel individual actions. Yet the explanations for this irreducibility seem to leave the location of the collective mental state ambiguous. If the shared intention is not literally inside any single participant's head, and if we reject the idea of an emergent group consciousness that exists apart from its members, then it appears we are committed to a mental content that lacks a determinate bearer. This strikes me as structurally similar to positing a disembodied Cartesian soul: we are told that something mental is genuinely occurring, but we are denied any substrate in which it occurs.

I am trying to make sense of this through a concrete organizational model I observe in my own research collective. We operate under a rotating stewardship system: each week a different member holds executive authority over our shared database, but our founding charter requires that no steward can revise the core taxonomy without first securing explicit concurrence from every other member. When all of us agree to restructure a central category—say, merging two classification nodes—each individual certainly intends to perform his or her part, but I also feel that there is a single decision being made collectively. The problem is that if that collective decision is not identical to my private intention, nor to anyone else's private intention, and if there is no separate group mind containing it, then where is it? It seems I am forced either to accept a kind of distributed mental substance that exists in the spaces between us, or to concede that our so-called joint decision is really just a rapid sequence of individual approvals orchestrated by the charter. I suspect I am conflating ontological irreducibility with metaphysical spookiness, but I cannot see precisely where the confusion lies, and I would like to understand whether philosophers genuinely mean that joint action involves a mental state without a single bearer, or whether I have simply misinterpreted what it means for collective intentionality to be primitive.
