I spent the better part of last evening in a sustained argument with a close friend about Gödel's modal ontological argument, and I left with the distinct sense that our disagreement turned not on whether the formal derivation is valid, but on whether its axioms about positive properties smuggle in the conclusion they claim to establish. I need to work through this properly in an essay. The specific tension I want examined is this: Gödel defines a God-like being as one that possesses every positive property, and he treats the property of being God-like itself as positive, which yields necessary existence through modal logic; yet if all positive properties belong essentially to such a being, then any contingent feature of reality—say, that this particular world with these particular physical constants exists—seems to become necessary as well, unless one can cleanly separate essential divine properties from contingent acts of creation.

Please write an essay that weighs whether Gödel's framework genuinely avoids this modal-collapse problem by distinguishing between properties that are positively necessary and those that are only accidentally instantiated, or whether the distinction collapses under scrutiny and the argument therefore assumes what it sets out to prove. Reach a defended conclusion—do not retreat into agnosticism—and address whether someone who accepts Gödel's axioms is rationally compelled to accept theism, or whether those axioms are only plausible if one already believes. In doing so, treat the contingency of creation and the modal status of physical constants as central test cases rather than peripheral objections.
