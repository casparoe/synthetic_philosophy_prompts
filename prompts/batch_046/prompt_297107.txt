I've come round to a specific flavour of epiphenomenalism: phenomenal states are causally inert, but our judgements about them are "structural echoes" — brain states that mirror the structure of the experience without being caused by it, tied together only by a nomological correlation. So the classic paradox of phenomenal judgement (how can we talk about experiences if they do nothing?) just dissolves. But what would actually count against this? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to awkward — arguments, thought experiments, or empirical findings that'd break it, plus which ones defenders of the echo-view would concede. Is it well-insulated or genuinely at risk? And has it been qualified into unfalsifiability — if so, is that a problem for a view like this? Separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic, dont present a contested reading as the consensus. I'm also wondering if mirror-correlation without causation can really carry the epistemic load, or if that's where the hidden problem is.
