I was chatting with a friend over pints the other night and we ended up on why some social rules just vanish suddenly. There's a pattern I've seen in a few places — like an office where everyone used to copy the whole team into emails, or a block of flats where people always said hello in the stairwell. At first only a couple of people stop doing it, not because it's harder or they forgot, but almost like they're signalling they aren't part of the old crowd any more. Then the meaning flips: doing the old thing becomes a badge that you belong to the out-group, and the convention collapses not because it's inefficient, but because sticking with it now carries a social cost that has nothing to do with the original co-ordination problem. I've read a little about David Lewis and his idea that conventions are basically solutions to co-ordination problems held up by common knowledge of what everyone prefers. This feels like a different failure mode altogether. Is there an established term for this kind of collapse, where a Lewis-style convention dies because adherence turns into an identity marker for an out-group rather than through any change in payoffs or common knowledge? If there isn't one, can you coin and define it, show exactly how it differs from a plain preference shift and from the kind of equilibirum drift you see in evolutionary game theory, and sketch a couple of boundary cases where it does and doesn't apply? Please answer in British English — spelling, phrasing, the lot.
