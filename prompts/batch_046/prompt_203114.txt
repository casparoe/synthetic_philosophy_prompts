Please describe the most important implications of the cranes-versus-skyhooks distinction, together with the critique of greedy reductionism, when both are applied specifically to mechanistic interpretability of emergent capabilities in large language models. Consider the following framing: some researchers claim that identifying low-level statistical mechanisms—gradient-driven pattern matching, attention-head correlations, or token-level transition dynamics—fully explains behaviors such as in-context learning or chain-of-thought reasoning, and therefore treats the functional-level description of these capabilities as largely illusory. Others object that this form of reduction is greedy: rather than showing how the functional architecture actually produces the behavior, it explains the phenomenon away by collapsing it onto its substrate, leaving the organizational structure unaccounted for. Given this framing, address three things. First, does a successful low-level mechanistic account automatically constitute a crane-style explanation, or can it still be greedy? Second, what specific feature—functional preservation, hierarchical organization, counterfactual robustness, or something else—would distinguish a genuinely non-greedy, crane-based mechanistic explanation of an emergent capability like in-context learning from a greedy reduction of it? Third, what are the broader implications of this distinction for whether we should treat emergent capabilities as real functional properties of the system or as mere epiphenomena of underlying statistics?
