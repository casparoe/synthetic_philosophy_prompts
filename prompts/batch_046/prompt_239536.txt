You are a professional philosopher of early modern epistemology and metaphysics, with deep familiarity with the interpretive debates surrounding Descartes's second Meditation. Before you build the taxonomy, take several minutes to brainstorm the full logical space of answers to the question: what exactly does the cogito argument establish? Map the standard divisions—whether the conclusion is only that a thinking event occurs at this moment, whether it requires a persisting substance behind the thinking, and whether it yields knowledge of the self's nature or only of its bare existence—then inject two less commonly mapped distinctions. First, consider whether the first-person reference in the argument functions as an indexical that acquires content solely through the act of thinking, or as a rigid designator that already picks out a specific entity. Second, consider whether a synthetic, non-biological system that generated structurally analogous first-person, introspective discourse could replicate the same epistemic move, and whether that possibility undermines or clarifies the original argument.

Your deliverable is a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts interpreters by their actual commitments, not by the labels they prefer. The tree must begin from a single root: acceptance that the argument carries legitimate epistemic force. Each internal node should be a concrete diagnostic claim or hypothetical case, phrased as a question that an intelligent reader could answer without already knowing which named interpretation it points toward. For example, ask whether the occurrence of thinking necessarily implies a thinker-substance, or whether it is consistent with the view that there is only a momentary event with no underlying owner. Include the branch on artificial cognition: if a system without biological substrate produced coherent, first-person reports with the same functional structure, would it be entitled to the same certainty, and does that affect how we understand the scope of the original inference?

Every leaf should carry either the name of a recognized interpretive stance—such as the minimalist or existential reading, the thick substantialist reading, the performative or anti-representational reading, or the indexical-reference reading—or be marked as unnamed and unoccupied. Each leaf must end with exactly one sentence describing that stance's most awkward commitment, the specific price its defenders must pay. Wherever your tree genuinely cannot separate two positions, flag the pair explicitly rather than papering over the overlap. Wherever a logical combination of answers yields an empty space, include it and briefly diagnose whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect in the literature.

The taxonomy should be rigorous, self-contained, and polished enough to serve as a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on Cartesian epistemology. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not rely on obscure archival details or precise edition-specific wording. Do not include verbatim quotations from Descartes's text or from secondary literature; paraphrase any specific claims in your own language. Write in clear American English, use indentation or clear branch notation so the logical flow is visible at a glance, and ensure that no branch depends on prior knowledge of the label it leads to.
