As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and moral psychology, I am drafting a long-form essay that enters the well-known exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on free will, moral responsibility, and the justification of punishment. Rather than replaying Dennett’s compatibilist defense of desert-based punishment as an indispensable social technology, or Caruso’s skeptical challenge that retributivism depends on a metaphysically dubious libertarian conception of the self, I want to develop a framing that neither author, to my knowledge, has articulated in precisely this form. The essay’s central claim is that legal institutions routinely engage in what I call retrospective agency reconstruction: after an offense has occurred, courts and communal narratives selectively foreground or suppress features of an agent’s psychological history—not in order to discover a pre-existing metaphysical fact about freedom, but to render the subsequent punishment narratively coherent with shared normative commitments. On this view, desert is neither a natural property detected by judgment nor a pure fiction to be eliminated in favor of quarantine-style rehabilitation; it is a performative status conferred through the ritual of judgment itself. I argue that this explains why retributive intuitions remain resilient even amid widespread acceptance of neuroscientific determinism: societies are not tracking an underlying fact about agency, but periodically reaffirming the coherence of a normative framework that requires public, symbolic closure.

My concern is whether this framing genuinely occupies a middle ground or merely collapses into one of the two poles once translated into institutional terms. If the reconstruction of agency is purely functional—aimed solely at maintaining social cohesion—does it not reduce to Dennett’s pragmatic compatibilism, with performative status serving as a decorative synonym for useful social technology? Conversely, if the reconstruction is acknowledged to be post-hoc and metaphysically groundless, does it not concede Caruso’s central eliminativist claim, leaving only the cosmetic difference of calling the process ritual rather than fiction? I also worry that the concept may be unfalsifiable: any observed feature of a legal judgment could be interpreted either as genuine tracking of responsibility or as strategic narrative selection, with no independent criterion to distinguish the two. Does the argument need an empirical or comparative-historical anchor—perhaps cross-cultural variation in how different legal systems weight psychological history—to avoid becoming an exercise in conceptual taxonomy with little purchase on actual penal practice?

Could you comment on the coherence and originality of this line of argument, particularly with respect to whether it successfully distinguishes itself from both Dennett’s and Caruso’s positions? I am especially interested in suggestions for sharpening the boundary conditions: under what institutional circumstances, if any, would retrospective agency reconstruction fail to justify a particular punishment, and what would that failure look like in practice? Any observations on the accuracy of my broad characterization of the two philosophers’ positions, on whether the framing introduces avoidable ambiguities, or on specific sections where the reasoning feels underdeveloped would also be valuable. I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations, reading lists, or citations, but conceptual critique and structural suggestions that could help me clarify where this proposed middle ground actually stands.
