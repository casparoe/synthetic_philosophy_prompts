I teach an upper-level seminar on moral psychology and social ontology—twelve graduate students, one 90-minute session. Last week, after discussing Bernard Williams and some recent work on collective moral emotions, a student asked whether a culture could deliberately engineer its dominant moral emotion—whether, say, a society could rationally decide to institutionalize shame rather than guilt, or vice versa, and what it would cost. I want an in-class structured deliberation and role-play that lets the group feel the force of that question from the inside, not just analyze it from the outside. To keep the exercise from collapsing into the familiar private-vs-public distinction, I have been working with a framing I call emotional infrastructure: moral emotions as institutionalized feedback loops embedded in legal rituals, educational scripts, and public ceremonies rather than as purely internal states.

Please design a runnable activity—roles, sequence, materials, symbolic stakes—that stages a miniature cooperative of twelve members who have jointly caused a serious but ambiguous harm. The group should be split into two institutional designs: one operating under a guilt-based repair protocol (private acknowledgment to the harmed party, material restitution, quiet reintegration) and one under a shame-based exposure protocol (public acknowledgment before the collective, temporary status degradation, collective witnessing of repair). Participants should experience both protocols in short rounds, then vote on which design to adopt for a final, higher-stakes round. Crucially, include a mechanism—perhaps an unexpected institutional crisis or a hybrid protocol attempt—that reveals why switching emotional infrastructures mid-process generates a kind of incoherence that neither pure framework resolves. After the exercise, provide a debrief that maps each round onto the theoretical distinction, identifies the specific moment where participants usually conflate emotional discomfort with moral failure, and is frank about what the simulation cannot reproduce (the long-term internalization of norms across generations) and therefore must be supplied by facilitation. Finally, note what will predictably go wrong the first time—likely role confusion or emotional spillover between rounds—and how to handle it.
