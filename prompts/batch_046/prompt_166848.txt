ok so for my poli sci / philosophy crossover seminar i've been trying to pin down my actual views on national memory and i want a consistency check before i present. here's where i stand:

(1) nations are basically imagined communities in Anderson's sense, not primordial things that just woke up in 1789.
(2) most of what counts as national tradition was invented in the 19th/early 20th c in Hobsbawm's sense, especially the ceremonial stuff.
(3) but invented traditions can become "real" in a functional sense once they're embedded in schools, armies, etc. — so "invented" doesn't mean "fake" to me.
(4) democratic legitmacy requires what i call mnemonic transparency: the state shouldn't systematically suppress archival records of its own violence.
(5) at the same time, i think every collective needs some strategic amnesia — Renan-style forgetting — to keep going, and that's morally okay if it's bottom-up from civil society rather than state-mandated.
(6) i also think the "age" of a nation shouldn't be measured by institutional continuity but by narrative density — how thick and self-referential the shared historical stories are.
(7) and i think small states have more right to mnemonic opacity than big ones, because their survival literally depends on cohesive myth.

do these hang together? i'm especially worried about (4) and (5) pulling apart, and whether (6) undermines (1)-(2) by letting recently invented nations score high on "age." but also tell me if there's a tension i missed, maybe between (5)'s bottom-up condition and (1)'s idea that the state is usually the one doing the imagining. rank the tensions by severity and say what'd be the cheapest fix for each. don't just list everything as equally bad — i want to know which ones are only apparent.
