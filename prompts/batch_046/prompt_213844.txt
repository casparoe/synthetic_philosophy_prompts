In my reporting on extended cognition and personal identity, I keep encountering an argument drawn from Hofstadter: the self is a strange loop — a self-referential pattern rather than a particular brain — so distributing it across two brains should be no more metaphysically troubling than performing the same symphony with two orchestras. Proponents use this to claim that identity is already distributed across minds, since we carry fragments of others in memory and culture, and that full duplication is therefore only a quantitative extension, not a qualitative break. Before I write this up, I want the analogy audited properly. Could you lay out the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the score, the conductor, the acoustic space, the listener who recognizes continuity — and then identify precisely where the mapping collapses? I am especially interested in cases the orchestra picture quietly suppresses: asymmetric persistence, in which one brain hosts only a satellite loop that references the primary loop without achieving the reflexive closure Hofstadter treats as essential; degradation of one substrate without the other knowing; and whether the two instances remain a single loop or become competing loops with diverging trajectories. Does the conclusion — that duplication poses no special metaphysical problem for personal identity — survive once these disanalogies are on the table, or was the symphony analogy smuggling in the very unity it claimed merely to describe? And if the analogy fails here, what would be a more accurate model for partial or distributed persistence of a strange loop?
