I've been thinking about disjunctivism ever since I got these tinted contacts for my migraines — they shift colors just enough that I'm never totally sure if I'm seeing the real shade or the filtered one, and it made me wonder about that old philosophy problem where people say hallucination and real seeing feel the same so they must share some inner mental thing.

Anyway, I was talking with two friends about it — you know, the view that veridical perception and hallucination aren't two versions of the same mental state but totally different kinds of thing. They got into it and I honestly couldn't tell who was wining. Maya said the argument from illusion still kills disjunctivism: if you can have a perfect hallucination of a red apple that feels exactly like seeing one, then both experiences share the same phenomenal character, which means there's a common mental ingredient — an appearance or sense datum — and disjunctivism is wrong to deny that shared core. She said my contact case actually proves it: my filtered view and my unfiltered view feel different, but a full hallucination and real seeing can feel identical, so there must be a mental state they share. Leo pushed back, saying Maya's smuggling in the conclusion: just because two experiences are subjectively indistinguishable doesn't mean they're the same kind of mental state. He compared it to two different diseases with identical symptoms — same feel, different underlying nature. He said in the contact case, I'm actually having two different perceptual relations to the world (one mediated, one direct), and even if I can't tell them apart introspecionally, they're still disjunct. Then he added this weird twist about gradual degradation: if you slowly dim the lights until you're in total darkness but still seeing black, there's no sharp point where the shared mental ingredient suddenly disappears, which makes the common-factor view look like it's tracking something metaphysically arbitrary. Maya replied that Leo's disease analogy misses the point because mental states are defined by how things seem to the subject, not hidden causal structure. She said if disjunctivists allow perfect hallucinations exist, they've already conceded there's a mental state with the same phenomenal profile as perception, and then they're just playing word games by calling it a different kind. She also said Leo's gradual-degradation point backfires: if there's no sharp cutoff, that's exactly why we need a common mental factor that persists across the change, not two radically different states that blur together. Leo said that's backwards — the blur supports disjunctivism because it shows we're tracking two different processes (engagement with the world vs. internal generation) that overlap in effects without sharing an essence. He ended by saying Maya's view makes illusion impossible in principle, since any error has to be a relation between a mental state and the world, but if hallucination is a totally different state with no world-relation, then there's nothing for the illusion to be wrong about — it's just a different mental event, not a misrepresentation.

They asked me to referee since I'm the one who brought it up with my contacts thing. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about each specific point? Not just who wins overall — I want to know if Leo's disease analogy holds water, whether the gradual-degradation thing actually helps anyone, and whether Maya's charge that disjunctivism makes illusion impossible is fair. Also be honest about what both are missing — I feel like they're talking past each other on something about introspection but I can't put my finger on it, and maybe there's something about the contact case that neither of them used right.
