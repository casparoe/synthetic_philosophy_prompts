Consider the published case judgments of Dr. Amara Okonkwo, a philosopher of social epistemology who has never fully systematized her account of epistemic injustice. In her analyses she consistently maintains the following four verdicts:

(i) In the 2014 Ferguson grand-jury proceedings, the systematic discounting of Black eyewitness testimony about police conduct—despite corroborating video evidence—constitutes testimonial injustice rooted in a racial credibility deficit.

(ii) Before the clinical recognition of “postpartum psychosis” as a distinct diagnostic category, women who experienced severe perinatal mental-health crises were routinely interpreted through the lens of generic “hysteria” or moral weakness; Okonkwo treats this as hermeneutical injustice produced by gendered gaps in collective medical conceptual resources.

(iii) In peer review for interdisciplinary journals, she argues that the rejection of ethnographic research written in narrative, first-person styles—common in several Indigenous research traditions—is frequently misclassified as “lacking rigor” rather than recognized as deploying different but legitimate epistemic norms; she classifies this as hermeneutical injustice tied to colonial epistemic power.

(iv) By contrast, she denies that epistemic injustice occurs when a well-funded pharmaceutical corporation suppresses negative trial data through legal and regulatory mechanisms. This is epistemic corruption or institutional fraud, she insists, but not injustice, because no specific knower is wronged in her capacity as a knower by an identity-based deficit; the harm is distributive and structural rather than directed at a marginalized epistemic subject.

Now consider a new case. A neuroscience laboratory has developed a “collective inference protocol” in which EEG signals from ten participants are algorithmically aggregated to produce a single group judgment about ambiguous visual stimuli. The aggregation weights each participant’s contribution by an “epistemic reliability score” calibrated on prior task performance in the majority-language population. One participant—who belongs to a linguistic minority with documented differences in spatial-cognitive framing—reliably produces neural responses that diverge from the aggregate but that are, by independent behavioral measure, accurate interpretations of the stimuli. The algorithm systematically downweights her input, and the research team reports her contribution as “noise.” She contends that the protocol wrongs her by treating her culturally shaped cognition as defective rather than different.
