I am a journalist who covers philosophy of mind and cognitive science for a monthly ideas magazine, and I am currently auditing an advanced graduate seminar on consciousness studies for a feature I am writing about why the hard problem persists despite decades of naturalistic progress. The instructor shared with me an exam question and a single student response, asking for my independent grading assessment rather than his own. The assignment asks students to do three things: first, explain David Chalmers's distinction between the hard problem of consciousness and the meta-problem of consciousness; second, clarify why the meta-problem is particularly urgent for physicalists rather than for dualists; and third, evaluate whether a complete naturalistic solution to the meta-problem—specifically one that combines the phenomenal concept strategy with an error theory of introspection—would thereby dissolve the hard problem itself, or whether the epistemic gap and the metaphysical gap remain genuinely independent even after such a solution, with careful attention to whether solving the meta-problem removes the explanatory burden or merely relocates it.

The student's answer reads as follows. The meta-problem of consciousness, as Chalmers introduces it in The Conscious Mind (1996), asks why we think there is a hard problem in the first place. It concerns our judgments that phenomenal experience is not reducible to physical processes. Physicalists should care because if they can explain away these judgments—say, through the phenomenal concept strategy, which shows that our concepts of consciousness are special and create an illusion of irreducibility—then the hard problem loses its force. Dualists, by contrast, already accept that consciousness is non-physical, so explaining why people think so does not threaten their view. However, I think solving the meta-problem does dissolve the hard problem. Once we have a complete naturalistic story—perhaps combining the phenomenal concept strategy with an error theory of introspection—there is nothing left to explain. The metaphysical gap collapses into an epistemic gap, and Chalmers's distinction between them is really just a distinction in our confidence levels. In fact, Chalmers himself admits that the meta-problem is the only real problem, because if you solve it, the hard problem becomes a pseudo-problem. Moreover, the meta-problem applies symmetrically to dualists: they also need to explain why people have physicalist intuitions. So the burden is shared equally.
