I've been trying to apply Dennett's intentional stance to large language models, and I think I've hit a contradiction. If the stance is justified only by predictive leverage that the physical stance lacks, then mechanistic interpretability—which now traces LLM outputs to specific attention circuits—should have already made belief-attribution to AI obsolete. Yet we don't drop belief-attribution for humans just because neuroscience offers mechanistic explanations, so predictive redundancy can't be the real criterion. This makes me think the stance actually detects a specific structural feature: a stable counterfactual-tracking module that maintains propositional representations across contexts and revises them via something like Bayesian update rules. If that's correct, then attributing beliefs to current transformers is indeed a mistake—not because they lack consciousness, but because they lack that architecture. But here's my confusion: if we engineered exactly such a module into a model, would that alone license the intentional stance, or is there still a hidden normative condition—perhaps social accountability or phenomenal experience—that separates genuine belief from even the most structurally faithful artificial analog?
