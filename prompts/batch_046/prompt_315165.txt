Exam question: Grice distinguishes sentence meaning, speaker meaning, and what is said, and introduces the cooperative principle to account for conversational implicature. A familiar difficulty concerns conventional implicature—meanings conventionally attached to expressions such as “but” or “therefore” that are not part of what is said and are not cancellable. Some philosophers argue that Grice’s intentionalist analysis of speaker meaning cannot accommodate conventional implicature, since these implications depend on linguistic convention rather than on the speaker’s particular communicative intentions. Others maintain that Grice can absorb them without abandoning his framework.

Write an essay that (a) explains Grice’s distinction between what is said and what is conventionally implicated, using “but” as your central example; (b) sets out the intentionalist analysis of speaker meaning Grice offers, emphasizing the recognition condition; and (c) evaluates whether this analysis can consistently treat conventional implicatures as part of speaker meaning, or whether they must be relegated to sentence meaning alone. Be precise about whether the speaker’s choice of expression can carry an intention directed at the conventional content.

Student answer: Grice’s cooperative principle requires that conversational participants make their contributions such as is required by the accepted purpose of the talk exchange. From this principle and its maxims of quantity, quality, relation, and manner, Grice derives conversational implicatures—meanings that are not part of what is said but are inferred by the hearer based on the assumption that the speaker is being cooperative. By contrast, conventional implicature is attached directly to particular words. For instance, when a speaker says, “She is poor but honest,” the word “but” conventionally signals a contrast between poverty and honesty, yet this contrast is not part of the truth-conditions of the utterance. If the speaker were to add, “and there is no contrast whatsoever,” the result would be contradictory, which shows that conventional implicatures are not cancellable, unlike conversational implicatures.

In his analysis of speaker meaning, Grice argues that to mean something is to intend to induce a belief in one’s audience, where the audience’s recognition of that intention plays a causal role in producing the belief. More specifically, the speaker must have a primary intention that the audience form a particular belief, and the speaker must also intend that the audience recognize this primary intention. This two-layered structure is often called the recognition condition. Because speaker meaning is defined entirely by these layered intentions, anything that is not the object of such an intention cannot count as part of what the speaker means.

Applying this to conventional implicature, we see a problem. The speaker who uses “but” does not need to form a separate, primary intention specifically directed at the contrastive content. Merely selecting the word automatically generates the contrast, regardless of what the speaker particularly intends. Therefore, the contrast belongs to sentence meaning, not to speaker meaning. The speaker means only that she is poor and that she is honest; the conventional device adds the contrast on its own.

Some defenders of Grice reply that the speaker’s choice of “but” over “and” reveals an intention to be understood as drawing a contrast. But this reply confuses the speaker’s general intention to communicate with the specific M-intention structure Grice requires. Choosing a word is not the same as intending that one’s audience recognize a particular belief-forming intention directed at that word’s conventional force. Since Grice’s framework demands that every element of speaker meaning be backed by a corresponding primary intention, and since no such primary intention exists for the contrastive element, conventional implicature must fall outside speaker meaning. It is part of what the sentence conventionally conveys, and the speaker activates it mechanically, not meaningfully.

Thus, Grice’s intentionalism can accommodate conventional implicature only by weakening the recognition condition—allowing conventional activation to substitute for intentional direction—which he should reject. The proper conclusion is that conventional implicatures belong to sentence meaning alone, and Grice’s distinction between saying and implicating should be restricted to conversational cases.
