Construct a thought experiment in animal ethics and speciesism that isolates the moral-status dispute by injecting a near-future, non-science-fictional twist. Design a one-paragraph scenario involving a chimpanzee or pig fitted with a stable, reversible cortical prosthesis that confers human-equivalent linguistic reasoning, autobiographical memory, and moral reciprocity, while leaving its original species-typical affective and sensory architecture intact; place it in a realistic research-lab or sanctuary workplace, not a trolley case, and do not invoke consciousness detectors, perfect predictors, or exotic physics. Engineer the case so that three positions diverge: a capacity-based anti-speciesist (who denies that biological species is morally relevant and grounds status in realized cognitive-functional abilities), a biological-speciesist (who holds that membership in Homo sapiens is necessary for equal rights regardless of augmented capacities), and a relational view (which grounds moral status in the capacity for mutual recognition within a shared social world rather than in internal capacities or biology alone). After presenting the scenario, do the following in order: state each view’s verdict and the reasoning principle behind it; identify the single feature—functional augmentation, biological origin, or social embeddedness—that drives the divergence, and show exactly what happens to all three verdicts if that feature is removed (e.g., the prosthesis is deactivated and the being reverts to baseline capacities); and include an honest note on how the defender of the view that comes out worst—most likely the biological speciesist—would attack the scenario, together with an assessment of whether that attack succeeds without begging the question. Lead with the scenario and a one-sentence summary of the three verdicts, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
