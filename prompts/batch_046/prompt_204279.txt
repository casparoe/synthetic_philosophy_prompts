As a retired physicist who spent much of my career studying irreversible thermodynamic processes, I have recently begun reading philosophy of decision and find myself dissatisfied with the usual treatment of the sunk cost fallacy. In economics and psychology, honoring unrecoverable past investments is generally condemned as a failure of rationality: a competent agent should weigh only marginal future costs and benefits, treating losses that cannot be recovered as irrelevant to present choice. Yet in physical systems, prior expenditure of energy or resources frequently reshapes the very state space within which future dynamics unfold—consider hysteresis in ferromagnetic materials, or the canalization of developmental pathways in biology—so that continuing along an established trajectory is sometimes the only physically realizable option, not a cognitive mistake. This suggests to me that the blanket normative prohibition against honoring sunk costs in human affairs may overlook an important structural possibility: when a prior commitment has genuinely altered the feasibility, cost, or available set of alternatives, continuing may be rational in a forward-looking sense rather than a mere bias.

Write an essay on whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. Weigh the standard economic argument—that rational choice requires exclusive attention to future prospects—against the claim that prior investments can sometimes rationally constrain present options by altering the structural landscape of choice, drawing on analogies to physical path dependence or biological commitment mechanisms if you find them illuminating, but without allowing those analogies to settle the matter by themselves. Address serious counterarguments: that human social and economic contexts rarely exhibit true hysteresis; that psychological mechanisms of commitment and identity preservation are distinct from objective structural constraints; and that invoking an "altered state space" risks providing a sophisticated rationalization for ordinary stubbornness or loss aversion. Reach a definite conclusion. Is there any coherent class of human decisions in which honoring a sunk cost is genuinely rational, or is the fallacy universal across all domains? Defend your verdict with reasons that engage both the normative economic framework and the structural-analogy challenge outlined above.
