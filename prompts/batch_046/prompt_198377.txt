I have no formal background in philosophy or cognitive science, but I have been thinking on my own about whether a large group of simple parts can ever amount to a single mind. The question first seized me after I encountered Douglas Hofstadter’s “Ant Fugue,” with its image of an ant colony named Aunt Hillary—thousands of individually mindless insects interacting so richly that the colony as a whole remembers, decides, and even converses. Since then I have tried to apply the same intuition to other cases, and I want to know whether my judgments cohere.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Aunt Hillary herself. I feel inclined to say yes: there is probably something mind-like in the colony. No ant understands anything, yet together they maintain memory across days, adapt plans, and respond to disturbances as one sustained entity. That looks more like thinking than like weather.

2. A human brain. Clearly yes. If neurons—mere cells—can combine into a conscious person, then “simple parts never make a mind” is already false.

3. A large corporation with thousands of employees. I say no. It makes decisions, adapts, and survives crises, but there is never a unified point of view or felt experience inside it; it is only people interacting within a legal frame.

4. A murmuration of starlings. Also no. Each bird tracks only its neighbors; the beautiful shape has no center, no memory between flights, and no “inside.”

5. The “Slow Hill” case. Imagine every ant in Aunt Hillary is replaced by a tiny robot that behaves identically, except each interaction now takes a thousand times longer—so a “thought” that once lasted a second now takes an hour. My verdict: the mind is still there, running in extreme slow motion. Substrate and speed seem irrelevant; only the pattern of interaction matters.

6. The “Split Hill” case. Now divide Aunt Hillary physically into two separated colonies, each with half the ants (or robots). Both continue to function like smaller versions of the original. I am genuinely torn. Part of me says both are now minds in their own right; part of me says the original single mind has been destroyed and replaced by two weaker ones. I cannot tell whether a mind divides like a liquid or whether it is all-or-nothing.

7. The “Playback Hill” case. Record every interaction in Aunt Hillary and run it as a perfect computer simulation. The screen shows the same patterns and “decisions,” but there are no bodies, no ants, no robots—only code. Here I feel firmly that there is no mind; it is a movie of a mind, not a mind itself.

My problem is that cases 5, 6, and 7 pull in conflicting directions. Case 5 suggests that only functional pattern matters. Case 7 suggests that something more than pure information—perhaps physical presence or causal interaction—does matter. Case 6 then raises the question of whether identity is preserved through division.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all seven verdicts? If so, what does it say about further cases—for example, a colony in which ants are replaced one by one with robots over months, or a colony whose interactions are paused for a year and then resumed? If no single principle saves them all, which verdicts must I abandon?
