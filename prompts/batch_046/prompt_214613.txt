I am a retired physicist recently turned to philosophy of science, and next month I present to a joint history and philosophy of science seminar my claim that Nietzsche’s framework in his essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life—monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—omits a mode specific to laboratory practice that I call calibration history: the accumulated record of failed instruments, corrected measurements, and discarded protocols. I argue that this mode is not mere antiquarian archiving but performs a genuinely critical function by undermining present methodological certainty, and that scientific education suppresses it in favor of heroic monumental narratives and neutral data archives, which paralyzes rather than serves present practice. The audience includes three historians of physics who specialize in twentieth-century instrumentation, two scholars of Nietzsche’s Untimely Meditations, and several working scientists skeptical of normative historiography. Prepare me for the objections: order them by likelihood and damage, phrase each as that specific audience member would phrase it, note which objections I should concede outright, which I should hold, and how to reply in two to three minutes. Identify the weakest point in my own position that I should defend rather than hope they overlook, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Separate what is settled scholarship on Nietzsche’s three modes from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed reading as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
