Consider the consistent case judgments of a clinical ethicist whose reasoning I have followed across several years. In Case 1, she held that a physician who declines to initiate dialysis for a consenting patient with end-stage renal failure allows death but does not kill; the omission is permissible. In Case 2, she held that stopping dialysis that has already been running for months, at the patient’s request, is also allowing death and is morally on par with Case 1, not a transition to killing. In Case 3, she held that a passerby who could rescue a drowning stranger without risk but walks away allows death; the omission is gravely wrong yet remains categorically different from pushing the stranger in. In Case 4, she held that a corporation that manufactures the only source of a life-sustaining synthetic enzyme and deliberately halts production—knowing that thousands who depend on it will die—acts closer to killing than to Case 3, on the ground that the corporation engineered and controls the dependency itself. In Case 5, she held that a surgeon who stops transmitting the activation signal to an implanted device that suppresses a fatal neural cascade—thereby letting the patient’s own pathology resume—is allowing death, analogous to Case 2, even though the surgeon must take a positive step to discontinue the signal.

Now consider Case 6. A centralized hub actively maintains a distributed network of implanted suppressor devices across fifty patients; each device only functions while the hub performs periodic maintenance. The sole administrator knows that skipping one maintenance cycle will cause all suppressors to fail and every patient to die from their pre-existing conditions, but she deliberately skips the cycle. She takes no action that damages hardware or initiates a new lethal process; the deaths are caused entirely by the patients’ underlying pathologies once suppression lapses.

If we want to preserve consistency with her prior judgments, what should she say about Case 6? Identify the closest analog among Cases 1–5, and lay out the two or three competing principles—tracking, for instance, withdrawal of an artificial intervention, engineered dependency, or direct causal initiation—that can each accommodate her earlier verdicts. For each principle, state what it predicts about Case 6. Then give your best guess at her actual verdict, with the reasoning that favors it, and specify the single further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the leading principles from one another.
