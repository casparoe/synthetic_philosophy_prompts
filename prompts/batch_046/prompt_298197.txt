I'm drafting the framing chapter for my seminar paper in a course on political philosophy and public space, and I want a consistency check before I lock in the architecture. I hold that commemorative practices are a form of public moral speech: placing a figure on a central monument or on a street sign expresses collective endorsement, so honoring someone whose actions caused serious harm can wrong present citizens regardless of other achievements. At the same time, I insist on historical indexing in moral evaluation—we should assess figures by the moral standards that were available and widely recognized in their own era, not by anachronistic present norms, lest we fall into a kind of moral arrogance that makes cross-temporal judgment impossible.

I also take a structural view of urban space: monuments and names do not merely sit in the landscape; they organize attention and shape what I call the moral ecology of the city, making some lives visible and others invisible. From this I derive a distributive visibility principle—commemorative space should be allocated in rough proportion to moral desert, with a strict threshold below which no honorific form, statue or street name, is appropriate. I distinguish sharply between indexical commemorative forms, such as street names embedded in everyday practical navigation, and iconic forms, such as statues that invite contemplative distance, and I hold that indexical forms should face stricter scrutiny because they lack the framing distance that permits critical reflection. I further accept that democratic legitimacy requires removal decisions to emerge from inclusive local deliberation rather than philosopher-led audit, and that abrupt removals can damage the moral ecology by destroying the pedagogical value that comes from confronting, rather than erasing, past wrongs. Finally, I maintain a case-level verdict: if a figure's primary historical significance derives from actions that fall below the moral threshold, then neither form is permissible, but if the wrongs are incidental to a larger legacy of public service, iconic preservation with contextual framing is permissible while indexical forms should still be changed.

Individually these commitments feel plausible, but together they may not survive. The pairing of historical indexing with the distributive visibility principle might seem to dissolve the threshold—if past standards excuse many figures, the principle loses bite—but I suspect they operate at different levels, culpability versus current allocation, so the tension may be only apparent. More seriously, my claim that honoring expresses wrongful endorsement is hard to square with my insistence that democratic processes and pedagogical continuity can justify maintaining a monument, at least temporarily; and my stricter standard for indexical forms pulls against my worry that abrupt removals harm the moral ecology. Could you audit this set? I want the tensions ranked by severity, each paired with the least costly revision—whether weakening the structural view, separating moral permissibility from democratic legitimacy, or revising the central-versus-incidental wrongs distinction—and I want you to say explicitly what each revision costs in coherence. Please do not treat every pair as equally contradictory; discriminate between genuine conflicts and those that dissolve on inspection.
