I've been part of an online reading circle that meets every Tuesday to work through older essays on newspapers and crowd psychology, mostly because one member works in tech and kept insisting that social media felt like something these writers had already diagnosed. We spent several months on Kierkegaard's argument that the modern press fabricates an abstract entity called "the public," which allows real individuals to dissolve into anonymity and avoid the kind of decisive commitment that true belief requires. Recently our discussion has stalled in a frustrating way: half the group claims that algorithmic platforms are essentially the same mechanism accelerated, since they still dissolve personal responsibility into a mass of reactive opinions, while the other half argues that personalization breaks the analogy entirely, because an algorithm feeds each user a custom stream rather than broadcasting one uniform message to an undifferentiated crowd. We have argued through the obvious differences—speed, economic incentives, anonymity versus pseudonymity, the absence of an editorial gatekeeper—and yet for months no one has changed position. I am trying to understand whether this impasse is verbal, arising from different uses of "the public" or "leveling"; whether it hides an empirical disagreement about how recommendation engines actually shape belief formation that neither side knows how to test; whether we are answering different questions, one about individual moral responsibility and one about systemic design, under a single heading; or whether it ultimately rests on bedrock intuitions about authenticity that neither camp can argue for. Given whatever diagnosis you offer, what would count as genuine progress— a conceptual distinction we have missed, a formal or empirical result that could settle part of the dispute, a change of question, or is the comparison itself ill-posed in a way that should make us abandon it?
