I'm working through Franz Brentano's Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkte (1874), specifically the sections on inner consciousness (innere Wahrnehmung) and the unity of consciousness. Here is the passage, rendered closely in modern terms but without quoting any edition: Every mental phenomenon is the object of an inner perception that accompanies it; this perception does not occur by means of a separate mental act, but belongs to the phenomenon itself as a kind of secondary reference. The phenomenon is directed primarily upon an object—say, a heard sound—and secondarily upon itself. Brentano insists that this secondary consciousness is infallible: we cannot be wrong about whether we are currently undergoing a mental act.

I'm trying to understand whether this "one act, two directions" structure commits Brentano to a self-representational theory—where the mental state represents itself—or whether it is actually an early form of higher-order representationalism in which a single act plays two representational roles that are conceptually distinguishable. The difficulty is that if the act is literally one and the same, then there seems to be no distinction between the representing state and the represented state, which is what self-representationalists want. But if there is any structural distinction between primary and secondary object, then it looks like Brentano has introduced a relational element that requires two terms, even if they are co-instantiated.

More specifically: Brentano distinguishes between presentation (Vorstellung) and judgment (Urteil), and he claims that inner perception applies only to presentations, not to judgments. If self-consciousness is restricted to the presentational layer, does that mean that on Brentano's view a subject could have a false higher-order belief about their own judgment—say, believing they have judged that p when they have not—while remaining infallible about the underlying presentation? Or does the restriction to presentations block the very possibility of misrepresentation at the higher-order level, and if so, is that a virtue (it solves the targetless HOT problem) or a vice (it makes self-consciousness too thin to account for reflective awareness)?

Please take a definite position: Is Brentano best read as anticipating a self-representational or a higher-order view? Explain which premise or distinction in the passage does the work for your reading, and state what each modern camp—say, a Rosenthal-style HOT theorist and a Kriegel-style self-representationalist—would have to concede or reinterpret in Brentano to accommodate your interpretation. Be explicit about whether the infallibility claim is compatible with modern accounts of misrepresentation, and if not, which part of Brentano's framework should be abandoned.
