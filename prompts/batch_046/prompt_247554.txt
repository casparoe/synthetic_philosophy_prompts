As a software engineer without formal training in political philosophy, I have been trying to map concepts from distributed-systems design onto institutional design—specifically, how to aggregate collective preferences without allowing well-resourced actors to capture the process. This led me to lottocracy and sortition: the proposal that legislative or advisory bodies be filled by random selection rather than election. I want to understand whether this is a structurally sound remedy to democratic deficits or merely an elegant but unworkable abstraction.

Write an essay on the controversy over whether sortition-based governance can be justified as a replacement for, or serious supplement to, elected representation in a modern state. Weigh the principal arguments in favor—elimination of campaign corruption, improved descriptive representation, and the creation of conditions for genuine deliberation—against the principal objections: that it severs electoral accountability, risks variable competence or motivation among selected citizens, and may produce outcomes lacking a clear democratic mandate. Crucially, evaluate a specific hybrid architecture I have been considering, in which a lot-selected deliberative chamber drafts legislation but the broader electorate retains ratification power through direct referendum; ask whether this solves the accountability deficit or merely relocates it to a different veto point. Reach and defend a definite conclusion—either that sortition is justified only under specific conditions you specify, that the hybrid model is the only coherent institutional form, or that the democratic costs remain insurmountable regardless of design. Reason through the institutional logic directly rather than appealing to authorities or literature.
