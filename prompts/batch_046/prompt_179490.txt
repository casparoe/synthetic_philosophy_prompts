I have been working with an orientation I call frictional pluralism: the view that in certain domains, practical reason should preserve irreducible tension between values rather than resolve it into a single metric or ordering. I want to know whether my specific judgments actually cohere around a principle, or whether the orientation is merely a label for inconsistent intuitions.

Here are my verdicts across six cases.

One, museum display: when a ritual object carries meanings that are ambiguous within its original culture and resist translation into universal moral categories, I prefer displaying it within its native framework—leaving its significance partly opaque—rather than reframing it within a unified narrative of human dignity that clarifies its importance but flattens its internal value structure.

Two, jurisprudence: I prefer a legal system that retains contradictory precedents reflecting different moral traditions over a codified system that resolves all conflicts through one consistent principle of liability, even if consistency yields more predictable rulings.

Three, philosophical translation: I believe a text should be rendered with its internal inconsistencies intact rather than reconstructed into a coherent system that the author never endorsed.

Four, research funding: given a pluralistic program that integrates incommensurable paradigms with moderate predictive success and a unified program with higher average success, I favor sustaining the pluralistic one.

Five, emergency public health: during an acute crisis with rising mortality, I support imposing a uniform protocol optimized for lives saved by one metric, even when this violates community values that cannot be mapped onto that metric—despite my general resistance to uniform protocols.

Six, constitutional design: I hold that a durable framework should embed irreconcilable values and institutionalize their conflict rather than selecting a single foundational principle.

These verdicts seem to cluster, but case five threatens the cluster. Is there a precise principle—perhaps distinguishing structural frameworks from operational interventions, or reversible institutional choices from irreversible immediate actions, or long-horizon commitments from short-horizon decisions—that saves all six? If so, state it formally, map each clause to the case that motivates it, and apply it to a case I have not considered: a binding international environmental treaty, lasting centuries but allowing periodic revision, that must choose between a unified global welfare standard and a patchwork of local ecological regimes that preserve incompatible values.

If no principle can accommodate all six without ad hoc adjustments, identify the outlier verdict that should be abandoned for coherence, state the simpler principle that survives, and propose a further test case—distinct from the treaty scenario—that would discriminate your principle from its closest competitor.
