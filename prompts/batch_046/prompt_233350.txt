A fellow graduate student summarized Adam Smith’s account in the Theory of Moral Sentiments as follows. First, she said that Smithian sympathy is essentially involuntary emotional contagion: seeing another person’s distress automatically induces a matching affective state in the observer. Second, she claimed that moral judgment consists solely in taking pleasure in this shared feeling, so that to call an action proper is just to report that it produces agreeable resonance. Third, she described the impartial spectator as nothing more than an internalized record of past social sanctions—an empirically formed habit of anticipation rather than an idealized standpoint—so that Smith’s ethics is purely descriptive and carries no independent normative force. Fourth, she inferred that because sympathy naturally weakens with social distance and with differences in rank, Smith must regard beneficence as a discretionary taste rather than a genuine virtue, and that justice, being enforced, is therefore the only real moral obligation. She concluded that Smith’s system ultimately collapses into a form of conventionalism in which morality tracks local approval patterns.

I am unsure how much of this captures Smith’s actual view and how much is a subtle distortion. Could you go through the summary point by point? For each claim, indicate whether it is a fair rendering, a common simplification, or a misrepresentation of Smith’s position—paying particular attention to his distinction between sympathy as imaginative projection and mere emotional mirroring, to the normative status of the impartial spectator, and to the relationship between propriety, merit, and the different virtues. Also, tell me whether her final conclusion—that Smith’s ethics is descriptively accurate but normatively empty conventionalism—actually follows from the parts that are correct.
