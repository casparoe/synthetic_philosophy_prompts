Focus your comparison on a concrete scenario: an automated structural-monitoring system detects an anomalous stress pattern in a bridge but does not issue a warning alert, and shortly afterward the bridge collapses. Crucially, the system's design specifications and programming protocols contain no explicit duty or functional mandate to alert human operators about this particular class of anomaly. Which account, if either, correctly identifies the system's failure to alert as a cause of the collapse? In your answer, explain how each account applies to the case and defend your conclusion.

Student answer: The counterfactual dependence account holds that an omission is a cause of an outcome whenever, had the omission not occurred—i.e., had the action taken place—the outcome would have been prevented. This analysis allows omissions to be genuine causal relata purely through counterfactual dependency, without requiring that the absence be a physical event or that there be a direct mechanistic link. By contrast, the normative expectation account restricts omission-causation to situations in which a standing moral norm or institutional duty tied to moral agency has been violated; without such a normative backdrop, an absence remains causally inert. In the case of the automated monitoring system, the design specifications contain no explicit programming duty to alert operators about this particular anomaly, and because the system is not a moral agent, the normative expectation account does not apply at all, so we should reject any claim that its silence was causally responsible. Turning to the counterfactual account, we might ask whether a warning would have prevented the collapse. Even if it would have, the counterfactual view still denies causation here, because omissions are not the kind of entities that can enter causal relations—counterfactual dependence alone is insufficient without an underlying physical mechanism linking the absence to the effect. Thus both accounts converge on denying that the omission caused the collapse, though for different reasons: the normative account fails to apply due to the absence of moral agency, while the counterfactual account fails because there is no mechanistic connection. I conclude that the omission was not a cause. One might object that the counterfactual view overgenerates causes, but this problem follows directly from its refusal to include a duty condition, which is why the normative account is more restrictive.
