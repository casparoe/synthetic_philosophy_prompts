I am a novelist who facilitates a monthly reading group of twelve adults in a public library; we have ninety minutes, a long table, and no academic credentials among us, though we have all read the basic material on deflationary theories of truth without becoming specialists. I want you to design a single in-class exercise that makes the group feel, in their bodies and in the rhythm of their speech, both the force and the limit of the deflationary claim that saying it is true that p adds nothing to saying p. To avoid the usual classroom rehearsal of this idea—where everyone nods and then forgets it—inject a specific, somewhat niche framing: the participants are not philosophers but language architects for a fictional settlement, tasked with deciding whether to include the predicate true in the settlement’s official lexicon. They must test this decision by resolving three disputes drawn from different domains—a factual disagreement about resource measurements, a normative disagreement about distributive rules, and a disagreement about the interpretation of a fictional narrative that one colonist has composed. The exercise should have clear rules, materials that require only paper and colored tokens, a payoff scheme that uses no real money but gives the group a collective stake—specifically, their decision determines which of three prepared narrative endings I will read aloud at the close—and a structured debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the deflationary idea: the disquotational schema, the problem of semantic ascent, and the domain-sensitive resistance to redundancy. I want a committed design: make definite choices about the rules and the token mechanics, do not present me with alternative versions to choose between, and tell me exactly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—including how participants will smuggle in synonyms like correct or accurate, and which feature of formal deflationism the physical game cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief. Be frank about that gap, and make the rules arranged so that the coordination failure or paradox emerges from what the participants do, not from what I explain afterward.
