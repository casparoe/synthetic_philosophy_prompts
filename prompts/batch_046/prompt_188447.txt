I am a graduate student in history and philosophy of science, and I sit on my university's Interdisciplinary Public History Committee, which approves conceptual frameworks for museum exhibits before they go to the board of trustees. We are currently reviewing a proposal for a permanent gallery titled "Agents Without Intent: Epidemics, Eruptions, and the Architecture of Modern Europe." The curators want to frame the Black Death (c. 1347–1351), the 1815 Tambora eruption and its 1816 climatic aftermath, and the 536 CE volcanic winter not as passive background conditions but as "structural agents" that actively reshaped labor regimes, state fiscal capacity, and migration patterns—comparable in explanatory weight to human political actors such as legislatures or monarchs. The proposal draws on actor-network ideas to argue that non-human entities can possess "distributed agency" without intentionality, and it asks visitors to treat pathogen transmission and aerosol-forced cooling as causally co-equal with human policy decisions in explaining, for example, post-plague labor statutes or grain-market collapses after Tambora.

The committee is split. Members from science studies and environmental history argue that this corrects a long-standing anthropocentric bias and reflects a settled consensus that climate and disease are not mere "context" for human affairs. Members from philosophy of history and social history object that attributing "agency" to pathogens and volcanic aerosols risks a form of environmental determinism: it treats human political responses—negotiating wages, organizing relief, enforcing mobility restrictions—as epiphenomenal effects of natural forces, thereby erasing the contingency and moral weight of human decision. They further worry that the framework smuggles contested metaphysics about causation and explanation under the guise of accessible museum narration.

Please draft the committee's formal decision memo recommending a concrete course of action—approve the framework with binding curatorial conditions, reject it outright, or approve a substantially revised version—together with a clear implementation or revision time frame (e.g., conditions to be met within one academic quarter). The memo should treat the strongest objection from the losing side with genuine intellectual honesty rather than as a straw man, and it should include an actionable bottom line that the board can vote on. In particular, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among scholars who work on historical explanation, agency, and environmental causation: do not present a contested philosophical view—whether about distributed agency, determinism, or the ontology of historical explanation—as if it were the consensus. Where the conceptual terrain is genuinely unsettled, say so explicitly, and show how the committee's recommendation navigates that uncertainty rather than resolving it by fiat.
