For roughly ten years I have watched a dispute in a local historical society about how to commemorate the closing of the Riverside textile mill. The administrative archive is unambiguous: board minutes, tax filings, and local newspapers from 1987 record a sudden shutdown caused by a broken supply contract, with more than two hundred workers dismissed within thirty days. Against this, the collective memory maintained by former workers and their descendants describes a gradual fading away across several years, mostly through voluntary departures, leaving perhaps a dozen people present at the final gate-locking, which they recall as quiet and almost ceremonial. Every participant in the society accepts both sources; no one claims the records are forged or that the memories are lies. Yet the conflict never resolves. One faction insists that public commemoration must be anchored to the documentary record, because allowing memory to override fact produces a false past. The other faction argues that the records capture only economic mechanics, while the memory preserves the social and emotional truth of the closure—its dignity, its slowness in human terms, its meaning for the town—and that to correct the memory with paperwork is to erase the very experience being commemorated. They have restated these positions for a decade without convergence. I am not asking which plaque is correct; I am asking what kind of disagreement this actually is. Do the two sides differ about hidden facts—perhaps about what counts as the real event—or about standards of evidence, or about which questions a commemoration should answer? Are they using the same words—truth, closure, the mill—to talk about entirely different objects? Or is the stall a sign that the question is ill-posed, forcing a single narrative onto something that genuinely has two incompatible dimensions? Give me a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says, not a survey of memory studies. And tell me, given that diagnosis, whether anything—an analytical distinction, a reframed question, a specific example—could move the debate, or whether no progress should be expected and why.
