Here is an argument I have been turning over that seems to prove far more than it should, and I cannot locate the leak. (1) The separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarian aggregation holds that distributing burdens and benefits across different individuals is impermissible because each person lives a single, non-transferable life: there is no overarching subject who experiences both the sacrifice and the compensating gain. (2) If the normative force of this objection derives from the metaphysical fact that the welfare-bearers are distinct entities, then it applies with equal strength to temporal parts of a single life: on a reductionist view of personal identity, my present self and my future self are not strictly identical, and each bears only its own slice of suffering or reward. (3) Consequently, if the objection is valid, intrapersonal prudential reasoning—accepting present hardship for the sake of a greater future benefit—should be blocked for exactly the same reason that sacrificing one innocent person to save five is blocked. (4) But prudence plainly permits, and often requires, such temporal trade-offs. (5) Therefore the separateness objection either collapses entirely or must be restricted to interpersonal cases by an ad hoc stipulation that has no independent justification.

The conclusion seems absurd because I do not think prudence is immoral. I would like to keep the core intuition that interpersonal aggregation differs from intrapersonal choice, yet I see no non-arbitrary line. Which premise or inferential move should be rejected, and what is the cost of that rejection?
