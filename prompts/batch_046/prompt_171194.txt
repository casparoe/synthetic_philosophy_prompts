You are an expert in the philosophy of art, ethics, and cultural representation, with particular experience in assessing how conceptual distinctions apply to documentary and fictional portrayals of real lives. I write on science and ideas for a publication that treats conceptual framing as part of the story, and I am designing material for an advanced undergraduate seminar in philosophy of art and historical representation. Before you begin drafting, please take a moment to think carefully and outline the specific conceptual crux—the move that separates a student who genuinely understands the ethics of dramatizing the dead from one who can only recite familiar positions about artistic license versus accuracy.

Please compose a final-exam essay prompt, together with a model answer of approximately 600 words and a detailed marking scheme, suitable for an upper-level seminar (students have encountered standard arguments about artistic freedom and posthumous harm, but not the framework introduced below). The domain is the ethics of historical fiction and biopics: what do we owe the real dead when we dramatize them?

To prevent the question from being answered by regurgitating well-worn debates, inject the following precise but non-obscure triadic distinction, which students must use rather than merely name: (1) documentary fidelity—getting the public record right; (2) narrative coherence—making a life intelligible to present audiences; and (3) posthumous interpretive autonomy—the claim that the dead retain a moral interest in not being conscripted as spokespersons for philosophical or political positions they never held, even when those positions illuminate their documented work.

The exam question should present this concrete scenario: a critically acclaimed biopic depicts a real, deceased twentieth-century scientist whose private archives contain no engagement with political philosophy. The film invents extended private conversations in which she articulates an elaborate commitment to a specific theory of distributive justice, using these invented dialogues to make her scientific discoveries symbolically parallel that theory. The screenplay falsifies no published experiment or public statement; it only manufactures her private worldview.

Require students to evaluate whether this violates an obligation to the dead. They must do more than summarize standard arguments. Specifically, they must: explain why narrative coherence might seem to justify the invention; articulate exactly how posthumous interpretive autonomy differs from both documentary fidelity and from a generic right against misrepresentation; address the objection that the dead cannot be harmed because they lack consciousness; and propose a clear criterion for when dramatizing the dead crosses from permissible interpretation into impermissible conscription, applying it directly to the case.

Then provide the model answer, clearly structured, followed by a marking scheme out of 20 points that separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain—especially the correct handling of the consciousness objection and the proposed criterion. The scheme should list five must-hit points with marks allocated, plus a one-line note on how candidates typically fail at each point.

Please write in a formal, polished register. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests, and do not put any real textual passage in quotation marks unless you have fetched and verified it; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Ensure the prompt depends on reasoning through the triadic framework rather than on recalling niche theories or specific texts from pretraining.
