My friend and I just finished a long, rather heated argument about lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism—specifically, the version that treats severe suffering and positive well-being not as points on a continuous scale but as incommensurable categories separated by a hard boundary, so that no quantity of mild pleasure can offset even a single instance of extreme pain. She insisted that this view, if taken seriously, does not merely change how we aggregate welfare; it dissolves the conceptual framework we use to reason about trade-offs altogether. I want to work through this more rigorously. Please describe the most important implications of adopting such a lexical, incommensurability-based negative utilitarianism for three domains. First, for public-policy reasoning under uncertainty, where decision-makers must choose between interventions that prevent rare catastrophic suffering and those that reliably produce widespread but modest benefit, and where the incommensurability claim seems to block any common metric for comparison. Second, for prudential rationality and personal identity over time, since an agent who anticipates crossing the lexical boundary into severe suffering might face a discontinuity in her reasons for action that standard temporal discounting models cannot accommodate. Third, for the internal coherence of the view itself—whether declaring pleasure and pain incommensurable actually undermines the negative-utilitarian priority of suffering-reduction, given that without cross-threshold comparison we might lack any principled basis for preferring the absence of extreme pain over the presence of extreme pleasure. I am not looking for a bibliography or citations; I want a conceptual analysis that takes the incommensurability claim at face value, traces its consequences precisely, and notes where the argument forces us to revise standard assumptions and where it risks collapsing into incoherence.
