Write a poem in exactly five numbered stanzas (1–5), with no title, introduction, or conclusion; present each stanza as a single list item, each item being one or two sentences in blank verse. The poem should take up Zhuangzi’s view of death, but avoid the familiar butterfly-dream gloss; instead, render the tension between the cosmological claim that death is only qi transforming and returning to the great root, and the skeptical claim—suggested in the tub-drumming episode and in the skull dialogue—that there is no persisting subject who could suffer death or be mourned. Use stanzas 1–3 to articulate acceptance of death as natural change from a voice that still distinguishes mourner from mourned; make stanza 4 the volta, dissolving that distinction by asking whose loss is spoken of when the form has already changed; and let stanza 5 hold the tension open, leaving it unclear whether emptying the heart removes grief or only exposes it as attachment without an owner. The imagery should be precise enough that someone who knows Zhuangzi recognizes the parables, but the philosophical work should be done by the grammatical dissolution of the subject across the turn, not by naming doctrines.
