I'm working out my position on individual moral obligations within large-scale collective harm structures—specifically climate-related consumption and industrial animal agriculture, with democratic voting in mass electorates as a parallel case—and I'd like a consistency audit before I present it. Here are my commitments:

(1) I grant the inefficacy objection in its strongest form: in both climate change and factory farming, a single agent's discrete choices make no perceptible difference to aggregate harm, and this is a structural feature of large-N problems rather than a mere epistemic limitation.

(2) Despite (1), I hold that individuals possess a direct, non-derivative moral reason to avoid contributing to these practices, one that does not depend on signaling, virtue cultivation, or reaching a coordination threshold.

(3) I think the strength of this reason varies with the harm's structure: it is stronger for meat consumption than for personal carbon emissions because the former involves direct, intended suffering of sentient beings, whereas the latter involves diffuse, probabilistic systemic risk—yet in both cases my individual causal contribution remains negligible.

(4) I apply the same structural analysis to voting in large democracies: one vote is virtually certain not to be decisive, so it exemplifies the same large-N inefficacy.

(5) Despite this parallel, I deny that there is any direct, non-instrumental reason to vote; I treat voting as at best instrumentally valuable or as a weak civic convention, lacking the moral weight I assign to abstaining from meat or reducing emissions.

(6) I reject expressive, symbolic, or virtue-ethical explanations for obligations in either domain—I want normative force to come from the causal-structural properties of the acts themselves, not from what they express about character.

(7) I endorse an expected-value framework for assessing wrongness in collective contexts: an action is wrong in proportion to its non-negligible expected contribution to harm.

(8) I maintain that eating meat is wrong, that failing to vote is not wrong, and that this asymmetry is stable and defensible.

Please go through these pair by pair and tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and what the least costly revision would be for each real friction. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. In particular, I want you to examine whether (2) can survive given (6) and (7) together with (1); whether the asymmetry between climate/meat and voting in (4)-(5) is justified or ad hoc; and whether (3)'s claim about differential strength conflicts with (1)'s uniform negligibility. Please state how confident you are in each judgment you make, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
