Below are the notes for an essay I am drafting on fakes, forgeries, and the value of originals. I want to defend a distinction between genetic authenticity—the actual causal origin of an object—and performative authenticity—the sustained role an object plays within a living practice of interpretation, curation, and aesthetic response. To test this, I construct a hypothetical case: a painting long attributed to a minor seventeenth-century Dutch circle painter—let us call him the Delft Circle Master—is revealed to be a technically perfect forgery executed in 1987. For four decades it hangs in a major museum, shapes curatorial narratives about Dutch Golden Age techniques, generates scholarly debate, and conditions the perceptual expectations of thousands of viewers. Once the deception is exposed, the museum removes it. My essay asks whether the art-historical and aesthetic insights produced during those forty years are retroactively annulled, or whether they retain what I call residual interpretive weight—value that persists independently of the object’s true causal origin. I also want to argue that originals possess a structural feature I term indexical vulnerability: they remain open to future revision, deterioration, and rediscovery in ways that forgeries, designed precisely to close off such openness, do not. I claim that this vulnerability is not a deficiency but part of the ethical and aesthetic significance of engaging with an original.

Please comment on these notes. I am particularly interested in three questions. First, does the distinction between genetic and performative authenticity hold together, or does it collapse into a simpler causal account once pressure is applied? Second, is the hypothetical Delft Circle Master philosophically illuminating, or is it too contrived to teach us anything about real debates over forgery and restoration? Third, can the notion of indexical vulnerability be clarified enough to do genuine philosophical work, or does it risk becoming an obscure metaphor? I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations—only an assessment of whether the argument is coherent, avoids well-worn positions in the philosophy of art, and is genuinely interesting rather than merely clever.
