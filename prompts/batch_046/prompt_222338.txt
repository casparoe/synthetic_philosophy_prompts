Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on Sen’s liberal paradox.

Camp A holds that the impossibility result reveals a genuine, structural tension: minimal liberalism—understood as a pairwise condition granting individuals decisive authority over protected pairs of alternatives—cannot coexist with an unrestricted Pareto principle in any non-trivial social-choice framework. They maintain that domain restrictions on the Pareto condition or reformulations of minimal liberalism either abandon the core liberal intuition that personal sovereignty is not subject to collective welfare trade-offs, or they save consistency only by rendering the rights protection vacuous.

Camp B holds that the paradox is dissolved by a correct formalization: minimal liberalism should be interpreted not as a pairwise veto extending across the full domain, but as a feasibility constraint that removes rights-violating alternatives from the social-choice agenda; the Pareto principle then applies only within the rights-respecting feasible set. They maintain that Sen’s proof demonstrates not a contradiction in liberal values, but a category mistake—applying collective-efficiency reasoning to pairs that should never have been jointly admissible.

The document both camps could sign should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: the formal conditions in Sen’s argument (Unrestricted Domain, the Pareto principle, Minimal Liberalism), what the proof establishes about their joint satisfiability, the normative stakes (whether individual sovereignty and collective rationality are both valuable and whether they must be reconciled in one framework), and an explicit acknowledgment that both sides accept the logical validity of the derivation given its premises.

2. The precise disagreements, each phrased in wording its own camp accepts. These should include: (a) whether minimal liberalism is correctly captured by a pairwise veto or by a feasibility-set constraint; (b) whether restricting the Pareto condition to the feasible set is a principled correction or an ad hoc rescue; and (c) whether the liberal intuition requires authority over specific pairs of outcomes regardless of context, or only over outcomes within a domain where the individual has standing.

3. The cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. At minimum, include: (i) whether applying the Pareto principle across alternatives that violate a protected right is conceptually coherent; and (ii) whether a social-choice rule that respects rights-as-feasibility-constraints but permits collective preference to order all remaining alternatives still counts as honoring minimal liberalism, or whether it strips rights of their veto-like force.

4. For each crux, a concrete formal case, argument, or variant both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s predicted interpretation or outcome recorded in advance. For instance, both might agree that examining a three-person, three-alternative scenario with exactly one protected pair is diagnostic: Camp A should predict that any rule avoiding contradiction must either violate the pairwise veto in that scenario or violate Pareto across the unrestricted domain, while Camp B should predict that restricting the agenda to the two rights-respecting alternatives permits a consistent ordering that satisfies both conditions in their properly restricted form. Specify what each camp would count as a successful demonstration and what would force a concession.

Format the output as a polished, formal joint statement—numbered agreed claims followed by a disagreements table or structured sections—without bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions or page numbers. Keep the analysis at the level of conceptual architecture and formal interpretation rather than historical commentary.
