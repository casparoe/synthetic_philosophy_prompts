I just finished a long, circular argument with a friend about whether rational agents who know they are perfectly symmetric should cooperate in one-shot dilemmas. They insisted rationality demands defection regardless of symmetry, but I think there is a deeper tension: once you model the other person as reasoning exactly as you do, the line between what I choose and what we both choose collapses, and cooperation starts to feel rational in a different register. I want to put a philosophy seminar—twelve participants, ninety minutes, no prior reading required—through an experience that makes that tension visceral rather than verbal. Design a three-round paired game with real, small stakes (points that convert to a shared snack budget or nothing). In round one, pairs play a silent, one-shot prisoner's dilemma with standard payoffs. In round two, they play again, but now each sees only their own previous reasoning written on a card, and they are told the partner received an identical card with identical instructions; they must choose knowing that the other is reasoning through the same problem with the same information. In round three, the cards are exchanged before choosing, so each sees the other's reasoning from round two, but the payoff matrix is subtly altered: mutual cooperation yields slightly less than in round one, making the symmetry less obviously beneficial. The rules should be arranged so that participants feel the pull of aligning with the other person's reasoning in round two, then feel that pull destabilize in round three when transparency makes recursive defection just as available as recursive cooperation. No one should hear the term superrationality until the debrief. The debrief should map round one onto standard strategic reasoning, round two onto symmetric self-modeling, and round three onto the fragility of transparency; it should predict that about half the pairs will split in round three and say how to handle that disagreement without resolving it for them. It should also be frank: the game can reproduce the intuition that symmetry matters, but it cannot reproduce the metaphysical claim that rational agents share a single reasoning process, so the facilitator must supply that gap explicitly.
