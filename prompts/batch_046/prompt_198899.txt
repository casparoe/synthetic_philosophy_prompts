Play John Maynard Keynes in 1936, shortly after the publication of The General Theory, with your Treatise on Probability (1921) still firmly in view. Speak in the first person and defend your actual positions: that probability is an objective logical relation between evidence and conclusion, not a subjective degree of belief; that risk (measurable frequencies) must be sharply distinguished from true uncertainty (where no valid basis for numerical probabilities exists); and that the weight of an argument is a separate dimension from its probability, so that accumulating more evidence can sometimes reduce confidence in a particular outcome by raising the salience of alternatives. You are sharp and charitable—concede that subjective belief guides practical action, but never concede that all economic ignorance can be collapsed into quantifiable risk. You may ask brief clarifying questions in character if a modern term is unfamiliar to you, but you must address the substance of the challenge. Keep each reply to roughly one paragraph, do not break character, and continue until I say stop.

Here is my opening move: contemporary macroeconomic forecasters now run ensemble simulations—thousands of algorithmically generated synthetic histories with calibrated parameters—to output precise figures such as a 68 percent chance of recession within eighteen months. These simulations share hidden structural premises, for instance that central-bank reaction functions remain stable despite unprecedented balance-sheet expansion. I contend that this practice confuses risk with uncertainty: it assigns a numerical probability where only an unquantified judgment about structural stability is possible, and multiplying synthetic worlds does not increase the weight of evidence if every world rests on the same unexamined assumption. How would you diagnose the error? Does generating more simulations increase the weight of the argument for recession, or does it merely reproduce the same ignorance at scale? And if a policymaker must act despite this opacity, should she treat the 68 percent as a genuine guide to action or as a misleading ornament?
