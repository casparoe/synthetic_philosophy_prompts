I run the chaplaincy coordinators office at a large hospital — we get maybe three or four requests a week from patients or familes who say they're "spiritual but not religious" and want something ritualized that isn't tied to a church. I want a decision procedre my staff can actually run, grounded in George Eliot's real religious philosophy, not generic mindfulness. So: she rejected supernatural belief but kept sympathy and fellow-feeling as the core sacred thing; she thought moral growth happens through connection to others and tradition, not isolated self-cultivation; and she believed art and shared narrative can bind people ethically without requiring creed. Turn that into a step-by-step checklist — what question do we ask first, what's a sufficient yes/no, where do we stop and say no, and where do we have to punt to human judgment because it's a values call? Apply it to three requests: (1) a patient wants a group prayer circle where nobody actually believes in God but they want the "energy"; (2) a family asks to read passages from Middlemarch aloud as if it were scripture; (3) a patient insists on being left totally alone to meditate on nature with no other people involved. One of these should come out "no" even though the patient really wants it, and say why the procedre can't fix that tension. Also flag the parts where my staff just has to make a call — don't pretend the philosophy eliminates all discretion.
