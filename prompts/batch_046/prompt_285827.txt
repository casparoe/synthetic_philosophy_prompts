I am preparing a graduate seminar on modal metaphysics and the rationalist tradition, and I want to press beyond the standard expositions. Please describe the most important implications of Gödel’s modal ontological argument—the version reconstructed from his unpublished manuscripts that defines a maximally excellent being through the framework of positive properties, derives necessary existence from the principle that any positive property is possibly instantiated only if it is necessarily instantiated, and relies on modal closure conditions—to the dialectical burden shared between modal rationalists and modal empiricists. Specifically, address whether the argument genuinely shifts the burden onto the empiricist to demonstrate the incoherence of the concept of a being that instantiates all positive properties, or whether the premises about property-positivity and modal closure already smuggle in substantive metaphysical commitments that an empiricist can reject without special pleading. In addition, discuss what the argument implies, if anything, for the problem of modal collapse: if all positive properties entail necessary existence and are closed under logical consequence, does the argument risk entailing that every consistent proposition is necessarily true, and can standard repairs—such as restricting closure to essential rather than accidental properties, or distinguishing axiological from logical entailment—preserve the argument’s force without ad hoc stipulation? Finally, please separate what is settled among specialists in philosophical theology and modal logic from what remains genuinely contested—particularly regarding whether Gödel intended a strictly axiological conception of divinity or a broader plenitude of possible worlds—and do not present a disputed interpretation of his premises or of the modal-collapse objection as though it were the consensus view.
