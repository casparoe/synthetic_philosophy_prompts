In debates about personal identity, philosophers constantly invoke the Ship of Theseus: replace every plank over time, and the vessel persists despite containing no original material; rebuild a second ship from the discarded planks, and intuition splits over which is the "real" ship. This is used to argue that human bodies—whose cells and atoms are continuously replaced—cannot ground identity through bodily continuity alone, and that we must instead appeal to psychological continuity or to reconstructed artifacts. Audit this analogy in detail: provide the correspondence table—what corresponds to the planks, the shipyard, the curator stockpiling old timber, the continuously repaired vessel, and the rebuilt duplicate? Then identify the precise fractures: living bodies are self-maintaining, metabolically open systems that regulate their own replacement, not passive artifacts assembled in a yard; psychological continuity does not obviously map onto either vessel; and the analogy may smuggle in the assumption that identity requires a single, spatiotemporally continuous substrate rather than a pattern maintained through dynamic exchange. Does the conclusion that bodily continuity fails still hold once the body is treated as a process rather than an object? If the analogy collapses under this pressure, what model—if any—better captures persistence through complete material turnover?
