I serve on my university's sustainability committee, and I have become preoccupied by what appears to be an unexamined philosophical tension in our institutional policies. On the one hand, we actively encourage every student and staff member to participate in local and national elections, distributing literature that treats abstention as a form of complicity in collective outcomes, despite the well-known fact that any individual ballot is extraordinarily unlikely to be decisive. On the other hand, when students recently petitioned for all campus catering to become plant-based — citing the substantial contribution of industrial animal agriculture to climate change — the administration rejected the proposal by invoking precisely the inefficacy objection: they maintained that one institution's procurement choices are too marginal to alter global supply chains, that diners remain free to consume meat elsewhere, and that the measure would therefore be purely symbolic.

What view of individual moral contribution to collective harm does this pairing of practices implicitly presuppose? Does the voting policy reject the inefficacy objection altogether, or can it be reconciled with it through a different theory of responsibility — perhaps one that distinguishes expressive or participatory value from causal efficacy? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit philosophical framework that renders both positions coherent to the committee, assess whether that framework is genuinely consistent, and indicate what practical changes would be required if the university adopted a uniform standard — whether consistently applying the inefficacy objection or consistently applying a collective-responsibility model — across electoral participation, dietary procurement, and institutional travel. Please reply in British English, and do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
