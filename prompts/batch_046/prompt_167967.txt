I'm a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, but I've been trying to map out my own intuitions about circular justification—cases where a source, method, or instrument is used to validate itself. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete scenarios; I want to know whether a single precise principle captures them, or whether I have to abandon one.

Case 1: Compiler bootstrapping. You compile GCC using an existing GCC binary to produce a new binary, then recompile the same source with that new binary and compare the outputs. Relying on this self-compiled result to conclude the source is correctly implemented feels legitimate—standard engineering verification, not vicious circularity.

Case 2: Cryptographic self-check. You compute a SHA-256 hash of a file using your own implementation of SHA-256, then verify integrity by comparing it to a previously recorded hash that was also produced by that same implementation. This feels illegitimate—using the very mechanism under scrutiny to generate the benchmark.

Case 3: Perceptual loop. You check whether your color vision is intact by interpreting a color-differentiation plate whose correct reading depends on normal color vision, with no external reference standard. Clearly circular and not okay.

Case 4: Inductive justification. You defend the principle that the future will resemble the past by observing that this principle has reliably worked in the past. This strikes me as circular in exactly the wrong way—not okay.

Case 5: Model self-assessment. You ask a language model to rate the logical consistency of its own reasoning trace, then treat that self-rating as independent evidence that the model is a reliable reasoner. This feels like bootstrapping: the source is certifying itself.

Case 6: The case I keep oscillating on. A biometric access system enrolls an administrator's fingerprint using Scanner A, then later verifies Scanner A's accuracy by scanning the same finger and matching against that enrolled template, with no cross-enrollment from a different device and no manual audit trail. Engineers deploy this routinely, yet philosophically it resembles Case 2 more than Case 1. I cannot settle whether it is legitimate bootstrapping or hidden circularity.

The tension is sharpest between Case 1 and Case 2: both involve a tool validating its own outputs, yet I treat them differently. Meanwhile Cases 3, 4, and 5 all feel wrong despite spanning perception, induction, and automated evaluation.

Is there a principle—perhaps about the independence of verification channels, the direction of epistemic dependence, or the presence of an external anchor—that delivers all six verdicts, including my uncertainty in Case 6? If the principle must be simple, which of my verdicts should I overturn? Please apply your proposed account to a case I have not listed: using a feature-selection algorithm to choose predictors for a dataset, then splitting that same dataset into train and holdout sets using the feature-selected representation, and citing the holdout accuracy as independent validation of the algorithm. Does your principle classify this as legitimate bootstrapping or as easy knowledge?

State the principle precisely, map each clause to the case it governs, predict the outcome for the feature-selection scenario, and be explicit about which of my verdicts the simpler formulation cannot accommodate.
