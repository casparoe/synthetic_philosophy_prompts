Readers of Fear and Trembling often analogize Abraham's teleological suspension of the ethical to the structure of justified civil disobedience or conscientious refusal: in both cases, an agent acknowledges a universal norm but claims that a singular, higher call authorizes an exception that does not overturn the norm generally. The analogy is used to argue that religious faith can accommodate ethical universality rather than annihilate it, since Abraham intends to return to the ethical order after the trial. Audit this analogy precisely: provide a correspondence table mapping the ethical universal, the teleological end, the singular call, and the intended return onto their counterparts in civil disobedience; then identify where the mapping breaks, especially regarding the reversibility of the suspended act, the presence or absence of a public justification, and whether the exception is meant to be exemplary or incommunicable. Does the analogy genuinely show that Abraham's suspension preserves ethics, or does it silently import from civil disobedience a rational transparency and public accountability that Kierkegaard's knight of faith explicitly lacks?
