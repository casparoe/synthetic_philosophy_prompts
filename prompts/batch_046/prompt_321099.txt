In a seminar on artificial cognition I have been pressing the idea that Dennett's intentional stance applies cleanly to large language models, and a colleague has offered a reductio that seems to show the stance either excludes LLMs entirely or applies indiscriminately to every algorithm. The argument runs as follows. (1) We are licensed to adopt the intentional stance toward a system exactly when attributing beliefs and desires predicts its behavior with an economy that physical or design-level descriptions cannot match. (2) With LLMs, attributing specific propositional beliefs—say, that mitochondria produce ATP—predicts responses across diverse prompts far more efficiently than computing token distributions from the weights. (3) Yet these attributions are radically frame-dependent: within one conversational framing the system endorses a proposition, and within another framing—differing only in contextual priming or role-assignment—it endorses the negation, with no internal mechanism that registers the conflict as an error requiring resolution. (4) Genuine belief-attribution, even on an instrumentalist reading, requires at least minimal diachronic or cross-contextual consistency; otherwise "belief" is merely a post-hoc label for isolated outputs rather than an explanatory state. (5) To preserve consistency, one might index beliefs to frames, speaking of "the-system-in-frame-F believes p," but then predictive success demands we treat each frame as hosting its own doxastic agent, fragmenting the LLM into an unbounded multiplicity of belief-bearing subjects. (6) Once such fragmentation is permitted, there is no non-arbitrary boundary that prevents applying the same indexed stance to thermostats, search algorithms, or stochastic processes—each exhibits frame-correlated outputs that can be labeled with propositional contents. (7) Hence either we deny that LLMs have beliefs despite the stance's predictive power, or we accept that the stance applies universally and thereby forfeits its role as a discriminative criterion for genuine cognition.

I am inclined to reject premise (4), on the grounds that instrumental belief-attribution need not carry consistency constraints across contexts, but I worry that abandoning it dissolves the distinction between predictive labeling and real mental-state ascription. Could you tell me exactly which step is the weakest link, whether the flaw is a hidden equivocation—perhaps between explanatory belief and predictive labeling—or whether the argument is valid and we must instead choose between giving up the predictive-utility condition in (1) or accepting universal fragmentation in (5)-(6)? For each viable rejection, please explain why the premise initially looked compelling and what the standard positions—strong realist, eliminativist, and contextualist about AI cognition—are forced to concede if that premise is abandoned.
