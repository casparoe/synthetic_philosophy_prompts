I was arguing with my roommate last night about this mutual-aid thing our building started - everyone chips in 2% of income and then we vote on where it goes. This month we're split between giving it to a local shelter that's turning people away and sending it to a cash-transfer program in rural Malawi that apparently helps about three times as many people with the same amount of money.

My roommate said if you live in a rich country you've automatically got stronger obligations to folks right here, especially since our own government policies and economic rules probably help keep that shelter crowded in the first place. I said that sounds like we're just picking people nearby because they're nearby, and if the other program helps people who are in much worse shape, shouldn't we send it there? But she brought up this thing she calls 'structural complicity' - she thinks our trade rules and tax deals might actually be part of what's making things hard in that Malawi region too, so either our duty to help is even bigger, or writing a check feels less like charity and more like fixing a mess we helped make.

I'm definately not asking who to vote for, but I can't tell if these are totally different kinds of reasons or if I'm just jumbling them together. Is there actually a philosophical difference between saying we owe more to people we're connected to through our institutions, versus saying we should just help whoever is worst off no matter where they live? And does it change anything if we think our own actions made them worse off? I feel like one of these arguments has a hidden mistake but I can't figure out which line it's in.
