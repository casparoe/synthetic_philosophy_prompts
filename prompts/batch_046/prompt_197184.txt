I am presenting the central argument of my dissertation chapter at an interdisciplinary symposium on long-term societal change. The audience comprises Aegean Bronze Age archaeologists, Maya lowland epigraphers, Polynesian ecologists, and comparative historians of state failure. The majority are methodological materialists who treat settlement depopulation patterns, skeletal stress markers, and paleobotanical sequences as primary evidentiary bases; they are generally skeptical of intentionalist reconstructions and particularly resistant to explanations that account for missing durable traces by postulating organizational forms that leave minimal archaeological signatures. Among them are two especially formidable interlocutors: a specialist in Eastern Mediterranean demographic contraction who maintains that palace redistribution networks genuinely dissolved rather than relocated into smaller-scale kin structures, and an ecologist who has modeled Rapa Nui resource dynamics as an essentially irreversible trajectory rather than an adaptive pivot.

I defend the position that the conventional narrative of collapse applied to the Late Bronze Age Levant and Aegean, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui systematically misclassifies organizational transformation as systemic failure, because it assumes that legitimate political authority must generate monumental, centralized, and durable material signatures. In each case, I argue, we observe not disappearance but reconfiguration: palace economies yielding to kin-based redistribution in the Bronze Age; dynastic monumentality giving way to dispersed council authority in the Maya region; and ancestral statue cults being superseded by competitive bird-man ritual networks on Easter Island. The apparent archaeological gap, I claim, is produced by what I call infrastructural invisibility—a shift toward organizational technologies such as perishable record-keeping, temporary assembly architectures, and oral contractual regimes that do not fossilize well. I need a Q&A preparation sheet: the objections these specialists will actually raise, ordered by how damaging they are and phrased in their disciplinary idioms, each with what I should concede, what I should hold, and where the reply is softest. I want the weakest point in my own framework—the risk that infrastructural invisibility renders my thesis unfalsifiable, since any absence of centralized material evidence can be reinterpreted as proof of distributed perishable organization—identified and prepared for explicit defense rather than left to chance. Please also include the objection I am hoping nobody raises: that my tripartite comparison suppresses genuinely incommensurable causal structures—climate forcing and maritime disruption in the Bronze Age, internal dynastic competition and drought in the Maya case, and ecological constraint coupled with social innovation on Easter Island—thereby manufacturing a false uniformity that serves my transformation thesis rather than emerging from the evidence.
