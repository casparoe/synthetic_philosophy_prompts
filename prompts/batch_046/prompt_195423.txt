The exam prompt asked students to address this question: Some philosophers deny that genuine moral dilemmas are possible on the grounds that obligations must satisfy a consistency condition analogous to logical consistency—if one genuinely ought to do A and genuinely ought to do B, and A and B are mutually exclusive, then requiring both violates the principle that ought implies can together with joint satisfiability. Defenders often reply by distinguishing conflicts of obligations from conflicts of values, and by appealing to moral remainders—such as appropriate regret or duties of reparation—that persist after an agent chooses one horn of the conflict. In particular, address whether the presence of such a remainder is sufficient to show that a conflict is genuinely dilemmatic rather than merely tragic, and consider whether an asymmetry between institutionally assigned obligations and personally adopted commitments changes the analysis.

Below is one student's complete answer.

The consistency objection starts from the idea that ought implies can, paired with the claim that obligations must be jointly satisfiable. If an agent genuinely ought to save child A and genuinely ought to save child B, but can only save one, then saying she ought to do both seems contradictory. Defenders such as Bernard Williams and Ruth Marcus respond that this misses the internal structure of moral conflict. Williams argues that after the agent chooses, she still bears a normative burden—guilt and a duty of reparation—toward the child she could not save, which shows the unfulfilled obligation was not canceled but only overridden in action. Marcus draws a sharper distinction: in a mere conflict of values one consideration is defeated and leaves no trace, whereas in a genuine dilemma both obligations survive the choice, leaving a moral remainder.

I believe the defense ultimately fails for three reasons. First, moral remainder is best understood as a psychological reaction rather than a normative fact. An emotionally resilient agent who feels no guilt after breaking a serious commitment has not shown the commitment was never binding; conversely, feeling guilty does not prove the obligation still holds. So remainder cannot serve as the criterion for a genuine dilemma. Second, Williams actually held that dilemmas are impossible because obligations are always ranked by weight or importance; there is never true symmetry, only an appearance of it that disappears upon closer inspection. Third, when institutional roles are introduced—for example, a physician's duty of care versus a parent's duty to her own child—the institutional obligation is structurally prior and therefore resolves any apparent conflict. These are not genuine dilemmas but hierarchical role conflicts that are settled by the authority of the role. Consequently, there are no genuine moral dilemmas.
