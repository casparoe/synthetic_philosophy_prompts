Construct a thought experiment designed to stress-test the wrong-kind-of-reasons distinction in epistemology and practical philosophy. The scenario should be confined to one paragraph, set in a realistic institutional environment such as a hospital ethics board or a university curriculum committee — nothing involving science fiction, perfect predictors, or exotic psychology. Engineer the case so that a single consideration — specifically, maintaining institutional trust among stakeholders who depend on a particular policy framework — serves simultaneously as a reason for a committee member to publicly endorse a policy and as a reason for that same member to privately adopt the belief that the policy is correct, yet the member's psychological makeup is such that sincerely performing the public endorsement is causally impossible without actually holding the belief. After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications: identify the specific belief and the specific action, name the shared consideration, and explain precisely why a wrong-kind-of-reasons theorist must classify that consideration as the right kind for the action but the wrong kind for the belief. Contrast this with what a unified-reasons theorist — one who denies that reasons come in ontologically distinct kinds — would say about the agent's overall rational situation. Then isolate the feature that does the work: the psychological inseparability of the belief and the action. Explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed, that is, if the agent could perform the endorsement through mere pretense without belief, and why the wrong-kind-reasons verdict becomes far more plausible in that modified version. Finally, present the unified theorist's best attack on the original case, arguing that the inseparability reveals practical reasons legitimately penetrating belief-formation rather than confirming a kind-distinction, and assess whether that attack succeeds or merely relocates the problem. Write continuously in prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or lists, as you would in a letter or email; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke niche theories or named figures that a generally informed model could not be expected to recognize from standard philosophical training.
