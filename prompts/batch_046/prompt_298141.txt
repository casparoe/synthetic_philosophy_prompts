Write a satirical, deadpan Frequently Asked Questions page (approximately 600 words) for a fictional consumer wellness app called "InView," marketed with the tagline "Complete Introspective Transparency." Adopt the voice of a relentlessly cheerful corporate customer-support portal: use numbered sections, bullet points, and brief sidebar callouts, and maintain an unbroken deadpan tone in which serious philosophical failures are treated as routine user-experience bugs that the latest software patch resolves.

The satire should target the philosophical overconfidence in first-person authority—the assumption that being acquainted with one's own mental states guarantees accurate knowledge of them. Rather than mocking users, exaggerate the corporate tendency to manage epistemic failure through branding and protocol. To keep the piece conceptually specific rather than generic, build it around two invented but philosophically grounded distinctions that the FAQ presents as proprietary technical features: (1) "silent misreporting," defined inside the document as the case in which a user is genuinely phenomenally acquainted with a mental state but systematically miscategorizes it because the available reporting vocabulary is borrowed from public, third-person domains; and (2) the "Conceptual Borrowing Protocol," the app's alleged remedy, which replaces privately generated descriptions with pre-loaded taxonomies derived from behavioral observation and biometric correlation, thereby "solving" misreporting by substituting externally validated labels for first-person reports.

Include the following elements:
- An opening "What is InView?" section that promises users "direct, unmediated access to every layer of conscious experience" and lists three "supported mental-state categories" that are obviously mismatched to phenomenology (for example, reconceptualizing anger as "thermal dysregulation in social contexts").
- A "Troubleshooting Silent Misreporting" section that describes the phenomenon in clinical, cheerful language—e.g., a user feels something precisely but calls it the wrong thing because their vocabulary comes from outside observation—and presents the Conceptual Borrowing Protocol as the fix.
- At least one invented "Customer Spotlight" testimonial illustrating silent misreporting: a user who reports experiencing "low-level existential dread," but whose biometric and linguistic data consistently map onto "post-lunch carbohydrate fatigue," with the FAQ calmly celebrating the correction as a successful "recalibration event" that improves transparency.
- A sidebar titled "How the Conceptual Borrowing Protocol Works" that lists three borrowed categories, explains in deadpan corporate prose why each improves accuracy over private description, and never admits that the substitution replaces the user's own knowledge with an external framework.
- A brief "Terms of Transparency" clause that quietly notes users grant InView the right to override first-person reports when confidence scores fall below a threshold.

The sustained irony must remain legible without winking: never break character to announce that introspection is unreliable. Instead, let the reader recognize that the app's "fixes" systematically eliminate first-person authority in the name of perfecting it, and that the cheerful optimism about "full transparency" is incompatible with the philosophical problems being managed. Do not cite real philosophers by name, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not reference specific published studies, books, or articles by title or date; keep all references internal to the fictional corporate universe of InView. Use American English and maintain a formal, polished register appropriate to a technical documentation brief.
