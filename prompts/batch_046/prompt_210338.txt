I've been working through the literature on negative utilitarianism and have come to defend a somewhat specific variant: lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, according to which suffering is lexically prior to well-being only beneath a certain phenomenological boundary, above which the two values become genuinely incommensurable rather than merely weighted differently. I want to push this further and claim that the incommensurability reflects not just a practical decision heuristic but a metaphysical discontinuity in conscious experience itself—one that makes cross-threshold aggregation conceptually incoherent, not merely morally objectionable. Before I commit fully, I need an honest map of where this position is exposed. What would have to be true for it to collapse? I am not asking for the familiar practical objections about decision-paralysis or the apparent arbitrariness of drawing the line; I want the deeper defeaters. Which empirical findings about the gradation of phenomenal states—say, if continuous integrated-information metrics turned out to scale seamlessly across both negative and positive valence domains—would genuinely threaten the view? Which formal results about value aggregation, and which carefully constructed trade-off cases at the boundary, would count as fatal rather than merely awkward? How should a committed defender respond to the charge that suffering and pleasure share a common neural currency? Would that be decisive, or could they absorb it by distinguishing phenomenal structure from informational substrate? I also want to know whether the view risks having been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every potential counterexample can be dismissed as occurring on the wrong side of the threshold, is that a legitimate insulation of its core commitment or a vice for a normative theory of this kind? Finally, please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, indicate which ones the view's defenders should themselves concede as genuinely counting against them, and assess whether the position makes distinctive predictions that rival frameworks—standard negative utilitarianism, two-level utilitarianism, or suffering-focused ethics—do not, and whether it risks anything conceptually that it is not already prepared to absorb.
