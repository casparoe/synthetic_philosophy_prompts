In a recent faculty workshop on scientific modeling, a colleague summarized Milton Friedman’s defense of unrealistic assumptions—often dubbed the “F-twist”—as follows: in positive economics, a model is to be judged exclusively by the predictive accuracy of its outputs, so the descriptive realism of its premises is entirely irrelevant; indeed, Friedman claimed that the more unrealistic an assumption is, the more powerful the theory, since extreme simplification is what allows science to isolate the causal mechanisms that actually matter. She then applied this reasoning to defend a current integrated-assessment climate model that aggregates all regional ecological damage through a single exponential decay parameter and treats spatial heterogeneity as negligible; because the model tracks global GDP trends within two percent, she argued that its ecological and geographic unrealism is not merely harmless but a positive methodological virtue of exactly the kind Friedman endorsed. Please audit this characterization. Does Friedman really treat predictive success as the sole universal criterion for model evaluation, and does his view entail that greater unrealism yields greater theoretical strength? Does her application to this climate-economy model follow legitimately from Friedman’s argument, or does it extend his reasoning into a domain where its conditions fail?
