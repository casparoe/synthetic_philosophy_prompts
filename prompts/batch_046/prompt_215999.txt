I spent the better part of last evening in a heated but good-natured argument with a friend about Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith, and I left with the nagging sense that neither of us had fully confronted what happens when these concepts are stripped of their phenomenological context and inserted into bureaucratic systems designed to eliminate ambiguity. My friend argued that Sartre's framework is essentially obsolete in an era where behavioral analytics and algorithmic recommendation architectures preempt choice before it even reaches consciousness; I countered that this preemption is not the defeat of Sartrean freedom but its perfect camouflage, because bad faith precisely consists in treating one's facticity—now perhaps digitized as user profiles and predictive data footprints—as if it were an unchangeable essence that excuses one from the burden of transcendence. We circled this for hours without resolving whether an institution can genuinely facilitate radical freedom or whether any institutional framing necessarily converts freedom into a managed performance metric.

With that unresolved tension in mind, I am asking for a sustained satirical piece—approximately 600 to 800 words—formatted as a deadpan internal corporate memorandum, tentatively titled Policy Directive 734: Implementation of Radical Freedom Protocols and Bad-Faith Prevention Measures. The voice should be that of an organizational psychologist or chief ethics compliance officer who has encountered Sartre's vocabulary secondhand but applies it with absolute bureaucratic sincerity, never breaking character to wink at the reader. The target of the satire should be the contemporary tendency of institutional and corporate frameworks to co-opt existentialist language—freedom, authenticity, self-definition—into mechanisms of surveillance, optimization, and disciplinary normalization. The humor and critical bite must come from the actual philosophical content: each policy recommendation should follow with logical rigor from Sartre's actual claims about consciousness as lack, about facticity versus transcendence, and about the self-deception involved in denying one's own freedom. For instance, the memo should treat the refusal of transcendence as a correctable performance deficiency, should frame quarterly essence-selection reviews as mandatory, and should address open-plan office surveillance not as an external imposition but as the institutionalization of le regard, rendered as a data-privacy audit category. Please inject one genuinely unexpected framing device that is not a standard undergraduate reference: perhaps reconceiving Sartre's concept of anguish as an occupational wellness metric subject to HR benchmarking, or treating the café waiter example as a case study in service-industry role-compliance. By the end, the piece should leave a reader feeling the genuine philosophical tension between Sartre's insistence that human beings are condemned to freedom and the bureaucratic fantasy that such freedom can be administratively optimized, measured, and corrected. Write without citations, reading lists, or references to specific editions or page numbers, and assume the audience knows Sartre's basic concepts but has not necessarily read the primary texts recently.
