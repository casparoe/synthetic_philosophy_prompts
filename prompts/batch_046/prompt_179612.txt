The prompt given to students was: “Evaluate whether karma functions as a genuine theory of moral desert—defined here as the view that agents receive outcomes proportionate to the moral quality of their actions—within classical Buddhist and Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika metaphysics. In particular, apply the course distinction between ‘desert-ownership’ (the metaphysical relation that binds an outcome to the agent who merits it) and ‘causal transmission’ (the mechanism by which results are delivered across rebirths). (1) Does Buddhism’s rejection of an enduring self undermine desert-ownership, or can causal transmission via the consciousness-stream preserve it? (2) Does Nyāya’s eternal ātman, mediated by adṛṣṭa, secure desert-ownership, and does it require psychological continuity? (3) How should each tradition treat unintended but foreseeable consequences—do these introduce moral luck that severs the link between action-quality and outcome, or can they be accommodated within desert? Conclude with an assessment of whether karma is best interpreted as a normative theory of desert or as a non-normative causal law of action and consequence.”

Student answer:

“I think karma can only partly be a theory of moral desert. In Buddhism, there is no enduring self, so strict desert-ownership is impossible: there is no single agent to own the result. But Buddhists solve this through causal transmission. Cetanā—the intentional volition—creates a karmic trace that conditions the next moment in the consciousness-stream. Because the stream is causally continuous, desert-ownership is preserved in a distributed form: the stream itself, rather than any particular person, owns the desert. This also means Buddhists reject the idea that unintended consequences matter for desert at all—only the intentional core counts—so there is genuinely no moral luck problem for them.

In Nyāya, by contrast, the eternal ātman secures desert-ownership directly. Through adṛṣṭa, an unseen moral force generated by action, results are carried to the same self across lives. But Nyāya requires psychological continuity for desert-ownership to hold: the receiving self must be mentally continuous with the acting self, remembering or being the same person in a psychological sense. Since rebirth obviously breaks psychological continuity, Nyāya actually faces a worse problem than Buddhism. Its causal transmission works—the ātman is the same substance—but without psychological continuity the normative link of desert is broken. So Nyāya fails to secure personal desert unless we add a memory condition, which the tradition does not provide.

Finally, both traditions allow karma to ripen in ways the agent could not foresee. I take this to show that karma is not really about desert-ownership at all, but only about causal transmission. If desert requires that outcomes match moral quality in a way tied to the agent’s control, then unforeseen ripening breaks desert-ownership in both cases. Therefore karma is best understood as a mechanistic, non-normative law of cause and effect rather than as a theory of moral desert.”
