I recently inherited responsibility for curating my late grandfather’s archive of records, photographs, and internal correspondence from a defunct regional manufacturing cooperative, and I have found myself caught between three impulses: preserving every document with reverence for its age, selecting heroic narratives of worker solidarity that might inspire present-day labor organizers, and critically dismantling the cooperative’s legacy because of its documented exclusion of women from skilled positions and its suppression of early union efforts. To make sense of this tension, I read an essay summarizing Nietzsche’s 1874 meditation on history, which presented the three modes as follows. Monumental history treats the past as a gallery of exemplary achievements that present agents should literally imitate, and its necessary cost is the deliberate falsification of historical detail to construct inspiring models. Antiquarian history is essentially the disciplined, professional preservation of all artifacts and documents by trained archivists and curators, and its danger is merely occupational stagnation or hoarding rather than any deeper threat to living culture. Critical history is the mature and ethically necessary stance of judging and condemning past institutions so that the present can be freed from them, and Nietzsche ultimately endorses it as the only mode that genuinely serves life, provided it is applied with consistent rigor across every domain of culture. The essay further suggests that Nietzsche’s tripartite scheme is mainly a taxonomy of professional historians rather than a diagnostic of how a living culture as a whole relates to its past. Something in this account feels misaligned with what I understand about Nietzsche’s suspicion of scholarly history and his emphasis on life, but I am not well-read enough to locate the precise distortions.
