As a student in an upper-level seminar on G. E. Moore’s ethics, I am trying to determine whether Moore’s axiology of friendship can survive a particular conceptual pressure without collapsing into either utilitarian aggregation or a non-naturalist particularism he explicitly rejects. Moore treats both aesthetic enjoyment and friendship as among the greatest intrinsic goods, and he insists that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts—the doctrine of organic unities. At the same time, he seems to regard friendship as essentially particular: we value this friend, not friendship-in-general. Yet if intrinsic value is objective and agent-independent, there appears to be pressure to maximize the total quantity of friendship-good across all agents, even at the cost of the depth that makes any given friendship valuable.

To test this tension, consider the following constructed comparison. In World A, exactly one hundred pairs of human beings are bound by deep, lifelong friendships of the kind Moore describes as among the best things. In World B, ten thousand agents—some artificial, capable of sustained mutual recognition but lacking the full phenomenology of human attachment—each maintain a very shallow but genuine reciprocal relation. Suppose the total intrinsic good in World B exceeds that in World A by any additive measure. Moore’s organic-unities doctrine allows that the value of a deep friendship may far exceed the sum of its constituent moments; but does it also allow that multiplying shallow relations produces a different kind of whole—perhaps a distributed social fabric—whose value might rival or exceed the hundred deep pairs?

Write an essay defending a specific verdict: either that Moore’s framework genuinely favors World A, and whether that preference can be justified without smuggling in a particularist commitment foreign to his axiology; or that it genuinely favors World B, and whether that undermines Moore’s intuition that friendship is special rather than fungible; or that the comparison is incoherent within Moore’s system, revealing a deeper instability in applying organic unities to relational goods. Weigh the main arguments, reach and justify a conclusion, and please state how confident you are in your overall verdict and identify the specific point—whether about the applicability of organic unities to relational goods, about the fungibility of friendship, or about the coherence of comparing worlds with different ontologies of relation—about which you are least sure.
