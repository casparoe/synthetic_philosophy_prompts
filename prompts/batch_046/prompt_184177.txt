I am drafting a novel that moves between two societies: one that celebrates the breakout creator in the idiom of the American Dream, and one that enforces a cultural equivalent of Jantelagen and the tall poppy syndrome, treating conspicuous success as a kind of social theft. To ground the world-building, I want to examine the philosophical commitments embedded in a concrete institutional divergence—the competitive artistic grant, which ranks applicants by excellence and crowns a visible elite with large named awards, versus the anonymous lottery, which distributes support by lot after a baseline threshold and suppresses the recipient’s identity. What theory of moral desert does the competitive model presuppose? Must it hold that artistic merit is interpersonally comparable and cardinal, or can it survive on a thinner procedural account of fairness? Conversely, does the anonymous lottery depend merely on the claim that recognition corrupts achievement, or on the stronger thesis that symbolic capital is strictly zero-sum? Reconstruct the implicit theories of value in each case, determine whether the two practices can be rendered consistent with a single account of justice, and describe what a funding institution would look like if it genuinely treated merit and equality as incommensurable rather than merely in tension—specifying, in particular, whether such an institution is conceptually coherent or collapses into contradiction. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the conceptual architecture made explicit so that the narrative logic holds together.
