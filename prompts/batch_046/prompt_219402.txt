In the literature on panpsychism and physical suffering, a specific impasse recurs with remarkable consistency. One position holds that if phenomenal properties are distributed across all fundamental physical entities—electrons, quantum fields, spacetime points—then there exists an astronomical, perhaps uncountable, quantity of minimal experience scattered throughout the universe, and that any negative valence at that scale aggregates into a morally overwhelming problem. The opposing position holds that suffering requires not merely phenomenal presence but a specific functional architecture—something like integrated affective processing or a unified subject of experience—so that micro-physical experiences, even if they exist, are morally inert because they lack the organizational conditions for harm. Both sides accept the same quantum-mechanical framework, agree that suffering is morally significant, and do not dispute one another’s physical premises; yet the debate stalls precisely at this exchange, with the panpsychist accusing the critic of smuggling in an unmotivated organizational threshold, and the critic accusing the panpsychist of conflating bare phenomenal presence with negative valence. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical—there is no hidden neuroscience result that would settle it—but rather concerns what counts as a moral reason, specifically whether the absence of organizational complexity can nullify the moral weight of an experience. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there an underlying formal question about the combination problem—whether micro-subjects combine into macro-subjects with summed, canceled, or emergent valence—that both sides are gesturing at without resolving? I want a structural diagnosis: is this a verbal ambiguity about “suffering,” a substantive metaphysical dispute about combination and emergence, a normative disagreement about thresholds for moral status, or a clash of bedrock intuitions regarding simplicity versus complexity in conscious systems? And given that diagnosis, could any conceptual distinction—say, between phenomenal presence and affective valence, or between distributed and unified suffering—actually dissolve or advance the debate, or should we treat the stall as permanent because the sides are asking different questions under one name?
