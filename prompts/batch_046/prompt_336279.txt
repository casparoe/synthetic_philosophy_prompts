As a novelist whose current manuscript turns on a protagonist who dies before the final chapter can resolve the motifs established in her youth, I have found myself unable to separate the craft of narrative closure from the philosophical problem of whether death is bad for the one who dies. I am not asking about annihilation in the abstract, but about what I have come to call "authorial absence"—the harm, if any, that accrues when a life structured like a plot is truncated before its themes can achieve coherence. Give me your actual credence, expressed as a precise number between 0 and 1, that death is genuinely bad for the subject, but evaluated specifically through a framework of narrative coherence rather than through the standard experiential-harm or deprivation accounts.

Please decompose your reasoning into three explicit probabilities: first, the likelihood that harm requires an experiencing subject in the present tense, as the Epicurean argument insists, rather than being analyzable as a relational or narrative loss sustained by the structure of the life itself; second, the likelihood that treating personal identity as a sustained narrative thread—rather than as a continuous psychological substance—either undermines or reinforces the Epicurean "no-subject" objection when the thread is cut; and third, the likelihood that a life valued partly for its completed arc makes premature death more harmful (because the narrative is left unresolved) or paradoxically less harmful (because an unfinished story avoids the degradation of a poorly resolved ending). Combine these terms into your overall figure, identify which sub-question is most sensitive to revision, and tell me what conceptual or phenomenological evidence—perhaps drawn from theories of diachronic identity or from the experience of readers confronting unfinished novels—would shift that particular term and thereby change your final number.
