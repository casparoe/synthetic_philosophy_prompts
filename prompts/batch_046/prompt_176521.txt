I'm drafting the ethical framework for a permanent public exhibition on historical responsibility, and I've tried to articulate my own position before opening it to peer review. I'd like to know whether the commitments below form a coherent package, or whether I'm quietly importing contradictions.

Here is where I stand:

(1) I distinguish two modes of evaluation: retrospective moral auditing, which asks what an agent owed given the moral knowledge genuinely available within their conceptual horizon; and structural legacy accounting, which tracks the long-run causal footprint of their actions irrespective of personal intent.

(2) I reject the 'man of his time' defence as a blanket moral excuse, but I accept a narrower epistemic mitigation principle: blame is appropriate only for failures to act on wrongness that was conceptually accessible to the agent, not for failures to transcend the entire moral framework of an era.

(3) Applying this, I hold that Jefferson should be judged severely through retrospective auditing. The arguments against slavery were not external to his world; he articulated them himself in writings on natural rights and then systematically violated them in practice. The epistemic mitigation in (2) therefore does not apply, and his personal hypocrisy is the central moral fact.

(4) For Churchill, I apply structural legacy accounting to both his imperial racism and his wartime decision-making regarding the Bengal famine, and I apply retrospective auditing only weakly. The institutional architecture of empire and the logistical overdetermination of famine conditions diluted personal agency to a point where structural accounting should dominate our public judgment.

(5) For Columbus, I hold that retrospective auditing should be suspended almost entirely. The catastrophic demographic consequences of 1492 depended on epidemiological dynamics outside any available conceptual framework; we should condemn the Columbian system he initiated through pure structural legacy accounting, without attributing genocidal intent to him personally.

(6) I insist on methodological consistency: the choice between auditing and structural accounting must be justified by case-specific features—epistemic access, institutional overdetermination—not by whether the figure is culturally celebrated or personally sympathetic.

The tension I feel is between (3), (4), and (6). Jefferson receives harsh auditing despite operating within a slaveholding society, while Churchill receives structural mitigation despite the fact that anti-imperial critiques were available in his own era. Meanwhile, (5) suspends auditing for Columbus, yet (2) seems to require auditing for any wrong that was conceptually available—enslavement and violent subjugation were criticized almost immediately after 1492, which suggests Columbus might fall under (2) after all.

At the same time, (1) and (6) seem to pull in opposite directions: (1) treats the two modes as answering different questions, which makes it tempting to apply both selectively, while (6) forbids selection driven by sympathy.

So: is this package jointly tenable? Which commitments genuinely conflict, which conflicts dissolve once the distinctions in (1) and (2) are applied correctly, and what is the cheapest revision—dropping the consistency requirement in (6), weakening the auditing of Jefferson in (3), or allowing some retrospective auditing of Columbus in (5)? Be specific about what each revision costs.
