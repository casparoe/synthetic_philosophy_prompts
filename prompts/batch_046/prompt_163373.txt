I'm preparing notes for a philosophy of science discussion group, and I want to make sure I'm not smuggling in assumptions about ontology identification that don't hold up when information is genuinely inaccessible. Here's the conceptual knot I'm trying to untangle, and I'd like an explanation of the mechanics rather than any literature survey.

Consider a scenario in which we have full access to the structural relations among observable phenomena, but permanent epistemic inaccessibility to whatever occupies the interstitial positions between those phenomena—call them latent relational nodes. Ontology O1 treats these nodes as distinct entities that mediate the observable relations; Ontology O2 eliminates them entirely and treats the relations as direct, unmediated connections among the observable phenomena themselves. Both ontologies reproduce every accessible prediction and every structural pattern we can detect, so the divergence lies entirely in the unobservable substrate.

My intuition is that there's a specific conceptual problem here that goes beyond standard underdetermination of theory by data. The difficulty isn't merely that we can't decide between O1 and O2; it's that our usual criteria for ontology identification—causal contact, descriptive uniqueness, or explanatory indispensability—seem to apply differently to the nodes than to the observable phenomena, but not in a way that cleanly favors elimination. If we invoke explanatory simplicity to prefer O2, we must first decide whether simplicity counts entities, relations, or some weighted combination of both, and that decision itself appears to depend on whether we already treat the nodes as accessible for the purpose of counting. Conversely, if we invoke a no-miracles argument for O1, we seem to be licensing ontological commitment to entities precisely on the grounds that they explain accessible patterns, even while we explicitly acknowledge that no further access to them is possible.

Can you explain, in intuitive terms, why this inaccessibility creates an asymmetry in ontology identification—specifically, why the hidden substrate information doesn't just leave us agnostic between O1 and O2, but actually destabilizes the criteria we use to identify ontologies in the accessible domain? I'm especially interested in whether there's a coherent middle position that allows us to treat the choice between O1 and O2 as substantive rather than merely conventional, without claiming any hidden epistemic access to the nodes themselves. If the resolution turns on a distinction between identifying entities and identifying structures, I'd like that drawn out clearly, along with what it costs us to maintain it.
