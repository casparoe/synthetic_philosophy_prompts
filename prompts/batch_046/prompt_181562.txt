A friend walked me into an argument that turns Darwin’s own evolutionary explanations against themselves, and I cannot see the exit. (1) Darwin held that human morality evolved from social instincts—sympathy, habitual cooperation, and the desire for social approval—rather than from rational insight into objective moral properties. (2) He also treated religious belief as an overextension of the same evolved tendency to attribute agency and intention to ambiguous phenomena, a byproduct of social cognition rather than a response to any divine reality. (3) If a class of beliefs can be fully explained by evolutionary utility, without any reference to truth-tracking or rational justification, then members of that class are debunked: they are either false or not truth-apt. (4) By (3), both moral judgments and religious beliefs are debunked. (5) But evolutionary explanations apply just as well to the cognitive faculties that produce scientific theories: our brains evolved for survival and reproduction, not for grasping abstract truths about natural selection. (6) So, by (3), Darwin’s own scientific conclusions—including (1) and (2)—are themselves debunked and unjustified. (7) Therefore Darwin’s evolutionary account of morality and religion is self-undermining. The friend claims this proves that evolutionary debunking arguments always overreach. I feel like something slipped between (4) and (6), or perhaps inside (3) itself, but I cannot name it. Which step should be rejected? Explain why it looked plausible, and say exactly what rejecting it costs for Darwin’s consistency—especially whether he can still maintain both (1) and (2) without falling into (7).
