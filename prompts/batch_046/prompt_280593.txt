I've been developing a position in moral philosophy according to which standing to blame is not a moral credential that can be forfeited through personal vice, but a strictly relational status—what I call normative addressivity—generated solely by the blamer's position in the right kind of second-personal relation to the wrongdoer: being the direct victim, an authorized representative, or a member of the shared normative community whose standards were violated. On this view, hypocrisy is a defect in the blamer's character and may diminish the epistemic weight or practical wisdom of their blame, but it cannot erode the underlying entitlement, since that entitlement is structurally assigned by the relation rather than earned by moral purity. I want to map what would genuinely count against this framework—not merely the intuitive pull of "who are you to talk?" cases, but which specific intuitions about resentment and forgiveness, which formal features of second-personal morality, and which results in adjacent domains (say, about institutional authority to blame, the conditions for legitimate forgiveness, or the distinction between being wronged and being entitled to hold another to account) it stands or falls with. Which defeaters would a committed relationalist concede as genuinely damaging, and which could they dismiss as conflating the propriety of blame with its force, its wisdom, or its rhetorical efficacy? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I'm especially interested in whether there is any case in which the relational structure remains intact but the hypocrisy is structurally parallel—say, the blamer actively participates in sustaining the very institutional conditions that make the wrong possible—such that the view must either collapse into recognizing a hidden purity condition or provide a principled explanation of why structural complicity differs from personal hypocrisy.
