I am drafting a novel in which a chef-philosopher conducts an underground seminar on the arithmetic of harm, and I need a workshop design that makes the argument visceral rather than statistical. For a group of twelve adults meeting for ninety minutes, design a structured activity—using assigned roles, binding rules, and small symbolic stakes—around the comparison between crop-cultivation mortality and direct animal slaughter, specifically weighing beef, chicken, and plant-based calories. The participants should first construct a communal menu and defend it under the explicit rule that only direct, intentional killing counts in the moral ledger; only after they have committed to portions and assigned themselves roles—farmer, slaughterer, field ecologist, diner—should the facilitator reveal the hidden toll of harvest machinery, rodenticide, and pesticide drift on wild rodents and invertebrates per calorie produced. Arrange the incentives so that the group discovers for itself that the choice with the least visible violence can carry the greatest distributed suffering, and that replacing beef with chicken or grains shifts rather than eliminates moral weight. Provide a precise timeline, the conditions and script for the reveal, a debrief that maps each stage onto the philosophical problems of indirect harm, moral distance, and the asymmetry between farmed and wild lives, and an honest account of which feature—most likely the epistemic opacity of agricultural supply chains or the difficulty of comparing sentience across trophic levels—the simulation necessarily fails to reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator. The participants should come away feeling the force of the idea that visibility and magnitude can diverge sharply in ethical accounting, not merely hearing it stated.
