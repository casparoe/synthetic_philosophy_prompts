In my art-history seminar last week, two classmates—I'll call them Maya and Jonah—became stuck in a disagreement about a ceramic installation at the museum we had visited. The work, created in 1974, is a large abstract form made of fired earthenware clay. Over the decades, conservators have replaced roughly sixty percent of the original material with a synthetic ceramic composite that is chemically analogous but manufactured through an industrial process rather than hand-thrown and kiln-fired. The museum's label still identifies the piece as the original 1974 work, not a reconstruction.

Maya argued that this labeling is misleading. She said that for an artifact like this, material constitution is not identical with the object itself: the statue is constituted by whatever clay happens to be present, but the specific artwork—the one the artist actually shaped—is essentially tied to the original spatiotemporal continuity of that particular clay. Once you replace the majority of the substrate with a synthetic substitute, she claimed, you no longer have the original object; you have a replica that happens to share the form. She added that biological organisms survive cell replacement because they possess self-maintaining causal processes, but artifacts lack any analogous mechanism, so gradual replacement does not preserve artistic identity.

Jonah replied that Maya was conflating the statue with the clay. He granted that the clay and the statue are distinct—if you reshape the clay into a bowl, the statue is destroyed but the clay remains—but he insisted that the statue can survive the gradual substitution of its parts so long as the form and its functional role as that specific artwork are continuously maintained. To support this, he noted that when a ceramicist fires raw clay, the material undergoes an irreversible chemical transformation into ceramic; if Maya were correct, she would have to say that the firing process itself destroys the original object and creates a new one, which seems implausible for anyone who thinks the finished pot is the same thing the artist intended.

Maya conceded that firing is a special case because it falls within the artist's intended transformative process, but she maintained that unauthorized industrial substitution is different. She proposed that an artifact's identity depends not merely on form or on material continuity alone, but on whether any material change falls within the constitutive rules of that kind of object—rules that include the artist's authorized procedures. Because the synthetic composite was introduced by conservators without the artist's involvement and outside the original craft process, the work's constitutive conditions were violated.

Jonah countered that this move abandons material constitution entirely. If the deciding factor is whether a change is authorized by the artist's intentions, then the artwork's identity is fixed by an intentionalist criterion rather than by any relation between form and matter. He asked whether Maya's view could even accommodate the possibility that two distinct objects—the original statue and the replacement composite—might temporarily coincide in the same space, since her framework seems to treat material and formal properties as inseparable only when the history is correct.

Neither would concede the other was right. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move, rating each argument from clearly mistaken to clearly correct? Please say who is right about which specific point, identify any subtle errors either makes, and explain what, if anything, both parties are missing about how material constitution applies to artifacts.
