I am preparing a module for an advanced applied-ethics seminar, and I have become convinced that Scanlon's contractualism is usually taught as an abstract criterion for wrongness without being translated into an institutional protocol that a non-philosopher decision-maker could actually run. I want my students to build exactly such a protocol, but it must be genuinely operational rather than a philosophical gloss, so I am asking for your help in drafting the specification. The practitioner is the chair of a regional health-allocation board who must approve or reject resource-distribution policies affecting identifiable patient groups and staff populations; the board has thirty minutes per proposal, no access to formal welfare economics, and a legal compliance officer without philosophical training who must co-sign every recommendation. The central theoretical difficulty is the aggregation problem within contractualism—how to adjudicate conflicts between a principle permitting severe burdens on a few and one distributing minor burdens across many—given that wrongness depends on whether affected parties could reasonably reject the governing principle.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure in the form of an ordered checklist: the sequence of questions asked, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stopping rules that yield approve, reject, or defer, and clear flags marking where judgment is ineliminable rather than mechanized. You must embed within it a specific operational innovation—call it a rejection audit—in which each affected group’s potential objection is articulated in that group’s own terms, without translation into a common metric of harm or utility, and then tested for reasonableness against a fixed set of conditions (for instance, whether the objection appeals to a generalizable principle, whether it rests on a preference that could not be shared by others in the same position, and whether accepting the principle would undermine the objector’s status as an equal). The audit must be executable within the thirty-minute constraint and must include a step at which the compliance officer can verify that the reasoning has been recorded without being required to evaluate its philosophical merit.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a proposal requiring a small nursing staff to work mandatory extended shifts in order to maintain full ICU capacity during a large seasonal patient influx; (b) a proposal denying a costly, non-curative but life-extending therapy to one identifiable patient in order to fund preventive care for hundreds of others; and (c) a proposal that will cause severe, documented psychological distress to a minority of staff members but will prevent moderate physical harm to a majority of patients—a case in which the procedure should yield a result that the practitioner is likely to find counterintuitive or unwelcome. For each case, show the audit steps, indicate precisely where the procedure stops, and flag the specific point at which the compliance officer’s lack of philosophical training creates a risk of misapplication.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: identify its failure points under epistemic uncertainty about the severity of burdens, explain why it is inapplicable when affected parties are not yet identifiable (for example, statistical lives or future generations), and acknowledge its silence on intergenerational trade-offs. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable should stand entirely on its conceptual architecture, and every step that involves a value judgment rather than a conceptual entailment must be explicitly marked as such.
