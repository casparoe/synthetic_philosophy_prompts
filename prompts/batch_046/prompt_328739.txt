As a journalist preparing a feature on agency for a science-and-ideas audience, I need a formal decision tree that sorts varieties of “free will worth wanting” by what a reader actually believes, not by the labels they know. The instrument should take a reader from a single root commitment—“There is a form of agency valuable enough to ground moral accountability, even if the agent could not have done otherwise in the precise circumstances of action”—through concrete branch nodes, to one of six named leaves.

Each node must be a yes/no or either/or question anchored in one of three diagnostic cases, phrased so that someone can answer without recognizing philosophical terminology:

1. The Frankfurt Lock: an agent acts from uncoerced motives, but a hidden controller stands ready to force the action should they hesitate; the controller never activates.
2. The Reversible Architect: an agent’s preferences were originally engineered, yet the agent now possesses an internal reflexive mechanism capable of revising those preferences over time, with no external blocker present.
3. The Mesh Divergence: an agent’s first-order and second-order desires align perfectly, but that alignment is the deterministic result of childhood conditioning that the agent never selected.

The tree must include these positions as leaves: (a) a Dennett-style gradualist view that treats free will as an evolved, graded capacity for self-control requiring neither alternative possibilities nor ultimate sourcehood; (b) a Frankfurt-style hierarchical mesh view that values reflective identification but remains neutral on whether the agent could have done otherwise; (c) an event-causal libertarian view that requires genuine indeterminism at the moment of choice yet denies that the agent must be an uncaused cause; (d) an agent-causal or source-libertarian view that demands the agent be the ultimate originator of the decision, not merely undetermined; (e) a hard determinist or basic-argument impossibilist view that denies any free will worth wanting is achievable; and (f) one combination permitted by the tree’s logic that is currently unoccupied—label it “vacant,” and briefly diagnose whether it appears incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Build the tree from logical space first: define the combinations of answers the three cases allow, then locate each named position within that space. Flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate—if two positions yield identical answers across all three diagnostic nodes, say so explicitly rather than obscuring the overlap. The instrument should be usable without web access: do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or references to obscure texts; rely only on widely recognized conceptual distinctions, supplemented by the niche structural feature of “reversibility of decision architecture” introduced in case 2. Write in a polished, formal register suitable for publication.
