I have been trying to understand whether historians can avoid Whig history without abandoning explanation altogether, and a study partner offered the following argument, which seems to prove that every justified historical narrative must be Whig. That conclusion feels wrong—surely one can explain the fall of the Roman Republic without measuring it against modern democratic progress—so I need to know where the chain breaks. Here are the steps:

(1) Every historical narrative selects certain events as causally significant and relegates others to background.
(2) Without a principle of selection, the narrative is arbitrary and therefore fails as an explanation.
(3) The only available principle is that the selected causes account for an outcome salient to the question now being asked.
(4) Any present question carries normative anchoring: the salience of an outcome depends on its relation to values the present audience holds.
(5) Therefore selecting explanatory causes necessarily involves measuring past events by their contribution to a present-valued outcome.
(6) Whig history is precisely the practice of interpreting the past as progress toward present-valued outcomes.
(7) So any justified historical explanation is an instance of Whig history.
(8) But if anti-Whig historiography is correct, justified explanations should avoid evaluating the past by present values.
(9) Hence anti-Whig historiography renders justified historical explanation impossible.

I especially want to preserve (1) and (2), since selection seems unavoidable and requires justification, and I accept (6) as a fair definition. Which premise should I reject, why does it look plausible despite being wrong, and what does abandoning it cost—particularly for the idea that we can understand past societies on their own terms? Is there an equivocation between explaining an outcome we care about and evaluating past actors by that outcome? Also, please say what specific argument or historical example would change your mind about which step fails, and identify the point in your own reasoning that is most likely to be mistaken.
