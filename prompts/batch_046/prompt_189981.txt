I am preparing the final exam for my graduate seminar on political thought in the twentieth century. Over the semester we have worked through Hannah Arendt's analysis of political deception, especially her argument that modern lying is dangerous not because it hides particular facts but because it corrodes the shared reality that makes judgment possible, and her distinction between the deliberate liar—who knows the truth and hides it—and the bureaucratic self-deceiver, who comes to inhabit a fabricated world so consistently that the distinction between fact and fiction collapses for him as well. I want an essay question that students can answer in ninety minutes at about 750 words, but that cannot be answered by summarizing her essay on the Pentagon Papers or by rehearsing the distinction between factual and rational truth. The question must instead require students to apply her framework to a case she never examined.

Please draft the exam question, a model answer of approximately 700 words, and a marking scheme. The question should present the following hypothetical: a democratic administration never issues false public statements, yet systematically restructures its classification and archival protocols so that the conceptual categories citizens need to evaluate security policy—say, the distinction between combatant status and civilian status in drone reports—are themselves classified, making factual verification structurally impossible without technically violating any statute against lying. Ask students whether this institutional opacity is best analyzed as a new form of Arendtian lying (because it destroys the common world), as something her framework misses (because she assumes deception operates through direct speech acts rather than through the architecture of information), or as a confirmation that her deeper concern was never deception per se but the elimination of the public realm in which facts can appear. The model answer should engage all three interpretations and take a defended position. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit analytical moves—such as correctly identifying Arendt's claim about the liar's knowledge, explaining the role of the common world, distinguishing factual from rational truth, applying the framework to structural rather than propositional deception, and evaluating whether Arendt's account needs supplementation—each assigned marks out of a total of 25, with a one-line note on the typical student failure for each point.
