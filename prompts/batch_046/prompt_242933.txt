I am serving as a teaching assistant for an advanced undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, and I am calibrating my grading for the final exam. The exam question asked students to explain the illusionist thesis regarding phenomenal consciousness (with reference to thinkers such as Keith Frankish and Daniel Dennett), to distinguish illusionism clearly from eliminativism about mental states, to compare the illusionist diagnosis of the explanatory gap with that offered by the phenomenal concept strategy (PCS), and to evaluate whether illusionism genuinely dissolves the hard problem or merely relocates it. Below is one student’s answer.

Illusionism, as defended by Frankish and Dennett, claims that phenomenal consciousness—the qualitative what-it-is-likeness associated with experience—is not a real feature of mental life but rather an illusion generated by introspection. When we introspect, we represent our mental states as having private, ineffable, immediately given properties. But these phenomenal properties do not actually exist; they are analogous to the appearance of a bent stick in water. The underlying mental states—perceptual judgments and information-processing states—are entirely real and can be described in functional or physical terms. Thus illusionism denies phenomenal consciousness but retains access consciousness and all other mental states.

This view must be distinguished from eliminativism. Eliminativists such as the Churchlands deny that folk-psychological mental states exist at all. Illusionists, by contrast, accept that beliefs, perceptions, and cognitive processes are real; they merely deny that these states possess an additional phenomenal character. In this respect illusionism is closer to physicalism than to eliminativism.

When compared to the phenomenal concept strategy, illusionism offers a different diagnosis of the explanatory gap. Proponents of PCS, like Loar and Papineau, argue that the gap arises because our phenomenal concepts are unique—perhaps indexical or demonstrative concepts that do not reveal the physical nature of the states they refer to. Illusionists, however, deny that there are phenomenal states for these concepts to refer to in the first place. Therefore the gap is not a cognitive limitation but a metaphysical mistake: we think there is a gap only because we mistakenly believe there are phenomenal properties that need explaining.

Dennett’s multiple drafts model supports this view by showing that consciousness is distributed across time and neural pathways rather than unified in a Cartesian theater. Because there is no central screen where experience comes together, phenomenal properties—if they existed—would have to be distributed across these drafts. But since they are not present in any individual draft, illusionism follows naturally: the appearance of unity is itself part of the introspective illusion.

One might object that illusionism cannot account for the fact that we genuinely seem to experience qualitative properties. The illusionist can respond that seeming is itself a representational state. The brain generates a representation with the content there is phenomenal redness here, and this representation is false, just as a hallucination is false. Consequently illusionism dissolves the hard problem rather than relocating it: once we explain how the brain produces these false representations, there is no residual mystery about consciousness.

However, there is a tension here. If the representation there is phenomenal redness is itself a mental state with representational content, then the illusionist must allow that this content is available to access consciousness. But then one might ask: is the availability of this false content itself not a form of phenomenal consciousness? After all, the representation is present to the subject in a way that feels immediate. In this sense illusionism may inadvertently reintroduce phenomenal properties at the level of representational content, collapsing back into a form of representationalism.
