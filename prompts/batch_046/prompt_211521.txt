I take this as clearly outside protection: direct, foreseeable harm to identifiable parties. Second, in a private messaging group of twelve acquaintances, one member argues that state welfare ought to be restricted to citizens; there is no call to violence, only a normative view. I think this is protected, even if it causes offence. Third, a university philosophy lecturer presents, without endorsement, an argument that full moral status tracks rational agency; two students report feeling devalued because they live with cognitive disabilities. I still regard the lecture as protected—academic freedom covers the presentation of arguments, and feeling devalued is not the right kind of harm—though I recognise an acute tension here. Fourth, a protest group stands outside a private residence at two in the morning with amplified speakers, chanting accusations of corruption directed at the occupant’s family and preventing sleep for several consecutive nights. This strikes me as unprotected: targeted harassment rather than political speech. Fifth, an anonymous whistleblower publishes verified internal documents revealing that a defence contractor is violating safety regulations; publication breaches an NDA and causes the firm’s share price to fall sharply. I think this is protected: the harm is economic and indirect, and the public interest is substantial. Sixth, a clinical psychologist, in a private one-to-one session with a vulnerable patient, repeatedly asserts that the patient is worthless and should abandon treatment; the patient subsequently attempts self-harm. This seems clearly unprotected, yet it occurs in a private, non-public setting—so I need a principle that distinguishes Case Three from Case Six without simply invoking publicity. Seventh, a journalist publishes verified but dated medical records showing that a cabinet minister was treated for depression five years ago; the condition is resolved and bears no obvious relation to current duties, yet publication triggers intense public scrutiny and personal distress. This is the case I keep revisiting: part of me thinks the harm is merely to reputation and sensibility, which Mill ought to tolerate; part of me thinks there is a genuine setback to the minister’s interests that carries no countervailing public value.

Is there a principle—perhaps refining Mill’s distinction between harm and offence, or introducing a further axis such as institutional power, fiduciary duty, or the direction of causal influence—that captures Cases One through Six, and what does it say about Case Seven?
