I am working through the case studies for my philosophy of rationality course, and my professor—Dr. Althea Vance—has graded six scenarios without fully naming her framework. Her verdicts: a shopper buys the first laptop that meets her minimum specs rather than comparing all fifty models, rational; an engineer spends months refining a bridge design that already exceeds every safety code, irrational; a student stops reviewing once she can explain ninety percent of the material, rational; a physician orders fifteen redundant tests for an unambiguous diagnosis, irrational; a firefighter adopts the first evacuation plan that clears the building, rational; and a hiring manager lowers her score threshold after twenty weak applicants and hires the first person who meets the revised standard, rational.

Next week’s case is a municipal water authority that approves the first filtration upgrade bringing contaminants below the EPA legal limit, declining to run a two-hour, zero-cost simulation that could reveal whether a minor adjustment achieves forty percent lower levels at the same price, because the upgrade already satisfies the regulatory aspiration. Predict Dr. Vance’s verdict—rational or irrational—identify whether she is implicitly running fixed aspiration-level satisficing, cost-sensitive bounded rationality with marginal analysis, or adaptive aspiration-level satisficing in which thresholds shift with the observed quality of the choice set, and point to the earlier ruling that best supports each interpretation. Then propose one further case that would distinguish the adaptive-aspiration reading from the fixed-aspiration reading, and state the consistency principle she would be adopting either way if she recommends coherence with her own track record.
