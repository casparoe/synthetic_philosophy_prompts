As a journalist covering philosophy and moral psychology, I have followed the long-running stalemate over Bernard Williams's integrity objection—specifically the scenario in which a husband rescues his wife without the interposing cost-benefit calculation that utilitarian reasoning seems to require, a case often summarized as involving one thought too many. For years the literature has reached an odd equilibrium: critics insist that accommodating the intuition forces utilitarianism to treat ground projects as fungible, which undermines the very point of having them; defenders respond that the intuition tracks a special-duty heuristic, a relational weighting, or an evolutionary bias that a sufficiently refined calculus can absorb. Both sides concede the phenomenology of the case, yet neither camp has revised its core commitment. I am asking for a diagnosis of the stall rather than another adjudication. Is the disagreement verbal—do the two sides use integrity and ground project to refer to different phenomena? Is there a hidden formal question about practical reasoning, perhaps about whether weighing a consideration necessarily alienates the agent from it, or about whether agent-centered prerogatives are reducible to deontic side-constraints? Or are the sides answering different questions—one normative and one about the conditions of agency—under a single name? My own hypothesis is that much of the impasse stems from an unrecognized ambiguity between deliberative integrity, which concerns what may enter one's reasoning, and executive integrity, which concerns whether one acts from one's commitments regardless of how they were formed; once separated, the objection may collapse into either an empirical claim about human psychology that remains untested or a conceptual claim about practical reason that both sides actually share. Given this framing, what kind of progress is possible—an experiment, a formal model of practical reasoning, a sharper conceptual distinction, or a reformulation of the original question? And please be explicit about what would change your mind: which specific argument or empirical finding would lead you to reject this diagnosis, and where in your reasoning is the failure most likely to lie?
