In strategic forecasting, practitioners often defend the "outside view" by placing a polity within a reference class—say, hegemonic empires or constitutional republics—to estimate remaining lifespan from historical base rates of collapse. A common analogy compares this to clinical medicine: just as a physician predicts a patient's mortality from actuarial tables for the species rather than from the patient's subjective reports of wellness, the forecaster should discount internal political narratives and rely on the class-level base rate. Critics reply with a counter-analogy: they claim the practice is like reading tea leaves, grouping Rome, the Ming, and the Soviet Union into one class that smuggles in false uniformity, as if every fever were the same disease regardless of pathogen.

Audit both analogies. For each, lay out the correspondence table—what plays the role of the physician, the patient, the pathogen, the tea leaves, and the reference class itself. Then identify where each mapping fractures: does an empire possess anything analogous to a biological species? Does the tea-leaf analogy illegitimately dismiss structural regularities that genuinely constrain state longevity? Next, inject a refinement: restrict the reference class not to all empires but specifically to nuclear-armed federal states undergoing rapid demographic aging, yielding a class with perhaps only two or three members. Does this precision rescue the medical analogy by making the "species" well-defined, or does it destroy the statistical basis of the outside view by reducing the sample below any meaningful base rate? Finally, determine whether the normative conclusion—that inside-view narratives should be subordinated to reference-class forecasting—survives once these analogical fractures and the small-sample problem are fully on the table, or whether the analogies were carrying the entire argument.
