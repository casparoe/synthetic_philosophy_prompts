After a long argument with a friend last night about whether collective political loyalty necessarily corrupts individual judgment, I drafted the following notes for an essay and would appreciate critical comments on their coherence and accuracy. My friend contended that Simone Weil’s well-known attack on political parties is essentially a theological complaint about idolatry—an expression of her Christian-Platonic disdain for collective passion—and therefore offers nothing to secular, pluralist political theory. I want to resist that reduction. I propose that Weil’s critique is structural: parties do not merely express false beliefs but reorganize the member’s cognitive and moral architecture. To capture this, I introduce the concept of a cognitive enclosure—a bounded epistemic regime that redirects what Weil describes as attention (l’attention) away from particular, present reality and toward an abstract, collective object of loyalty. In this framing, party membership is not only an idol but an attentional technology: it binds the capacity for direct moral perception, which Weil regards as the purest form of generosity, to the service of an imaginary collective body. Weil observes, if I understand her correctly, that parties manufacture collective passions and require members to think with the group rather than alone; I want to extend this by suggesting that such loyalty constitutes a form of attentional theft, appropriating cognitive resources that could be directed to the suffering of specific others.

My friend countered that this framework may apply only to rigid, hierarchical parties and fails to account for modern decentralized movements—activist networks without formal membership or doctrine. I think the concept of cognitive enclosure can still apply wherever there is a symbolic boundary and a loyalty test, but I worry I may be stretching Weil’s notion of attention beyond its original religious-ethical context. I also wonder whether I am conflating her specific critique of party machinery with a more general sociology of group cognition.

Could you comment on these notes? Specifically: (1) Is my structural, non-theological reading of Weil’s party-critique defensible, or does it ignore essential elements of her metaphysics of attention? (2) Does the idea of cognitive enclosure add analytical value beyond familiar concepts like groupthink or epistemic closure, or is it redundant? (3) What conceptual objections or historical counterexamples should I address if I develop this into a full essay? I am not seeking bibliographic recommendations or citations; I want to know whether the argument holds together and whether I am misrepresenting Weil’s core commitments.
