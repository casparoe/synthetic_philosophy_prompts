I've been trying to work out whether morality can be read straight off the facts about human life, and I think I've built an argument that dissolves the old gap between "is" and "ought"—except that it dissolves it too well. Here is the chain.

(1) When people call something "unnatural," they are never just describing it; the word packs description and condemnation together into a single move.
(2) If an evaluation is inseparable from the factual conditions that reliably trigger it, then describing those conditions fully already includes the evaluation—there is no missing "ought" left over.
(3) Judgments that unprovoked harm is wrong, or that cooperation is good, are triggered everywhere by the same biological and social conditions.
(4) So a complete scientific account of those conditions already contains the evaluation that harm is wrong; we do not need to import morality from outside.
(5) This seems to close Hume's gap legitimately: we derive moral conclusions from descriptive ones because the evaluation was embedded in the fact all along.
(6) But dominance behavior, tribal favoritism, and aggression are also widespread and reliably triggered by biological and social conditions.
(7) By the logic of (2), describing those conditions should also embed the evaluation that they are right, which means cruelty and bias come out as morally correct.

I can feel that something slips between noticing that evaluation is embedded and treating every embedded evaluation as authoritative, but I cannot name the exact slide. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I would prefer to keep the idea that moral judgments have deep factual roots rather than save the conclusion in (6), but I want to understand the price.

Please respond in exactly five points, numbered, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction or conclusion. State how confident you are in your diagnosis, identify the single point about which you are least sure, and say whether the error lies in treating inseparability as entailment at step (2) or in overgeneralizing the dissolution of the gap at step (5).
