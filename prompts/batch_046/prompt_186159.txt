Question 3: Camus argues that absurdism arises from teh tension between our human need for clear meaning and the universe's indifferent silence. He rejects both religious leap and nihilistic surrender as forms of philosophical suicide. Meanwhile, contemporary philosophers like Susan Wolf have proposed hybrid accounts—her fitting fulfillment view holds that meaning in life requires both subjective engagement and objective worth.

Write about 400 words: (a) explain why Camus thinks we must live without appeal, using Sisyphus; (b) outline Wolf's hybrid view and how it differs from purely subjective theories; (c) assess whether an absurdist can consistently adopt Wolf's framework without betraying the core absurdist commitment.

Student answer:

Camus thinks the absurd is the conflict between our desire for rational clarity and the world's refusal to provide it. If we turn to religion, we deny the absurd by imposing transcendent meaning; if we embrace nihilism, we deny it by giving up the search altogether. Both are philosophical suicide. The right response is to live in constant revolt, like Sisyphus pushing his rock. We have to imagine Sisyphus happy not because the task is meaningful, but because he scorns the gods and embraces his fate.

Wolf's fitting fulfillment view says that a life is meaningful when the subject is actively engaged in projects that are objectively valuable. This avoids pure subjectivism—where anything you enjoy counts—by requiring that the projects actually matter. But Wolf's idea of objective value isn't fixed by nature; it depends on cultural consensus about what counts as worthwhile, like art or science or moral improvement. So her view is hybrid but still somewhat flexible.

Can the absurdist accept this? I think yes, but only if we reinterpret Wolf's objective component through the absurdist lens. Sisyphus is actively engaged in rock-pushing, and if we treat that engagement as objectively valuable within his own value-system, then he achieves fitting fulfillment. The absurdist doesn't need the universe to provide value; he can generate it through his own committed engagement. So Wolf's view actually helps the absurdist escape nihilism without committing philosophical suicide, because the value comes from the agent's own stance rather than from an external cosmic order.
