I am drafting an essay on whether the classic contrastive framing of the Great Divergence—commonly rendered as asking why industrial modernity emerged in Britain rather than in Qing China—is conceptually well-posed as an explanatory question. Rather than a full draft, here are the notes and central argument I intend to develop, and I would welcome comments on logical coherence, on whether I mischaracterize the relevant historiography, and on whether my core distinction actually does any work.

Argument outline and notes:
1. Contrastive why-questions in both philosophy of science and history typically presuppose that the two outcomes share a common causal background up to a specific divergence point, so that we can isolate the factor that tips one case into the actual outcome and the other into the counterfactual. I want to press whether Britain and Qing China in the mid-eighteenth century satisfy this condition, or whether they were already structurally incommensurable systems.
2. Rather than treating them as two parameter-settings of a single "pre-industrial society," I propose that Britain and Qing China had different energy regimes, fiscal-military structures, and territorial logics that are not easily modeled as variations on a shared template. Consequently, asking "why not China?" may implicitly treat China as a counterfactual Britain—an interventionist counterfactual in which we hold China's social structure constant and toggle a single factor such as coal geography or patent law—when in fact altering those factors might require altering the Qing state itself.
3. This suggests that the well-posed form of inquiry is not binary contrast but comparative structural analysis: not "why Britain and not China?" but "what specific institutional and geographical configurations in Britain permitted a transition that was structurally unavailable in Qing China given its own internal logic?" The shift is from explaining an absence elsewhere to explaining a presence here on its own terms.
4. I also want to examine whether the choice of which term is treated as the explanandum—Britain's industrialization or China's non-industrialization—carries hidden normative baggage about the inevitability or desirability of industrial modernity, and whether that choice affects the kind of counterfactual we construct.
5. A further concern: if Britain's path was in some respects overdetermined by the conjunction of Atlantic access, coal proximity, and particular state capacities, then the contrast with China may be less a causal competition between two near-equals and more a category mistake that assumes a shared pool of potential industrializers.

Specific requests for feedback:
- Am I applying the distinction between interventionist and structural counterfactuals correctly in this historical context, or am I importing a philosophy-of-science framework that does not map onto historical explanation?
- Does my proposed reframing—moving from binary contrast to structural comparison—actually resolve the conceptual problem, or does it merely rename the difficulty?
- Is there an obvious body of scholarship that has already addressed this framing issue, which I should acknowledge rather than present as novel?

Please comment on accuracy, clarity, and whether the argument is interesting. I am particularly interested in whether the critique of counterfactual stability is convincing or whether it relies on an overly rigid view of historical structures.
