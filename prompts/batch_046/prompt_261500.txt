You are giving the opening remarks at a one-day workshop on institutional moral education. The audience is split: clinical psychologists and anthropologists familiar with the guilt-culture versus shame-culture framework sit on one side, and high-school administrators who must vote Friday on a pilot disciplinary program sit on the other. The workshop's framing assumption—which you should treat as live, not settled—is that an institution can deliberately cultivate either guilt or shame as its dominant moral emotion, and that this choice carries normative weight rather than being merely descriptive. In ten minutes, frame the debate without resolving it, doing so through one concrete procedural mechanism: the design of a public apology protocol in the pilot program. Show exactly where guilt's internal, self-punitive orientation and shame's social, visibility-dependent orientation generate incompatible rules for who must speak, to whom, and under what conditions of witness; be explicit about what is sacrificed if the protocol is built to amplify one emotion over the other. Speak so that the experts feel the philosophical precision carried by the mechanism itself, and the administrators feel the urgency of Friday's vote; do not cite authors, studies, or texts, treating the conceptual framework as shared background but the institutional stakes as unresolved.
