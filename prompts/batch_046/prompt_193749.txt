I have been studying Gödel's modal ontological argument in the version associated with Dana Scott, in which a being is God-like if it possesses every positive property, and one of the axioms identifies necessary existence itself as positive. From there, the proof uses modal logic S5—specifically the B axiom together with the Barcan formula for cross-world quantification—to derive that if such a being is possibly instantiated, then it is necessarily instantiated, hence actually exists.

My difficulty is with the modal status of the property-set itself. If God-likeness is defined as the conjunction of all positive properties, and if necessary existence is included in that conjunction, then in any possible world where a being lacks necessary existence, that being simply fails to be God-like in that world. This seems to make the proof circular in a specific way: it does not identify a single individual that persists across worlds with the same essential properties, but instead defines the subject world-by-world relative to whether that world validates its necessary existence. Moreover, because S5 validates the Barcan formula—so that if something is possibly instantiated there is a domain element available for that instantiation—doesn't this formal feature conflate the logical possibility of instantiation with an ontological commitment to trans-world identity conditions? I am trying to see whether Gödel's derivation genuinely establishes necessary existence, or whether it merely encodes a modally rigid definition that makes the conclusion true by semantic fiat, and how one might separate the logical machinery from the metaphysical claim without simply abandoning S5 altogether.

Please respond in American English, and address the confusion in my framing directly rather than answering the question as though it were straightforward.
