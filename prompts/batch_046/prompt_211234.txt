I'm restructuring my graduate seminar on Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I want to frame the discussion around what strikes me as an implicit normative hierarchy in the text: monumental history serves the active will but necessarily distorts; antiquarian history serves preservation but paralyzes; critical history alone subjects the past to the demands of present life, and therefore represents the genuinely philosophical achievement of the three. I had planned to connect this to contemporary debates about monument removal and decolonized archives by suggesting that modern democratic societies are approaching the critical maturity Nietzsche describes—having outgrown both heroic monumentalism and antiquarian reverence for origins. Before I commit to this framing, I want to check whether I'm misreading his typology as a developmental sequence with critical history as its telos, rather than as three irreducible temperaments that must remain in tension for life to flourish at all. More specifically, am I wrong to think that critical history can operate as a stable, dominant mode without the very monumental and antiquarian energies it seeks to dissolve?
