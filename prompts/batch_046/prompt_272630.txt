I'm leading a single sixty-minute session for an interdisciplinary undergraduate reading group — mostly juniors and seniors outside philosophy — on the narrow question of what Descartes's cogito actually proves: whether it establishes only the bare occurrence of thinking with no further properties, or whether it also licenses a substantial, enduring self with personal identity and bodily presence. Design the teaching arc, not the lecture itself. In what order must the pieces come — the method of doubt, the suspension of empirical belief, the distinction between appearance and reality — and why does each depend on the previous? Choose one carrying scenario or thought experiment (not the wax argument, not a brain-in-a-vat variant) that makes vivid both the pull toward a "thick" self and the precise reason that pull fails; specify what that scenario must include and what it must deliberately omit. Then map the misconceptions: that the cogito is a syllogism, that it proves continuity of personal identity over time, that it requires awareness of a body, or that "I think" already smuggles in a substantive ego — and say exactly where in the sequence each should be intercepted. What end-of-session check would reveal whether a student grasps the proof's narrowness rather than just repeating "I think, therefore I am"? What modern parallel — for instance, predictive-processing models of minimal selfhood — should I openly set aside at this level, and why? Finally, identify the point in your proposed dependency order where the reasoning is weakest and state what specific objection to that ordering would cause you to revise the plan. Also say what would change your mind about the choice of carrying scenario: which feature, if shown to confuse rather than clarify the thin-self conclusion, would make you abandon it for a different example?
