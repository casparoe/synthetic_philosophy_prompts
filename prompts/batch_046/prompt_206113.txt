I'm delivering the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary symposium I'm co-organizing, tentatively called The Will and the Waveform, which brings together analytic philosophers of art, historical musicologists, and practitioners of live electronic and algorithmic composition. Roughly half the audience has worked through Schopenhauer's aesthetics in detail; the other half are composers and sound designers who think in terms of signal processing and spectral morphology and have never encountered the distinction between Platonic Ideas and the direct objectification of the Will. I need about eight minutes of remarks that use Schopenhauer's specific claim — that music is not, like painting or poetry, a representation of a Platonic Idea, but rather the immediate copy of the Will itself — to do genuine structural work: it should explain why these two groups belong in the same room without reducing the musicians to illustrators of philosophical theses or the philosophers to mere listeners. The framing should be rigorous enough that the specialists recognize I am not flattening Schopenhauer into a generic music-is-emotional trope, which means I must preserve his hierarchy of the arts and his particular view that melody, as the analog of the Will's striving across levels of objectification, occupies a privileged position, while also avoiding the false claim that he valued all musical forms equally or that he would have endorsed every kind of electronic improvisation.

Please write these remarks in a voice that respects both technical precision and artistic practice — the tone of a longtime rationalist-blog reader who is comfortable with metaphysical abstraction but refuses to let it become decorative. The speech should not offer false reconciliation or suggest that philosophy explains the music; instead, it should let Schopenhauer's asymmetry between representation and direct expression generate the tension that holds the symposium together. It must be accessible to the non-philosophers without being condescending, and it should land as an invitation rather than a lecture.

Do not include bibliographic references, reading suggestions, or citations; I want the argument to stand on its own conceptual structure. The remarks should feel conceptually dense — in the weeds, not a survey — yet remain fully intelligible to someone who has never opened The World as Will and Representation, because the idea itself, not any scholarly apparatus, is what justifies the gathering.
