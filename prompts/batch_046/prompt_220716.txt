I'm in an intro course on early modern phil and my roommate built this argument about Descartes that I find suspicious because the conclusion is way too wild, but I can't tell exactly where the gears slip. (1) The cogito works: whenever there's a thought directed at one's own existance, the act of thinking guarantees there's a thinker. (2) What's proven is totally bare—no body, no memory, no history, just pure thinking going on. (3) Now run it diachronically: tomorrow morning I think "I am here," and the cogito proves there's a thinker then too. (4) But nothing inside the proof licenses me to identifcation tomorrow's thinker with today's, since all the usual conditions—body continuity, psychological links, whatever—lie outside what was established. (5) By the same token, when you think "I exist," your cogito proves there's a thinker in your head, but from the outside I have no cogito-based reason to match that thinker with you rather than with some disconnected mental flash. (6) So taken consistently, the cogito should scatter us into populations of momentary thinkers—me-now, maybe-me-tomorrow, you-now-maybe—none trackable or re-identifiable across time or across people. (7) Yet Descartes treats the cogito as a stable foundation from which he eventually rebuilds other minds and his own continuous self. (8) So either he smuggles in unproven identity conditions, or the cogito actually dissolves the self and other people into disconnected flashes of awareness.

I want to keep (1) and (2) if I can—there's something right about how stripped-down the proof is—but (6) feels like it can't be right. Is the mistake in (4), where we assume identity needs explicit proof rather than being given in experience? Or in (5), where we confuse first-person access with third-person ignorance? Or is there a scope shift between "there is a thinker" and "there is this particular thinker"? If I have to pay somewhere, I'd rather pay in (5) than in (1), but I want to know what that costs me.
