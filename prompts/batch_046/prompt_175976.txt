I hold a qualified deflationary view about metaphysical disputes—such as those over composite objects, moral properties, or abstract entities—according to which they are largely verbal: once the meaning or functional role of the relevant terms is fixed, no further substantive world-directed question remains. I have recently injected into this view an idea I call functional pluralism: that rival metaphysical vocabularies are not merely notational variants but are engineered to serve distinct explanatory and predictive functions, so that selecting one carries empirical commitments about which theoretical roles a given domain must fulfil. On my account, therefore, apparently verbal disputes conceal hidden weight, being disputes about which functional profile the world, or our best theory of it, requires. What would count against this position? I want an honest map of its exposure. Specifically: which observations, formal results, or conceptual moves—concerning, say, the intertranslatability of rival frameworks, the absence of functional differentiation, or the collapse of engineering talk into standard metaphysical commitment—would genuinely undermine it, and which would be merely awkward? Which defeaters should defenders of the view concede as damaging, and which may they legitimately dismiss? Is there a risk that the position can be qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind? Please answer in British English, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
