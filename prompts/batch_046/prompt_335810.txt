I teach a graduate seminar in moral philosophy and moral psychology — fourteen students, seventy-five minutes, one session, with no prior exposure to the specific framing protocol I have in mind. Design a structured in-class deliberation, with explicit rules and a non-monetary payoff scheme, that makes participants feel the conceptual instability of distinguishing "genuine moral preferences" from "bias artifacts" rather than merely discussing it.

The activity should proceed in three rounds. In Round 1, each student privately records direct-evaluation judgments on three cases: distributive fairness between strangers, the moral status of non-human animals, and obligations to distant others in need. In Round 2, students are randomly assigned hidden "generation conditions" — direct evaluation, evaluation after a framing manipulation, or evaluation after a role-reversal prompt — and must audit an anonymized set of judgments drawn from all three conditions. They vote, with brief written justification, on whether each judgment should be retained, revised, or discarded as a bias artifact. The hidden information structure should be arranged so that several judgments produced under the direct-evaluation condition are, by the students' own auditing criteria (instability under framing or dependence on social role), classified as artifacts, while judgments produced under framing are retained precisely because they align with the auditors' own Round 1 responses.

Inject a niche but precisely defined twist: the scoring mechanism rewards "cross-condition consistency" — points for retaining or discarding judgments in ways that cohere with one's own Round 1 responses — yet the hidden condition mapping makes perfect consistency impossible without either adopting a purely procedural theory of preference that eliminates substantive moral content, or conceding that the preference/bias distinction collapses into a reflexive loop in which the auditing criterion undermines itself.

Please specify: the exact roles and rules for each round; the hidden information structure and the precise moment and manner of its revelation; the point-based payoff scheme and how it creates tension; the debrief that maps specific moments in Rounds 1–3 onto parts of the philosophical problem; a frank prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run and how the facilitator should recover; and an explicit note on which feature of the preference/bias boundary — for instance, its dependence on a theory-neutral baseline for rational agency, or its vulnerability to second-order reflexive instability — the activity structurally cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator in the closing remarks.

Write in American English, in a formal, polished register appropriate to a philosophy professor requesting a pedagogical design. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask participants to identify named theories by author or date; let the conceptual problem emerge from the rules and payoffs themselves.
