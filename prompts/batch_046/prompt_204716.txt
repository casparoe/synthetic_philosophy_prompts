Please describe the most important implications of the Pasadena paradox—where a gamble has well-defined probabilities and payoffs yet lacks any well-defined standard expected value—for the axiomatic foundations of rational choice. In particular, address the following points: (1) Does the paradox genuinely undermine the completeness of preference orderings, or does it only show that expected-utility maximization must be supplemented by dominance reasoning or by weak and relative expectation frameworks? (2) If an agent can choose whether to enter such a gamble once or to face it in a repeated or sequential structure where undefined expectations might compound, what does the failure of absolute convergence imply about intertemporal consistency and the transitivity of choices over time? (3) The St. Petersburg paradox produces an infinite expectation that can be tamed by bounded utility, whereas Pasadena's expectation is not even conditionally convergent. How does this structural difference affect whether a rational agent should treat undefined-expectation gambles as genuinely incomparable, as dominated, or as admissible only under a relative-utility framing? (4) Finally, consider whether accepting undefined expectations forces a revision in how we model practical risk—such as long-tail catastrophic risks with well-defined probabilities but unbounded scaling—where standard convergence fails. Which axiomatic commitments—completeness, continuity, independence—are worth preserving if the cost is leaving some pairs of gambles permanently unranked, and what does that trade-off reveal about the limits of quantifying rational preference?
