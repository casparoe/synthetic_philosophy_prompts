I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend about the logic embedded in modern constitutional design, and I need help making the implicit philosophy explicit. Every liberal constitution I can think of institutionalizes a formal, collective mechanism for removing a chief executive who governs outside the law—impeachment, constitutional court removal, or an equivalent parliamentary procedure—while maintaining an absolute criminal prohibition on private citizens using lethal force against that same executive, even if he has clearly become a tyrant in the classical sense: ruling for private gain, suppressing law, and destroying the common good. The pairing strikes me as philosophically loaded. Does this institutional arrangement presuppose an Aquinian framework in which authority can be genuinely forfeited, but only a public authority—not a private individual—can recognize and execute that forfeiture? Or does it instead presuppose a Weberian claim that legitimacy resides in procedural monopoly rather than in the moral status of the ruler? Reconstruct the implicit theory of authority, legitimacy, and violence that makes both practices coherent together. Be precise about what the pairing strictly requires versus what it merely accommodates: could a pure consequentialist about state violence accept both rules, or does the asymmetry between institutional and private removal only make sense if one already believes that tyranny dissolves authority in a way that creates new moral permissions? And if we actually adopted Aquinas's conditions for licit tyrannicide—where private action becomes permissible once authority is manifestly forfeited—what exactly would have to change in constitutional and criminal law? Would we need to legalize private judgment about tyranny, or could we preserve the prohibition by redefining what counts as legitimate authority? I want to know whether the modern practice is philosophically underdetermined or whether it quietly rules out Aquinas's view by design.
