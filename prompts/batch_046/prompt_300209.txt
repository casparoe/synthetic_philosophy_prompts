I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, applying for a three-month, part-time interdisciplinary grant to produce a working paper. I need a polished one-page research proposal that turns a half-formed intuition into a tractable philosophical question. The intuition: the Ship of Theseus paradox feels intractable mainly because we implicitly treat spatiotemporal material continuity as essential to an object's identity, yet in software engineering we routinely treat a continuously deployed system as "the same product" after every underlying module has been replaced, provided its functional contracts, state-transition rules, and interface specifications are preserved. I want to investigate whether persistence should be reconceived as functional-role continuity rather than material continuity, and whether applying this framework to artifacts like ships actually dissolves the paradox or merely relocates the problem to defining what counts as the "function" of the object in the first place.

Please structure the proposal with these sections, in this exact order and with approximate lengths:

1. The precise research question (about 150 words): state the metaphysical problem clearly, distinguish the standard material-continuity framing from the proposed functional-role framing, and formulate the central question so it has a determinate yes-or-no answer.

2. Why existing positions leave it open (about 200 words): briefly characterize the usual responses—continuity of form, continuity of parts, and conventionalist or deflationary solutions—and explain specifically why none of them settle whether functional preservation is sufficient for identity in non-software artifacts.

3. Approach and method (about 250 words): describe how the project will examine analogies between engineered-system persistence and physical-object persistence, assess whether functionalist accounts from philosophy of mind can be extended to artifacts without circularity, and explain why this angle is preferable to a standard metaphysical analysis of material constitution.

4. What a result looks like and failure conditions (about 150 words): define success as a clear criterion for artifact identity that applies coherently to both ships and software systems; define failure as either the functionalist framing collapsing back into material essentialism or becoming purely conventional with no explanatory bite.

5. Scope, timeline, and deliverable (about 150 words): confirm the three-month part-time scope, specify a 3,000-word working paper suitable for an interdisciplinary workshop as the deliverable, and note one realistic risk that could make the project too big.

Keep the tone formal and suitable for a grant committee. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, citation requests, or instructions to look up specific texts. Do not ask me to verify quotations.
