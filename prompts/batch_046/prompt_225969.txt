I'm drafting a novel where one of my protagonists is a moral philosopher — don't ask why, it just felt right at 2am — and I got stuck trying to make her argument about Scanlon's contractualism sound convincing. Specifically, she's grappling with the agregation problem: how do you justify saving five strangers over one if wrongness is defined by principles nobody could resonably reject?

I mentioned this to two friends over coffee, and they immediately started fighting about it. Friend A said Scanlon's view allows aggregation through comparative weighing: the principle "save the five" is rejectable by the one who dies, sure, but the five can each reject the principle "save the one," and since the collective claim is stronger, contractualism actually permits — even requires — saving the greater number without collapsing into utilitarianism. Friend B said that's a distortion; Scanlon treats each person's complaint as individually decisive, so the one person can veto any principle that sacrifices them for others, which means contractualism genuinely can't justify saving the five unless there's some hidden tie-braker. Friend A shot back that B is treating "reasonable rejection" like a veto system, but Scanlon's framework is about mutual justifiability, not absolute vetoes, so weighing is built in. B said that's just smuggling utility-maximizing math through the back door and that if you allow weighing like this, you've lost what's distinctive about contractualism entirely. They turned to me and said "you're the writer, you decide," but I was just trying to finish my latte.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Who's closer to Scanlon's actual position on agregation, and is Friend A's comparative-weighing move a legitimate development or a betrayal of the view? Also, what are both of them missing — maybe something about ex ante reasoning or risk — and does either side actually address what happens if the one person is you?
