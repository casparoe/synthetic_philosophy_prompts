Here is an argument that extends Galileo's accommodationist hermeneutic and seems to destroy the very authority it was designed to protect. (1) Where Scripture conflicts with a demonstrated conclusion about the natural world, the biblical passage should be read non-literally, because the Holy Spirit accommodates language to common understanding rather than teaching natural science. (2) If accommodation applies to one domain of well-established knowledge, consistency demands it apply to all—historical criticism, ethics, and social science—since none of these lie closer to the Spirit's purpose of salvation than astronomy does. (3) But literal readings of Scripture routinely conflict with modern conclusions in those other domains. (4) So consistency requires treating those passages as non-literal accommodations too. (5) Once every conflict is resolved by metaphorical interpretation, Scripture retains no determinate literal content capable of grounding doctrine or moral obligation. (6) Yet Galileo introduced this principle precisely to protect scriptural authority from being undermined by Copernican science. (7) Therefore the principle proves too much: it saves Scripture from astronomy only by dissolving its authority everywhere else. Which step is weakest? In particular, is there a defensible boundary—perhaps tied to the distinction between salvation-relevant and salvation-irrelevant claims, or between demonstrative and interpretive disciplines—that lets Galileo restrict accommodation to natural philosophy without arbitrary special pleading? And what does drawing, or refusing to draw, that boundary cost for his view of scriptural inerrancy?
