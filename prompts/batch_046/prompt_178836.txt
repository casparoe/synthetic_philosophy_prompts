I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind and formal systems seminar, and I am writing the final examination. I want one essay question that deploys Hofstadter's tripartite distinction—frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages, as developed in Gödel, Escher, Bach—to analyze a conceptual problem that goes beyond standard recapitulations of self-reference. The problem I want students to confront is what I will call recursive frame collapse: a symbolic system in which an outer message instructs the interpreter to treat the frame message itself as part of the inner message, while a nested inner message attempts to reconstruct a new interpretive frame from within the old one. The result is an oscillating interpretation that is neither purely syntactic nor purely semantic, and that destabilizes the usual boundary between the rules of interpretation and the content being interpreted.

Please write the exam question suitable for a 90-minute exam with an expected answer of approximately 800–1000 words. Then provide a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain. The marking scheme should specify that an excellent answer must: (1) correctly articulate how Hofstadter's three-level architecture is meant to stabilize interpretation by separating the context-establishing frame from the content-carrying inner message; (2) explain precisely where recursive frame collapse disrupts that stabilization, without reducing the phenomenon to mere ambiguity, contradiction, or infinite regress; (3) take a reasoned position on whether meaning in such a case resides in the interpreter's response, in the formal rules of the system, or in the relational dynamics between the collapsed levels; and (4) evaluate the specific objection that recursive frame collapse is merely a disguised version of the frame problem from artificial intelligence. The scheme should also list the mistakes that should cost marks—for instance, conflating frame collapse with simple self-reference, treating Hofstadter's levels as physical rather than interpretive layers, or failing to address the AI frame-problem objection.
