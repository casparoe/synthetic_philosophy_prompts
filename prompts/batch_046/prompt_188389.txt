I'm designing a decision-tree taxonomy for an advanced epistemology seminar on the Meno problem—specifically, why knowledge is more valuable than true belief— but I want to avoid the usual “name the five classic answers” format. Build a tree with exactly three branch levels yielding seven terminal leaves, using only concrete diagnostic questions that a reader could answer without knowing philosophical labels in advance.

At Level 1, ask whether there is any universal, domain-independent value gap, or whether the gap is restricted to beliefs that guide action or enter testimony. At Level 2, for those who affirm a gap, ask whether the missing ingredient is intrinsic to the mental state, instrumental for practice or coordination, or both (so that the intrinsic/instrumental distinction collapses). At Level 3, split the intrinsic branch by whether the missing property is cognitive-achievement (justification, understanding, creditworthiness) or modal-robustness (safety, stability across nearby worlds); split the instrumental branch by whether the advantage is practical (reliable action-guidance) or social (teaching, testimony, collective coordination); and split the “both” branch by whether the synthesis requires an explicit articulable account (logos) or can be satisfied by implicit, non-transparent reliability.

Embed one niche concrete case at a branch node: a subject who receives true beliefs about ambient temperature through a perfectly reliable but fully opaque neural implant, with no accessible justification and no understanding of why the outputs are correct. Use this case to split positions on whether transparency or explicit articulability is necessary for the value gap to arise.

At each leaf, place one named position if the combination is occupied—e.g., Platonic tethering, virtue-epistemic credit, reliabilist modal anchoring, pragmatist action-guidance, epistemic-public-goods—or mark it “unnamed” if empty. For every unnamed leaf, give one sentence diagnosing why the space is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). For every named leaf, state its single most awkward commitment in one sentence.

Also flag any pair of named positions that the tree genuinely cannot separate, explaining whether they collapse onto one leaf or sit on adjacent leaves with no principled divider.

The audience is philosophy graduate students; the tree should be built from logical space rather than from a list of theorists. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation lists, or recommendations for further reading. Structure the response as follows:

1. The Diagnostic Questions (~180 words): list the branch nodes in tree order, with the implant case placed exactly where it functions as a splitter.
2. The Taxonomy Tree (~280 words): nested description of the seven leaves, each with its name (or “unnamed”) and its one-sentence awkward commitment.
3. Unoccupied Combinations and Diagnoses (~120 words): explain the empty branches.
4. Unseparable Pairs and Collapses (~120 words): identify which named views land together and why.
5. Note on the Implant Case (~80 words): explain precisely how the opaque-implant scenario operates as a diagnostic tool in the tree.
