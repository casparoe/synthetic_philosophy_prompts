I'm preparing a conceptual taxonomy for an upper-level philosophy-of-history seminar on the temptation to write the past as progress toward the present. Rather than sorting students by whether they call themselves presentist or contextualist, I want a decision tree that classifies historiographical stances by their answers to concrete normative and explanatory dilemmas.

Please construct a branching taxonomy with exactly six leaves. Each internal node must pose one diagnostic question that divides the logical space without presupposing familiarity with named schools: for instance, whether retrospective moral evaluation of past actors necessarily undermines causal explanation of their actions in terms of reasons they actually held; whether an institution that later enabled democratic participation may be assessed positively even when its founders explicitly intended the exclusion of specific groups; and whether contingency in historical development is compatible with any normative framing of the trajectory, or whether acknowledging contingency automatically collapses into a non-evaluative narrative.

Frame every node as a case or claim a student could answer without knowing which historiographical label it corresponds to. The leaves must include: (a) a strict anti-progressivist stance that rejects all normative framing of historical change; (b) a moderate contextualist stance that separates explanation from justification but permits retrospective structural assessment; (c) a position that allows evaluation of institutional outcomes but not of the intentions of the agents who produced them; (d) the view I will call critical progressivism, which holds that progress narratives are legitimate only when they systematically register the harms and exclusions that constituted the conditions of that progress, rather than treating those costs as incidental; (e) one logically available combination that appears unoccupied in the literature, with a brief diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and (f) a stance that treats moral progress as an emergent property of institutional arrangements rather than as attributable to the intentions or characters of historical agents.

For any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, flag them explicitly and explain why they collapse into one another. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for specific citations. Avoid verbatim quotations from named texts; if you reference a well-known formulation, paraphrase it without quotation marks. The audience is students capable of reasoning about normative historiography who have not memorized a canonical list of positions.
