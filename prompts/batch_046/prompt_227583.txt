The debate over whether individual actions—casting a vote in a large election, reducing personal carbon emissions, or abstaining from meat—carry moral weight when their counterfactual impact on the collective harm is negligible has repeated the same moves for decades: expected-utility arguments, appeals to collective obligation, virtue-based replies, and the insistence that one remains “part of the problem.” Yet neither the defenders of the inefficacy objection nor their critics appear to shift position. My hypothesis is that the stalemate is not ultimately about empirical causation but about a hidden disagreement over what makes a moral reason action-guiding—specifically, whether an obligation must trace through to a direct counterfactual difference in harm, or whether structural complicity in a harmful practice can generate a duty independent of individual efficacy. Diagnose this impasse: is it a verbal dispute about “making a difference,” a substantive disagreement about the conditions for moral responsibility, or are two distinct projects—one in instrumental rationality, one in political solidarity—operating under a single heading? I want a committed diagnosis, supported by what each side actually claims about causation, complicity, and obligation, together with an honest judgment about whether anything—such as a formal model of collective causation, a sharp distinction between causal contribution and structural complicity, or a reframing of the moral question—could genuinely advance the debate, or whether the disagreement rests on bedrock intuitions that should be acknowledged as such.
