I am a journalist who covers philosophy of science and intellectual history for a general-interest publication, and I am preparing a feature on competing accounts of historical explanation. For the feature’s satirical sidebar, please write a deadpan editorial memorandum of approximately 550 words, issued by the fictional Office of Historical Methodology to all contributing scholars of the fictional Quarterly of Applied Historiography. The memo should announce, in purely bureaucratic prose, a new mandatory submission guideline called the Narrative Torque Index, which quantifies the extent to which an explanation derives its force from plot architecture, character motivation, and dramatic irony rather than from subsumptive law. The satire should exaggerate real features of narrative explanation—its resistance to falsification, its dependence on literary conventions, its quiet tendency to treat outcomes as inevitable—so that a specialist in historiography recognizes the parody as fair; yet these objections must never be stated outright, and should emerge only from the absurd specificity of the index’s calibration rules, the penalties for low-torque submissions, and the invented case study used to illustrate full compliance. Woven seamlessly into the continuous text, include a brief description of how reviewers will apply a standardized torque meter to determine whether a given account of the French Revolution possesses adequate dramatic coherence. The target is the institutional overreach of narrative historiography and its smuggling of teleology into causal claims, not any named individual. Write entirely as continuous prose—no headings, bullet points, boldface, or tables—as one might compose a long formal internal letter. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific scholars beyond the invented institutional framework.
