I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on democratic theory, and I'm preparing to facilitate a 90-minute graduate seminar session for about twelve students who have read selections from Dewey's writings on democratic inquiry and the problem of the public, but who keep falling back on describing democracy as essentially a mechanism for aggregating pre-existing preferences. I want an in-class activity—structured as a simulation with roles, rules, and a debrief—that makes them feel the force of Dewey's experimentalist view: that democracy is not a voting procedure applied to fixed opinions, but a form of social inquiry that begins in an indeterminate situation and proceeds by testing proposed solutions against shared consequences. The idea should emerge from what the participants do, not from a lecture I deliver afterwards.

Design a simulation in which the group is divided into roles representing different affected parties in a collective resource problem—say, the management of a shared urban energy commons where indirect effects such as maintenance failures, downstream pollution, and temporal delays are initially hidden from each party. The rules should be arranged so that in the first phase participants are forced to vote or bargain on the basis of private preference profiles, and then in a second phase they receive constrained information that reveals their initial problem-definition was wrong: the "resource" is not what they thought, and the consequences of any decision depend on interactions they could not have anticipated alone. They should then have to redesign their decision procedure itself—moving from aggregation to some form of collaborative inquiry—before reaching a collective outcome. Include specific roles or rules, a timeline across the 90 minutes, a payoff or evaluation scheme that does not use real money but makes coordination failure genuinely costly to the group, and a debrief that maps each phase onto Dewey's distinction between "the crowd" and "a public," between opinion and inquiry, and between political democracy and social intelligence.

Please also include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—such as students treating the second phase as merely "more information for the same vote" rather than as a restructuring of the problem—and how to handle it without breaking the simulation's internal logic. Additionally, be frank about which feature of Dewey's view the activity cannot reproduce—for instance, the long temporal duration of real democratic experimentation, or the institutional embedding of inquiry in schools and press—and indicate how the facilitator must supply that missing element in the closing discussion. In framing the theoretical interpretation, please separate what is relatively settled in the Dewey scholarship from what remains genuinely contested among specialists—particularly around whether Dewey's experimentalism requires a specific metaphysics of experience or can be detached from his broader pragmatic epistemology—and do not present a contested interpretive claim as if it were the consensus view.
