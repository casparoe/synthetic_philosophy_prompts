Imagine Pierre-Simon Laplace, who died in 1827, is confronted with the 20th-century development of chaos theory: specifically, that deterministic nonlinear systems can display exponential sensitivity to initial conditions (positive Lyapunov exponents) and that this creates what physicists call effective unpredictability—not merely practical difficulty, but a structural feature in which arbitrarily precise knowledge of initial conditions yields only exponentially decaying predictive horizons. A further modern argument holds that in some deterministic systems, the sequence of future states exhibits properties analogous to algorithmic randomness: the future is fixed by the dynamical laws, yet no computation shorter than running the system itself can generate that future state from present data, even in principle.

Predict how Laplace would respond to the claim that his demon—the intelligence knowing all forces and all positions—would still be unable to compute certain deterministic futures, not because of measurement error, but because the dynamics are computationally irreducible. Would he treat this as a purely epistemic limitation that leaves ontological determinism untouched, or would he be forced to revise his conviction that perfect knowledge of laws and conditions entails perfect prediction? Have him distinguish carefully between the thesis that the future is causally determined and the thesis that the future is derivable from present knowledge, and ask whether chaos theory introduces a meaningful philosophical distinction between hidden determinism and manifest unpredictability.
