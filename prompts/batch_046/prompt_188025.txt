Two people in my study group got stuck in an arguement after a philosophy of mind talk about teleosemantics and whether it actually naturalizes mental content. One said look, teleosemantics says mental content comes from proper functions fixed by selection history, right? But then what about my belief that the quantum computer crashed? There's zero evolutionary history for that specific concept. So either teleosemantics can't account for half my mental life, or it has to say my belief gets it's content from some broader biological function—like detect error states—which is so vague it could apply to a broken smoke detector too. The other said he was being too literal: teleosemantics allows derived proper functions, where learning and language use inherit biological functions and extend them. The first guy said okay but then you've got derived functions all over the place—my laptop's error log has derived functions from it's designers, so why doesn't it have mental content? She said the difference is that human derived functions are embedded in intentional practices like reasoning and language, not just causal feedback. He told her that sounds like she's smuggling intentionality back in to save the theory, which is circular if the whole point was to naturalize content without presupposing it. She then said even if there's circularity, every naturalistic theory has to start somewhere, and at least teleosemantics explains why misrepresentation is possible—something pure causal theories can't do—because a representation can fail it's proper function.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step? Like, who's right about the evolutionary-history problem, about derived functions applying to artificial systems, about whether intentional practices are being smuggled in, and about whether misrepresentation is the real win for teleosemantics? And say what both of them are missing—maybe there's a distinction they're glossing over that makes the disagreement less sharp than it looks?
