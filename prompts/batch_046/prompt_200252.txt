hey, i'm writing the final for my upper-level pragmatics semanar and i want an essay question that won't let students just regurgitate the usual Brown-Levinson summary. the domain is bluntness vs politeness -- specifically dutch directness, japanese indirectness, and whether white lies are wrong -- but i want them to wrestle with a framing that isn't in the standard textbooks.

the injection: think of indirectness as a handicap signal in Zahavi's sense. directness is cheap; indirectness is costly because it forces the listener to do interpretive labor and risks face-loss if misread. so in a high-context system, a white lie isn't just "being nice" -- it's part of a costly commitment mechanism. in a low-context dutch-style system, the same lie is cheap talk and therefore carries a different moral weight. the question should make them decide if there's still a universal verdict.

essay prompt (90 mins, ~1200 words): "is there a unified moral evaluation of white lies across high-context and low-context communicative systems? defend or reject universalism, but do so by addressing three things. first, whether the wrongness of deception tracks the cost-structure of the signal -- i.e., whether a lie embedded in a handicap-like politeness norm is morally different from cheap direct deception. second, whether dutch 'bespreekbaarheid' and japanese 'honne/tatemae' distribute epistemic burdens differently between speaker and listener, and whether that redistribution changes who is being wronged. third, whether treating politeness as costly signaling undermines the intuition that white lies are harmless social lubricants, or whether it actually explains why they feel harmless in some contexts and manipulative in others."

model answer (around 600 words): start by distinguishing moral universalism about deception from context-sensitive evaluation. note that if indirectness is a handicap, then producing it demonstrates social investment; the listener's interpretive work is the price of admission. a white lie in this framework inherits credibility value -- it's expensive to maintain consistently -- so it functions less like fraud and more like a credible commitment to harmony. contrast this with dutch directness, where the speaker bears the risk of offense; here transparency is the costly signal, and a white lie breaks that contract because it's cheap evasion. so the moral wrongness isn't identical: in high-context systems, the harm of deception might lie in corrupting a costly coordination mechanism, while in low-context systems it lies in violating a transparency norm that itself serves as the handicap. reject simple universalism, but don't collapse into pure relativism: argue that what is constant is not "lying is wrong" but "corrupting the dominant costly signal is wrong," with the content of the signal varying by culture. conclude that white lies are not harmless lubricants everywhere; in handicap-heavy systems they may actually preserve social capital, but that doesn't make them morally neutral -- it makes them structurally significant.

marking scheme (20 marks total):
- applies handicap/signaling logic specifically to communication, not just repeats Zahavi (5 marks). typical B mistake: mentions Zahavi but doesn't connect cost to listener labor.
- distinguishes speaker-risk vs listener-risk burden distribution (5 marks). typical B mistake: describes dutch and japanese norms separately without comparing the cost allocation.
- addresses universalism directly, not just "it depends on culture" (4 marks). typical B mistake: lists cultural differences and stops.
- connects cost-structure to moral evaluation of deception (4 marks). typical B mistake: describes costs but never says why they matter for wrongness.
- coherent conclusion that doesn't treat the framing as decorative (2 marks).

A answer: hits all five, especially noting that if politeness is a handicap then white lies have structural credibility value that makes them different from cheap deception, and uses that to refine rather than abandon universalism.
B answer: describes both cultural norms accurately, mentions handicap idea, but treats it as an add-on and fails to say why signal-cost should affect moral judgment.
