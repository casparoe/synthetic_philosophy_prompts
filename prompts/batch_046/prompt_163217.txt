I recently assumed supervisory responsibility for the expressive-arts track at a residential grief-counseling center where counselors guide small groups through curated encounters with tragic art—films, elegiac music, narrative poetry, and dramatic texts—as part of a structured emotional-processing protocol, and after an incident last month in which a participant experienced prolonged dysphoria rather than the intended cathartic release, I realized we have no reproducible method for deciding, under time pressure and with varying clinical expertise among staff, which works are appropriate for which groups and in what sequence. I would like you to construct a practical decision procedure that translates the philosophical and psychological literature on the paradox of tragedy—why people voluntarily seek out art that evokes sadness, grief, or fear, and under what conditions that experience is restorative rather than harmful—into an ordered set of steps a counselor can run in about fifteen minutes before a session. The procedure should begin with diagnostic questions about the work itself, addressing whether it offers the kind of emotional distance or framing that allows for meta-emotional awareness, whether its sorrow is representational or affectively contagious, whether it provides narrative closure or sustained ambiguity, and whether it invites identification or observation; it should then move to ordered questions about the viewer's present emotional state, prior exposure history, and group dynamics that determine readiness; it must include explicit stopping rules where the answer is necessarily no, explicit hand-off points where the decision turns on values—such as whether catharsis is even the appropriate therapeutic goal for this particular group, or how much dysphoric risk is acceptable—rather than on clinical or aesthetic facts, and an honest marking of which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Please apply the procedure to three cases: a screening of a classical Greek tragedy, a contemporary film about parental loss that offers little narrative resolution, and an instrumental musical piece with no representational content whatsoever, including at least one case where the procedure produces an answer the counselor will find unwelcome. In doing so, please distinguish carefully between what is genuinely settled in the relevant literature—such as the existence of the paradox itself, the role of perceived safety in allowing negative-emotion enjoyment, and the importance of some form of emotional distance—and what remains genuinely contested, including whether catharsis requires a specific kind of plot structure, whether meta-emotional awareness always enhances the experience or can sometimes undermine it, and whether instrumental music without narrative can trigger the same paradoxical pleasure; do not present any of these contested positions as if they were the consensus view. The entire response should be written in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a detailed letter or email, and it should not include any bibliography, reading list, citation, or reference to texts I should consult.
