I am defending a seminar thesis next Thursday to an interdisciplinary ethics-and-decision-theory workshop of about ten people: three formal decision theorists who know the standard proof of Good’s result on the expected value of information and its extensions to costly acquisition inside out; two climate ethicists who reject quantifying intergenerational obligations and distrust formal decision theory in policy contexts; two philosophers of science who specialize in deep uncertainty, model robustness, and the problem of assigning probabilities to climate tipping points; and one climate-policy practitioner primarily concerned with institutional paralysis and decision deadlines. All have read my two-page handout.

My thesis is that Good’s theorem—stating that the expected value of information is non-negative under standard rationality conditions—generates a defeasible procedural obligation to acquire additional climate-impact forecasts before committing to irreversible geoengineering interventions, even when (a) the computation required carries its own carbon footprint, (b) the improved forecasts mainly benefit distant future agents rather than present decision-makers, and (c) the decision framework is non-archimedean in treating catastrophic tail risks as lexically significant. I further claim that this obligation is suspended only once acquisition delay crosses what I call an “informational expiry point,” after which the forecast can no longer alter the decision window.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. Order the objections by how likely and damaging they are for this specific audience, phrased in the language each subgroup would actually use. Include, at the end, the single question I am hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes a hidden assumption in my “informational expiry point” concept.

Keep the entire response under 650 words. Separate what is settled in decision theory (e.g., the formal conditions under which Good’s theorem holds) from what is genuinely contested in my normative application (e.g., whether procedural rationality can override direct carbon costs, or whether non-archimedean framing resolves deep uncertainty), and do not present any disputed premise—such as the moral priority of information-seeking over immediate emission reduction—as if it were the consensus view in the room.
