I've been formalizing a nested version of the hole argument and it now seems to prove too much about the ontology of spacetime points. (1) If two solutions to Einstein's field equations differ only by a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside a compact region R but not inside R, they represent the same physical situation—this is the standard anti-substantivalist lesson. (2) Now construct a nested sequence of subregions R₁ ⊃ R₂ ⊃ R₃ ⊃ ... within R, and apply a distinct diffeomorphism φₙ inside Rₙ that fixes Rₙ₊₁ pointwise, yielding a sequence of models M₀, M₁, M₂, ... (3) By transitivity of physical equivalence, M₀ and every Mₙ represent the same world; taking the formal limit Mω, it too is physically identical to M₀. (4) In Mω every point in the intersection ∩Rₙ has been permuted infinitely often, so point-identity collapses: the model describes only a bare metric field with no localized points—what one might call a genuinely pointless manifold. (5) So consistent application of (1) entails that spacetime points are physically unreal. (6) Yet a diffeomorphism is defined as a smooth, invertible map on a point-set manifold; without points there is no domain to permute, hence the reasoning in (1) cannot be formulated within the ontology it delivers. (7) Therefore (1) appears to undermine its own formal precondition. I want to keep both (1) and (5), since general covariance and point-free structuralism each seem independently defensible, but then I must reject something in (2)–(6). Which step fails, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, is the error treating physical equivalence as transitive over an infinite construction, assuming that a formal limit model belongs to the same solution space, or supposing that point-free ontology cannot accommodate the concept of a map?
