Act as a philosopher of ethics with expertise in decision-theoretic treatments of normative uncertainty. Before ranking, take a moment to brainstorm the hidden failure modes that arise when moral uncertainty extends not just across first-order theories but to the meta-level—specifically, when an agent is genuinely unsure whether moral realism is correct and therefore whether there is any fact of the matter about which moral theory is “true.”

Then rank the following five strategies for navigating moral uncertainty into tiers (S / A / B / C / F) according to how well each survives that meta-level challenge: (1) maximize expected choiceworthiness using credence-weighted first-order theories; (2) the moral-parliament approach of aggregating prescriptions across theories; (3) strict deference to one’s single most credible first-order view; (4) suspension of moral judgment in favor of purely prudential reasoning; and (5) treating meta-ethical commitments as hyperpriors in a hierarchical model that conditions first-order weights on the probability of realism.

For each tier, justify the placement in exactly one sentence that explicitly addresses how the strategy handles the possibility that moral values lack the kind of ontological status required for “being wrong about them.” Do not provide citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; argue from conceptual structure alone.
