I'm preparing a single fifty-minute session for first-year graduate students in an interdisciplinary history-and-philosophy program, and my own recent work on archival silence has made the standard "history is written by the victors" framing feel simultaneously too glib and too hard to defend with precision. I need a teaching plan—not the lecture itself—for how to move them from that cliché to a defensible, nuanced position about historical relativism, power, and perspective, specifically through the idea that absence in the archive can be structurally produced rather than merely accidental. The audience will enter with one of two stances: either that victors' bias is obvious and uninteresting, or that if we simply correct for bias we recover an objective history untouched by power.

Plan the explanatory arc: state what conceptual dependency has to be in place before the relativism question even makes sense to them—likely something about how narrative framing relates to available evidence rather than to "facts" in isolation—then identify the one concrete historical episode that should carry the entire session. Choose an episode where the absence of sources is itself the philosophically interesting feature, not just a gap to be filled, and explain why the usual examples (the American Revolution, the fall of Rome) fail for this pedagogical purpose. For each, specify exactly where in the arc it should be anticipated and defused. Provide one diagnostic question I can ask near the end that reveals whether they have grasped that historical relativism concerns the conditions of narrative possibility rather than global skepticism about every particular claim. Finally, state explicitly which portion of the specialist literature—perhaps the technical debate between narrative constructivists and critical realists about evidentiary norms—I should openly refuse to explain at this level and why leaving it out protects the arc. Keep the response under 400 words, and lead with the bottom line in a single sentence.
