Write a short story that takes seriously the question of whether artifacts have politics, placing the debate in a setting that is not the usual historical example of a bridge or a nuclear reactor. Your protagonist is a senior infrastructure engineer in a mid-sized city where, by recent ordinance, physical structures—bridges, sewer mains, electrical substations—have been granted procedural standing in zoning and maintenance hearings, not on the grounds that they possess consciousness or interests, but because decades of data have shown that their material configurations predictably include and exclude specific populations regardless of the intentions of present-day officials. The engineer must defend a proposal regarding an old suspension bridge that was constructed with load limits and pedestrian chokepoints that effectively barred heavy commercial vehicles and large public gatherings from reaching the waterfront district; though the original zoning codes that justified those constraints have long been repealed, the bridge's physical form continues to shape residence patterns, emergency access, and economic activity. During the hearing, opponents and allies frame the bridge's politics in three distinct ways: as residing in the intentions of its original designers, as residing in its present causal effects on human life regardless of anyone's intent, and as residing in the social practices—inspection routines, funding priorities, labor contracts—that stabilize its operation. The engineer should come to see that widening the deck or removing the barriers would change the bridge's distributive effects, but would not be a politically neutral act of optimization: she discovers that the bridge's current maintenance regime depends on proprietary tension-cable techniques controlled by a single remaining firm, so that any structural redesign binds the municipality to that firm's long-term service contract, embedding a new form of dependency into the concrete and steel. The story should not deliver a clear verdict on where the politics "really" lie; instead, it should conclude with the engineer submitting a technically viable redesign that she explicitly describes as conceptually unsettled—acknowledging that the choice of materials, dimensions, and maintenance protocols is inseparable from a choice about who will be able to live, work, and assemble in the waterfront district, and that treating the bridge as a mere instrument to be improved risks obscuring the history already solidified in its form.
