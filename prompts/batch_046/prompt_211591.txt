I am preparing a fifteen-minute presentation for our regional sixth-form philosophy symposium, to be held at a partner college next Thursday. The thesis I shall defend is that Frankfurt cases do not merely furnish isolated counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities, but reveal a deeper structural phenomenon I call 'intervention opacity': in situations where an unseen mechanism stands ready to block any deviation without the agent's knowledge, the agent's internal epistemic position is indistinguishable from one in which genuine alternatives exist, even though the modal reality is hidden. From this I argue that moral responsibility ought to be reconceived not in terms of access to alternatives, but in terms of 'effective deliberative control'—that is, whether the agent's reasoning was genuinely responsive to reasons, irrespective of whether unchosen paths were structurally available. I apply this specifically to institutionally constrained environments, such as compulsory curriculum boards or algorithmically mediated selection processes, where hidden veto structures are pervasive but invisible to the agent. My audience comprises three groups I must anticipate precisely. First, two students from the host college who have studied compatibilist responses closely and will contend that even within Frankfurt-style scenarios there remains a minimal, unexercised alternative—however remote—that preserves a weakened principle of alternative possibilities. Second, our teacher-advisor, who holds a libertarian view and maintains that any form of determinism or hidden structural constraint undermines desert-based moral blame altogether. Third, a larger group of peers from neighbouring schools who know only the standard counterexample—in which an agent acts while an unactivated intervener waits in the background—and who tend to assume that responsibility automatically tracks any conceivable 'could have done otherwise' condition. I have sketched my argument in three stages: I first show that in the standard case the agent lacks open alternatives yet retains responsibility because the counterfactual mechanism never actually interferes; I then extend this to cases of intervention opacity, where the agent is unaware that a blocking mechanism—whether an administrative veto or an algorithmic filter—is present; finally I conclude that, because agents cannot distinguish from the inside between genuinely open paths and structurally blocked ones, responsibility must depend on the quality of deliberation rather than on the existence of alternatives. Please prepare me for the objections these groups will actually raise. List them in the order of likelihood and potential damage to my position. For each objection, phrase it exactly as that particular audience member would phrase it. Indicate clearly what I should concede, what I should hold firm, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope they overlook. And please include the one objection I am privately hoping nobody asks.
