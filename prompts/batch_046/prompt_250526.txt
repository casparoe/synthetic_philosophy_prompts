I'm working through the literature on whether pain is necessarily bad, especially in light of clinical conditions like pain asymbolia, in which patients report vivid nociceptive sensations without any corresponding distress or negative affect. In a philosophy-of-medicine reading group and while drafting a seminar paper, I have kept encountering a specific argumentative pattern that I cannot quite map onto the standard catalog of fallacies, theoretical adjustments, or pluralist positions. The pattern is not a single theory being patched with an exception, but rather a kind of normative relocation: whenever one dimension of pain is shown to be dissociable from its badness, the interlocutor shifts the locus of badness to whichever dimension remains intact in the counterexample, treating that remaining dimension as if it had been the intended bearer of the normative property all along.

Here are two concrete shapes it takes. First, a defender appeals to the intrinsic affective quality of pain — the feeling of hurt itself — as the source of its badness. When presented with asymbolia patients who can localize and describe their pain precisely yet report no suffering, the defender does not abandon the claim; instead, they relocate the badness to a cognitive-evaluative layer, insisting that the patients still implicitly judge the sensation as undesirable or harmful. But if one then points to clinical reports of patients with certain frontal or parietal disruptions who treat the sensation with curiosity or indifference and appear to lack even that evaluative stance, the defender relocates again, this time to representational content: the pain is bad because it signals tissue damage, regardless of how it feels or is evaluated. Then, if one notes cases of significant tissue damage that occur without any nociceptive signal — congenital insensitivity, for instance — the defender shifts to a functional account, saying pain is bad in virtue of motivating protective behavior. Yet when faced with chronic neuropathic conditions in which the pain motivates no useful protection and indeed seems purely debilitating, they do not concede that this particular instance is not bad; instead, they describe the motivational role as distorted and preserve the claim that the underlying functional norm is still violated, hence the pain remains bad. At each step, the specific feature originally cited as the source of badness is effectively abandoned, but the conclusion that pain is necessarily bad is maintained by attributing the normative weight to a newly highlighted feature.

What I am after is whether this move has an established name or diagnosis. It is not quite a motte-and-bailey, because there is not a single defensible core and an indefensible extension being swapped in and out; it is more like a serial migration of the normative property across dissociable dimensions of pain — affective, evaluative, representational, and motivational. Nor is it a standard ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis, since nothing is being added to protect a predictive theory; rather, the core normative claim is being re-anchored. Is there a concept in philosophy of science, philosophy of medicine, or argumentation theory that captures this exact pattern of dimension-hopping in normative defense? If not, can you coin and define a precise term, with boundary conditions that distinguish it from ordinary theoretical refinement, from equivocation, and from the kind of genuine pluralism that holds pain is bad in multiple independent ways? I would also like to know where the edges lie: does the phenomenon apply only when the dimensions are genuinely dissociable by clinical or phenomenological evidence, or does it extend to purely conceptual distinctions? I want the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its borders sit — rather than a bare label.
