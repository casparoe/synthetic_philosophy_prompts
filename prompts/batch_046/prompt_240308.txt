I want you to translate the ethical debate about historical fiction and biopics—specifically what obligations dramatists owe to the real dead—into the precise vocabulary of archival science.

First, the aesthetic-autonomy view: artists bear no special duty to historical accuracy and may freely reorder, invent, or suppress biographical details for dramatic effect. Second, the harm-based view: an obligation arises only if misrepresentation causes tangible injury to descendants, living communities, or the public historical record. Third, a framework I will call custodial fidelity: a human life is analogous to an archival fonds, so that inventing dialogue, altering the provenance of motives, or corrupting the contextual metadata of actions constitutes a breach of custodial duty regardless of whether anyone is harmed, because the duty is to preserve the integrity of the creator's original context rather than to serve present audiences.

For each position, restate its core claims using only archival concepts—provenance, custodial integrity, arrangement, descriptive metadata, access rights, and respect des fonds. Then deliver the residue report: identify which philosophical claims translate cleanly into archival practice, which survive only through analogical stretching or distortion, and which have no counterpart whatsoever in how archivists manage records. Give particular attention to whether artistic interpretation has any equivalent in archival description, or whether it is structurally incompatible with custodial logic.

Conclude by assessing the direction of failure. Does the residue indicate that archival ethics lacks the conceptual resources to evaluate artistic representation, meaning the debate about dramatizing the dead requires a non-archival vocabulary? Or does it reveal that the philosophical positions—autonomy and harm—are themselves under-formalized, and that treating lives as fonds exposes hidden obligations that those frameworks systematically overlook?

Keep the translation rigorous: do not import aesthetic assumptions into archival terminology and present them as native translations. Treat the residue—not the successful mapping—as the intellectually important part of the answer.
