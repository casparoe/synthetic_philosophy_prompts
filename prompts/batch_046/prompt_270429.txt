As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past two years working through philosophy of science and the interpretation of quantum mechanics, I am presenting a short position paper next Thursday to a philosophy-of-physics reading group. The audience is precisely this: two doctoral students who are committed Everettians and have studied the decision-theoretic derivations of the Born rule—including the Deutsch and Wallace arguments and the subsequent refinements by Saunders and others—in considerable detail; three physicists turned philosophers who remain agnostic on interpretation but are instinctively suspicious of importing normative decision theory into physical ontology; and one senior philosopher of science who holds that any attempt to "derive" objective probability within a deterministic branching framework is a category mistake from the outset. I am defending the thesis that Everettian quantum mechanics does not solve the Born-rule problem but merely relocates it: specifically, that the decision-theoretic derivation succeeds only by smuggling in an independent normative postulate—namely, that rational agents ought to weight outcomes by the squared-amplitude measure—so that what looks like an ontological dissolution of probability is in fact its displacement onto an unjustified principle of rational preference. My argument runs in five steps. First, the bare Everettian ontology presents deterministic branching with no intrinsic probabilistic structure and, absent further assumptions, no reason to prefer amplitude-weighting over equal branch-counting or any other measure. Second, the decision-theoretic apparatus introduces a rational agent who assigns credences across branches. Third, the derivation demonstrates that such an agent, if rational, should align her credences with the squared-modulus measure. Fourth, the justification for selecting that particular measure—rather than treating it as an arbitrary stipulation—depends on treating physical amplitude as a direct determinant of rational preference, which is not extracted from the branching ontology itself but imposed upon it. Fifth, this move conflates an ontological parameter with an epistemic norm, committing what I take to be a subtle category error: the derivation does not show that probability emerges from the physics, but only that a particular decision rule reproduces the quantum formalism if one already accepts that rule's normative authority. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a polished defense of my view. Order the objections by the combined likelihood that they will be raised and the damage they would do if I answer them poorly, with the most dangerous first. For each, provide a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes, and flag explicitly the weakest point in that reply—where I am being concessive, where I am relying on intuition, or where the objection genuinely bites. State clearly what I should concede up front to maintain credibility with this group, and what I must hold firm if my central thesis is to survive the session. Finally, identify the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the softest joint in my own position—and sketch how I should prepare to defend that point rather than hope it goes unmentioned. Keep everything in the weeds of the decision-theoretic literature: avoid generic philosophical discussions of "what is probability?" and stay focused on the specific move from branch ontology to rational credence, particularly on whether amplitude-squared weighting is ontologically grounded or decision-theoretically stipulated, and on the problem of zero-measure or near-zero-measure branches that any weighting scheme must accommodate.
