I'm developing a research program that treats Avicenna's flying-man argument not as a historical precursor to Cartesian dualism, but as a live framework for asking whether first-person certainty can be structurally isolated from any embodied sensory manifold. To keep the project from becoming either antiquarian or reductively anachronistic, I'm orienting it around three potential lines of work. Line A asks whether the argument's logical form survives if we replace Avicenna's suspended-in-void subject with a disembodied predictive-processing model that lacks proprioceptive error signals yet retains a self-model: can there still be an immediate, non-inferential awareness of existence, or does the absence of sensory prediction collapse the certainty? Line B asks whether the flying man's awareness functions as a crane-like reduction of selfhood to pure indexical reference, or whether it operates as a skyhook that smuggles in an unexamined ontological commitment to a substantial soul—specifically, whether the argument illicitly converts an epistemic privilege into a metaphysical category. Line C asks what the argument implies for contemporary debates on minimal phenomenal selfhood in altered states or artificial systems—whether a system can satisfy the conditions for Avicennian self-awareness without meeting any recognizable criteria for consciousness, and whether that distinction matters for how we assign moral or epistemic status.

Assume I have about two years, a background in analytic philosophy of mind with some formal logic, but no extensive training in medieval Arabic philosophy, and that I must avoid greedy reductionism—collapsing a genuinely metaphysical claim about the soul into a merely cognitive-scientific claim about self-modeling. Given these constraints, which of these three lines should I prioritize, in what order, and are there others you consider more important?
