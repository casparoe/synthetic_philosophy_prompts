I am writing a dissertation chapter on Frankfurt-style counterexamples to the principle of alternative possibilities, and I want to avoid rehearsing debates that have been largely static since the 1990s. I am particularly interested in which remaining open conceptual questions actually constrain how we should model moral responsibility when determinism is structurally engineered rather than merely hypothetical—say, in algorithmic governance or clinical neurointervention. I see three upstream issues: first, whether the flicker-of-freedom objection genuinely isolates a residual modal alternative in Frankfurt cases or instead conflates causal efficacy with modal accessibility; second, whether the failure of PAP should redirect attention to sourcehood conditions, and if so, which formulation—manipulation-based, reasons-responsive, or historical—is most resilient when applied outside traditional agency; third, whether progress on either front is still tractable through pure conceptual analysis or now requires empirical integration. Rather than a survey, I want a defended prioritization for a philosopher working without laboratory access: rank these lines by importance for restructuring responsibility theory, defend your criteria—upstream dependence, tractability in isolation, resistance to dissolution by revised cases—and flag hidden dependencies, such as whether resolving the flicker problem is a prerequisite for advancing sourcehood models or whether they can proceed in parallel. Which one or two deserve the next three years of work?
