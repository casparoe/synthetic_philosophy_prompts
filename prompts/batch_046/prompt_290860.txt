I am designing the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Kant’s Groundwork and the Metaphysics of Morals. I need one 90-minute essay question on Kant’s universalizability tests that cannot be answered by reciting the four formulations and running them through the standard examples (false promising, suicide, neglect of talents, beneficence). Instead, the question should present a maxim that embeds an epistemic condition: “Whenever I have reasonable but not conclusive evidence that a person in need will misuse direct aid, I will withhold that aid and redirect resources elsewhere, on the condition that my judgment of likely misuse is sincere and based on available data.” Students must determine whether this maxim fails the Formula of Universal Law (FUL) via a contradiction in conception, a contradiction in will, or neither; then they must apply the Formula of Humanity (FH) and say whether it yields the same verdict or a different one. Crucially, they must take a definite, argued position on whether any divergence between FUL and FH in this case is merely apparent or genuinely threatens Kant’s equivalence thesis — and if the latter, whether the problem lies in the maxim’s formulation, in the interpretation of one formula, or in the equivalence claim itself. Please write the exam question, a model answer of approximately 700 words that takes a committed stance rather than surveying options, and a marking scheme out of 25 points built around five must-hit moves: (i) precise logical reconstruction of the maxim and isolation of its embedded epistemic condition (5 points); (ii) correct application of FUL with explicit distinction between contradiction-in-conception and contradiction-in-will, plus a definite verdict on which applies (5 points); (iii) correct application of FH, including whether treating the recipient merely as a means occurs, with a definite comparative verdict (5 points); (iv) direct engagement with the equivalence thesis, arguing whether divergence is real and, if so, where the fault lies (5 points); (v) argumentative clarity and precision, including one characteristic mistake that should cost marks in each category (5 points). For each criterion, specify what distinguishes an A-level performance from a B-level performance, and identify the specific error students typically make (e.g., collapsing the epistemic condition into a general permission to judge others, or treating FH as a redundant restatement rather than an independent test). The prompt should be self-contained: do not ask students for bibliographies, citations, or references to secondary literature, and do not rely on obscure interpretive debates that require specialized knowledge of recent scholarship.
