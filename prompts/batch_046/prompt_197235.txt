Design a reusable decision procedure for a director of a long-running public research funding body who must decide whether to terminate or continue an initiative that has accumulated substantial unrecoverable investment but now faces strongly negative interim results. Ground the procedure in philosophical distinctions among forms of persistence—irrational sunk-cost honoring, rational commitment devices, constitutive institutional identity, and epistemic value of failure—rather than in pure expected-value economics. Specify the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where termination is mandatory, the points where continuation is permitted despite the losses, and the explicit hand-off points where the question is one of values rather than rationality and the procedure must defer to human judgment. Apply the procedure to two cases: (a) a thirty-year ecological monitoring program whose original climate models have been falsified, but whose continuous dataset is now irreplaceable for unrelated research; and (b) a clinical-trial infrastructure built around a disproven biomarker hypothesis, where maintaining the cohort for unrelated comorbidity tracking requires continuing recruitment under false pretenses, and shutting it down destroys the only long-term dataset of its kind. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer that will feel wrong to the director. If the premise that honoring sunk costs can ever be rational beyond functioning as a commitment device is conceptually confused, or if the task demands more determinacy than philosophy actually permits, say so plainly instead of forcing a false precision. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
