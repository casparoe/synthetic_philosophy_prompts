Exam question:
Consider the absent-minded driver problem. A driver must travel a highway with two consecutive exits: Exit A (poor road, payoff 1) and Exit B (destination, payoff 4), with missing both yielding 0. The driver knows the layout but suffers from absent-mindedness: upon reaching either intersection, he cannot recall whether he has already passed Exit A. He must decide at that point whether to continue or to exit. First, explain the tension between the ex ante optimal plan and the ex post rational choice available at each intersection, being precise about the expected-payoff reasoning under imperfect recall. Second, evaluate whether resolving the tension by treating each intersection decision as made by a distinct temporal part of the agent—each part maximizing only its local expected payoff—actually solves the problem, or whether it merely relocates the conflict. In particular, discuss whether any coordination or commitment mechanism is available across the parts, and be clear about the respective roles of imperfect recall and of diachronic agency.

Student answer:
The absent-minded driver faces a sequential decision problem with imperfect recall. Ex ante, before reaching the highway, the driver can calculate that the best plan is to continue past the first intersection and then take the second, since this yields a payoff of 4, whereas exiting immediately yields only 1 and missing both yields 0. This is the optimal strategy if the driver can commit to a rule.

However, once at an intersection, the driver lacks the memory to determine whether he is at Exit A or Exit B. From this ex post perspective, he assigns equal probability to being at either exit. If he continues, he risks being at Exit B and missing the destination entirely (payoff 0), while if he is at Exit A he will reach Exit B and obtain 4; so the expected payoff of continuing is 0.5 × 4 + 0.5 × 0 = 2. If he exits, he receives 1 at Exit A and 4 at Exit B, giving an expected payoff of 0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 4 = 2.5. Therefore, ex post rationality recommends exiting.

This creates a paradox: the plan that was best from the outset is not the one that seems rational at the point of action. To resolve it, one might adopt a partitioned-agent model, as Davidson proposed in his analysis of weakness of will, in which each temporal part of the driver is treated as a separate agent with its own utility function. Under this view, the part at the first intersection knows that if it continues, the part at the second intersection will face the same decision problem and will also apply the local maximization rule. Because both parts share the same reasoning structure, they can coordinate implicitly on the ex ante plan without any external commitment device: the first part continues because it anticipates that the second part will exit, and the second part exits because that is locally optimal.

But this resolution is illusory. The partitioned-agent model does not actually provide a coordination mechanism; it merely redescribes the ex post reasoning at each node. Since each part lacks access to the other's mental states and there is no binding commitment, the first part has no rational basis for expecting the second part to behave differently from itself. Consequently, treating the agent as partitioned relocates the conflict rather than dissolving it. The real issue, then, is not imperfect recall per se, but the absence of a credible commitment technology that could bridge the diachronic gap. Without such technology, the ex ante planner has no authority over the ex post parts, and the paradox remains intact.
