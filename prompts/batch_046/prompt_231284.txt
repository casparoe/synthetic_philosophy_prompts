I'm auditing a philosophy of mind seminar that just covered epiphenomenalism, and I've become fixated on a second-order wrinkle that standard summaries tend to skip. The first-order problem is familiar: if phenomenal properties lack causal efficacy, then my judgment "I am now in pain" is produced entirely by neural machinery, not by the pain itself, which seems to undermine first-person authority. But there's a reflexive layer that rarely gets aired: the judgment "phenomenal properties are causally inert" is itself, on the epiphenomenalist's own account, physically caused without any contribution from phenomenal consciousness. So the theory's own central articulation is causally orphaned in exactly the same manner it ascribes to ordinary phenomenal judgments. I want a poem that holds both levels in suspension without resolving them.

Write a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with the ABA rhyme scheme — in the voice of a phenomenal judgment attempting to trace its own causal lineage. Use two refrains. The first refrain (line 1, then lines 6, 12, and 18) should state the physical-causal claim: that the brain's processes generate the report without the feeling's participation. The second refrain (line 3, then lines 9, 15, and 19) should be the judgment's claim either to self-knowledge or to the causal inertness of experience. The formal architecture should do philosophical work: for eighteen lines the two refrains circle each other without touching, mirroring how the physical account and the phenomenal attribution keep passing without causal contact; then, in the final quatrain, they must sit side by side (lines 18 and 19) in a forced proximity that enacts the stalemate rather than solving it. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with the paradox of phenomenal judgment recognizes both the first-order and this second-order, self-undermining formulation, but it should not name-check philosophers, cite texts, or include bibliographic references. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
