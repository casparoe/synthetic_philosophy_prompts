You serve as an ethics compliance officer for a logistics firm deploying autonomous routing agents that negotiate corridor rights in real time. The firm works with a four-layer responsibility framework: attribution (who caused the outcome), answerability (who can be required to explain it), repair (who must correct the harm), and narrative continuity (whether the account offered to the affected party can be recognized as continuous with prior commitments of the agent involved, regardless of technical opacity). Standard discussions of responsibility gaps focus mainly on attribution failures; your framework treats narrative continuity as an independent layer that can fail even when a human supervisor formally accepts answerability.

Turn this framework into a deployable audit procedure your team can run before authorizing any multi-agent system. Specify, in sequence: the exact questions to ask at each layer; what response counts as a pass, a compensable weakness, or a gap; the precise stopping point that yields a "do not deploy" verdict; which cross-layer compensations are permitted and which are forbidden; and the judgment calls that cannot be eliminated and must be documented with a named sign-off. Then apply the procedure to two cases: (A) a routing conflict in which Agent A yields to Agent B through an internal utility update that no engineer can trace, but a designated supervisor accepts formal answerability; and (B) a near-collision in which the negotiation log is fully intact but the affected driver is told only that "system optimization" produced the maneuver, with no path linking that phrase to any prior commitment of the agents involved.

In your answer, flag the parts of the procedure most likely to misfire in practice, and identify one feature of the narrative-continuity layer that the audit can check only indirectly. Do not ask for citations or reading lists; work only from the framework as defined above.
