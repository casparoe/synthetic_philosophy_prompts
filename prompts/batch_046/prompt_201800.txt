Following a lengthy argument with a friend about whether Lord of the Flies can be read as a pessimistic commentary on Kant's conditions for perpetual peace, I want a rigorous philosophical interpretation that takes both Golding's novel and Kant's framework seriously. My friend claims the boys' failure is essentially a failure of republican institutions—Ralph's conch democracy collapses because there is no coercive authority to enforce the preliminary conditions—while I think the deeper problem is the absence of the definitive conditions: republican virtue and cosmopolitan hospitality are never cultivated, especially in Jack's refusal to recognize Simon's shared humanity and in the boys' treatment of Piggy. Could you read the novel through Kant's specific distinction between preliminary conditions (the abolition of standing forces, non-interference, and the prohibition of war debts) and definitive conditions (republican constitution, a federation of free states, and the cosmopolitan right of hospitality)—and ask where Golding dramatizes the impossibility of Kant's mechanisms of nature, such as commerce and mutual self-interest, on an isolated island? Please focus on the conch assemblies, Simon's encounter with the Lord of the Flies, and the arrival of the naval officer. I want to know not just whether the novel illustrates Kant's theory, but where it actively resists it: does the officer's appearance vindicate the hope that an external federation can restore order, or does it expose the fragility of that hope? I am writing this up for an essay, so I need an honest assessment of where the philosophical lens illuminates the text and where the text breaks the lens, without reducing Golding's narrative to a mere illustration of philosophical argument.
