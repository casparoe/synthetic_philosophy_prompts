I have recently begun volunteering with a program that teaches practical language skills to immigrants who have just arrived in my city, and the experience has made me wonder whether people who speak entirely different languages can ever genuinely share the same concepts. A friend who recently completed a seminar in philosophy of mind summarized Quine's well-known argument about radical translation for me. He explained that in the gavagai scenario, a field linguist hears a native speaker utter the word as a rabbit runs by, yet there is no objective fact of the matter whether it denotes a whole rabbit, a brief temporal slice of a rabbit's existence, or a part of a rabbit not separately identifiable from the body, because every observable behavior is equally compatible with each interpretation. Since the speaker's mental states are inaccessible, Quine holds that reference is not discovered inside the speaker's mind but is instead fixed only by the analytical hypotheses—the simplifying assumptions—that the linguist builds into her translation manual. My friend then drew a stronger conclusion: he said Quine therefore denies that human beings possess mental representations with any determinate content at all, and that the practical upshot is that cross-linguistic understanding is fundamentally impossible. On this view, any conversation between speakers of different languages that appears successful is really just an illusion maintained by coincident behavioral patterns, not by shared thoughts or genuinely common meanings.

The summary feels mostly right about the rabbit example and about Quine's skepticism toward mentalistic explanations of reference, but I suspect it overreaches in its conclusions about mental representation and about the impossibility of communication. Could you audit it for fairness? Please indicate which parts accurately capture Quine's position, which parts conflate indeterminacy of translation with the claim that translation is impossible or arbitrary, and whether the conclusion that cross-linguistic understanding is illusory actually follows from Quine's premises. I would particularly like to know whether Quine intended his thesis as a skeptical crisis that undermines everyday communication, or rather as a structural observation about translation manuals that leaves ordinary linguistic success intact.
