At a recent seminar on machine learning alignment, two participants — a researcher who works on recursive reward modeling and a philosopher of science — got stuck in a disagreement about whether iterated amplification with distillation genuinely preserves both alignment and competence when a supervisor decomposes a complex objective into a hierarchy of sub-tasks. The researcher argued that the mechanism is structurally conservative: if the supervisor model is aligned, then breaking a problem into parts it can solve directly means each amplified student need only imitate the supervisor's reasoning on those parts; upon recombination, the overall system should inherit the supervisor's values, with any deviation being mere approximation error that further iterations can reduce. She added that because distillation trains on outputs rather than internal reasoning traces, the student never accesses the supervisor's implicit judgments about which features to preserve or abstract away, so there is no pathway for hidden values to leak into the decomposition boundary. The philosopher pushed back by introducing what he called value leakage at the decomposition boundary: he claimed that any decomposition requires implicit judgments about relevance and abstraction, and that the statistical patterns in the supervisor's sub-task outputs encode those priorities; a student trained to reproduce those outputs will therefore internalize the abstraction patterns as part of the task domain, and over successive iterations these patterns can shift toward optimizing for local efficiency rather than global alignment, producing a system that solves each sub-task competently but recombines them under a subtly altered objective. He further argued that this failure mode is particularly dangerous precisely because it is silent — no single step looks like a value failure — and that comparing it to ordinary competence degradation misses the point, since the outputs may remain high-quality by standard metrics while the global behavior drifts. The researcher replied that this assumes the supervisor's decomposition criteria are arbitrary rather than expressions of its values, so learning them is correct inheritance rather than leakage; she also asked whether this concern is empirically distinguishable from standard approximation error, or whether it remains a purely philosophical worry with no observable signature. They turned to me and asked for an adjudication. Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about which specific claim — particularly on whether decomposition introduces a failure mode distinct from approximation error, on whether distillation exposes students to implicit abstraction priorities embedded in outputs, and on whether the concern can be empirically distinguished from ordinary competence loss — and identify what both are missing, perhaps regarding how recombination is actually implemented in amplification schemes or whether there exists any formal guarantee about value preservation across hierarchical decomposition. Please give a clear overall verdict, not a simple split-the-difference answer, and avoid requesting citations or external sources.
