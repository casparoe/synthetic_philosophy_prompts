Having spent thirty years in condensed-matter physics and turned to philosophy only after retirement, I have become preoccupied with the backward-induction resolution of the centipede game and whether its logic extends, without distortion, to actual scientific practice. I have constructed the following argument, which seems to show that the induction undermines the rationality of sustained collaboration itself, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) In the canonical centipede game, two agents alternate over finitely many nodes; at each node the active agent may take a modest immediate payoff or continue, with mutual continuation yielding a larger shared payoff at the next node. Under common knowledge of rationality, backward induction forces both to take at the first node.

(2) Consider a multi-stage scientific collaboration—say, an international consortium maintaining an open-data repository across successive funding cycles—structured as a sequence of disclosure decisions. At each stage, a participant can defect unilaterally by withholding or misrepresenting data for local credit (a modest immediate payoff), or continue cooperating, which yields a larger collective payoff at the subsequent stage (enhanced reputation, cumulative discovery).

(3) The formal structure is identical: a long but finite horizon, observable actions, escalating joint payoff, and an ever-present unilateral defection option that dominates the local cooperative payoff.

(4) Therefore, if the agents possess common knowledge of each other's rationality, backward induction applies with full force, predicting immediate defection at the first funding cycle or publication milestone.

(5) Yet sustained, rational collaborations of precisely this form are commonplace in science, extending across dozens of stages without universal defection.

(6) One might object that the horizon is not genuinely known to be finite, or that agents lack common knowledge of rationality. But if either condition fails, then backward induction is blocked not by a flaw in its logic but by the absence of its premises; consequently, it applies only to idealized abstract games and never to any real-world sequential interaction, which renders the theory either false or vacuously true.

(7) Hence, either backward induction proves that all sustained scientific cooperation is irrational, or there is a hidden structural disanalogy between scientific collaboration and the centipede game that saves cooperation but only at the cost of saving the induction.

(8) I cannot locate the disanalogy. Is it in the payoff structure, in the observational conditions, in the common-knowledge assumption, or in the very framing of scientific cooperation as a sequential game? Which premise should I reject, and what does that rejection commit me to?
