Here are my verdicts on killing in war across different combinations of cause, consent, belief, and coercion, and I want to know whether any defensible version of the moral equality of combatants can accommodate them, or whether I must abandon that equality to preserve the pattern.

Case 1: A volunteer soldier fighting for an obviously unjust war of territorial expansion—no credible defensive claim—shoots and kills an enemy soldier who is defending his own country. I think this killing is wrong, but I do not think it is the moral equivalent of murdering a civilian; there is a kind of mutual tragedy in combat, yet the aggressor’s act remains seriously wrongful.

Case 2: A draftee in that same unjust war, who sincerely believes the state propaganda that his nation was attacked and is now defending itself, kills an enemy soldier in battle. I think this is significantly less wrong than Case 1—perhaps excused almost entirely—despite the fact that he contributes to the very same unjust cause.

Case 3: A soldier fighting for an objectively just cause—unprovoked aggression being repelled—kills an enemy soldier in the course of defense. I think this is fully permissible, and perhaps required if necessary to stop the aggression.

Case 4: A mercenary with no ideological commitment fights for the unjust side purely for pay. He takes no pleasure in killing and treats prisoners humanely, but he kills an enemy soldier in battle. I think this is worse than Case 2 and roughly on a par with Case 1, perhaps slightly worse because he lacks even the false moral belief that might partly excuse the volunteer.

Case 5: A soldier who knows his cause is unjust—he understands the legal and moral facts, recognizes the aggression, and is not deceived—but continues fighting because he will be executed for desertion. He kills an enemy soldier. I think this is almost as bad as Case 1, but I feel a slight mitigation relative to the volunteer, because coercion is present; yet it is far less mitigated than Case 2, because he lacks false belief.

Case 6: A civilian in an occupied territory, not part of any organized force and wearing no uniform, picks up a rifle and kills an invading soldier in direct defense of her home. I think this is permissible. Crucially, I think it is not morally equivalent to the unjust soldier’s killing in Case 1, even though both involve killing an enemy combatant.

My tension lies here. I want Cases 1, 4, and 5 to be wrong and Case 3 to be right, which already strains strict moral equality. At the same time, I want Case 2 to be largely excused and Case 6 to be permitted despite lacking institutional combatant status. This pushes me toward a view in which permissibility depends on the justice of the cause combined with the agent’s epistemic position and coercive circumstances, not on uniform combatant status alone.

But the ordering is hard to stabilize. I think Case 4 is worse than Case 5, because the mercenary chooses profit over conscience while the coerced soldier acts under duress. Yet I also think Case 1 is somewhat mitigated by false belief compared to Case 5, where belief is true but coercion is present. So my implicit ranking runs: Case 2 (best), Case 3 (rightful), Case 5 (bad but somewhat mitigated), Case 1 (bad with modest mitigation), Case 4 (worst), with Case 6 sitting outside the ranking because she is not a combatant at all but is still permitted.

Is there a principle—perhaps one that combines liability to defensive harm, excuse conditions, and institutional role—that delivers this exact ordering? Or does saving every verdict require such complexity that I should drop one? In particular, must I reject the moral equality of combatants entirely, or can I retain a weakened version that applies only when epistemic and coercive conditions are equal?

Finally, what does the principle you defend predict for a case I have not listed: a soldier fighting for an objectively just cause who is genuinely convinced—through no fault of his own—that his cause is unjust, and who therefore hesitates to kill an enemy who is in fact an aggressor? Does his false belief diminish his right to defend, or does the objective justice of his cause preserve full permissibility despite his mistaken conscience?
