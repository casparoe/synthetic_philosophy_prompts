I am taking a seminar in philosophy of biology, and my study partner has been remarkably consistent in her judgments about which entities count as biological species, though I suspect that more than one principled rule could generate exactly the same pattern of verdicts. She holds that lions and tigers are distinct species despite occasional captive hybrids; that the salamanders forming the classic ring around California's Central Valley constitute a single species even where the northern and southern ends overlap without interbreeding; that domestic dogs and gray wolves belong to one species; that a lichen is not itself a species, though its fungal partner is; that bacteria engaging in widespread horizontal gene transfer do not form true species in the relevant sense; and that a synthetic minimal bacterial cell engineered with a reduced genome—viable and replicating, but lacking any evolutionary ancestry—is not a species.

We are now discussing a hypothetical but biologically realistic case: a fertile, self-sustaining hybrid population that arose approximately two hundred generations ago from two recognized bird species on an isolated island, interbreeds freely within itself, is reproductively isolated from both parental populations through behavioral and temporal barriers, occupies a distinct ecological niche, yet possesses a genome that is essentially an even mixture of the two parent lineages and has developed no evolutionary innovations beyond that hybrid combination. I want to predict her verdict on whether this island population counts as a new species. Is she implicitly running a reproductive-isolation criterion modified for allopatry and potential gene flow, an evolutionary-lineage criterion that requires an independent evolutionary fate and historical continuity, or perhaps a functional-ecological criterion that weights niche occupation and reproductive coherence differently? Which of her earlier judgments best support each of these readings, what should I expect her to say about the hybrid population, and what further case—perhaps involving a chronospecies boundary in the fossil record or a long-lived clonal plant lineage—would best tell the competing rules apart?
