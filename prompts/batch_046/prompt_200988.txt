Here is the question I gave them:

Consider a modified veil of ignorance in which the parties do not know whether they will be born into the present generation or into a future generation that inherits whatever ecological and institutional conditions we leave behind. Applying Rawls's difference principle, could a society justify economic arrangements that maximize present growth but impose long-term depletion costs? In your answer, address whether the original position can be extended across generations without violating Rawls's assumption that justice is a matter of cooperation among contemporaries for mutual advantage, and explain how the maximin rule applies to this intergenerational case.

Here is my friend's answer:

The veil of ignorance requires that people choose principles of justice without knowing their social position, natural talents, or particular values. From behind this veil, rational parties would adopt the difference principle, which permits economic inequalities only when they improve the situation of the least advantaged members of society. If we extend the veil so that people also do not know which generation they belong to, then any policy that risks severe resource depletion becomes difficult to justify, because the worst-case scenario is being born into the depleted future. Rawls argued in Justice as Fairness that the original position applies only among people living at the same time, since mutual cooperation requires contemporaries. Therefore, extending the veil across generations violates the basic structure of his theory and cannot be done consistently. Nevertheless, applying maximin reasoning to the intergenerational case means we should prefer a steady-state economy with zero growth, since that eliminates the risk of being the worst-off future generation. This shows that high-growth policies fail the difference principle once generational position is unknown.
