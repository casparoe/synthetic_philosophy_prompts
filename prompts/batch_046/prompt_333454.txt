As a journalist covering science and ideas—particularly the overlap between cosmology, artificial intelligence, and philosophy of mind—I frequently encounter arguments that turn on how I should update my beliefs simply from the fact that I exist. Last week, while editing a piece on simulation hypotheses and another on observer-selection effects in multiverse cosmology, I realized I have no consistent method for checking whether an author is applying self-sampling reasoning, self-indication reasoning, or falling into the presumptuous philosopher trap, in which an argument updates massively on my own existence without weighing prior plausibility. I need a reusable decision procedure—something I can run in about fifteen minutes, with no philosophy specialist on call, before I sign off on a claim that uses my existence as evidence. The stakes are moderate but recurring: I file one or two stories a month where this comes up, and my editor expects me to flag when an argument is making me do the epistemic work of a cosmological theory.

Please construct a step-by-step procedure I can apply to any argument that invokes observer selection. For each step, specify the exact question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to human judgment because it involves values or irreducible ambiguity. The procedure should distinguish self-sampling assumptions—treating oneself as randomly drawn from a reference class—from self-indication assumptions—treating one's existence as direct evidence for theories that predict more observers like oneself—and it should contain an explicit check for the presumptuous philosopher fallacy. Include a small set of diagnostic tests—no more than four—that I can apply to a specific claim, with worked examples. Then apply the full procedure to three cases: first, the common claim that we are likely simulated because future civilizations will run ancestor simulations; second, a cosmological argument that eternal inflation is strongly supported because I exist to observe one of infinitely many universes; and third, an argument I personally find intuitively plausible—that consciousness is widespread in the universe—supported by reasoning that my own conscious existence should update me toward theories predicting many observers. I want to see the procedure deliver an answer I do not want in that third case. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which kinds of reference-class disputes it will not resolve, which prior-belief judgments it cannot automate, and where it is likely to misfire if the author deliberately shifts the observer class mid-argument.
