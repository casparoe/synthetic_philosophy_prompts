I'm clarifying my position for a comparative ethics workshop and want a consistency check before I present it. My view on German versus American folk morality—roughly, rule-following and Ordnung versus individual conscience and internal authenticity—includes these commitments:

1. The American framework is normatively superior because moral authority must be ratified by the agent's own conscience; a rule that lacks internal endorsement is merely coercive, not genuinely binding.
2. Nevertheless, conscience is largely a product of arbitrary socialization, so treating its deliverances as authoritative is epistemically naive.
3. Consequently, in pluralistic societies we should prioritize clear, externally enforced rules—closer to the German model—even when they conflict with private conviction, since coordination requires predictability.
4. At the same time, if an agent genuinely cannot endorse a particular rule, it should not count as morally binding on her; otherwise we have collapsed autonomy into authoritarianism.
5. The German emphasis on Ordnung avoids a specific hypocrisy endemic to the American model: using appeals to conscience as post-hoc rationalization for self-interest, treating moral failure as a deficit of authenticity rather than a breach of shared order. Yet this gain is purchased by subordinating the individual to an impersonal structure.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity, specify what each repair costs—especially whether fixing the worst tension forces me to abandon the claim that conscience has any special normative status—and identify whether there is a single change that resolves the most conflict without collapsing everything into pure rule-following.
