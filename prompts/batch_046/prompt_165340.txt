In recent debates about generative AI and labor, commentators often invoke Marx’s framework of machinery: they treat a trained model running inference as the modern equivalent of factory machinery—dead labor dominating living labor—and claim the dynamics Marx described (displacement of workers, extraction of surplus value, downward pressure on the rate of profit) apply more or less unchanged. The analogy is used to argue that AI automation will necessarily deepen exploitation in the same structural way as nineteenth-century mechanization.

How far does this analogy actually hold? Then separate the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from those that threaten the intended conclusion. In Marx, machinery is fixed capital that transfers value gradually through physical wear and depreciation; an AI model reproduces its outputs at near-zero marginal cost and depreciates mainly through obsolescence rather than use. Marx’s worker operates the machine within a wage relation and produces commodities for sale, but here the user is not employed by the cluster, the product is often information rather than a commodity produced for exchange, and surplus extraction—if it occurs—flows through platform rent or data extraction rather than through the direct appropriation of surplus labor-time in production. Also consider whether the absence of “living labor” inside the model breaks the analogy or merely relocates it: does exploitation shift to the data laborers and low-wage annotators who produced the training set, or to the users whose prompts generate further value?
