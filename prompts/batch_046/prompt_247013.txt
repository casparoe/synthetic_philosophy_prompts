Here is an argument I have been turning over on my own, and I keep feeling that it must contain a hidden slip, yet I cannot name it. (1) If I say I want to eat the whole cake but also say I want to be healthy, we usually treat the second statement as closer to my "real" preference, because it is what I would want if I were not acting on impulse and were fully consistent with my other values. (2) So to discover what anyone genuinely prefers, we should not ask what they actually choose, but what they would choose after removing confusion, bias, and internal contradiction — call this their idealized preference. (3) Now apply this not just to one person, but to all of humanity at once: the correct guide to collective action is not the sum of our actual desires, but the result of every individual undergoing this same cleaning process and then reaching agreement with everyone else who has done the same. (4) But notice that my cleaned-up self is not isolated: what I would want depends partly on what I know about others, and in particular on what their cleaned-up selves want, since cooperation and fairness only make sense relative to them. (5) This means each person's idealized preference is a function of everyone else's idealized preference, so we are not computing separate answers and adding them up; we are solving one large circular system in which every variable depends on every other. (6) If that system has exactly one consistent solution, then there is indeed a single "true" collective will, and we should follow it. (7) But if the system has two or more consistent solutions — say, one where everyone values privacy above all else and one where everyone values transparency — then there is nothing inside the system itself that picks between them, because in each case every person's preferences are perfectly rational and perfectly aligned with everyone else's. (8) Therefore either there is no unique collective will, in which case the whole idea of discovering "what humanity really wants" collapses into arbitrary selection, or we must introduce an outside rule to break the tie, in which case we are no longer deriving values from idealized preference but imposing them. (9) Yet it still feels right that my desire for cake should not outweigh my desire for health just because the first one is louder in the moment. So the argument seems to prove that the concept of a coherent, extrapolated collective will is either empty or authoritarian, which I do not want to believe. Where does the reasoning go wrong? I would prefer to save (2), since the idea that impulse should not rule seems sound, and I am also reluctant to give up (5), because the interdependence looks unavoidable once you take other people seriously. If I must choose, I would rather reject (7) or (8), but I need to understand what rejecting them actually commits me to — does it require me to deny that multiple equilibria are possible, or to accept that an external principle is legitimate? Please walk through the steps, identify the one that fails, explain why it looked plausible, and tell me exactly what I pay for fixing it.
