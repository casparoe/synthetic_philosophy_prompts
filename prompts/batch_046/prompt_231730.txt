The frame problem is conventionally presented as the difficulty of specifying, for an agent that updates its beliefs after acting, which of its prior beliefs remain true without having to enumerate all of them. In practice, however, the difficulty has fractured into at least three overlapping but conceptually distinct failures: a representational failure (how formal systems encode state-change rules without combinatorial explosion in large knowledge bases), a computational failure (whether relevance can be determined tractably in high-dimensional or continuous state spaces), and a normative failure (how an agent ought to decide which changes matter given its goals, values, and the risk of hidden dependencies that do not register in its explicit ontology). These are not merely technical variants; they pull in different methodological directions—efficient encoding tends to favor sparse symbolic representations, tractability favors approximate statistical or heuristic methods, and normative adequacy requires explicit reasoning about what should count as relevant.
