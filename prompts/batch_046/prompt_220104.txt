First, I accept something close to Margalit's view that there is a thick, non-derivative duty to remember wrongs committed against those who can no longer speak for themselves—the dead, the erased, the disappeared—and that this duty is not extinguished by the passage of time or waived by the preferences of survivors who happen to remain. Second, I follow Rieff in holding that active forgetting is not necessarily denial or cowardice; in some post-atrocity contexts it is a genuine moral labor, and a community may have collective reason to let certain details fall out of active circulation in order to rebuild civic trust. Third, I maintain an asymmetry about authorization: a living victim may forgive and legitimately ask that her specific wrong not be constantly reactivated in public discourse, but no third party—whether a survivor organization, the state, or a later generation—may unilaterally forget on behalf of those who are absent or dead. Fifth, I also hold that if memory is curated, filtered, or managed for social or political effect, it ceases to be genuine remembrance and becomes constructed narrative, which betrays the moral point of honoring wrongs, which is to register the reality of suffering rather than to shape it.
