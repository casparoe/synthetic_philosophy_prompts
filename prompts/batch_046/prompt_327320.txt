I am a high school senior organizing a one-hour philosophy workshop for about twelve peers, ages sixteen to eighteen, in a classroom with movable desks. I want a structured role-play that makes participants feel, through their own choices, the conflict between a collective duty to remember and the moral case for forgetting—specifically the anti-reductionist claim, often discussed in connection with Margalit and Rieff, that remembering is not merely storing information but maintaining an ongoing social relationship, so that forgetting can be an active restructuring of moral community rather than a passive absence. To keep the exercise from becoming a generic debate about monuments, I want to inject a somewhat niche framing: the group serves as a curatorial council for a dissolved digital community whose members have all vanished, and they must design an algorithmic decay schedule for the remaining traces—assigning different rates and modes of erasure, rewriting, or sealing—where each rule embodies a theory about what the living owe the dead versus what they owe themselves. Roles should include an archivist who defends preservation as testimony, a survivor who argues that active erasure is necessary to prevent the dead from colonizing present moral space, and a consistency-checker who applies identical decay rules across disparate cases. The rules and stakes must be arranged so that the group hits a coordination failure or paradox—perhaps discovering that applying their own principles consistently requires them to erase the very testimony they just defended—before anyone names the philosophical positions. Please design the full activity: role cards with private incentives, a non-monetary payoff or scoring mechanism, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the specific mechanism that forces the paradox to emerge from action rather than lecture. Include a debrief that maps our concrete decay decisions onto reductionist versus anti-reductionist theories of memory, states clearly what participants should come away seeing about forgetting as moral practice, predicts what will go wrong the first time with a concrete fix, and is frank about which phenomenological feature of being-forgotten—such as the subjective experience of the erased—this simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; the design should be fully runnable without external sources.
