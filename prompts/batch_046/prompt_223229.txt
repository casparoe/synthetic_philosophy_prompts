I am in the middle of drafting a novel about a retired naturalist who becomes obsessed with what he calls the two great inversions of modern thought, and last evening a well-read friend tried to lay out the parallel for me over dinner. She put it this way: Darwin demonstrated that nature produces structures of astonishing functional sophistication—eyes, echolocation, social instincts—through a mechanism that possesses no foresight, no plan, and no comprehension of the ends it achieves; it is, in her phrase, pure competence without comprehension operating on a cosmic scale. Turing, she continued, performed the symmetrical move: rather than asking whether a machine harbors an inner mind or genuine understanding, he proposed that we judge artificial intelligence solely by its external behavior, thereby establishing that machine competence can be complete while cognition is entirely absent. From these two moves, she drew a sweeping conclusion: both thinkers dismantled the same ancient illusion, that competence must be explained by an underlying comprehension, and together they leave us with the stark principle that wherever we observe sophisticated behavior—whether in a bird’s wing or in a chess program—we are never justified in inferring any inner life or understanding behind it.

Something about this account feels both luminous and suspiciously neat, but my own reading is too patchy to locate the distortion. I want to know whether this is a fair rendering of the two arguments, or whether it smuggles in conflations that a careful reader should resist. Specifically: does Darwin’s theory actually deny comprehension to biological agents themselves, or does it only deny that nature as a whole designs with comprehension? Does Turing’s behavioral test amount to a proof that machines lack understanding, or does it merely suspend the question in a way that leaves cognition untouched? And does the final conclusion—that competence never licenses an inference to comprehension—follow legitimately from pairing an evolutionary mechanism with an epistemological criterion, or does it confuse how complexity originates with how we ought to recognize it? If parts of the summary are standard simplifications rather than errors, I would like those separated out as well.

Please treat this not as an exam question but as an accuracy audit I can use: tell me which elements are sound, which are overstatements or misrepresentations of Darwin’s or Turing’s actual positions, and whether the conclusion drawn from them survives once the distortions are corrected.
