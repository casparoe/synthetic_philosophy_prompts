I'm building a classification instrument for a rationalist forum thread on the procreation asymmetry — specifically, to sort people by what they actually think rather than by whether they call themselves "Benatarian" or "anti-Benatarian." I want a decision tree with six to eight leaves, but not one built solely around the standard "harm of existence versus benefit of non-existence" axis. Instead, the tree should branch on three pressure points that I think cut deeper: (1) whether the asymmetry survives the non-identity problem when applied specifically to happy lives (not just to suffering ones); (2) whether the asymmetry extends, collapses, or inverts when we replace evolved biological agents with artificial minds that have designed well-being functions; and (3) whether adopting a genuinely symmetric person-affecting restriction dissolves the asymmetry or merely relocates it inside a different part of the theory.

Each internal node must be phrased as a concrete claim that someone could agree or disagree with without already knowing which leaf it leads to. The leaves should include at least: an actualist denial of the asymmetry; an impersonal-value view that preserves it; a risk-averse symmetry view that denies obligations on both sides; a view on which the asymmetry collapses for engineered minds; and one deliberately unnamed branch representing an unexplored or unstable combination of answers. For each named leaf, include a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment — the claim its defenders have to swallow.

After mapping the tree, diagnose every unoccupied combination: is it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Also flag any pair of leaves that the tree genuinely cannot separate — for instance, if two positions share all three branching answers but differ only in underlying motivation or metaethical framing. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for specific citations; this should be answerable from conceptual reasoning alone.

If this task rests on a confused premise — for example, if the three pressure points don't actually partition the logical space, if the unnamed branch is incoherent rather than unexplored, or if the tree forces a separation that doesn't exist — say so plainly instead of constructing a fake taxonomy. If you think one of the named positions is actually empty, say that too.

Format the response in four named sections, in this order: first, "Branching nodes and flow" (the tree structure with the split-question at each node, roughly 300 words); second, "The leaves" (named positions, the unnamed branch, and each awkward commitment, roughly 250 words); third, "Unoccupied branches and inseparable pairs" (diagnosis of empty combinations and flagged indistinguishability, roughly 200 words); fourth, "Notes on construction and pushback" (where you assess whether the framing works and note any failures of the task, roughly 150 words). Write for an audience that has read the standard arguments but is tired of rehashing them in the usual grooves.
