For a seminar of ten writers and philosophers—ninety minutes, one circular table—design an activity that makes the group feel the fracture in the principle of indifference when applied to continuous spaces, specifically through Bertrand’s chord paradox. Set up three betting stations, each claiming to generate a “random chord” in a circle by a different physical rule: selecting two points on the circumference, selecting a random radius and then a random point along it, and selecting a random interior point as the chord’s midpoint. Give each station identical token stakes and a payoff structure that rewards predictions about whether the chord exceeds the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, but forbid cross-station comparison until a final reveal round. The rules and incentives must be arranged so that participants initially treat the three methods as equivalent expressions of a single shared randomness, then discover—through accumulated results—that they converge on probabilities near one-third, one-half, and one-fourth. State clearly what participants should leave feeling about the limits of epistemic humility when no coordinate system is canonical.
