Question: Compare Harari's account in Sapiens, Diamond's argument in Guns, Germs, and Steel, and Graeber and Wengrow's analysis in The Dawn of Everything regarding the emergence of large-scale human cooperation. Your answer should address: (a) how each author explains the transition from small foraging bands to societies numbering in the thousands or more; (b) the role they assign to material conditions—geography, agriculture, technology—relative to collective belief, narrative, or ritual practice; and (c) whether they treat hierarchy and inequality as inevitable correlates of scale. In your conclusion, evaluate whether these three frameworks are fundamentally incompatible or can be reconciled through a distinction between integrative and modular scaling.

Student's answer:
Harari locates the decisive shift in the Cognitive Revolution, when shared fictions—religions, nations, legal codes—allowed strangers to cooperate. For Harari, material conditions are secondary: they constrain but do not determine social form, because any material base can be interpreted through an imagined order. Diamond offers a stark contrast. His geographic determinism holds that Eurasia's east-west axis and its suite of domesticable plants and animals made large, complex societies inevitable there, while other continents lacked the biological prerequisites. Culture, in Diamond's view, plays almost no independent causal role. Graeber and Wengrow reject both linear narratives. They argue that societies such as Çatalhöyük and the early Mesopotamian urban centres of Uruk and Eridu were essentially egalitarian, lacking palaces, temples, or permanent elites, and that archaeological evidence shows people moved seasonally between hierarchical and egalitarian arrangements. This proves that scale and agriculture do not mechanically produce hierarchy; instead, conscious political experimentation drives social form. Consequently, Harari's narrative determinism and Diamond's geographic determinism are both wrong, because Graeber and Wengrow show that material conditions and shared myths are always subordinate to deliberate collective choice.
