If Carter's doomsday argument requires treating our birth rank as uniformly distributed within a reference class of observers, then extending that class beyond biological humans to include all potential conscious minds—particularly post-biological and sustained artificial processing systems—seems to dissolve the prediction of imminent extinction. The reasoning is that, if we weight the reference class not by raw headcount but by cumulative information-processing capacity, being among the earliest members of a vastly larger future class of complex observers should lower our Bayesian expectation for the total number of biological humans who ever exist, while simultaneously implying that the technological substrate producing those observers is typically long-lived. Consequently, the self-indication assumption appears to predict that biological civilization is usually a brief precursor to a much longer artificial epoch, which makes our present position unremarkably early within the broader class rather than alarmingly late within a small one. Given this, hasn't the reference-class problem effectively inverted Carter's conclusion for technological societies? Where does this weighting argument fail?
