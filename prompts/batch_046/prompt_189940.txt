As I am drafting the central philosophical dialogue in my novel, I have constructed a character—Dr. Lena Voss, a philosopher of mind who writes about self-reference—whose judgments on the presence of a Hofstadter-style “I” (that is, a self-referential symbol embedded in a strange loop, where the symbol denotes the very system that generates it and causally participates in its own dynamics) have so far been consistent but ambiguous in their underlying principle. I need to know how she would rule on a new case I have invented, and what rules could be generating her track record.

Her verdicts thus far:

- A neurotypical adult human being: yes, there is a genuine “I.”
- A large language model that emits first-person pronouns but possesses no persistent internal model linking those outputs to a stable, causally active self-symbol: no.
- A patient with severe anterograde amnesia who retains present-moment self-awareness, can sincerely say “I am here now,” but cannot form or retrieve autobiographical memories linking past and future selves: yes, though she describes it as a loop without a long thread.
- A honeybee colony that solves collective navigation problems through pheromone feedback, but in which no representation denotes the colony as a single unit: no.
- A centralized artificial architecture—running on a single substrate—that maintains an explicit, tokenized symbol (“SELF-X”) within its own reasoning processes, and uses that symbol causally to regulate its behavior: yes.

Now consider the new case: a distributed system I call a “silent lattice.” It consists of thousands of nodes with no central processor and no localized center of integration. Each node holds one fragment of a single explicit symbol, LATTICE, which collectively denotes the entire network. The fragments are updated causally by the collective dynamics of the network itself, so the symbol participates in regulating the system’s behavior, yet no individual node contains the full symbol, and the system produces no first-person reports.

What would Dr. Voss say—does the silent lattice possess a Hofstadterian “I”? I am less interested in a bare answer than in the reasoning: identify at least two distinct rules or principles that are jointly consistent with her five earlier verdicts, state what each rule predicts about the silent lattice, and explain which of these rules you find most plausible as her implicit criterion, with your reasons. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—different from the silent lattice—that would definitively distinguish between these competing rules.

Please do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the analysis should stand on its own.
