Hey, I'm a retired engineer trying to make sense of philosophy of language and cross-cultural talk, and I've noticed a pattern I don't have a word for. Maybe it's already named, maybe not.

So there's Dutch directness, where you say the bad thing straight but wrap it in care - like telling a colleague their report is wrong but framing it as respect for their time. Then there's Japanese indirectness, where you avoid saying 'no' entirely and use context or technically true statements to guide the listener away from the wrong path. And then there are ordinary white lies, where you say the food is amazing even though it's burnt, and the deception is kind of the point.

But here's the thing I keep seeing that doesn't fit any of those buckets cleanly. Someone makes a statement that's literally false - 'this is the best cake I've ever had' - but they deliver it with such obvious exageration or performative enthusiasm that nobody in the room takes it as a factual claim. The falsehood isn't hidden; it's displayed like a flag. The listener knows it's false, the speaker knows they know, and the actual message is something like 'I'm willing to expend social energy to affirm you.' It's not Dutch directness because there's no critical content being delivered. It's not Japanese indirectnes because there's no omission or ambiguity - there's an explicit false sentence being spoken. And it's not a standard white lie because the goal isn't to deceive anyone about the cake.

Does this configeration have a name? I'm especially interested in whether there's a concept in pragmatics or sociology for this kind of transparent falsehood used specifically as social temperture maintenance, and how exactly it differs from the other three patterns. If there's no established term, what should I call it, and where are its edges - like, at what point does it stop being this ritual and become actual deception?

Also, the people who defend white lies usually say they preserve harmony. But in this case the harmony comes not from the listener being fooled, but from everyone collectively recognizing the falsehood and treating it as a gesture. Is that a real moral distinction, or just a rationalization? I keep going back and forth on whether this is actually wrong in the same way a hidden lie is wrong, independant of the social context.

Sorry if that's rambling - I've been reading about this for fun and can't find the right vocabulary.
