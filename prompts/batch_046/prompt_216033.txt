Having retired some years ago from a career in experimental physics, I have lately begun attending philosophy-of-biology seminars, and I find myself repeatedly troubled by the casual confidence with which speakers refer to DNA as "the genetic code" or to a genome as "carrying information". Before I read further, I should like to determine whether my own intuitions about these claims can be captured by a precise principle, or whether they are merely inconsistent prejudices. To that end, I offer below my unvarnished verdicts on six concrete cases, not as argued positions but as the reactions of someone trained to distinguish physical causation from symbolic representation.

First, the canonical bacterial gene: a triplet sequence that specifies an amino-acid chain through the ribosome and tRNA machinery. Here I have no doubt; this is genuinely a code, and the sequence bears semantic information about the protein. Second, a synthetic biologist encodes an English sentence into a bespoke DNA strand using an arbitrary symbol-to-base mapping and stores it in a bacterium that never expresses it; recovery requires a human decoder with the key. The DNA functions for me like magnetic tape — it stores data, yet it is not operating as a biological genetic code. Third, a mammalian non-coding region that has been conserved for millions of years yet produces no transcript and has no known regulatory function. I am inclined to deny that it presently carries genetic information, whilst its conservation suggests it may once have done so, or might do so under conditions not yet observed. Fourth, the methylation pattern across a gene promoter, which determines expression without altering the underlying base sequence. This strikes me as genuinely informational — perhaps even part of the code — but it is not information encoded in the DNA sequence itself. Fifth, a salt crystal grown under conditions that produce occasional lattice defects arranged, by design, into a readable binary sequence. I should call that engineered storage and deny that the crystal possesses any natural code, even though the pattern is causally determined by the growth conditions. Sixth, and this is the case to which I keep returning: in DNA origami, a long scaffold strand is folded into a nanoscale shape by hundreds of complementary staple strands, with the scaffold's sequence chosen solely for structural complementarity and not to specify any biological product. Is the scaffold here an instance of genetic information, or has the concept been stretched beyond recognition?

Could you propose one or more principles — stated with sufficient precision that each clause can be matched to the case that motivates it — that capture these six verdicts, or as many as possible? For each principle, please indicate its verdict on two further cases I have not mentioned: a viral genome that hijacks the host ribosome to replicate itself, and an epigenetic mark that is heritable across generations but never inscribed in the DNA sequence. Crucially, please state honestly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without strain, and whether saving them requires sacrificing simplicity. I should also be grateful if the response separates what is broadly settled in the philosophy of biology — for instance, that genetic coding involves a specific decoding mechanism rather than mere correlation — from what remains genuinely contested, such as whether genetic information must be semantic in the philosophical sense or can be purely causal and correlational; please do not present a contested interpretation as though it were the consensus among those who work on the topic.
