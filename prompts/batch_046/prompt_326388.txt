I have recently been caught in a family debate about disaster-price-gouging and whether it is meaningfully different from selling a kidney or buying political influence, and I want to understand my own commitments rather than pick a label. Build a decision tree—seven or eight nodes, concrete cases only, no question that asks whether one agrees with Walzer or Titmuss—that sorts a non-specialist into one of six positions on what should and should not be for sale. The cases must include: selling a kidney with full consent and no economic duress; charging ten dollars for bottled water after a hurricane when supply is genuinely scarce; and vote-selling in a democratic election. Include also a niche case: a government allows people to pay a fee to advance in a refugee-resettlement queue, with the revenue used to fund extra resettlement slots so no one is displaced—ask whether this is permissible, wrong for the same reason as vote-selling, or wrong for a different reason. At each node, ask not just whether the transaction is wrong, but what makes it wrong—intrinsic degradation of the good, unfair distribution of access, contingent coercion, or corruption of motivation—so the tree separates people who reject all markets in a given domain from those who reject them only under conditions of desperation or inequality. End each path with a brief description of the view rather than only its academic name, map the logical space of answer-combinations, and explicitly note which combinations are unoccupied—whether because incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any two positions that these particular cases fail to distinguish, and keep the audience as people reasoning about policy without philosophical training.
