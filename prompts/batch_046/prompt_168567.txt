In a seminar I am co-leading on the philosophy of technology, we have been trying to isolate what John von Neumann actually meant by the survival of a technological lineage—distinct from mere physical persistence—through the lens of his theory of self-replicating automata. To test whether we are tracking his view correctly rather than importing modern intuitions about durability, please evaluate the following four proposed criteria for genuine technological survival: (A) reproductive fidelity of design information across successive generations; (B) functional drift that preserves core organizational logic despite changes in material form; (C) complete independence from any particular physical substrate; and (D) autonomous error-correction that operates without an external designer or repair mechanism.

Rank these four criteria from most essential (assign score 4) to least essential (assign score 1) according to what von Neumann's own framework would support, providing a brief justification for each placement that explicitly connects the criterion to his distinction between the logical architecture of an automaton and its contingent physical embodiment. In addition, identify which one of these four is most commonly conflated with von Neumann's actual position in popular and semi-technical discussions of his work, and explain briefly why treating that criterion as central misrepresents his account of replication as an organizational rather than material achievement.
