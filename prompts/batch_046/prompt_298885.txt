I'm preparing a single 55-minute seminar session for about eighteen philosophy undergraduates on weakness of will and intertemporal choice. We have worked through the familiar accounts of akrasia and hyperbolic discounting, but my students keep treating the agent as a unitary planner who merely miscalculates future rewards. I want them to feel the pressure of a more awkward framing—what we might call distributed hyperbolic discounting—in which weakness of will is not one person failing to stick to a plan, but a sequence of temporally indexed "selves" with only partial authority over one another, so that the present self effectively free-rides on commitments made by past selves. On this view, conventional commitment devices fail not because the agent lacks willpower, but because those devices presuppose a single decision-maker who can bind herself, whereas the actual problem is an intertemporal collective-action problem among partly autonomous stages.
