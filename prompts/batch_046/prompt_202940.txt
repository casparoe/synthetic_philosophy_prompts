Question: In The Descent of Man, Darwin offers evolutionary explanations for both the human moral sense and religious belief. Outline (a) how he derives morality from social instincts, reasoning, and habit; (b) how he distinguishes the origin of belief in spiritual agents from the origin of belief in a single Creator; and (c) whether he treats moral progress as inevitable. In doing so, distinguish Darwin’s own framework from later evolutionary-psychological accounts that treat morality or religion mainly as group-level adaptations.

Student answer:
Darwin argues that the moral sense is not an innate special faculty but develops gradually. Humans share social instincts with other animals, such as the impulse to defend companions. Over generations, these instincts become stronger because tribes with more cooperative members survive and reproduce, so the moral sense functions as a group-selection mechanism. Once humans develop reasoning, they extend sympathy beyond the tribe and form general rules of conduct. Habit then reinforces these rules into a sense of duty, which Darwin calls conscience.

For religion, Darwin says belief in spiritual agencies first arises from reasoning about unseen causes—dreams, shadows, and death make people imagine invisible beings. He treats belief in a single Creator God as essentially the same reasoning applied on a larger scale: people seek an unseen cause for the cosmos itself. Darwin also notes that religion strengthens social cohesion, so it is maintained by group-level selection.

As for progress, Darwin clearly thinks it is inevitable: once reason is present, it will always extend sympathy and improve moral rules, since no rational being can prefer harm to the general good. Social conditions might slow this process, but the direction is fixed and irreversible.
