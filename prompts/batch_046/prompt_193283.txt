I have been trying to work out my own position on the ethical significance of war, largely through Hegel's account in the Philosophy of Right, and I want to check whether the following commitments form a coherent package or whether some of them have to be abandoned.

(1) I accept the claim that war is not merely an evil to be eliminated but serves an ethical function for the state: it interrupts the privatization of civil society, reminds individuals that their particular interests are contingent, and preserves the state's vitality against stagnation.

(2) I take the state's universal will to be the actualization of ethical life, which means that in a conflict of states the individual has a duty to risk life and property for the universal, and that individual rights are subordinate to this universal claim in the context of war.

(3) At the same time, I want to maintain the distinction between limited war and total war: enemies are recognized as states with equal rights, not reduced to criminals or enemies of humanity; therefore there are internal ethical limits on destruction, and prisoners retain a legal status rather than being subject to unconditional annihilation.

(4) I also believe that modern technological and ideological warfare tends necessarily toward the total form, dissolving the recognition of the enemy's ethical standing and mobilizing entire populations in ways that erase the boundary between combatant and civilian.

(5) Nevertheless, I think that even modern conflicts can be justified by the ethical function described in (1), provided the state genuinely faces an existential threat to its ethical order, and I do not think that (4) automatically cancels the justification in (1).

My question is whether this set hangs together. In particular, does my acceptance of (2) commit me to endorsing whatever form of war the state demands, including the total form described in (4), so that (3) becomes an empty aspiration? Or can I consistently hold that the state's right to demand sacrifice is conditional on the preservation of the enemy's ethical recognition, and if so, does that condition undermine (1) by making war's ethical function depend on a form that modern conditions make impossible? Furthermore, is there a hidden tension between (1) — which values war precisely because it exposes the contingency of private existence — and (3) — which imposes stable ethical constraints that seem to protect private existence even in conflict?

Please treat this as an audit rather than a lecture. Order the tensions by severity, identify which apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection, specify for each genuine tension what exactly would have to give, and say what the cheapest revision is — whether abandoning (5), weakening (2), or reconceiving (1) — together with what each revision costs me in terms of the broader Hegelian framework I want to retain.
