I'm working through the philosophy of sleep and consciousness with a study group, and we've been trying to pin down whether dreams have experiential status during the night or only upon waking. I've come to hold a somewhat hybrid position that I think avoids both the standard phenomenological appeal and the usual behaviorist dismissal: dreams are not occurrent phenomenal experiences in real-time during sleep, as Malcolm argued, but they are also not purely post-awakening confabulations in Dennett's stronger cassette sense. Instead, I think dreams possess what I'd call deferred experientiality — their phenomenal texture is genuinely constructed during mnemonic consolidation in REM-related processing, but there is no unified subject undergoing them in the moment; the experience is assembled retroactively yet draws on real neurocognitive content rather than invented narrative. This lets me say dreams are not illusions of consciousness, but also not consciousness occurring during sleep.

What would count against this view? I want more than the standard objections — not just that people report vivid experiences upon waking, so there must have been an experiencer, or that Dennett's cassette model has been undermined by REM replay studies. I want to know which specific findings in sleep neuroscience, say about lucidity, targeted dream incubation, or the absence of prefrontal activation, would actually damage deferred experientiality versus merely complicate it. I also want to know whether there are conceptual problems: does deferred experience collapse into either Malcolm's non-experiential report or Dennett's post-hoc fiction, or does it risk being unfalsifiable? Which intuitions about first-person authority over one's own mental life does it have to deny, and are those denials fatal or merely awkward? Finally, rank the defeaters: what observation or argument would be decisive, what would be merely embarrassing, and is there a qualification I could add that would save the view but render it philosophically hollow?
