I'm preparing notes for a graduate seminar on interpretations of quantum mechanics, and I keep returning to the same stall: the debate over whether the wavefunction is psi-ontic, representing a direct feature of physical reality, or psi-epistemic, representing an agent's partial knowledge about an underlying ontology, has been active for decades. Both sides work with identical equations and the same experimental record—including well-known constraints such as those associated with Pusey, Barrett, and Rudolph—yet neither camp seems to have conceded ground. The onticists treat state-overlap as a direct signature of reality, while epistemicists consistently reconstruct that overlap within hidden-variable, relational, or retrocausal frameworks that preserve ignorance at a deeper level. My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden empirical disagreement at all, but a conceptual deadlock rooted in an unexamined binary: both sides assume the wavefunction must be either a state—analogous to a point in classical phase space—or an epistemic distribution, thereby neglecting a third possibility in which the wavefunction operates more like a dynamical law governing a more fundamental ontology, a view that receives support in certain Bohmian treatments. Is this diagnosis right?

If the impasse is indeed conceptual, I want a precise diagnosis of its structure rather than a survey of interpretations. Are the two sides actually answering different questions—one about the ontology of the quantum formalism, the other about the semantics of state ascription—under the shared label "reality of the wavefunction"? Is the disagreement partly verbal, turning on incompatible standards for what counts as real in physical theory? Or is it a substantive clash about theoretical virtues—simplicity, locality, determinism—using the ontic/epistemic distinction as a proxy? Please ground your answer in what proponents of each view actually argue, not in generic caricatures. Furthermore, given your diagnosis, what would genuinely count as progress? Would a new formal result closing every epistemic reconstruction finally settle it, or would a conceptual distinction—perhaps one that separates law-like from state-like entities—dissolve the debate by showing it was ill-posed? And if no progress should be expected, explain whether the appropriate response is to abandon the framing altogether rather than to keep seeking a resolution within it.
