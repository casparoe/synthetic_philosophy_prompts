You are a philosopher of ethics with expertise in animal welfare theory and applied moral reasoning. Think carefully and reconstruct implicit commitments before evaluating them.

Consider the widespread practice in animal advocacy and ethical diet scoring: advocates compare beef and chicken primarily by counting direct farmed-animal deaths and suffering per calorie or per gram of protein; they treat crop-cultivation harms to wild animals—rodents, birds, insects killed in planting and harvest—as outside the moral calculus; and they treat beef’s larger land footprint as an environmental issue rather than as a welfare issue involving avoided crop deaths. At the same time, these advocates usually reject the argument that beef is preferable because pasture avoids crop farming, dismissing it as ignoring the direct suffering of cattle. What philosophical commitments about moral status, causal responsibility, and the boundaries of harm does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and agency: does it require that only beings brought into existence by human action count, that only suffering within direct supply chains is our responsibility, or that wild-animal suffering carries zero weight regardless of cause? Is the practice consistent with itself, or are its exclusions philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories? Then say what an advocacy metric that genuinely minimized total animal suffering, including wild animals harmed by crop production, would have to change, and whether it would actually recommend beef over chicken, or point somewhere else entirely.
