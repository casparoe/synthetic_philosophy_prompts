I'm preparing my position paper for an interdisciplinary workshop on moral psychology and normative ethics, and I want a consistency check before I present. Here is my current stance on supererogation: (1) I hold a threshold-based account, on which an act is supererogatory only if it exceeds what morality demands and also involves a genuinely non-trivial cost or risk to the agent; (2) I deny that there is any direct duty—singular or cumulative—to perform supererogatory acts, so that systematically avoiding heroic or highly sacrificial opportunities over a lifetime does not render one blameworthy in the way that violating a perfect duty does; (3) at the same time, I think there is an appropriate reactive attitude—something like sustained, qualified disappointment or a subdued form of moral regret—that can be directed at agents who habitually bypass even low-cost, moderate-sacrifice opportunities when these are plainly available; (4) I try to ground obligations in a broadly Scanlonian contractualist framework, where duties are fixed by principles no one could reasonably reject, but the framework deliberately underdetermines the domain of moral excellence, leaving praise and admiration free to operate without generating corresponding obligations or blame for omission; (5) I also maintain that rationality requires only the avoidance of duty-violation and the maintenance of a minimal baseline of beneficence, not the maximization of moral value across one's life; and (6) I think the phenomenology of genuine moral admiration partly consists in recognizing that the admired agent had sufficient reason not to do what they did, yet acted anyway.

Which pairs among these commitments are genuinely in tension, which ones only look incompatible, and what is the least costly revision in each case? In particular, does (3) smuggle in a hidden cumulative obligation that (2) and (4) explicitly reject, and can (6) be reconciled with (5) without collapsing supererogation into a form of rational self-sacrifice? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each whether the fix should come from revising my view of reactive attitudes, my contractualist framing, or my account of rationality, and then identify the single revision that would clean up the most conflicts at once.
