I am writing a short philosophical essay for my ethics course, and I have found myself holding a set of judgments about commemorative practices that I cannot fully systematize. I hope you can help me identify the underlying principle, test it against further cases, and tell me whether consistency requires me to abandon any of my intuitions.

Here are my verdicts.

First, consider a town square statue of a colonial founder who chartered the municipality but also ordered the forced removal of an indigenous community. I believe the statue should be moved to a local history museum with a contextual exhibit, not destroyed or left in place.

Second, a public high school bears the same founder's name. I think the name should remain, but the school must revise its curriculum and signage to acknowledge the forced removal explicitly.

Third, a residential street is named after a celebrated poet who, in her later years, publicly defended an authoritarian regime responsible for mass violence. I think the street should be renamed entirely, because residents must navigate it daily and are thereby compelled into a kind of involuntary tribute.

Fourth, a small park bench carries a plaque honoring a nurse who saved dozens of lives during a pandemic but was later found to have stolen medical supplies for personal profit. I think the plaque should stay, but it should be physically amended—perhaps with an added inscription—rather than removed.

These judgments feel stable to me, yet they are difficult to reconcile. The founder is treated differently in bronze and in institutional naming; the poet's street is renamed while the nurse's bench is kept with modification. I wonder whether the distinction lies in what I will call mnemonic friction: the resistance a commemorative object offers to reinterpretation. A statue in a square is focal and silent; a school is functional and can be layered with new meaning; a street name is navigational and therefore imposes honor through routine use; a bench is local and minor enough to bear correction without erasure.

But I am not sure this captures everything. So I ask: Is there a coherent principle—perhaps concerning spatial imposition, functional role, or the possibility of contextual amendment—that delivers all four verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn?

Please also apply your principle to two cases I have not considered. One is a proposed ghost projection: at night, the silhouette of the removed founder's statue is projected onto its empty pedestal, accompanied by a brief explanation of the removal. Should this be permitted? The other is a municipal holiday named after the poet, celebrated with public funding for arts programming. Should it be abolished, retained with recontextualization, or renamed?

Finally, if your principle forces me to drop one of my original four judgments, say explicitly which one and why.
