I'm applying for a three-month summer research grant and need a two-page prospectus. My working idea is that standard treatments of Kavka's toxin puzzle all presuppose a single, diachronically continuous agent, and that the puzzle's real difficulty lies in intertemporal authority rather than in decision-theoretic consistency. To set the stage: a benefactor will pay one million dollars if, at midnight, you sincerely intend to drink a mildly toxic but harmless substance the next morning; he detects your mental state directly; once paid, you have no further reason to drink, yet you must have genuinely intended it at midnight. The literature usually frames this as a clash between causal and evidential decision theory, or as a problem about rational intention versus rational action. I want to ask what happens if we drop the unitary-agent assumption and instead model the midnight self and the morning self as distinct loci of decision-making authority linked only by precommitment mechanisms.

Write the proposal with the following components. Second, explain why existing resolutions leave this open—specifically, show that causal-decision-theoretic, evidential-decision-theoretic, and "intention is not action" responses all tacitly assume diachronic authority without defending it, and identify exactly where that assumption does the work in each case. Third, outline the method month by month: in month one, construct three formal sketches of partitioned-agency models (delegated authority, preference-aggregation across time-slices, and institutional commitment structures); in month two, apply each sketch to the toxin case and check for consistency between the recommendation given to the midnight agent and the recommendation given to the morning agent; in month three, test whether any successful model generalizes to related precommitment puzzles—such as self-binding against predictable weakness of will—without ad hoc adjustments. Fourth, specify success and failure conditions: success requires at least one model that resolves the paradox without attributing irrationality to either time-slice and that distinguishes the toxin case from standard Ulysses-contract scenarios; failure occurs if every model either reproduces the original paradox or depends on implausible assumptions about intertemporal control. Fifth, state the realistic scope: what can be completed in three months (the three sketches, the toxin application, and a preliminary generalization check) versus what belongs to the larger project (full formalization, comparison with empirical work on precommitment behavior, and defense of the framework against reductionist objections).
