I have been trying to understand how a group should make a decision when the reasoning is broken into parts, and I keep running into a puzzle that feels both practical and philosophical. Imagine a seven-person committee that must evaluate a proposal that can only be adopted if three conditions are met: it is environmentally safe, it is economically fair, and it is technically feasible. Each member casts a yes-or-no vote on each of the three conditions, and also on the overall proposal itself. Now suppose the results come back split in a strange way: a majority rejects the safety claim, rejects the fairness claim, and rejects the feasibility claim individually, yet a different majority votes in favor of adopting the proposal; or the reverse could happen, with most members accepting all three conditions but a majority still rejecting the proposal. I understand this kind of split is sometimes discussed as a discursive dilemma, and that there are two standard ways to resolve it—either aggregate the votes on the parts and derive the group decision logically, or aggregate the votes on the whole and let that outcome stand regardless of what the parts say. Each approach protects something important—rational consistency on one side, democratic autonomy on the other—but neither feels fully satisfying, especially when members disagree not just about the facts but about how tightly the parts should constrain the whole.

To make the problem more concrete, suppose the committee introduces an additional rule before voting: each member must assign a self-reported confidence score to the logical coherence of their own vote bundle—how well their votes on the three conditions align with their vote on the final proposal—and the aggregation mechanism discounts incoherent bundles by an amount proportional to that confidence deficit. In other words, a member who votes that all three conditions fail yet still supports adoption, but admits low confidence in that combination, has their vote weighted downward, while a member with a coherent bundle and high confidence is weighted upward. The question is whether this coherence-weighted adjustment genuinely resolves the tension between logical consistency and democratic legitimacy, or whether it merely relocates the problem into the calibration of confidence and the hidden definition of coherence. Some might argue that it unfairly privileges members who happen to have consistent intuitions, while others might say it is the only way to prevent collective irrationality without ignoring the group’s direct judgment on the outcome.
