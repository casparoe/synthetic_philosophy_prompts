As a retired physical chemist recently drawn to philosophy, I attended a discussion where Dr. Chen and Prof. Alvarez argued over whether Williams's integrity objection—specifically the idea of having one thought too many—applies to long-term scientific commitments.

Chen said a researcher defending a discredited theory out of loyalty to past collaborators or to her own career investment is making exactly the mistake the objection identifies: she inserts a moral calculus where only epistemic judgment should govern. Abandoning the theory solely because it has become professionally costly, Chen added, would be a parallel error—both sides improperly weigh non-epistemic considerations.

Alvarez countered that the original case concerns direct personal attachment, not institutional work. Scientific loyalty is not analogous to spousal love; reasoning about promises to colleagues, funding obligations, and the value of sustained inquiry is part of the correct calculus, not an extra thought. He further argued that past commitments supply genuine reasons, so treating all loyalty-based reasoning as illegitimate mistakes the nature of integrity in collaborative projects.

I could not referee. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about whether the objection transfers to science, whether loyalty can ever be an improper addition, and what—if anything—both overlook about how integrity operates across long-term research programs. Please write your answer in American English.
