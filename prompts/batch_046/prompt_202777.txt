You are an expert in practical philosophy, decision theory, and the ethics of institutional commitment. Before offering your verdict, please briefly outline the conceptual structures at play—distinguishing the logic of financial investment from that of reputation and public commitment—and then address the situation directly.

My colleague and I co-direct a small nonprofit that restores urban wetlands. Last year we publicly launched a flagship boardwalk project—an elevated wooden path with interpretive signage—after securing a restricted grant that covered exactly its budget. Midway through construction we discovered that the wetland's hydrology makes the boardwalk ecologically disruptive and that a simpler, cheaper trail system would serve both visitors and the ecosystem far better. The grant allows us to amend the project plan, but doing so requires returning 15 percent of the funds already received and publishing a brief explanation of the change.

My colleague insists we must finish the boardwalk anyway. His reasoning goes beyond the lumber and labor already bought; he says that by publicly committing to the boardwalk, we have built up a kind of institutional credibility, and that credibility is now an investment we have already made. If we amend the plan, he argues, we do not merely pay the 15 percent penalty and return some funds—we also discard the credibility we accumulated, which means everything we spent to earn it was wasted. He compares our position to a bidding contest where both sides keep putting in resources just to avoid being the one who walked away empty-handed, and he says that if we change course now, we become the party that paid in but received nothing, leaving us with neither the boardwalk nor the credibility. I feel there is a hidden mistake in treating credibility this way, but I cannot pinpoint whether the error lies in the comparison itself, in the way he counts costs, or in something deeper about the nature of commitment.

Is his analogy appropriate? In particular, can institutional credibility genuinely operate as a kind of non-recoverable investment that creates rational pressure to continue a failing course, or is he misapplying a pattern that only makes sense with money? Please separate what is well established in the study of commitment and escalation dynamics from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding whether reputational or moral commitments can generate the same rational traps as monetary ones. Do not present any single interpretation as the unanimous consensus, and avoid naming specific scholars or texts; analyze the structure of the argument itself.
