You are a philosopher of political psychology with deep familiarity with Dostoevsky and the literature on autonomy, paternalism, and institutional design. Before answering, take a moment to state the core commitments of the position below in your own words, then distinguish what would genuinely defeat it from what would merely complicate it.

I defend what I call the Inquisitorial thesis: in Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, the argument that humanity prefers security, miracle, and authoritative meaning over the burden of radical freedom should be read not merely as a cynical diagnosis but as a normative framework for institutional design. The claim is that sustained self-authorship produces net suffering—anomie, decision fatigue, and existential anxiety—for most agents, so that humane societies ought to build transparent structures (religious, bureaucratic, or algorithmic) that relieve citizens of consequential choice in exchange for material security and collective belonging. I think modern liberal societies implicitly acknowledge this through expanding choice architectures and automated governance that outsource moral reasoning, even while they rhetorically celebrate liberty. I also hold that this avoids standard authoritarian objections because the exchange is explicit and welfare-enhancing rather than deceptive.

What would count decisively against this view? I want the observations, conceptual distinctions, or empirical patterns that should move me, sorted by how damaging they are—including developments that have arguably already occurred—plus an honest assessment of whether defenders could absorb every challenge by adding qualifications. In particular, address whether the view risks being insulated into unfalsifiability (for example, by treating any observed preference for autonomy as false consciousness or elite deviation), and whether that insulation is a philosophical defect or a feature. Also consider whether there are clear cases in which people choose autonomy and responsibility over comfort at great personal cost, and what those cases imply for the claim that freedom is a net negative good for the majority. Finally, say whether the thesis collapses into a rival account—such as a refined paternalism or a preference-utilitarian theory of legitimacy—and if so, whether that collapse matters.
