Compare Freud's explanation of religion in The Future of an Illusion with the historical-psychoanalytic argument he advances in Moses and Monotheism. The first work treats religious doctrines as illusions sustained by infantile helplessness and the projection of a protective father-figure; the second derives Jewish monotheism from the traumatic murder of Moses and the later resurgence of a repressed collective memory, emphasizing latency and the return of the repressed. To what extent is the trauma-based genealogy in the later book a development of the wish-fulfillment model, and to what extent does it replace or contradict it? Specifically, examine whether the father-figure retains an identical explanatory function in both accounts, and whether the shift from individual neurosis to collective historical trauma undermines Freud's claim that religious belief is essentially an illusion comparable to a childhood fantasy.
