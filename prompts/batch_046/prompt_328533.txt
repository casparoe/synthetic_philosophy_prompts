Question 3 (30 points): Compare Leopold von Ranke's ideal that the historian should show the past as it actually was with the scepticisms about historical objectivity developed by E.H. Carr in What Is History? and Hayden White in Metahistory. In particular, address: (a) whether each thinker believes primary-source criticism can yield an account independent of the historian's present concerns; (b) how each distinguishes—if at all—between evidential underdetermination (where the sources do not settle a particular factual dispute) and narrative overdetermination (where the same settled evidence supports multiple coherent stories); and (c) how each would advise a historian writing about the 1848 Paris uprisings who faces contradictory eyewitness reports that agree on the broad outcome but diverge irreconcilably in detail. Be precise about where Carr's and White's critiques overlap and where they diverge.

Student Response:
Ranke's program demands that the historian suppress personal bias and reconstruct the past through rigorous critical analysis of archival sources, especially state papers and diplomatic correspondence. He holds that the past possesses a definite structure that critical philology can recover, and that the historian's role is analogous to that of a judge weighing testimony—minimizing interpretation so that the sources speak directly. For Ranke, both evidential underdetermination and narrative overdetermination are failures of method: if sources conflict, further archival work should resolve them; if multiple stories seem possible, the historian has not yet found the correct emplotment dictated by the documents themselves.

Carr accepts that primary sources must be critically evaluated, but he argues in What Is History? that historical facts are never given independently of interpretation. Every historian selects and arranges facts according to present theoretical commitments, so Ranke's mirror-like objectivity is impossible. Carr, like White, believes that historical explanation is governed by underlying tropological structures—metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, and irony—that shape how historians prefigure the archival field before any empirical analysis begins. Nevertheless, Carr insists that interpretation is constrained by evidence: not every narrative is admissible. Carr therefore recognizes evidential underdetermination—where sources genuinely fail to settle a dispute—but denies narrative overdetermination, since he holds that once the historian applies proper critical method and weighs the relevant evidence, the documents themselves determine not only the facts but also the correct narrative form, leaving no legitimate room for alternative emplotments.

White extends Carr's insight into a radical narrative constructivism. In Metahistory, he argues that historical writing is essentially a literary operation governed by the same four master tropes. For White, evidential underdetermination is ubiquitous, but narrative overdetermination is inescapable: the same body of evidence about 1848 can be emplotted as romance, tragedy, comedy, or satire without any empirical criterion for choosing among them. Because White holds that all historical narratives are equally valid literary constructs, he advises the historian to select the emplotment that best serves present ethical needs or aesthetic preferences, even if that means relying on reports that have been shown to be internally contradictory, provided she does not fabricate documents outright. Carr and White therefore share a common anti-positivist stance, but Carr retains a commitment to evidential constraint that White abandons entirely.

Applying these views to the 1848 case: Ranke would advise weighing the provenance and internal consistency of each eyewitness account, discarding reports that fail critical tests, and reconstructing the sequence of events with minimal narrative embellishment, presenting the result as a direct transcript of the past rather than as a constructed story. Carr would advise the historian to acknowledge that her decision to privilege workers' testimony over police reports already reflects an interpretive framework—perhaps a Marxist one—while insisting that she justify this selection by showing that the excluded reports are genuinely less reliable. White would advise that since the reports are irreconcilable, the historian should recognize her account as one legitimate narrative among others; she might choose a tragic emplotment emphasizing revolutionary violence, with neither being closer to the actual past, though both must cite the same core archival materials.
