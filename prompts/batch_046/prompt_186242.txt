Write the joint statement that advocates of mnemonic sovereignty and advocates of archive fidelity could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration on conflicts between a community’s collective memory and the established historical record. The statement should address not generic disputes but a specific, constructed test case—the Meridian Accord—where an oral tradition holds that a foundational reconciliation between an indigenous council and a colonial administration occurred at a river confluence in 1847, led by Elder Tahn and Administrator Voss, whereas carbon-dated timber, recovered correspondence, and land-survey logs place a different meeting in 1851, ten kilometres away, with different representatives, suggesting the community’s account conflates two separate events and elevates one elder’s symbolic role.

The deliverable must contain: a numbered section of agreed claims that both camps can endorse without reservation, covering the nature of collective memory, the fallibility of archives, the non-identity of memory and record, and the real harms that both erroneous correction and uncritical preservation can inflict; a disagreements table in which each residual dispute is stated in neutral wording that each side accepts as a fair rendering of its own position, with no straw-manning; a list of cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp—including whether collective memory possesses intrinsic normative authority beyond epistemic value, whether the power-asymmetry of an archive’s production undermines its corrective authority, and whether a distinction exists between “restoring the record” and “displacing the memory”; and, for each crux, the concrete bearing of the Meridian Accord case, together with each side’s explicit prediction or assessment of what that case shows.

Use British English throughout. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure named theories; instead, inject the conceptual distinction of “mnemonic reparative authority”—the claim that correcting a community’s memory can itself constitute a harm that demands redress, not merely an epistemic update—directly into the framing of one crux, so that the debate turns on a precise normative mechanism rather than on generic appeals to “truth” or “identity.” Format the output as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by the disagreements table, with the cruxes and their associated predictions presented in clearly labelled subsections. Ensure that every formulation is one both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than rhetorical.
