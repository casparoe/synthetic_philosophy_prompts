I've been drafting a short essay that tries to move the Buddhist doctrine of anatta away from the standard Western taxonomy—eliminativism versus reductionism about personal identity—and toward a question about boundary conditions in predictive processing. The core claim is this: if cognition is hierarchical free-energy minimization, then the "self" is not a substance or even a bundle, but a cluster of interoceptive and proprioceptive predictions that happen to be assigned high precision weights. Anatta, on this reading, is not the denial that such a cluster exists, but the denial that any metaphysical boundary separates it from other predictive processes in the environment or in other minds. The skandhas are not parts of a hidden owner; they are just differently weighted prediction errors. Rebirth then needs no transmigrating soul—only causal continuity in precision-weighting patterns across organisms—which is consistent with the Abhidharma analysis of dharmas as momentary and ownerless.

I'm particularly unsure about three things. First, does this framing genuinely capture the classical Theravada or Madhyamaka position, or does it smuggle in a physicalist ontology those traditions would reject? Second, if the self-boundary is merely pragmatic, what distinguishes moral responsibility across lives from mere causal influence? Third, have I missed an obvious objection—perhaps from the personal-identity literature or from Buddhist philosophy—that undermines the predictive-processing analogy?
