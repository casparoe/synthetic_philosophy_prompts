I've noticed a recurring pattern in how complex scientific models are defended and then deployed, and I'm unsure whether it has a specific name. A research group builds a model with premises that are widely acknowledged to be false—say, that economic agents possess unbounded rationality and perfect information, or that an ecological system can be partitioned into non-interacting subsystems. When methodologists criticize the model for its unrealistic foundations, the authors invoke the familiar instrumental defense: false assumptions are permissible, even necessary, when the goal is isolating causal mechanisms or generating accurate predictions, and the model should be judged by its outputs rather than its premises. So far this is standard. But then, in policy briefs or regulatory testimony derived from the same model, the authors treat the model's internal architecture as if it were a direct representation of reality. They do not merely claim that reducing variable X will lower outcome Y; they claim that X drives Y through the specific pathway Z that the model encodes, and they prescribe interventions targeted precisely at that pathway, even though the pathway exists only because of the simplifying assumptions that were just defended as literally false. I have seen variants of this with macroeconomic forecasting models and with climate-economy integrated assessment models. Is there an established concept—perhaps related to, but distinct from, the standard defense of unrealistic assumptions—that captures this particular shift between instrumental defense and ontological interpretation? How should one distinguish it from ordinary model overreach or from straightforward equivocation? If there is no established term, what would be the precise boundaries of the phenomenon: must the same individuals make both moves, must the moves occur in distinct institutional contexts, and does it require that the false premises be individually necessary for the predictive success being claimed?
