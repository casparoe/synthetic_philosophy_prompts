Write a short story related to mind uploading and gradual replacement, but avoid the usual "scan consciousness into a server" trope. Instead, your protagonist is replacing their biological neurons module by module over about a decade with synthetic analogues that replicate function but are engineered to dampen emotional salience — they process risk and moral weighting with less noise. They're an academic who used to write about personal identity and was convinced that gradual replacement preserves the self so long as psychological continuity holds. By year six, half their brain is synthetic, and they've started noticing that their old philosophical arguments feel foreign, like reading a diary they happen to remember writing. The story should centre on a dinner with an old friend who hasn't seen them in years and remarks on how calm they've become. Include a moment where the protagonist tries to defend the view that there's no sharp boundary where "they" end and the replacement begins, but they realise they can no longer feel the visceral pull of that position, even though the memory of holding it is intact. Meanwhile, the friend asks whether their new willingness to endorse sacrificing one life to save five — a view they used to recieve with horror — is proof that the original person has been replaced, or just evidence that they've genuinely changed their mind. Keep the tone ambiguous: don't make it horror, don't make it triumph, just leave it unclear whether this is loss, maturation, or neither. Use British English and keep things casual.
