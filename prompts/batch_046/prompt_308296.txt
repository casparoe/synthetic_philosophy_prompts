I am preparing a colloquium Q&A with Dr. Kira Mendel, a moral philosopher who defends what she calls integrated maximalism about moral saints: she holds that maximal moral goodness is not only desirable but partially constitutive of human flourishing, but only if we reject Susan Wolf’s framing that moral and non-moral goods compete within a fixed budget of attention and energy. Mendel’s distinctive claim is that moral saints restructure their “attention economy”—the allocation of cognitive and affective resources—so that moral perception becomes the default evaluative frame rather than a costly overlay, which lets them pursue personal projects that are morally inflected rather than sacrificed. She also accepts a “saturation thesis”: beyond a certain threshold, further moral improvement requires not greater self-denial but a transformation of the self’s evaluative framework, guided by practical wisdom rather than by trade-offs. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to restate the view but press its actual content. I want at least one question on whether her attention-economy model collapses into adaptive preference formation—where saints simply learn not to want what they cannot have—with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the restructuring is rational and open to revision rather than adaptive. I want at least one question on how her saturation thesis avoids being an arbitrary quantitative ceiling: does she have a non-circular criterion for the threshold, and what does she say to the objection that any such criterion smuggles back the non-moral goods she claims to have subordinated? Include a follow-up anticipating her appeal to a virtue-ethical conception of practical wisdom. I also want a question that pits her view against the standard literature on supererogation: if moral saints flourish precisely by going beyond the call of duty, does her framework erase the distinction between duty and supererogation, or does she preserve it inside the transformed evaluative framework? Add a follow-up for the answer she is most likely to give. Finally, include at least one question asking what her view implies for artificial moral agents—can an AI be a moral saint on her account, and does her attention-economy model require a phenomenology of moral attention that machines necessarily lack?
