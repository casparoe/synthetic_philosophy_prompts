I'm taking a seminar on political ethics, and we've been working through the problem of dirty hands—the idea that political actors sometimes do moral wrongs in order to prevent catastrophe, and that this might be structurally unavoidable rather than merely excusable. Our discussion turned to an institutional practice that seems to go further than the classic individual case: in several democratic systems, there exist classified continuity-of-government protocols that authorize executive actors, during extreme emergencies, to suspend specific legal protections—warrant requirements, transparency obligations, certain due-process guarantees—but only on three conditions. First, the suspension must never be publicly acknowledged. Second, no written record may attribute the decision to a named official. Third, the authorizing framework itself must remain hidden not only from the public but from ordinary legislative oversight and democratic reform channels. The protocols are not unknown to everyone—senior civil servants and security lawyers are trained in them—but they are institutionally invisible: they cannot be cited, debated, amended, or repealed through standard democratic procedures.

This design strikes me as philosophically different from a politician who lies or orders a harmful act and then faces accountability. Here, accountability seems to be dissolved structurally rather than merely risked. I want to understand what philosophical commitments this institutional arrangement presupposes. Does it require the view that democratic legitimacy is purely instrumental—that it must be preserved as a public façade but can be suspended when it conflicts with survival, provided the suspension remains invisible? Or does it presuppose a specific theory of moral responsibility, one in which blame requires identifiable authorship, so that distributing decisions across anonymous institutional structures literally eliminates the object of blame? Could it instead rest on a rule-consequentialist view of institutional design: the rules must exist and be followed, but public knowledge of them would undermine the democratic culture they are meant to protect, so secrecy is part of the optimal rule?

Could you reconstruct the implicit theory, or competing theories, of political morality, democratic legitimacy, and moral responsibility that this practice only makes coherent sense under? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and say which rival philosophical views it quietly rules out—for instance, whether it is compatible with a deontological insistence that moral wrongs require acknowledgment and redress, or with a participatory-democratic view that citizens must be able to consent to the rules that bind them even in crisis. Finally, tell me what would have to change—not just practically, but in the underlying philosophical commitments about the relationship between knowledge, power, and moral accountability—if we replaced this hidden-protocol model with a fully transparent one in which emergency powers are publicly authorized, named officials take recorded responsibility, and the framework is subject to democratic revision.
