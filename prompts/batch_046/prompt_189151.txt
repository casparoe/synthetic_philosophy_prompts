I cover the intersection of philosophy and public life, and lately I have been investigating how biography functions in moral education. Rereading Plutarch’s Parallel Lives, I was struck by his practice of pairing a Greek and a Roman figure—Alexander with Caesar, for instance—not to declare a winner but to let their contrasting temperaments illuminate one another. He does not conceal their vices or excesses; indeed, he often places a subject’s failure adjacent to his virtue, as if to warn the reader that the same force can produce both. From this I have begun to formulate, in my reporting notes, what I call the proximity paradox: a life can serve as a moral mirror only if the reader feels close enough to recognize herself in it, yet biography necessarily selects and shapes events into a narrative that risks making the subject either too distant to emulate or too flattened to learn from. Plutarch’s comparative framing, I have assumed, resolves this paradox by preserving complexity through contrast rather than through exhaustive realism.

This assumption, however, has led me to a puzzling tension that I want to check for a hidden misconception. Contemporary philosophers who discuss moral exemplars—particularly within virtue ethics and moral education—often warn against hagiography, by which they appear to mean any biographical narrative that sanitizes its subject into an unattainable ideal. Yet Plutarch’s Lives are not sanitized; they are deliberately comparative and morally ambiguous. So is the modern philosophical suspicion of biography as a source of moral knowledge actually directed at a different phenomenon altogether—perhaps at single-subject, celebratory narrative rather than at comparative biography? Or, more sharply: if Plutarch’s parallel structure is designed precisely to prevent the reader from treating any one life as a direct template, then is it a category error to group his work with the kind of hero-worship that critics rightly reject?

What I am trying to understand is whether there is a precise conceptual distinction—distinct from mere genre labeling—between the mirror function of comparative biography, where the reader calibrates her own character against a pair of imperfect exemplars, and the template function of hagiography, where a single life is presented as a normative standard. If such a distinction holds, does it salvage biography as a legitimate pedagogical tool, or does the act of narrative selection itself—the choice of which events to include, which virtues to highlight—inevitably collapse comparative biography back into a hidden form of hagiography? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want to know whether my framing of the proximity paradox captures a real philosophical problem, or whether it rests on a false presupposition about how Plutarch’s method relates to modern critiques of moral exemplarity.
