I convene an advanced undergraduate seminar in moral psychology and historical explanation — eighteen students, one ninety-minute session. Design a runnable in-class activity that makes participants experience, rather than merely discuss, the explanatory tension between Christopher Browning’s situational analysis of a reserve police battalion and Daniel Goldhagen’s argument for deep-rooted eliminationist antisemitism as a primary driver of perpetrator behaviour. Provide embedded briefings so students need no prior specialist reading.

The activity should take the form of an escalating “resource-classification protocol” conducted in four rounds. Divide the eighteen students into three groups of six, each receiving identical ambiguous directives from a “central authority” (played by you or delivered via scripted cards). In round one they classify inanimate objects; in round two they apply the classification scheme to hypothetical persons; in round three they receive an instruction to “optimise efficiency” with discretionary latitude; in round four they discover that a hidden record has tracked whether any group exceeded, resisted, or modified the directive — and, crucially, whether individual members had previously received different “background briefs” that primed either institutional loyalty or categorical moral distinction.

Specify the rules, low-tech materials, minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff scheme relying solely on social recognition (public display of group choices, no real money). Before naming either historian, the debrief must map: round-one compliance onto Browning’s mechanisms of peer pressure and incremental authority; round-three discretionary expansion onto Goldhagen’s claim of voluntary ideological initiative; and the hidden background-brief variable onto the core dispute over whether situation or character dominates. Include an honest admission of which explanatory feature — for instance, the multi-decade cultural embedding Goldhagen stresses, or the bureaucratic anonymity Browning highlights — a ninety-minute classroom format structurally cannot reproduce and you must therefore supply verbally.

Also state what will predictably go wrong on first running — for example, students recognising the moral analogue too quickly and performing virtue, or failing entirely to notice the hidden priming mechanism — and propose a concrete adjustment.

Please begin with the round-by-round rules and materials list, followed by the debrief mapping and the failure analysis. No preface, no restatement of the brief, and no bibliography or suggested reading.
