I've been sketching out notes for an essay comparing Buddhist anattā with contemporary analytic theories of personal identity, and I'd appreciate comments on accuracy, coherence, and whether I'm steelmanning the Buddhist side fairly rather than just projecting familiar categories onto it. The motivation came from a recent discussion with friends about split-brain cases and mind-uploading scenarios—topics that circulate often in rationalist forums—where the implicit model of identity seemed to rely on a kind of psychological essentialism that Buddhist metaphysics might reject more radically than the standard Parfit-style reductionism. Rather than rehearse the usual five-skandha versus bundle-theory comparison, I'm trying to push the argument through a more specific Abhidharma framing that I haven't seen engaged with seriously in these spaces.

My central claim turns on the distinction between pudgalanairātmya—the absence of self in the aggregates of form, feeling, perception, volition, and consciousness—and dharmanairātmya—the absence of inherent existence (svabhāva) in the dharmas themselves, particularly as developed in the Sarvāstivāda versus Sautrāntika debates over temporal existence. I take the Sautrāntika position to hold that only present dharmas exist with full reality, while past and future ones are mere conceptual designations (prajñapti). If that is correct, then the causal continuity (santāna) that substitutes for the self is not an ontologically robust process but a strictly nominal series: no present mental state genuinely contains or is diachronically connected to a past mental state, only a present dharma that stands in a causal relation to a now-nonexistent predecessor. This seems to undermine not just substantial identity but also narrative-identity theories—such as those associated with MacIntyre or Dennett—that depend on psychological connectedness having present ontological grounding. By contrast, the Sarvāstivāda grants existence to dharmas in all three times, though with different modal statuses (present ones possessing bhāva, past and future ones possessing avasthā without present efficacy). That looks structurally analogous to four-dimensionalist worm theories in analytic metaphysics, except that each temporal slice is itself empty of svabhāva. So my essay asks whether anattā, coupled with these Abhidharma ontologies, dissolves the narrative self more thoroughly than reductionism does, or whether it merely relocates unity into the causal laws of pratītyasamutpāda—leaving open whether those laws can do the work of personal identity without smuggling in a hidden substrate.

I'd particularly like feedback on three points. First, am I correct in attributing to the Sautrāntika a presentist ontology of dharmas, and does that genuinely conflict with narrative continuity, or am I imposing an anachronistic analytic frame? Second, does the Sarvāstivāda parallel to four-dimensionalism hold, given that they distinguish the bare existence (asti) of past dharmas from their present functional mode? Third, if both schools deny svabhāva at the dharmic level, can the conventional designation (prajñapti) of a person be robust enough to support practical concerns—moral responsibility, long-term planning, or the self-modification strategies discussed in rationalist circles—without collapsing into either pure fiction or a reified substance? I'm not looking for citations or a reading list; I want to know whether the conceptual architecture holds together and whether it genuinely advances the discussion beyond the standard bundle-theory versus ego-theory dialectic.
