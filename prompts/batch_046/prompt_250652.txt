I'm in a philosophy of science seminar and we've been arguing about whether a theory can be 'right but just early,' using Malthus as the obvious example. You know the story - he said population outstrips food, then the industial revolution happened and everyone called him wrong. But some people defend him by saying his math still works, it's just that technological growth bought us time, or that the mechanisim kicks in only after growth hits a ceiling. My tutor mentioned something she called 'rescue by temporal displacement,' though I'm not sure that's a real term.

What I'm trying to figure out are the most important conceptual implications if we allow this kind of move for long-run predictions in general. Specifically, there's a difference between saying Malthus had the right mechanisim but misjudged the timeline, versus saying the mechanism is only active in certain 'historical phases' that we haven't entered yet. The second version feels different - it seems to protect the theory by shrinking its domain every time a prediction fails. Does that kind of phase-shifting rescue destroy falsifiablity in practice, or is it a legitimate scientific strategy for theories meant to operate over centuries?

And how should we distinguish it from just adding ordinary auxiliary hypotheses? I'm not after a reading list, just want to map the stakes for whether 'right but early' can ever be a respectable scientific defense.
