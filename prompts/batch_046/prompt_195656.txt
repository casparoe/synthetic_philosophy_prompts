Assume the role of a professional political philosopher specializing in conceptual analysis of democratic institutions. Before writing, take a moment to brainstorm: what would make a criterion for democratic backsliding genuinely normative rather than a disguised partisan judgment, and why do Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the US serve as especially demanding conceptual stress-tests rather than straightforward empirical illustrations?

Then draft a concise one-page research proposal—approximately 800 to 1,000 words, appropriate for a four-month fellowship or dissertation prospectus—addressing the following precisely framed question: Can "democratic backsliding" be separated analytically from disagreement about legitimate institutional reform, and can we construct a non-circular criterion that identifies backsliding without presupposing a contested liberal-democratic baseline?

In your proposal, explain why existing answers leave the question open. Note that the literature often relies on expert-coded indices that embed normative assumptions, or treats executive aggrandizement and norm erosion as self-evidently regressive without defending the baseline of "normal" democracy against which the slide is measured. Argue that these approaches do not establish whether the concept can do independent normative work.

Propose an alternative conceptual approach rather than an empirical or purely dismissive one. Specifically, develop a "reversibility framework": an institutional change counts as backsliding not because it violates a substantive liberal ideal or because it was enacted through formally legal procedures, but because it systematically disables the mechanisms—electoral, legislative, or judicial—through which citizens could reverse it through ordinary political contestation. Defend why this functionalist criterion avoids circularity better than standard proceduralist and substantive accounts, and address the obvious objection that reversibility itself may depend on prior normative commitments about what counts as "ordinary" contestation.

Use Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the US not as empirical case studies requiring data collection, but as conceptual stress-tests: identify one institutional change in each context that one camp calls backsliding and another calls legitimate reform, and show how the reversibility framework adjudicates it without merely restating the partisan division.

Specify the deliverable: a decision tree or typology for classifying institutional changes, explicit conditions under which the framework fails (for example, if reversibility collapses into a procedural criterion in every test case), and a brief assessment of what progress looks like after four months.

Keep the scope realistic: conceptual reconstruction and normative argument only, no fieldwork or original data gathering. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or requests for further reading; refer to general positions by name if useful, but build the proposal on its conceptual architecture alone. End with a short justification for funding philosophical work on this problem—specifically, how clarifying the concept shapes practical judgments about constitutional monitoring, international assessment, or democratic assistance.
