(1) Genuine joint action requires shared cooperative intentions in roughly the Bratman mold—interlocking intentions, mutual responsiveness, and commitment to the joint activity—so that each participant's plan essentially refers to the others'.

(2) Nevertheless, a distributed organization—say, a research collective in which no single member grasps the full plan and pairwise mutual knowledge is absent—can still form intentions and act jointly, provided its roles are functionally integrated.

(3) Collective intentionality is ontologically irreducible: it is not merely an aggregation of individual mental states, but a distinct state that the group itself instantiates.

(4) The correct philosophical method for identifying joint action is functionalist and third-personal: we should classify cases by organizational and behavioral patterns, not by access to private mental states.

(5) Consciousness and subjective awareness are not necessary conditions: a system of non-conscious but functionally integrated modules can genuinely participate in joint action if it achieves the same mutual responsiveness and role structure.

(6) Mere causal coordination without organizational integration—two strangers independently pushing the same cart with no awareness of each other—is never joint action, only parallel individual action.
