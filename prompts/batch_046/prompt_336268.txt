Following a recent personal reckoning with the ethics of reproduction—specifically, whether a deliberate decision to remain childless can be grounded in something more rigorous than risk aversion—I want a formal adversarial-collaboration document drafted between two opposing camps on antinatalism and Benatar’s axiological asymmetry.

The first camp defends the asymmetry in its canonical form: for any potential person, the presence of suffering counts against bringing them into existence, while the absence of pleasure (when they never exist) does not count in favor, yielding a pro tanto reason against procreation unless expected suffering is negligible. The second camp rejects that this asymmetry supports a general reproductive prohibition; they maintain that procreation is permissible or valuable when lives are expected to be good, deny that non-existent beings can be “harmed” by not being created, and typically appeal to person-affecting moral restrictions or to a pluralist conception of welfare that includes meaningful agency and relational depth.

Draft the joint statement these parties should have produced. First, enumerate the claims both can sign—about the inevitability of suffering in human lives; about the formal logical structure of the asymmetry; about what empirical findings (for example, baseline distributions of chronic suffering or well-being across populations) would be relevant; and about the distinction between judging a life as worth living and judging the act of creating it. Second, state the precise disagreements in wording each side accepts for its own view. I want this section to address explicitly whether the asymmetry applies only to hedonic states or extends to eudaimonic goods such as autonomy, narrative coherence, and relational belonging—a substitution I take to be a live crux rather than a verbal shift—and whether moral evaluation of procreation should be token-specific (assessing particular reproductive decisions) or modal (assessing the practice under aggregate risk profiles across possible lives). Third, identify the cruxes: sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. I suspect the central ones include: (a) whether “naturalness” or “continuity of human projects” carries independent axiological weight capable of offsetting suffering; (b) whether predictive capacities about a future child’s welfare are reliable enough to justify procreation under deep uncertainty; and (c) whether the asymmetry survives if hedonistic goods and harms are replaced by pluralist welfare metrics that weight alienation and agency-loss differently from physical pain. For each crux, specify a concrete hypothetical case both sides agree bears on it—for instance, a decision context in which prospective parents carry high genetic risk for a condition causing both severe physical pain and progressive loss of autonomous agency, yet also possess strong family narratives about resilience and intergenerational meaning—and record in advance what each camp predicts about how that case should be evaluated. Fourth, indicate whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, explaining which terms—such as “harm,” “benefit,” “obligation,” or “potential person”—are being used with different extensions or intensions, and whether aligning on a single usage dissolves any apparent disagreement. The final document should be formal, neutrally phrased, and suitable for both parties to sign, with the residual disagreement rendered exact and predictive rather than rhetorical.
