The debate between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws of nature has cycled through the same moves for decades—best-systems analysis versus governing relations, the counterfactual stability of regularities versus the modal force of dispositional essences, objections about explanatory circularity and ontological extravagance—without either side conceding ground, despite broad agreement on the actual physical regularities and on the truth of most scientific counterfactuals. My working theory is that the stall is not empirical—no experiment is expected to settle it—but structural: Humeans treat “law” as a question about descriptive and predictive economy, asking which system best summarizes actual patterns, while anti-Humeans treat it as a question about the source of modal constraint, asking what in the world makes it the case that those patterns could not have been otherwise, and the two sides therefore answer different questions under one name. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about whether a world with identical categorical bases but different regularities is genuinely possible, or about whether explanatory priority entails ontological priority—that keeps the dispute alive? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually commits to rather than a survey of positions, together with an honest judgment about whether any conceptual distinction—say, between metaphysical grounding and nomological grounding, or between summary-statements and constraint-mechanisms—or any formal result about the uniqueness of best-system summaries could actually break the impasse, or whether the question is ill-posed because it conflates descriptive architecture with modal architecture.
