I cover the philosophy of rationality and scientific judgment for a general audience, and lately I have been investigating how thinkers navigate confidence when they leave the territory of direct, granular feedback. In a recent roundtable with researchers in cognitive science and philosophy of science, one participant defended what she termed "structural deferral"—a strong form of the outside-view stance holding that, in domains lacking rapid, individualized error correction (including much of philosophy, macroeconomic forecasting, and long-range strategic planning), rational agents should not merely weight base-rate disagreement more heavily but should treat their own inside-view reasoning about specific claims as epistemically subordinate, almost like an uncalibrated instrument whose readings must be filtered through the reference-class failure rate. She argued this applies even when one's internal reasoning feels rigorous, because the feeling of rigor is itself generated by mechanisms that have never been independently calibrated in that domain. I want to take this seriously, but I also want to avoid turning it into an unfalsifiable skepticism that simply redefines "calibration" to exclude any first-person improvement.

Play it straight: what would have to be true for structural deferral to be wrong, or at least seriously overstated? I want an honest assessment of how exposed this view is. Which empirical results—perhaps showing that experts in feedback-poor domains can improve accuracy through structured reasoning protocols, adversarial collaboration, or explicit decomposition—would count decisively against it? Which formal distinctions between types of reasoning (mathematical deduction, causal modeling, normative evaluation) would show that the inside/outside asymmetry collapses in some domains but not others? What would defenders of the view themselves concede as genuinely damaging—say, a demonstrated case where a group improved collective calibration without relying on outside-view base rates—versus what they might dismiss as merely awkward? And does the position risk insulating itself by treating any first-person reasoning improvement as secretly dependent on hidden feedback loops? I am not looking for a bibliography or reading list; I want a conceptual map of the view's vulnerabilities, ranked from fatal to merely uncomfortable, including whether a well-insulated version of the view is still a virtue or a vice for a theory of epistemic modesty.
