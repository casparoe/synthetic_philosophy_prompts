After a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about whether our university still makes sense organizing its curriculum around the conventional triad of the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution, I am writing to ask for a philosophical clarification rather than a historical survey. My friend maintained that these three labels are essentially retrospective constructions—conceptual artifacts imposed by later historians and educators that paper over deep continuities in social practice, knowledge production, and artistic form—while I kept sensing that even invented categories might track real discontinuities if they correspond to shifts in institutional norms or epistemic self-understanding. I want you to describe the most important implications of treating these period divisions as historiographical inventions rather than as naturally occurring epochs. In particular, what does this thesis commit us to regarding the ontology of historical change, the legitimacy of retaining these labels in education and public culture, and the conditions under which a claim about revolutionary discontinuity could still be justified? I am especially interested in whether accepting the invented-status view obliges us to abandon all talk of revolution in intellectual history, or whether it merely demands greater precision about which specific practices, textual traditions, or institutional arrangements actually warrant such a label, and how this affects reasoning about long-term causal processes when one cannot consult specialized archival literature. Please present your answer as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written in the manner of a careful letter that addresses each implication in turn through sustained paragraphs rather than through any segmented format.
