I'm delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on moral cultures and institutional change, convened in the university conference centre. The audience comprises about forty people: sociologists familiar with the honour–dignity–victimhood taxonomy, moral philosophers working on recognition and status, and practitioners—school administrators and community mediators—who navigate these norms daily but have little formal philosophical training. I need a ten-minute address that takes up the question of whether honour culture, dignity culture, or victimhood culture is morally superior, and I want this question to do genuine structural work rather than serve as ornament: the speech must recognise that framing 'moral superiority' is itself a move characteristic of dignity culture, yet it must avoid the false neutrality or institutional self-congratulation that dignity cultures often disguise as impartial procedure. To prevent the address from being a mere recitation of well-worn sociology, please build the argument around a conceptual tension I want to call 'credential honour'—the tendency of dignity-based institutions to reproduce honour-like status hierarchies through bureaucratic recognition mechanisms (degrees, peer review, citation metrics) that claim to measure intrinsic worth—using this idea to show why asking which culture is best already privileges the evaluative vocabulary of one contender.

Please write the address in a tone that is welcoming yet intellectually rigorous, calibrated so that the philosophical point lands for specialists without losing the practitioners in the room. After the speech, append a brief reflective note—no more than a short paragraph—distinguishing which claims rest on relatively settled distinctions within the sociology of moral cultures, which depend on genuinely contested theses (for example, whether victimhood culture necessarily undermines reciprocity, or whether dignity culture genuinely eliminates status competition), and where the notion of 'credential honour' extends or takes liberties with the standard taxonomy. Do not present contested views as consensus, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the reasoning should stand entirely on its own.
