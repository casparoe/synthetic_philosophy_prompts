I sit on the governance board of a cooperative research institute that adopted an automatic, exceptionless rule: any member who misses three consecutive collaborative sessions without prior notice loses voting rights for six months. The policy was designed specifically to eliminate subjective judgments about excuses. A founding member—who has never missed a session in fifteen years—recently hit that threshold after being unreachable for three weeks while coordinating emergency relief logistics in her hometown following an unforeseen disaster. The board is divided. Some members argue that exempting her undermines the rationale for having an automatic rule and invites arbitrary favoritism; others claim that applying the sanction here would be a form of moral blindness, treating a situation with radically different contextual features as if it were ethically identical to ordinary absenteeism. I am inclined toward the second view but worry I am rationalizing an exception. Is my reluctance best explained as a failure of consistency—an unwillingness to apply a principle I endorsed—or does it reflect a defensible position that moral reasoning can be genuinely responsive to the particular constellation of features in a case, such that two actions falling under the same abstract description may nonetheless differ in their moral significance? If the latter, how can one defend an exception without collapsing into ad hoc preference?
