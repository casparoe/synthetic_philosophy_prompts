I'm a senior in high school applying for a six-week Summer Philosophy Research Program, and I need to write a two-page proposal for a conceptual project that doesn't just rehearse the familiar Rawls-versus-MacIntyre framing. My half-formed idea is this: both liberalism and communitarianism usually treat membership as a binary status—you're either embedded in a community that supplies your shared goods and constitutive obligations, or you're an unencumbered individual bearing rights of exit and autonomous choice. But in practice, most people inhabit layered or partial memberships—professional networks, linguistic groups, digital platforms, local neighborhoods—simultaneously, with very different exit costs, degrees of reversibility, and depths of constitutive attachment for each. I want to ask whether the classic communitarian critique—that liberalism falsely imagines the self as detached from any social fabric—still carries force once we recognize that modern subjects are never fully detached, but are instead embedded in overlapping communities with conflicting claims. At the same time, I want to ask whether liberalism's defense of individual rights collapses once we admit that "exit" is never costless and that rights of recognition themselves depend on communal structures.

The existing debate, as I understand it, tends to treat this as a dispute about the metaphysics of the self rather than as a problem about the architecture of membership itself. That leaves open whether there is a stable intermediate position—perhaps something like a layered liberalism that takes embeddedness seriously without sacrificing normative protections, or a portfolio communitarianism that permits partial exit without dissolving loyalty obligations—or whether any attempt to combine them produces an unstable hybrid. For the project, I would construct a conceptual taxonomy of membership types organized around three axes: exit cost, reversibility, and constitutive depth; apply it to three concrete cases—professional licensure, linguistic assimilation, and participation in large-scale open collaborative projects—to determine where liberal protections and communal obligations genuinely conflict versus where they only appear to; and then defend one of three conclusions: that the debate should be reframed entirely around layered membership, that one side conceptually absorbs the other without remainder, or that the tension is irreducible and should be treated as a design feature for institutions rather than a philosophical bug. A useful deliverable would be a decision framework that institutional designers could use to assess when an obligation of loyalty is legitimate and when a protected right of exit is required, without needing to resolve the underlying metaphysics of personal identity. The project could fail if the taxonomy turns out to be purely descriptive with no normative bite, or if every case collapses back into the standard binary. Given the six-week scope, I would limit the analysis to the three cases above, avoid historical scholarship, and focus on conceptual clarification, argument reconstruction, and the explicit criteria that would decide between my three possible conclusions.
