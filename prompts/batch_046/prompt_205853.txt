I'm preparing an independent study paper for my philosophy seminar on how people defend the possibility of meaning in a universe that seems indifferent to human projects, and I have noticed a specific argumentative loop that keeps appearing in discussions of absurdism and contemporary subjective-objective accounts of meaning. The pattern works like this: an interlocutor raises the absurdist worry that, without any cosmic purpose, our attachments—to art, to particular people, to moral causes—are arbitrarily selected. Rather than arguing that one attachment is objectively superior to others, the defender responds that meaning arises from genuinely caring about something, and that this care itself provides the reason for the project. But when the interlocutor presses further and asks why that particular care is not itself arbitrary—why not care about counting grains of sand instead—the reply is that for this person the care is not arbitrary at all; it is simply part of their actual motivational structure. The defense thus closes the circle: the care legitimizes the project, and the project is meaningful because the care is real. I have seen this move specifically in debates about artistic creation, intimate relationships, and ethical commitment, always as a response to the charge of meaninglessness rather than as ordinary practical reasoning. Does this particular loop—using the reality of a subjective attachment to block the question of its own arbitrariness—have an established name in the literature on absurdism or on what philosophers call meaningful lives? I am aware that it touches on internal reasons and on views that combine subjective fulfillment with objective worth, but it seems distinct from standard internalism because it functions specifically as a defensive reply to the absurdist challenge rather than as a general theory of motivation. If there is no precise term, what should it be called, and where should its boundaries be drawn—for instance, how does it differ from simple authenticity, or from the claim that reasons are necessarily perspective-dependent, and does it fail in cases where the subjective attachment itself is the product of manipulation? I am not looking for a reading list; I want to know whether the carving is already recognized, how it relates to the nearest concepts, and if not, what definition and boundary cases would properly capture it.
