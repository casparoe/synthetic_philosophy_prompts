I'm part of a philosophy of mind study group where two members, Elena and Marcus, have been debating a specific case that tests the boundaries of the extended mind thesis. Elena has spent fifteen years using a bespoke annotation and taxonomy system to organize her research in phenomenology; the system's categorization scheme has become so internalized that she reports restructuring her offline reasoning around its conceptual architecture. She argues that this is not mere reliance on an external database but a case of genuine cognitive extension—the system's organizational structure functions as part of her reasoning apparatus, not just as a retrieval aid. Marcus disagrees, insisting that what Elena describes is deep habituation with a sophisticated tool rather than constitutional inclusion. He points out that she can disconnect entirely and continue thinking, albeit less efficiently, while the system can do nothing cognitive without her input, which suggests an asymmetry incompatible with the parity principle. Elena responds that this asymmetry also holds for her hippocampus—she can think without it, and it cannot think without her brain—so Marcus's criterion would exclude internal organs too. Marcus then introduces the worry that Elena is falling into the coupling-constitution fallacy: tight functional integration does not automatically entail that an external artifact is literally part of the mind. He asks whether, on her view, a pilot's autopilot becomes part of the pilot's cognition simply because they are tightly coupled during flight, and whether her view forces her to accept that prolonged use of any organizational tool gradually turns it into mental tissue—a sorites-style consequence she should reject. Elena counters by distinguishing what she calls structural entrenchment from mere coupling: unlike an autopilot, which executes intentions formed independently, her taxonomy system actively shapes the conceptual space in which her intentions and judgments arise, functioning more like a scaffold that has become load-bearing in her cognition rather than an auxiliary device. She also accepts that incorporation might be gradual, comparing it to how neural pathways strengthen over time, and asks Marcus whether he denies that prosthetic limbs can gradually become incorporated into the body schema. They turned to me to referee, but I found myself genuinely stuck on whether structural entrenchment provides a principled boundary or merely relabels deep tool dependence.

Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: assess Elena's initial claim about constitutional inclusion, her hippocampus analogy, Marcus's coupling-constitution and autopilot objections, and her structural-entrenchment reply, then rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. Finally, say who is more right overall and identify what, if anything, both are missing about how to draw the boundary between extended cognition and sophisticated tool use.
