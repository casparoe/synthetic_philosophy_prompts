Having spent my career in empirical science before turning to philosophy, I take Benatar's asymmetry between the absence of pleasure and the absence of pain to be more than a reproductive ethics: if absence of suffering is genuinely good for a potential being while absence of pleasure is not bad, then any decision that brings a sentient creature into existence—including ecological policies that sustain wild animal populations—carries an unrecognized moral weight in favor of non-creation. I hold that this extends antinatalist reasoning into conservation and interspecies ethics, suggesting that preserving habitats for reproducing species may be ethically costly rather than unambiguously good. What would genuinely count against this extended position? I want arguments, formal results, or conceptual distinctions that should move me: for instance, whether decision-theoretic baselines that treat non-existence as zero-value rather than positively good dissolve the asymmetry; whether evolutionary considerations about the prevalence of suffering in wild life undermine its empirical premise; or whether a distinction between actual and merely possible persons breaks the inference from asymmetry to pro-extinctionist or antinatalist conclusions. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, indicate what defenders of the asymmetry themselves concede as damaging, and assess honestly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—and, if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a normative claim of this scope.
