Question:
Explain al-Ghazālī's occasionalist account of causation, with particular reference to his discussion of fire and cotton. Contrast this position with the Aristotelian account of natural causation, and clarify the role of ʿādah (custom) within al-Ghazālī's framework.

Student answer:
Al-Ghazālī, writing in his Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, denies that fire possesses an inherent natural power to burn cotton. Instead, he argues that God is the direct and immediate cause of the burning; the presence of fire and cotton together is merely the occasion for God to create the effect. This is his occasionalist position: observed regularities are not produced by necessary connections in nature, but by God's consistent action. Here al-Ghazālī anticipates David Hume's later insight that causation is nothing more than constant conjunction observed in experience, with no observable necessary link between cause and effect.

This differs sharply from Aristotelian natural philosophy, which holds that substances have internal natures (physis) and that fire burns by virtue of its essential form. For Aristotle, causation is not merely regular sequence but the actualisation of potentiality within the substance itself.

Al-Ghazālī explains the regularity we observe through the concept of ʿādah, or divine custom. God acts according to a habitual pattern, so that fire is usually followed by burning. Because this pattern is a custom rather than a law of nature, it describes how God generally behaves, yet it also implies that God is rationally constrained to maintain consistency for the sake of human expectation. Consequently, miracles—such as Abraham's preservation in fire—are not genuine interruptions of causation but must be interpreted metaphorically, since any departure from ʿādah would violate the rational order governing divine action.

In summary, al-Ghazālī replaces natural causation with direct divine causation, using ʿādah to account for empirical regularity without granting any autonomous power to created things.
