As a journalist covering the history of ideas, I am preparing a long-form feature on how early-twentieth-century philosophical treatments of sexuality and domestic life hold up against contemporary institutional experiments that their authors never imagined. I am particularly drawn to Bertrand Russell's Marriage and Morals, where he attempts to decouple sexual intimacy from legal obligation while still defending marriage—understood as a long-term, child-centered partnership—as a necessary social institution. The standard accounts of Russell's view tend to focus on his advocacy for open marriage, his critique of jealousy, or his support for divorce reform, but they rarely examine the conceptual architecture that allows him to defend both individual sexual liberty and the functional necessity of stable pair-bonding for child development.

For the piece, I want to move beyond summarizing Russell's well-known positions. Give me an example of a conceptual issue that arises specifically within his framework when it is applied to a modern cooperative parenting arrangement—say, a multi-parent collective in which three or more adults share rotating primary caregiving responsibilities, maintain separate romantic lives outside the collective, and explicitly reject any central dyadic bond—yet still seek to provide the emotional continuity and economic security Russell associates with successful child-rearing. The example should identify the precise point of friction: whether it is Russell's assumption that economic independence for women requires a single male provider (which collapses in a collective income pool), his belief that emotional continuity for children depends on a stable dyadic attachment (which is distributed rather than absent), or his claim that sexual relations should be free of moral stigma only when they do not undermine the reproductive household (which becomes ambiguous when reproduction is collective but romance is dispersed).

Please construct the case so that it is not merely an anecdote about modern polyamory, but a genuinely conceptual problem that exposes an internal tension in Russell's account—one that a reader could grasp without knowing any specialized literature beyond Russell's own arguments about marriage, morality, and social function.
