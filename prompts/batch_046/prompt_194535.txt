I'm giving a toast at the retiremant dinner for my old high school history teacher, Ms. Chen, 34 years in the classroom. Half the room is family and students who just want to hear she was kind and patient; the other half is colleages who know she assigned Plutarch's Parallel Lives every year but always paired them with modern figures she called "mixed exemplars" — people whose best and worst traits were tangled up so you couldn't copy the good without risking the bad. She hated hagiography and said biography should be moral equipment for living, not a hall of fame, but she also hated turning lives into case studies that let you feel smart about someone else's failures. I'm in philosophy grad school now and I want the toast to actually do structural work with that idea: honor her without making her a saint, and without making her an "interesting case" of complexity, for an audience that includes people who've never heard of Plutarch. Write it so the philosophical point — that we learn from lives we can't fully imitate — shapes what I can and can't say about her.
