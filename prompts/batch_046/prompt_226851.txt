I run a small discussion workshop for grad students in phil and soc, usually about 10-12 people, 90 min. I'm taking a course on social ontology right now and the textbook examples feel too clean, so I want an activity that makes them feel the actual weirdness of money being a social kind — not just "it's made up" but the specifc machinery where value lives in anticipated acceptance, not in individual heads or in the object itself. The idea I want them to run into is the gap between collective agreement and mere summed-up preferences: like, a currency can collapse even when every single person still likes the token, just because the coordination loop snapped.

Design a two-round trading game with assigned roles (merchants, a central bank, outsiders) and small real stakes — maybe candy or points they can trade for a snack at the end. In round one they treat colored chips as obviously valuable; in round two the bank announces a new rule that retroactively changes what counts, and they have to negotiate whether the old chips are still "money." The rules should be arranged so they discover the collapse themselves before anyone names it. Then a debrief that maps each moment onto parts of the construction story — collective intentionality, status functions, the acceptance loop — and be honest about what the simulation can't reproduce (probably the historical/state-coercion side) and how to handle the student who refuses to play along and breaks the illusion. Write this in American English, and in the debrief take a committed position: argue for one specific interpretation of the collapse moment and defend it against the obvious alternative, don't just lay out both sides and leave it open.
