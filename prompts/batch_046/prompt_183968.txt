I am preparing a seminar on Scanlon's contractualism and the well-known difficulty it faces with the aggregation problem, in which the rejection of a principle by one affected party seems to carry no less weight when five are saved than when one is saved. For our literary pairing I have chosen Ursula K. Le Guin's short story 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas', the plot of which I take to be common knowledge: a radiant city whose happiness, art, and prosperity depend entirely on the prolonged, abject suffering of a single child imprisoned in a closet, with every citizen free to walk away into an unknown darkness should they find the bargain intolerable. Rather than asking for a standard comparison between contractualist non-aggregation and utilitarian calculus, I want you to apply a framing I am injecting for this exercise — call it 'compound complaint scaling'. On this view, when a principle permits harm to one person in order to distribute benefits across many, the victim's complaint against that principle does not remain a discrete unit but acquires structural weight because each beneficiary stands in a direct, justifiable relation to the victim; the complaint is thus multiplied not arithmetically, but through the network of relations the principle creates. This is neither orthodox Scanlonian pairwise comparison nor a simple threshold view, and I want you to treat it seriously as a conceptual possibility.

Read the story through this lens, but do not reduce it to an illustration. Where does Le Guin's depiction of the citizens who remain — knowing, accepting, even loving the city — illuminate something about 'reasonable rejectability' that formal argument tends to suppress? Where does the narrative's deliberate withholding of the child's interior life protect the contractualist focus on principles rather than on psychological sympathy, and where does that same withholding risk silencing the very complaint that must be heard for the principle to be tested? Does the option to 'walk away' function as a kind of individual exit that dissolves collective responsibility, and if so, how does that bear on whether the principle governing Omelas could be justified to each person affected? I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations — work from the philosophical concepts and the story's common knowledge alone. Conclude by saying what Le Guin's fable contributes to the aggregation debate that a purely argumentative treatment cannot, and be honest about where the literary texture resists the philosophical reading rather than confirming it.
