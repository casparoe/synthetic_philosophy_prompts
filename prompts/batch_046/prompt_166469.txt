I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have noticed a recurring pattern in my own thinking that I cannot quite name. When I try to explain a quick, low-stakes decision—why I picked one route home or reached for a particular object—the reason often feels as though it arrives just after the choice, like I am drafting a justification in real time rather than reporting an actual cause. By contrast, noticing that I am cold or in pain feels instantaneous and unmediated, with no sense of assembly. I would like a dialogue between two equally careful thinkers who disagree about what this contrast means. One should argue that all introspection is fundamentally this kind of interpretive reconstruction, and that the apparently "direct" bodily cases are merely constructions that occur too quickly for the stitching to be visible. The other should defend the view that there genuinely are two distinct modes—direct registration of states like pain versus narrative reconstruction of motives—and that seeing the seams in the second mode does not discredit the reliability of the first. They should respond to each other's specific examples and distinctions rather than talking past one another, and neither should be made to sound foolish. I am not looking for citations or names of scholars; I want to know whether the reasoning itself holds up.
