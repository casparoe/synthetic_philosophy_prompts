I've been trying to wrap my head around Nietzsche's eternal recurrance since I retired from the lab, and I keep bumping into what feels like a category error but I can't quite name it. He describes this loop where everything comes back identically, and I immediately think of Poincaré recurrence in statistical mechanics — same macrostate eventually, if you wait long enough in a bounded system. But then there's the heat death problem: if the universe is expanding and cooling, the state space isn't bounded, so the recurrence theorem doesn't apply. So I started wondering: is the philosophical version actually a claim about the physical topology of time being circular, or is it purely a normative claim about how to will your own life? Because if it's the first, modern cosmology with the heat death seems to kill it dead; if it's the second, why all the cosmic physics language? I think I'm smuggling in a false presupposition that it's meant to be empirically testable like a physics hypothesis, but then I also came up with this idea of "thermodynamic identity conditions" — like, even if particle positions eventually repeated, if entropy is strictly monotonic across cycles, can we really call them the "same" world? Does philosophy actually talk about this hybrid physical-ethical muddle, or am I just confusing metaphysics with thermodynamics?
