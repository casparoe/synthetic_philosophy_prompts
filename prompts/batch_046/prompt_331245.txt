Since I began designing a reflective decision architecture for an autonomous system, I have become personally preoccupied with whether any agent that trusts its own reasoning can avoid reflective paradox without sacrificing the autonomy that makes self-trust valuable. Last week I discussed this with two colleagues, Mira and Elias, and their exchange reached an impasse that I cannot resolve on my own.

Mira began by stating the classic Löbian obstacle in its strongest form. If an agent is logically strong enough to represent its own belief conditions, and if it genuinely trusts itself—meaning it accepts that whenever it believes P, P is true—then Löb’s theorem allows it to derive belief in P from the mere supposition that belief in P implies P. For Mira, this is not a quirk of omniscient logicians but a structural feature: any agent with sufficient self-modeling capacity and unconditional self-trust collapses into a form of dogmatism or must abandon the consistency conditions required for rational autonomy.

Elias replied that this result applies only to idealized agents with perfect logical omniscience and complete access to their own proof procedures. Real embedded agents, he argued, possess bounded reasoning, approximate self-models, and noisy access to their own cognitive states. They cannot actually execute the Löbian derivation because they lack the precise formal representation of their consistency conditions that the theorem requires. The obstacle is therefore a limit result about impossible agents, not a practical constraint on self-trusting systems.

Mira countered that one can weaken the conditions without escaping the problem. Even a resource-bounded agent can maintain graded self-trust—say, trusting that belief in P raises its probability by a calibrated margin. If the agent can represent this graded reliability within an epsilon-bound, then a modified, probabilistic version of the Löbian argument goes through. The agent either overestimates its reliability and becomes overconfident, or systematically underestimates it and loses the autonomy that self-trust was meant to preserve. For Mira, procedural escapes fail at the meta-level: trusting one’s reasoning methods rather than specific outputs still requires trusting that one’s method-selection is reliable, which reintroduces the same self-referential loop.

Elias responded that this “shadow Löb” argument smuggles omniscience back in through the side door. To trigger even the weakened derivation, the agent must be able to quantify over its own approximate beliefs with enough precision to construct the relevant fixed point—precisely the capacity that bounded agents lack. Moreover, he argued, procedural self-trust need not be grounded in internal logical derivation at all; it can be anchored in external empirical calibration, such as tracking past predictive performance. This breaks the self-referential structure because the justification for trusting the method comes from observed history rather than from a logical theorem about the agent’s own belief system.

Mira’s final reply was that empirical calibration itself becomes self-referential once the agent asks why it should trust its tracking mechanism. If the agent uses its own reasoning to evaluate whether its past-performance tracker is reliable, it has re-entered the loop. The only stable positions, she concluded, are either a deliberately bounded rationality that refuses full reflective access to its own consistency conditions, or an acceptance that complete self-trust is incompatible with logical strength.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each of Mira’s and Elias’s moves, say who is right about the specific claim being made and where, if anywhere, the reasoning overreaches. In particular, address whether the shadow-Löbian argument genuinely avoids omniscience, whether empirical calibration can break the loop without external dependency, and whether procedural self-trust at the meta-level is vulnerable to the same obstacle. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—perhaps a distinction between logical self-trust and practical self-correction, or a role for architectural design that neither has considered. I am especially interested in whether there is a stable intermediate position that preserves meaningful autonomy without falling into either dogmatism or perpetual self-doubt.
