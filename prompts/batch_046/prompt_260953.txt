I have just come out of a long and exhausting argument with a close friend about whether supererogation is a genuine moral category or only an illusion produced by theories that mistakenly treat scalar reasons as if they generated binary obligations. She defended what she called the continuity thesis: moral reasons come in degrees, and drawing a sharp line between “required” and “beyond the call of duty” is as arbitrary as declaring a specific shade of gray to be the boundary of darkness. On her view, acts we call supererogatory—giving away nearly all one’s income, risking one’s life for a stranger—are simply actions supported by very strong reasons that happen to fall on the far end of the scale; calling them “optional but praiseworthy” is just a socially convenient way of excusing non-compliance without admitting that our obligations extend further than comfort allows. I tried to resist by appealing to the asymmetry of blame: we do not blame people for failing to be heroes, and blame tracks obligation. She replied that blame tracks expected compliance within social coordination, not metaphysical duty—societies calibrate demands around moderate burdens, so the absence of blame for heroic failure shows only where our coordination stops, not where reasons disappear.

We circled for hours without resolution, and then she pressed a more specific challenge I could not cleanly answer. She proposed a thought experiment about what she termed distributed heroic costs. Imagine that a single agent can save one hundred lives, but only by bearing a catastrophic personal cost—say, permanent disability. Intuitively, this seems supererogatory: profoundly admirable, yet not required. Now imagine that the same one hundred lives can be saved if one hundred different agents each bear an identical, minute fraction of that catastrophic cost—each suffers only a brief, minor inconvenience that, summed across all hundred, equals the original catastrophe. In the distributed case, it suddenly feels obligatory for each agent to contribute their small share. But if obligation is a function of reasons and costs, and the aggregate reason-to-cost ratio is identical in both scenarios, why does distributing the burden transform a supererogatory act into a collective obligation? If I answer that in the distributed case each individual cost falls below an obligation-threshold, then I seem committed to saying that in the single-agent case the cost is also just an extremely high but non-obligatory reason—collapsing supererogation into extreme optionality. If I answer that collective contexts generate new obligations, I owe an explanation of why aggregation does not generate obligations everywhere—why, for instance, ten people each giving a small sum to save a life does not make it obligatory for each, even though the collective effect equals one person giving the full amount.

Write an essay addressing this controversy. Take a clear and defended position: either uphold the coherence of supererogation against the continuity thesis and the distributed-cost challenge, or argue that supererogation collapses and that moral evaluation should be reconceived in purely scalar terms, with social coordination explaining the appearance of binary obligation. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it with careful reasoning; do not end on an agnostic or purely balanced note.
