I am planning a single ninety-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have just finished an introductory module on consciousness. They arrive with a working notion of qualia—private, ineffable, intrinsic qualities of experience—but have never encountered the claim that this very concept might be incoherent rather than merely mysterious or dispensable. I want to teach the position sometimes called 'quining' qualia: the argument that positing such properties involves a conceptual contradiction or category mistake, not just an empirical gap. Plan the seminar's explanatory arc, not the lecture itself. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual pieces must be in place before the incoherence charge can be heard as anything other than denial of consciousness, and which pieces must follow so it is not collapsed into eliminativism or skepticism. Second, identify the single carrying example or constructed scenario that should do the work—specify what properties it must possess and what properties it must lack to serve this specific argument rather than functioning as a generic intuition pump for functionalism or physicalism. I want the example to avoid the standard zombie or inverted-spectrum tropes; instead, build it around a fully transparent artificial system that emits perfect first-person phenomenological reports with no hidden substrate, but design it precisely so that the absence of 'inner qualia' is not an empirical ignorance claim. Third, map the misconceptions: I expect 'this is just saying consciousness doesn't exist,' 'this is an argument from ignorance about what's inside the box,' 'incoherent means scientifically unexplained,' and 'if this applies to color experience it applies to belief too.' For each, indicate exactly where in the sequence it should be headed off. Fourth, provide one check-for-understanding question I can ask at the end that distinguishes a student who grasps the incoherence claim from one who has merely become skeptical about other minds or thinks I've changed the subject to AI. Finally, state explicitly what I should leave out at this level—historical attributions, neuroscientific detail, specific formalizations—and say so openly.
