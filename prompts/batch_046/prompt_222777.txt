Write a villanelle—nineteen lines, ABA rhyme scheme, with two refrains that alternate and finally converge in the closing quatrain—staging the dispute between evidentialist and permissive accounts of belief, specifically the tension between W. K. Clifford's prohibition on believing without sufficient evidence and William James's defense of a right, in live, forced, and momentous options, to let our passional nature decide. Let the first refrain carry Clifford's maxim in paraphrase: that belief must always be proportioned to available evidence, and that it is wrong everywhere and always to believe on insufficient grounds. Let the second refrain carry James's counter-claim in paraphrase: that when an option is genuinely living, forced, and momentous, and when evidence is necessarily incomplete, we have not only the right but sometimes the duty to believe despite the risk. Do not resolve the poem in either speaker's favor. Instead, use the villanelle's structural logic—the refrains turning in isolation, avoiding direct contact through the middle tercets, then suddenly occupying the same space in the final two lines—to enact the philosophical deadlock: the same imperatives, unchanged in wording, should feel altered in force as each repetition is shadowed by the rival claim. In the central tercets, include a concrete image that captures James's idea that certain beliefs are self-fulfilling or world-creating—belief in a cooperative arrangement that becomes real only because it is adopted—without reducing it to an abstraction. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of belief or ethics of belief recognizes both positions without caricature, and it should stand on its own: a reader unfamiliar with the debate should feel the formal pressure of two irreconcilable demands being forced into proximity by nothing other than rhyme and repetition.
