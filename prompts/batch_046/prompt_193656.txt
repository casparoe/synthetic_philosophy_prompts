I've been working through the Stoic dichotomy of control on my own, and I'm convinced that its modern revival is flattening something important. I think the classical split—what lies in our power versus what does not—made practical sense in a world where causation was mostly immediate and visible, but it falters now that ordinary actions feed into distributed systems: climate dynamics, economic markets, algorithmic feedback loops. So I've sketched an essay arguing that we need a three-part map rather than a binary. First, direct agency: actions with immediate, determinate effects. Second, structural influence: actions that genuinely shape conditions over time without guaranteeing specific outcomes—voting, reducing consumption, changing institutional norms—where we initiate causal paths but do not direct them. Third, pure contingency: events with no causal path running through us at all. My worry is that popular Stoicism tends to absorb structural influence into either direct agency—telling people they should simply do their part—or into pure contingency—insisting that you cannot control the world, only your own reactions—which erases the particular moral weight of actions that are causally real but non-deterministic. Could you comment on these notes? Specifically, I want to know whether the three-part distinction actually captures a gap the binary misses or whether I am only renaming familiar distinctions; whether there is an internal contradiction in caring about structural influence while remaining detached from outcomes; and whether my historical claim—that the binary suited antiquity because causation was more visible then—is defensible or merely romanticizes the past. I am asking for conceptual feedback on the reasoning itself, not for citations, reading lists, or bibliographic guidance.
