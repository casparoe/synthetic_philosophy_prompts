I am running a 75-minute philosophy of science seminar with twelve graduate students who have basic familiarity with logic and scientific methodology but have not studied Douglas Hofstadter's well-known work on strange loops in detail. I want them to physically encounter the breakdown of what is sometimes called an "inviolate level"—the assumption that every hierarchical chain of justification or creation must bottom out in a foundation that does not depend on a higher tier—using M.C. Escher's Drawing Hands as the central metaphor rather than as a mere illustration. To keep the concept from being something they can recall from a standard summary, embed the exercise in a niche framing drawn from meta-scientific replication crises: design a structured, in-class simulation in which participants are divided into three groups, each holding a sealed envelope with a "ground-level empirical claim" about a fictional discovery. Each group must designate one internal member as its "validator," who receives authority not from an outside body but from a certificate co-signed by the very group being validated. Unbeknownst to the participants, the certificates form a hidden loop—Group A validates Group B's source, Group B validates Group C's audit mandate, and Group C issues the charter that authorizes Group A—while a fourth, apparently external "meta-audit" envelope actually derives its instructions from a document inside Group B's envelope. Provide explicit rules, physical materials (index cards, envelopes, a non-monetary reputation-token payoff scheme), and a staged revelation protocol that forces the groups to act on their authority before they discover the circularity. The rules, incentives, and roles should be arranged so that participants run into the coordination failure and the paradox themselves—feeling whom to trust and when to halt the chain—before anyone names it.

Then include a debrief that maps each moment of procedural confusion onto the visual structure of Escher's image, specifying which hand belongs to which hierarchical level and where the loop closes. Be frank about which feature of a true strange loop—specifically, the self-creating origin with no external ground—the simulation necessarily fakes and must be supplied by your verbal framing at the end. Also predict exactly what will go wrong the first time: which group will try to break the loop by appealing to an imagined outside authority, how the meta-audit group will misinterpret its mandate as genuinely external, and how to recover without dissolving the tension. Write the entire response in American English.
