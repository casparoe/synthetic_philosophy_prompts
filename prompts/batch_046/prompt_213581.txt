I've been working through some problems in algorithmic contract design, and I've come to hold a fairly strong view about normative bargaining benchmarks. Specifically, I think that for any convex, compact utility-possibility set with clearly defined disagreement points, the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution should be treated as the primary normative benchmark rather than merely one rival to the Nash bargaining solution. My reasoning is that its individual monotonicity axiom blocks the kind of strategic inflation of fallback positions that becomes especially dangerous when bargaining is mediated by automated protocols—where agents can programmatically adjust their outside options—while Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives allows precisely that kind of manipulation without changing the outcome. I also think the proportional consistency built into Kalai-Smorodinsky captures something important about fairness under heterogeneous contributions that the symmetric Nash product obscures.

What would count against this view? I don't just want a catalog of standard objections; I want to know which observations, formal results, or conceptual distinctions should actually move me away from treating Kalai-Smorodinsky as the default. For instance, are there well-constructed bargaining environments—perhaps with non-convex feasible sets, with more than two agents, or where disagreement points themselves are endogenous to the protocol—where individual monotonicity produces outcomes that are intuitively perverse or strategically unstable? Has the monotonicity property, rather than preventing manipulation, been shown to introduce a different vulnerability, say to misreporting of the feasible set itself? I'd also like an honest assessment of whether defenders have qualified the view so heavily—restricting it to two-person convex cases with exogenous disagreement points—that it has become unfalsifiable in practice, and if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical weakness or just proper domain restriction. Finally, are there results in cooperative game theory or in applied mechanism design—perhaps involving iterative negotiation or boundedly rational learning dynamics—that show the Kalai-Smorodinsky point is not the one actually reached by real agents, and does that matter for its normative status? Please rank the potential defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed defender would concede as genuinely counting against the position from what they would dismiss as outside proper scope, and include whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that is a problem for a benchmark of this kind.
