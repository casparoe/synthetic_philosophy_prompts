hey, i'm a phd student in philosophy of mind working on intentional stance stuff with ai, and i've hit a conceptual snag that i can't tell is already named. there's this pattern i keep seeing in debates about whether llms have beliefs or knowledge, and i want to check if it's a recognized failure mode or if i'm just being pedantic.

here's the concrete situation. people run a model through a battery of prompts in some domain - say, moral reasoning or basic physics - and it responds consistently with outputs that imply belief P. then someone shows that if you change the framing, maybe embed the question in a fiction or add a contradictory premise, the model outputs not-P. with a human interlocutor, that kind of instability usually counts against attributing a stable belief: you'd say they don't really hold P, or that their belief is weak and context-bound. but with the model, defenders often preserve the attribution by treating the contradictory outputs as surface-level noise, prompt artifacts, or preformance errors that don't reach the underlying representational state. meanwhile the consistent outputs are taken as direct evidence for the belief. so confirming behavior proves the mental state, but disconfirming behavior only proves there's interference - never that the state is absent.

i've seen this happen with attributions of deception, too: a model produces misleading outputs in some contexts, and people debate whether it's 'really' deceptive, but the contradictory cases - where it's honest - don't get used to deny deception; they just get explained away as the deception being context-dependent. it feels like there's a structural asymmetry in how we apply the intentional stance to artifical systems compared to people, but i don't know if that's just my bias talking.

does this have an established name? i know about the intentional stance itself, and about stochastic parrots and the usual debates, but i'm asking about the specific argumentative move, not the big picture. is it a variant of confirmation bias, or is there something in the philosophy of psychology about protected mental-state attributions? if there's no term, can you help me carve out exactly what it is and isn't - like, how is it different from ordinary interpretive charity, and what are the boundary cases where it collapses into just being reasonable flexibility? please don't use headings or bullet points or bold stuff, just continuous prose like an email. also don't need citations or a reading list, i just want to know if i'm reinventing the wheel. thanks!
