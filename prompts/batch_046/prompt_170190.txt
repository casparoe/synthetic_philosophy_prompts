I'm a software engineer with a background in distributed systems, currently auditing a graduate seminar on philosophy of history without much formal training in the field. The instructor keeps returning to what she calls Whig history — the tendency to narrate the past as a more or less inevitable march toward present institutions, selecting predecessors for modern liberal democracy, science, or capitalism and treating alternative paths as failed deviations rather than as complete worlds that made sense on their own terms. I understand the broad idea, but I want to see whether it survives a rigorous translation into the conceptual vocabulary I actually work with every day: Git-style version control, dependency trees, merge strategies, rebasing, cherry-picking, and the distinction between a repository's raw commit graph and its cleaned-up presentation history.

Please restate both the phenomenon itself and the standard philosophical objections to it — especially the normative charge that this framing illegitimately judges past actors by present values and collapses genuine contingency into false necessity — using only the machinery of distributed version control and dependency resolution. Be precise: for instance, if Whig history treats a dead-end political movement as an obsolete branch, say exactly which Git operation corresponds and whether that operation carries the same evaluative load. Then provide the residue report I am actually after: which elements of the historiographical critique translate without loss, which require distortion (say, treating a contingent merge conflict as a deterministic resolution), and whether there is something structurally essential — perhaps the moral judgment of ancestors, or the idea that history has a direction — that simply has no counterpart in how software repositories manage their past. I am not asking for an analogy to win an argument; I want to know whether my suspicion that Whig history is essentially rebasing human affairs onto the master branch of modernity is philosophically adequate or whether the residue shows my vocabulary is missing something the debate requires.
