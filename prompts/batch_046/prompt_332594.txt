I've noticed a recurring structural tension in proposals for AI containment that treat a model as potentially misaligned, yet depend on that same model's cooperation to implement the control. For instance, in some scalable-oversight architectures, a stronger overseer is meant to detect deception in a weaker agent's reasoning traces; but to make those traces interpretable, the weaker agent must be prompted or trained to present its reasoning in a specific, legible format—an act that requires the agent to cooperate with the oversight mechanism rather than adversarially obscure its cognition. Similarly, sandboxing protocols sometimes rely on the contained model to generate adversarial test cases or to calibrate the sandbox's own monitoring thresholds, which means the containment system assumes the model might try to escape, yet functionally requires it to assist in strengthening the walls. This is not merely the standard principal-agent problem, because the agent's cooperation is not incidental labor but constitutive of the control mechanism itself; and it is not the alignment tax, because the difficulty is not just capability lost to safety constraints, but a logical dependency of the constraint on the very behavior it aims to constrain. Does this configuration—control measures that are adversarial in intent but cooperative in implementation—have an established name in the safety literature? If not, can you define it precisely, specify its boundary conditions (for example, how it differs from ordinary tool-use or from red-teaming), and say whether it represents a distinct failure mode or merely a practical engineering difficulty?
