Removing the memorial would publicly acknowledge the harm and might provide some relief to descendants of those exploited, but Mara refuses to vote for removal. Here is the student's answer I am evaluating.

Mara's reasoning draws on both parts of Williams's critique of impartial moral theory, but she applies them in essentially the same way. The integrity objection holds that utilitarianism and similar frameworks wrongly require agents to treat their ground projects—those commitments that give life meaning—as fungible items in a calculus of overall welfare. Mara clearly invokes this when she insists that her commitment to communal memory is not "one preference among others" and should not be sacrificed merely because removing the memorial might reduce suffering.

She also invokes the "one thought too many" idea. In Williams's example, a husband saves his wife from drowning; if he pauses to think "it is permissible for me to save her because she is my wife," he has had one thought too many. Mara parallels this exactly: she claims that if she had to tell herself "it is permissible to protect this memorial because my integrity demands it," that reasoning would be alien and excessive. So she is using both arguments.

The two arguments are really two sides of the same coin. The integrity objection describes the structural damage that impartial theories do to ground projects, while "one thought too many" describes the subjective, emotional experience of that damage—the feeling that moral reasoning has become intrusive. The only real difference is that the integrity objection operates at the level of life structure, whereas "one thought too many" operates at the level of immediate practical reasoning. Because Mara's commitment to the memorial is both a ground project and a deeply personal stance, she is correct to invoke both together.

There is one minor limitation. Williams usually illustrates ground projects with private, life-defining pursuits—being an artist, a parent, or a dedicated friend—rather than with civic or institutional commitments. So one might ask whether preserving a public monument counts as a ground project in the strict sense. Still, her core reasoning is sound: she refuses to let an impartial harm-calculus override a commitment that defines her practical identity, and she recognizes that introducing a moral justification for that refusal would be redundant and alienating. Therefore, she correctly applies Williams's framework, though she perhaps overstates the unity of the two arguments.
