I sit on our university's Governance Innovation Panel — I'm also taking a course on institutional ethics this term and this case has come up in both places, which is a bit confusing. We mostly deal with boring procurement rules but this term we've been asked to advise on a pilot scheme for the interdisciplinary research programme. The proposal is to ring-fence about 8% of the pot and allocate it via an internal prediction market: staff and postdocs get reputation credits (not real money) and bet on which proposed projects will hit their targets three years out. There's an audit board that resolves the bets, but here's the twist — the resolution criteria aren't fixed in advance. Each project lists three success dimensions, say publication quality, public engagement, and reproducibility, and bettors can weight them differently in their portfolios. So one person might be betting on high citation impact, another on societal uptake, and the market price ends up being some weird aggregate.

My co-panelist, Dr Chen, thinks this is basically futarchy with extra steps and wants us to recommend approval with conditions. She argues that if we're serious about epistemic democracy we should let beliefs about outcomes drive funding, not just seniority. But another member, Prof Okonkwo, reckons it's a category error — governance isn't forecasting, and turning research priorities into a betting pool collapses plural values into a pseudo-objective price. He also points out that junior staff will just mirror senior bets, so it's not even genuinely epistemic. We need a formal memo for the Vice-Chancellor's office with a clear recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and the reasoning needs to actually address the philosophical worry about whether prediction markets can legitimately replace, even partly, collective deliberation. Please treat Okonkwo's concern seriously, not just dismiss it, and say what principle we'd be adopting either way. Could you also flag the closest analogue in our own precedants? We previously approved a peer-review lottery for small grants, treating review as noisy, but rejected a proposal to rank departments purely by citation metrics. Don't need a bibliography — I just want the argument straight.
