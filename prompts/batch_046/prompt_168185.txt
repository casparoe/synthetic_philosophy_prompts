I'm preparing materials for a graduate seminar on formal epistemology and practical reasoning, and I've been trying to move Hofstadter's idea of jumping out of the system—JOOTSING—away from the standard MIU-puzzle and self-referential mathematics into a domain where the stakes are institutional rather than logical. Specifically, I'm interested in whether an administrative body operating under an explicit, finite rule-set can face a genuinely undecidable case within that system, and then be resolved from the outside without invoking self-reference, infinite regress, or any exotic metaphysics. Could you construct a thought experiment that meets the following specifications?

Design a realistic, non-science-fictional scenario—no longer than a concise paragraph—involving a local historic preservation commission that functions according to an explicit, closed set of rules (call it the P-system) governing what renovation permits may be issued. The rules should be analogous to Hofstadter's transformation rules: they specify permitted operations on permit applications based on formal properties of the proposal, not on substantive values. Present a specific permit application that, according to rigorous internal derivation within the P-system, is neither permitted nor forbidden—genuinely undecidable inside the framework. Then introduce an external agent, such as a state ombudsman, who resolves the case by applying a principle that is not derivable within the P-system and that does not depend on self-reference or an infinite hierarchy of meta-rules. The language should remain ordinary bureaucratic or legal English rather than formal logical notation, and the audience should be people familiar with basic epistemology but not with advanced logic.

After presenting the scenario, please run the check: state the belief or decision at stake, the method used inside the system, and the relevant nearby rule-variations or "nearby worlds" in which the board might have reached a different internal verdict, explaining precisely why the P-system delivers an undecidable result rather than a negative one. Then show how the ombudsman's external principle resolves the case, identify the exact structural feature that makes the jump out possible—whether it is the presence of an external authority, the introduction of a value-laden meta-rule, or something else—and explain what happens if that feature is removed: does the dispute collapse back into an ordinary interpretive disagreement that the P-system could eventually settle? Finally, include an honest note on how a defender of the P-system's completeness would attack the case—specifically, how they might argue that the ombudsman has not solved an internal problem but merely changed the subject by substituting a different framework altogether. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want this to stand on its own conceptual architecture.
