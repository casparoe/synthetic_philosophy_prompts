As a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently taken up philosophy in earnest, I am designing an upper-level examination for an interdisciplinary seminar on ancient philosophy and philosophy of science. I want a single essay question—answerable in ninety minutes, at roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words—that treats Aristotle’s defense of natural slavery in the Politics not as a historical artifact but as a conceptual problem that must survive contact with modern biology. Specifically, the question should require students to confront whether Aristotle’s claim that certain human beings are “naturally” slaves—lacking the full deliberative part of the soul—can be coherently maintained once one rejects fixed essential natures for behavioral traits and instead adopts a framework of phenotypic plasticity and environmentally triggered caste differentiation, such as the caste systems observed in eusocial insects where morphology and behavior are developmentally plastic rather than essentially predetermined. The prompt must be unanswerable by mere summary of Aristotle’s text; it should force the student to determine whether the normative conclusion of natural slavery can be separated from Aristotle’s metaphysics of substance and teleological function, and if not, whether the collapse of essentialist biology undermines the argument entirely.

Please provide the following, in a formal register appropriate for an academic examination:

1. The exam question itself.
2. A model answer of approximately seven hundred words.
3. A marking scheme totaling twenty-five marks, structured around at least five specific conceptual moves or distinctions that distinguish an excellent answer from a mediocre one. For each criterion, state the marks assigned, describe the precise move required, and include a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cause a candidate to lose marks there.

Do not include any bibliography, reading list, or request for citations; the answer should stand on its own reasoning. Avoid framing the issue in terms of standard introductory labels that a student could recite without engaging the biological analogy I have introduced.
