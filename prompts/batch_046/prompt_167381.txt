I have been recording the judgments of a senior colleague in our comparative-historical philosophy seminar regarding whether particular societies should be said to have "collapsed" or merely to have undergone severe but continuous transformation. Her assessments, offered without explicit theoretical framing, are these.

First, the Late Bronze Age Eastern Mediterranean (c. 1200 BCE): she judges this a genuine collapse, because the palatial administrative systems, Linear B literacy, and interregional exchange networks dissolved within a generation, and no functionally equivalent successor structures emerged within the same cultural framework.

Second, the Classic Maya lowlands (c. 800–950 CE): she denies collapse, insisting that although large-scale monument construction and divine kingship ended in the southern lowlands, local polities persisted in the north, rural agricultural life continued uninterrupted, and there was no sharp demographic break; what occurred was a political reconfiguration, not a societal dissolution.

Third, Rapa Nui (Easter Island, c. 1600–1800): she affirms collapse, citing the rapid deforestation, population crash, abrupt termination of the moai-building cult, and the replacement of the old priestly elite by a warrior-based Birdman cult—an internal rupture that destroyed the prior collective self-ordering system.

Fourth, the Western Roman Empire (fifth century CE): she denies collapse, arguing that Roman law, Christian ecclesiastical organization, and urban economic life persisted under barbarian kings who deliberately adopted Roman administrative forms; the imperial office disappeared, but the societal framework it governed did not.

Fifth, the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 1900–1300 BCE): she denies collapse, noting the absence of destruction layers or invasion horizons, and interpreting the urban decline as a gradual, centuries-long eastward shift of population and material culture into the Gangetic plain.

Next term she will examine the case of the Merina Kingdom of Imerina (Madagascar) following the French annexation of 1897: the monarchy was abolished, Queen Ranavalona III exiled, the aristocratic andriana caste dispossessed, the state religion suppressed, and Antananarivo occupied; yet the highland population experienced no demographic crisis, the rice-terrace agricultural system continued with minimal disruption, the Malagasy language and local kinship structures remained intact, and French rule operated largely through indirect administration that preserved indigenous rural institutions as instruments of governance.

Predict her verdict on whether the Merina case constitutes a societal collapse. Then identify at least two plausible interpretive principles—consistent with her prior five judgments—that would yield divergent answers here, specifying precisely what each principle predicts about this case. State which principle you believe she is implicitly running, defend your best guess about her actual verdict, and propose one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the competing principles.
