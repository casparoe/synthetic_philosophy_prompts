I was chatting with my roommate who's helping design a museum exibit on 19th-century global history, and she showed me these new curatorial guidelines that some European boards have adopted. Apparently they now require that any narrative about, say, the 1815 Tambora eruption or the spread of cholera in the 1800s treat those events not as background conditions but as "historical actors" with their own "causal weight"—the volcano "restructured trade routes," the pathogen "reshaped urban labor markets"—and they actually ask curators to assign an explicit "agency weighting" to non-human forces alongside human ones. At the same time, the same framework insists we avoid "environmental determinism" and keep human institutions as the primary locus of moral responsibility for outcomes. I'm trying to figure out what philosophical commitments this practice actually presupposes, and I'm getting stuck.

If we're giving germs and climate shifts this kind of ontological parity in our explanatory framework—treating them as agents with causal profiles rather than mere conditions—are we implicitly committing to something like an actor-network ontology where agency is just distributed relational effect? Or can a traditional, human-centered philosophy of history accomodate this language as purely metaphorical without collapsing? And there's another tension I can't shake: the guidelines demand causal democracy between human and non-human actors, yet they still want us to blame governments, not the climate, for failed adaptation policies. Does that mean the practice strictly presupposes a dualist theory of causation—one kind for explanation, another for moral attribution—or is it philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival theories of responsibility and agency? I want someone to reconstruct the implicit epistemology and ontology here, say whether the practice actually needs this "agency weighting" idea or could survive with a straightforward probabilistic-causal framing, and tell me what would have to change in historiography and public policy if we dropped the actor-language but kept all the factual claims about environmental impacts.
