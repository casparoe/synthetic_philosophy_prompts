Some interpreters take these metaphors to support opposite conclusions about whether virtù is primarily an institutional capacity—designing dikes, canals, and constitutional rotation—or an embodied, improvisational disposition belonging to the prince himself. Student answer:

Machiavelli treats fortune as an external force that determines roughly half of human affairs, leaving the other half to free will or virtù. This is why he compares fortune to a river: like a flood, it can overwhelm a state that has not prepared dikes and canals in advance. The metaphor shows that virtù consists mainly in institutional foresight—building constitutional mechanisms that channel fortune’s energy before it strikes. By contrast, the image of fortune as a woman who must be beaten and struck down applies mainly to the founding moment of a state, not to its ordinary operation. From this I conclude that in a well-ordered republic such as Rome, collective institutional rotation serves as a substitute for individual princely virtù. Because Rome’s constitutional cycle distributes authority among offices and classes, the republic’s collective structures absorb the half of action that fortune controls, leaving the other half to be managed by law. Therefore, a republic with robust rotation is effectively immune to fortune’s swings, since its institutions cover both halves: the institutional half replaces personal virtù, and the legal half replaces fortune’s randomness. Machiavelli praises Rome precisely because its founding violence (Romulus) was eventually replaced by this self-correcting machinery, which eliminates the need for further personal intervention by a prince.
