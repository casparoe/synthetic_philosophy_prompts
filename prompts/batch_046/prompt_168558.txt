As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind and bioethics, I have been trying to make sense of the proposal—often associated with David Pearce—called the hedonistic imperative, which holds that we should use biotechnology to eliminate suffering and engineer stable gradients of well-being. Coming from a background in control systems and evolutionary biology, I find myself puzzled by what looks like a category mistake in the underlying argument. In contemporary neuroscience, negative affect is not simply an unpleasant residue of evolutionary history but an adaptive error-correction signal: within predictive processing frameworks, aversive states carry informational content that updates an organism’s interoceptive models of threat and reward. If one were to recalibrate the hedonic set-point through germline editing so that all phenomenal experience lies on a positive gradient, one would not merely reduce suffering; one would appear to excise the very negative feedback term that allows the brain to distinguish harmful from beneficial environments. Without that signal, risk-evaluation and belief-updating would seem to collapse, analogous to disabling the error channel in a feedback controller. Given this functional architecture, is the hedonistic imperative not essentially a proposal to disable the brain’s primary adaptive signaling system? Or do its defenders show how a post-suffering architecture can preserve the informational and motivational functions of negative valence without retaining its phenomenal character?
