As a journalist covering science and public-health policy, I repeatedly have to evaluate study protocols and regulatory proposals that justify real harms to specific people by appealing to large aggregate welfare gains. I want a reusable, fifteen-minute decision procedure grounded in the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarian aggregation—the idea that treating benefits and burdens as fungible across different lives violates the moral reality that each person is a distinct center of value, not a vessel for total utility. To keep this from becoming a generic ethics checklist, I want the procedure to turn on two specific operational tests I have sketched: a compensatory-linkage check, which asks whether any burden imposed on an individual is tied within that same life trajectory to a proportionate benefit rather than being traded across lives; and an identity-swap invariance check, which asks whether the justification for the proposal still holds if the specific individuals who bear the burdens and those who receive the benefits are randomly permuted. Please construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions with explicit stopping rules—indicating where a negative answer halts the process and demands either rejection or an explicit value-based justification—followed by a judgment-call section listing exactly which distinctions the procedure cannot resolve without human decision, such as conflicts over personal identity across time or the moral status of future versus present persons.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three cases I might actually cover in my reporting: first, a proposed forty-person neurostimulation trial in which thirty-five participants are expected to show durable cognitive improvement, but five face a documented risk of irreversible alterations to emotional temperament with no therapeutic pathway available for them; second, a pandemic-preparedness challenge study in which healthy young volunteers are deliberately exposed to a novel pathogen to accelerate vaccine development that would primarily protect elderly populations, with the volunteers gaining only minimal direct medical benefit; and third, a hospital triage protocol during a surge crisis that diverts critical-care resources away from five identifiable patients with poor prognoses in order to save twenty patients with good prognoses, where the committee knows the names of all affected parties. For each case, run the questions in order, mark where the procedure yields a clear failure, where it stops for value judgment, and highlight the challenge study as the case likely to produce an answer I will find professionally unwelcome—specifically, that it treats a widely defended research model as ethically deficient despite its enormous projected aggregate benefit. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do, including whether it applies differently when the beneficiaries are statistical future persons rather than present ones, and flag any step that depends on a contested philosophical premise about the boundaries of a single life.
