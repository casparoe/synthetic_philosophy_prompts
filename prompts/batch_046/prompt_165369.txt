I sit on the evaluation committee of a regional public-arts foundation that is rewriting its grant rubric for the coming funding cycle. One member has proposed adopting Leo Tolstoy's account of art as our primary criterion: art's distinctive value lies not in technical excellence or conceptual novelty but in its capacity to transmit the artist's genuinely felt emotion to an audience, functioning like an emotional infection, and works that lack this transmission should be ineligible regardless of other merits. A second member wants to strengthen the proposal by requiring reviewers to assess whether the artist's emotional state at creation was sincerely experienced rather than performed, since Tolstoy treats such sincerity as a necessary condition of true infection. A third member objects that this standard is unworkable for a public body: it asks reviewers to judge private mental states and audience emotional responses without intersubjective measures, it risks systematically excluding abstract and conceptual work that does not aim at direct emotional transmission, and it replaces pluralistic cultural funding with the enforcement of a single aesthetic theory. The board must vote next quarter on whether to incorporate an infectious-sincerity standard into the rubric, reject it outright, or adopt a modified version with safeguards. Please draft the committee's formal decision memo recommending a concrete course of action together with an implementation framework if adoption is advised. The memo should present the strongest Tolstoyan argument honestly rather than as a strawman, address whether reviewer subjectivity and the exclusion of non-emotional art can be contained, and include clear reasoning that the dissenting commissioners could respond to directly. Keep it to about two pages of institutional prose suitable for circulation before the vote; do not include a bibliography or citation requests, as I will supply references separately.
