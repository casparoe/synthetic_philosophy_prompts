You serve on the Scientific Review Panel of the National Office for Long-Range Resource Planning. The panel must decide whether to renew ten-year funding for the Malthusian Dynamics Project, a longitudinal study that applies classical population-resource pressure models to regional water and grain reserves. Over four decades, the project's core prediction—that demographic pressure would trigger critical resource shortfalls by 1995 and again by 2015—failed both times, largely because of agricultural innovations that the original framework did not anticipate. The team now defends the model as right but early: they maintain that the underlying exponential dynamics remain intact, cite recent satellite data showing accelerating soil depletion, and argue that a newly introduced adaptive-technology buffer parameter merely shifts the crisis curve temporally without altering its functional form. Critics on the panel reply that each failure has been rescued post-hoc—the buffer's activation conditions were specified only after the missed predictions occurred—and that this violates the Office's 2018 Principle of Predictive Accountability, which requires long-run models either to show calibrated accuracy within a twenty-year window or to demonstrate systematically rescuable failure rather than ad hoc immunization. They frame the defense as a classic Duhem-Quine auxiliary-hypothesis maneuver that protects the theoretical core by eroding its testability.

Draft the panel's formal decision memo. It should recommend either renewal with binding conditions or termination with a transition plan, state the bottom-line recommendation clearly, provide reasoning the researchers could actually respond to, and treat honestly the strongest opposing consideration—specifically, whether a theory that consistently misses its timeline can retain scientific value if its structural mechanism may eventually be vindicated. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Limit the memo to one page, no more than 400 words.
