I'm a mathematician fluent in categorical logic and homotopy type theory—objects, morphisms, functors, toposes, the univalence axiom, dependent types, subobject classifiers, and fibered categories—but with minimal background in analytic metaphysics. Restate the three principal contemporary positions on the problem of universals—realism (multiply instantiable universals exist independently), nominalism (only particulars exist; universals are mental or linguistic constructions), and trope theory (only particularized properties exist, with similarity explained by resemblance among tropes)—entirely within that vocabulary. For each position, specify what plays the role of a universal (e.g., a global object, a type in a universe, a term of a classifier, an equivalence class), what plays the role of instantiation (e.g., a point, an element, a section of a fibration, a map into a classifier), and what plays the role of resemblance or sameness of property (e.g., equality of types, homotopy equivalence, isomorphism in a fiber, a path in an identity type). Then provide the residue report: which metaphysical claims translate cleanly, which require distortion (for instance, treating trope resemblance as literal path-equality forces the trope theorist either into realism about equivalence classes or into a collapse of particularity), and which have no expression at all—especially whether the realist's claim that numerically distinct particulars share literally the same universal can be captured without assuming that equality of properties implies equality of their carriers. In particular, address whether using the univalence axiom to identify equivalence with equality dissolves the trope theorist's insistence on distinct-but-resembling properties, and whether nominalism about universals can be modeled as a restriction to a base category with no global sections except constants. Conclude by assessing whether the untranslatable residue shows that categorical logic lacks metaphysically necessary distinctions, or whether those distinctions were artifacts of an imprecise informal vocabulary that formalization correctly eliminates.
