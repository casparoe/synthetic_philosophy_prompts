I'm defending a thesis at next week's interdisciplinary seminar: that the common-knowledge-of-rationality assumption in epistemic game theory should be replaced by a finite-stabilization condition, where agents reach a fixed point of mutual belief expectation after finitely many iterations determined by the informational structure, rather than requiring an infinite hierarchy. I argue this preserves predictions in the muddy-children and coordinated-attack puzzles while being more plausible for bounded reasoners, and that it addresses a conceptual over-idealization rather than serving as a mere technical relaxation.

The audience is precisely: two modal epistemologists who treat CKR as conceptually indispensable and will defend the infinite-hierarchy view; three philosophers of science skeptical that game-theoretic models bear on actual reasoning; one cognitive psychologist who studies bounded rationality and will demand empirical grounding; and a decision-theoretist chair familiar with updateless reasoning but neutral on epistemic foundations.

Prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they'll arise, phrased as each subgroup would phrase them. For each: a concise reply suitable for a two-minute exchange, a note on where that reply is weakest, and whether I should concede part of the point upfront. What should I concede in my opening remarks to lower defenses? And identify the single objection—likely involving collapse back to CKR under standard completeness assumptions—that I'm hoping the chair doesn't ask, with how I'd handle it if pressed.
