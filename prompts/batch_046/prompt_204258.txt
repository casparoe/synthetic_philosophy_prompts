Interpreters of Kripkenstein's rule-following considerations have been deadlocked for decades. One camp reads the skeptical paradox as demonstrating that there is no fact of the matter about what a speaker means by an expression—say, plus versus quus—unless that fact is grounded in communal practices of correction and assent; on this reading, the skeptical solution is a positive thesis about how semantic normativity is socially constituted, not a concession to meaning-skepticism. The opposing camp holds that the paradox rests on an illicit demand for a metaphysically basic, non-circular account of intentional content, and that once we distinguish between the normative determinacy of a rule (whether there is a correct next step) and the explanatory determinacy of a theory (whether we can specify the implementing mechanisms without circularity), dispositional or causal-tracking accounts survive unscathed. The literature has cycled through refinements—finite dispositional states, ceteris paribus clauses, teleosemantic supplements—without either side conceding ground.

My working theory is that the stalemate is not primarily about the ontology of meaning but about competing standards for philosophical explanation: specifically, whether any adequate account of rule-following must preserve an irreducible first-personal authority over one's own intentions, or whether a fully third-personal, functional characterization of communal correction practices is sufficient. I want a diagnosis, not a survey of positions or a verdict on which interpretation of Kripke is correct. Is the disagreement verbal—are the sides using meaning or normativity in different senses? Is it substantive but hidden—do they differ not about semantics but about what counts as a non-circular explanation, or about whether the first-person perspective is an explanandum or an explanans? Or is there a concealed formal or empirical question—perhaps about the learnability of infinite rules from finite training, or about the logical structure of normative ascription—that could, if settled, dissolve the impasse?

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement concretely, engaging with what each side actually says about error, community, and the conditions for correct application. Give an honest assessment of whether the deadlock is a sign that the underlying question is ill-posed, or merely that it is genuinely hard.
