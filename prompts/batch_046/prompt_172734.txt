I am a retired molecular biologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working through classical Chinese philosophy without formal training in either philology or analytic metaphysics. Next month I present at an interdisciplinary workshop on death and personal identity hosted by a philosophy department. My audience will be mostly analytic philosophers of personal identity and philosophy of mind—people for whom death is a boundary problem for the persistence conditions of a subject—plus two scholars of comparative philosophy who know the received texts well and are reflexively suspicious of anachronistic framing, and a few philosophers of biology who will scrutinize any scientific analogy I import. I want to defend the following thesis: in the chapters conventionally associated with Zhuangzi's reflections on death—notably the discussion in what is usually called the "Perfect Happiness" section and the well-known butterfly dream passage—the text should be read not as counseling emotional equanimity or mystical union, but as advancing a process-ontological claim. Specifically, I argue that Zhuangzi's notion of hua, transformation, describes death as a continuous redistribution of organizational form within an open field of qi, rather than as the termination of a bounded substance. Drawing on my background in open biological systems, I propose that this undermines the analytic framing of death as "the end of my experiences," because it denies that there is a stable, persistent "my" to which termination applies in the first place. I do not need a bibliography or reading list; I need a strategic prep sheet for the Q&A. Please list the eight or so objections I will actually face, ordered by how likely they are to arise and how much damage each would do, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would actually use. For each objection, provide a concise reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes, a note on where that reply is weakest or soft, and an indication of what I should concede up front rather than defend. Include the one reply I must land cleanly if the proposal is to survive, the objections that have real force that I should acknowledge rather than deflect, and the single question I am hoping nobody asks—phrased exactly as a hostile interlocutor would put it. I am particularly concerned about charges of anachronism, of equivocating between metaphysical and psychological senses of "self," and of misusing biological concepts such as organizational closure and thermodynamic openness; please make sure these appear in the appropriate form. Do not include citations, references to specific secondary literature, or requests for further reading.
