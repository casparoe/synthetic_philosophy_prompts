For our seminar's consistency audit, I have drafted the following profile of my commitments on the ethics of space colonization, and I ask you to examine whether the set is jointly tenable.

My commitments are these. First, I hold that complex, self-organizing biospheres that evolved independently on celestial bodies possess direct, non-instrumental moral value; we ought not radically alter or destroy them, even if doing so would yield substantial benefits to sentient beings. Second, I hold that simple geological and chemical formations possess no intrinsic moral value whatsoever and may be used freely as resources. Third, I accept a strict intergenerational Lockean proviso applied to extraterrestrial resources: an appropriation is legitimate only if it leaves every actual and potential future rational agent with prospects no worse than they would have faced absent that appropriation. Fourth, I believe that establishing self-sustaining human settlements beyond Earth is not merely permitted but a collective moral imperative, given the existential fragility of single-planet conscious life. Fifth, I reject any state or corporate sovereignty over celestial bodies, yet I also maintain that settlers may acquire exclusive, transferable property rights over specific territories and resources they have made productive through labor and investment.

Please audit this set. Identify which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only appear to conflict but dissolve upon inspection, and for each genuine tension rate its severity, specify what the cheapest consistent revision would be and what it costs. Then tell me which single commitment, if revised or dropped, would clean up the greatest number of tensions with the least damage to the overall view.
