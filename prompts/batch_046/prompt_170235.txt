I'm working through Leibniz's rationalist ethics for a seminar paper, and I've constructed an argument that starts from his definition of justice and ends up dissolving moral obligation altogether, which seems like it proves too much. Here are the steps. (1) For Leibniz, justice is not conventional but an eternal truth of reason: it is the charity of the wise, meaning the rational ordering of goods according to a calculus that promotes the greatest perfection of rational beings. (2) Because every human soul is a monad that expresses the entire universe from its own point of view, complete wisdom would entail a distinct perception of all moral relations simultaneously; moral error is therefore nothing more than confused perception, not a free choice against reason. (3) But moral obligation, on any plausible account, applies only to agents who have the capacity both to understand the law and to act accordingly—ought implies can. (4) If justice requires the distinct perception available only to fully wise agents, then agents with confused perceptions lack the cognitive capacity required to be subjects of justice; they fall outside the scope of moral law entirely, like stones or infants. (5) Meanwhile, agents who do possess sufficient wisdom already act justly by necessity, since reason cannot motivate against its own clear perceptions. (6) So every agent is either outside moral law due to confusion, or inside it but necessarily compliant; there is no room for the kind of free, blameworthy wrongdoing that Leibniz's theodicy needs to explain. (7) One might respond that moral obligation is hypothetical: we are bound to pursue wisdom itself. But if the disposition to pursue wisdom is itself part of the pre-established harmony distributed among monads, then the obligation binds only those already inclined to fulfill it, leaving the confused with no genuine duty. Where does this argument fail? I would most like to preserve (1) and (3), but I want to know exactly which step contains the hidden slide—whether it is an equivocation on capacity, a scope error about who falls under the law, or something else—and what giving up that step costs for a Leibnizian.
