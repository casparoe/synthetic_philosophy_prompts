I'm trying to determine what I actually think about dirty hands in politics, not by citing authorities but by testing my reactions to concrete cases. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1. A defense minister authorizes a drone strike that she knows will kill three non-combatants, because intelligence shows it is the only way to stop an imminent attack that would kill hundreds. She authorizes it; the attack is stopped. Verdict: the act is morally wrong in itself—those three deaths are on her hands—but it is justified. She should not celebrate; she owes public acknowledgment and some form of moral repair (perhaps personal sacrifice or formal apology), but she should not be criminally punished.

Case 2. A president, facing a pandemic, lies to the legislature and the public about supply shortages to prevent panic-buying that would deprive hospitals. The deception succeeds; no one dies because of the lie, and she reveals the truth six months later. Verdict: dirty hands, but the stain is lighter than in Case 1. No rights were violated, only trust was temporarily broken. She owes eventual transparency, but the moral repair required is less severe—perhaps institutional, not personal.

Case 3. A legislator accepts a large private bribe in exchange for casting the deciding vote on a universal healthcare bill that saves tens of thousands of lives. Verdict: not an instance of justified dirty hands at all. The democratic process itself has been corrupted for private gain. Even though the outcome is good, the wrong is institutional corruption, not agent-contamination, and it is never permissible.

Case 4. A mayor, acting on credible but unconfirmed intelligence of a bomb at a political rally, orders police to arrest every attendee without probable cause and detain them for forty-eight hours. The bomb is found; no one dies. But dozens of innocent people are injured during arrest, and many suffer lasting trauma. Verdict: this is worse than Case 1, and I think it is not justified. She harmed the very people she was protecting, treating them as means rather than accepting collateral damage to outsiders. The contamination is severe and unjustified.

Case 5. A diplomat secretly promises an authoritarian ally that her government will remain silent on its human rights record for one year, in exchange for the ally releasing fifty political prisoners. Verdict: justified dirty hands. By remaining silent she becomes complicit in injustice, so she is agent-contaminated; but she does not corrupt her own country's institutions, since secrecy in foreign bargaining is normal. She owes moral repair—perhaps public advocacy for the prisoners once the deal expires.

Case 6. A candidate, knowing her opponent is genuinely corrupt and dangerous, forges documents that appear to show he committed a specific crime he did not commit. She leaks them; he loses; she wins; the forgery is discovered after the election. Verdict: not justified. This feels different from Case 2: both involve deception for a good outcome, but here she has corrupted the mechanisms of justice and democratic choice from within. It is institutional corruption disguised as political strategy.

Case 7. A governor breaks an explicit campaign pledge—"no new taxes"—to invoke emergency powers and fund flood defenses after an unprecedented storm. Verdict: not dirty hands at all. A promise made in one context can be overridden by moral emergency; no wrong is done to anyone, only a commitment is revised.

The tensions I cannot resolve: Case 1 and Case 4 both involve harming innocents to save lives, yet I treat one as justified and the other not. Case 2 and Case 6 both involve deception for public good, yet I treat one as acceptable (with repair) and the other as impermissible corruption. My tentative distinction is that dirty hands applies only when (a) the agent personally violates a moral constraint she bears, producing agent-contamination that demands acknowledgment or sacrifice, and (b) the wrong does not corrupt the institutional norms—democratic deliberation, rule of law, fair process—that make political authority legitimate. If (b) fails, it is not dirty hands but corruption, never justified. If (a) fails but (b) holds, there may be a broken promise or strategic omission, but no moral stain.

Can this two-part framework accommodate all seven verdicts without contrivance? If not, which verdict should I drop—Case 4's condemnation, Case 6's condemnation, or Case 2's acceptance? And what does the framework predict for cases I have not listed: a leader ordering torture of a captured terrorist to locate a bomb; a politician secretly diverting treaty-bound military aid to a democratic insurgency to prevent genocide; and a cabinet minister publicly denouncing her own party's policy after voting for it in private? Please state the principle precisely, identify the clause that motivates each case, note where the reply is weakest, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn.
