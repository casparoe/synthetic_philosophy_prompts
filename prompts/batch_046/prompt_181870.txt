I'm a phd student in philosophy of mind and I'm getting pulled into the insect welfare debates for a side project. I've been convinced by the scale argument — you know, the idea that even if there's only a tiny chance insects are sentient, the sheer numbers mean the expected suffering is massive, maybe dominant. But I want to stress-test it properly, not just list objections I've seen on blogs.

Specifically, I think the argument survives the usual "we don't know enough about consciousness" pushback because it's probabilistic, and it also survives the "insects don't suffer because they lack a cortex" reply because that just lowers the prior rather than breaking the logic. So my position is: the scale argument is structurally sound and should shift our priorities unless we can show either that the prior is essencially zero (not just low) or that aggregation across individuals fails for some non-obvious reason.

What would actually count against this? I want the defeaters ranked: which ones are fatal versus merely awkward, and which ones defenders of the scale argument (including me) should honestly concede versus dismiss. I'm especally interested in whether there's a hidden modal mistake in going from "expected value is high" to "moral priority is high" — like, maybe high-variance, low-confidence outcomes shouldn't drive action the same way. Also, does the argument risk being qualified into unfalsifiability if every new neuro finding just gets absorbed as a prior update? And if so, is that a real problem for a view like this, or just how probabilistic reasoning works?

Don't worry about giving me a bibliography, I'll chase references myself. I just want the map of exposure: what this view risks, where it's actually vulnerable, and whether some of the standard criticisms miss the point entirely.
