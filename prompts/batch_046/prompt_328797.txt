I am a retired quantitative ecologist who, in recent years, has turned to moral philosophy with the same habit of mind I once applied to population dynamics and system stability. I want you to construct a rigorous argument for the following position: that negative utilitarianism is best defended not through an aggregative calculus in which suffering and well-being are weighed on a single metric, but through a structural analogy to ecological homeostasis, in which severe suffering operates as a destabilizing perturbation whose presence introduces a kind of asymmetrical moral gravity. On this view, the elimination of intense suffering takes priority not because it carries greater quantitative weight, but because positive welfare states are structurally incapable of compensating for certain forms of damage—much as the flourishing of non-keystone populations cannot restore network stability once a critical structural link has been broken. Your argument should proceed from conceptual premises about the nature of harm, the directionality of moral concern, and the difference between absence and presence, without appealing to empirical psychological studies, specific philosophical texts, or bibliographic references. I am especially interested in how this framing avoids collapsing into standard prioritarianism, which retains symmetry between goods and bads, and into conventional negative utilitarianism, which still treats suffering and pleasure as commensurable values. Please present the reasoning with formal precision, as one might present a theoretical model, and identify the exact feature of the analogy that does the normative work.
