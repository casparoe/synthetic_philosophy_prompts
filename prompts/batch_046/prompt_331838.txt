As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I have been working through some popular writing on path dependence in history—the idea that once a society takes a particular institutional or technological turn, the costs of reversal compound until the original choice looks inevitable in retrospect. The analogy that keeps surfacing, both in these texts and in casual conversation, is the QWERTY keyboard: a layout that may not be optimal but persists because every typist, every manufacturer, and every training manual has been built around it, so that switching becomes prohibitively expensive regardless of merit. I find this comparison intuitively powerful because in my own work I deal with legacy dependencies and technical debt every day, yet I suspect the analogy may be doing hidden argumentative work that I cannot fully see, especially regarding whether history is genuinely locked in or merely expensive to rewind.

Could you audit it for me? I would like an explicit correspondence table—what in historical development plays the role of the physical keyboard, the learning curve, the network of manufacturers, the hypothetical superior layout, and the collective cost of migration—followed by the disanalogies that actually matter for the philosophical claim. In particular, software systems can sometimes be refactored: the internal architecture is rewritten while the external behavior is preserved, which suggests that some dependencies are shallow rather than deep. Does history permit an equivalent move—say, a constitutional or cultural restructuring that leaves observable social outcomes unchanged but alters the underlying logic of the institution? If so, does that undermine the claim that history is genuinely locked in, or does it merely distinguish two layers of path dependence, analogous to the difference between a deprecated API call and a fundamental data-model constraint? I also want to know whether the QWERTY analogy quietly smuggles in the assumption that all historical lock-in is contingent and expensive-to-reverse rather than constitutive of present identity, and whether the conclusion that the same outcomes would not have arrived anyway survives once that smuggling is exposed. If the analogy collapses at any of these points, what would be a more honest comparison for the kind of institutional inertia that is not merely costly to undo but conceptually inseparable from what the society now is?
