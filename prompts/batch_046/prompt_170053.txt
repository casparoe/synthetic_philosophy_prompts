Write the full list of desiderata that any adequate theory of why death is bad for the one who dies must meet if it is to survive the Epicurean challenge without collapsing into the view that prenatal non-existence is an equal misfortune. Specify the phenomena it must explain: the asymmetry between past and future non-existence, the possibility of misfortune in the absence of an experiencing subject at the time of the putative harm, the difference between dying and being dead, and what might be called distributed harm—where the badness is not localized to a moment but is spread across a counterfactual life-trajectory that never actualizes. List the distinctions it must preserve: between harm-to and harm-for, between deprivation and experiential suffering, between the value of a life and the welfare of its owner, and between interests that require consciousness and interests that do not. State the constraints it must respect: no commitment to a persisting subject after death, consistency with the claim that dead persons have no present interests, compatibility with a physicalist account of personal identity, and neutrality on whether the dead can be wronged. For each desideratum, provide the motivating case or argument—paying special attention to the three moves in the Epicurean triad (no subject, no experience, therefore no harm)—and flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Then identify which single desideratum does the most work in excluding the deprivation account, which does the most to exclude the desire-fulfillment account, and whether there exists any jointly satisfiable subset of requirements that retains both the asymmetry intuition and the no-subject constraint. If the list reveals an unsatisfiable cluster, say so explicitly; that tension is the central result I want to see. Keep the weight on the criteria, their interactions, and the residue they leave, not on a scorecard of named theorists.
