I am a graduate student in a philosophy of social science seminar, and two classmates—Jordan and Riley—got into a sustained disagreement during a discussion of methodological individualism versus holism. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander; I do not know which side is stronger, and I want an impartial point-by-point assessment rather than advice on what I should believe. Here is the back-and-forth as I remember it.

(1) Jordan opened by defending a qualified methodological individualism: every social explanation must eventually be anchored in individual actions, beliefs, and choices. He granted that macro-level descriptions—say, “high unemployment causes political polarization”—are useful heuristics, but insisted they are only shorthand for mechanism-based stories that link macro conditions to micro-level decisions, in the style of analytical sociology. He added that if a social property is genuinely irreducible, it becomes ontologically mysterious.

(2) Riley replied that this concedes far too little. She pointed to institutional “selection environments”: a labor market, for instance, does not merely aggregate individual job searches. Its credential requirements, wage-setting rules, and hiring norms constitute a downward-causal structure that selectively rewards certain traits and eliminates others, thereby reshaping the very preference profiles and cognitive frames of individuals over time. The macro structure, she argued, does causal work that is not captured by summing micro-behavior.

(3) Jordan countered that selection is still mechanistically reducible. Institutions are stable patterns of interaction sustained by individual compliance and sanctioning. What Riley calls “downward causation” is really just a diachronic feedback loop: individuals adapt to incentives, and those adaptations reproduce the pattern. He said evolutionary game theory can model this entirely at the individual level, so there is no need to invoke irreducible social-level powers.

(4) Riley objected that Jordan misses “relational emergence.” Consider the property of being a creditor. Its causal powers depend on a legal-institutional framework and on the existence of counterparties who are debtors. These are relational properties: they do not inhere in any single individual. Explanations that invoke them—such as why a bank run propagates through a specific network topology—refer to structural features (interdependence, liquidity constraints) that are properties of the network, not decomposable into node-level attributes without losing explanatory content.

(5) Jordan responded that relational properties are just higher-order descriptions of pairwise interactions. In principle, one could specify every interaction rule, every contractual relation, and every belief update for every agent, constructing a complete micro-foundation. Holism, on this view, is an epistemic convenience driven by computational limits, not an ontological necessity. He challenged Riley to identify a social explanation that could not, even in principle, be reconstructed this way.

(6) Riley said Jordan’s “in principle” move renders methodological individualism unfalsifiable and therefore methodologically empty. If every successful macro-explanation is dismissed as “merely heuristic,” then the individualist program becomes a definitional stipulation rather than an empirical research strategy. She noted that analytical sociology itself routinely relies on macro-level regularities—such as segregation patterns or organizational mortality rates—to identify which mechanisms are relevant in the first place, suggesting that macro-data guide micro-inquiry rather than the reverse.
