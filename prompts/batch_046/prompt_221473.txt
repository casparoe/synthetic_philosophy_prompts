I’m working through a problem for a graduate seminar on metaphysics, and I’ve become convinced that there is an unacknowledged collapse in the Lewisian system that undermines its claim to be a theory of modality at all. Lewis holds that “actual” is indexical—functioning like “here” or “now”—so that every concrete world is actual to its own inhabitants. He also insists that these worlds are spatiotemporally isolated, causally closed systems with no inter-world interaction. Now, if actuality is purely indexical, then being “the actual world” just means being the world relative to which a speaker is situated. That suggests the only metaphysical difference between my world and any other concrete world is my location within one of them. But if that is right, then modal realism seems structurally identical to what we might call “indexical actualism with ontological excess”: there is exactly one world that is actual-relative-to-me, and the rest are concrete but non-actual-relative-to-me entities—much like abstract possible worlds are for the actualist, except that Lewis’s are causally inaccessible duplicates rather than non-existent abstractions.

The problem is that this appears to drain modality of its distinctive explanatory content. On this reading, saying “I could have been a musician” does not invoke a genuinely possible state of affairs; it merely invokes a concrete world that is causally isolated from me and non-actual-relative-to-me. All the modal work is being done by speaker-relative indexing, not by any feature of the worlds themselves. So why do modal realists treat world-pluralism as providing a substantive account of possibility and necessity, rather than as a form of actualism that populates the ontology with causally inaccessible duplicates? Am I wrong to think that indexicality undermines the explanatory power of concreteness?
