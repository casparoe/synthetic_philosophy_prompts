Next month I defend my workshop paper at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-psychiatry meeting. My thesis is that Hacking's looping-effects model needs structural revision for algorithmically mediated diagnosis: when predictive classifiers trained on passive digital traces—such as keystroke dynamics or social-media metadata—assign psychiatric risk categories before any clinical encounter, they generate an "anticipatory loop" in which the classification precedes and constrains patient self-interpretation, rather than emerging from clinician-patient interaction. The audience includes two philosophers of science who have published on Hacking's interactive kinds, three clinical psychiatrists who routinely use DSM-5-TR decision-support tools and regard philosophical critiques as detached from ward realities, and a historian of medicine who thinks looping is merely social construction rebranded. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to arise, phrased as each subgroup would phrase them, with for each: whether I should concede, hold firm, or partially qualify; the weakest point in my reply; and the specific objection I am hoping nobody raises. End with a one-paragraph summary of the vulnerability in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than conceal.
