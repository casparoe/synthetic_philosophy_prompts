I'm working through a comparative-constitutional-law exercise with a study group, and we're trying to map the implicit logic behind a particular French administrative tribunal's rulings on laïcité and religious or cultural accommodation over the past decade. We have six verdicts on record, and they seem jointly consistent with more than one coherent principle, which makes predicting the seventh genuinely uncertain.

The tribunal's track record is as follows. First, it upheld a public high school's prohibition on a teacher wearing a large headscarf in the classroom. Second, it struck down a municipal ordinance that banned religious head coverings in all public parks and streets. Third, it permitted a town hall to display a Christmas nativity scene in its lobby during December, treating it as a longstanding cultural tradition rather than state-sponsored proselytism. Fourth, it prohibited an identical nativity display in the lobby of a local public hospital, ruling that healthcare settings demand stricter institutional neutrality. Fifth, it allowed a municipal swimming pool to schedule women-only hours at the request of local Muslim women, framing the measure as advancing gender equality rather than as religious accommodation per se. Sixth, it denied a request by male university students for men-only study hours in the public library, holding that gender separation in an educational setting violates republican equality regardless of which gender initiates the separation.

The pending case involves a large public university that has converted an unused study room in its central library into a multi-faith quiet room—devoid of religious symbols, open to students of any faith or none for prayer, meditation, or silence. The university approved it as a neutral allocation of space; a local secularist association is now challenging it as an unconstitutional breach of laïcité within a state educational institution. For consistency with the tribunal's own precedents, should the court uphold the university's decision or strike it down? I am less interested in a bare prediction than in identifying the competing rules that best explain the first six verdicts, stating what each rule predicts about the prayer room, specifying the closest analog among the six, giving an honest assessment of which principle the tribunal would be adopting either way, and naming the further case that would best distinguish the rules—for instance, if the room were reserved exclusively for one tradition, or if it were maintained by a private contractor rather than the university itself.

Please say what would change your mind: which reinterpretation of one of the six precedents, or which feature of the prayer-room arrangement, would lead you to abandon your preferred principle for the runner-up, and where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure. Organize your response in the following sections, in this order: "The competing principles" (roughly 200 words), laying out the two or three rules that fit the track record; "Prediction for the prayer room" (roughly 250 words), with your best-guess verdict, the closest analog, and the principle being adopted in each direction; "The distinguishing case" (roughly 150 words), describing the scenario that would settle the matter; and "What would change my mind" (roughly 100 words).
