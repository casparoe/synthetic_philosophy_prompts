There's this well-known tension in Sen's liberal paradox where giving each person sovereignty over just one personal decision—like what books they read or who they sleep with—can block unanimous social improvements that even they'd prefer. But the weird thing is that real constitutions and courts actually do embed something like that: they protect "personal spheres" even when every affected party would be happier with the collective outcome. So what philosophical theory of value or authority does that legal practice actually presuppose? Is it that individual rights are literally non-aggregative, or is it just a practical heuristic for avoiding tyranny? And here's the part that puzzles me: the same governments that enforce these veto-rights also run regulatory agencies that do straight-up cost-benefit analysis, treating preferences as fully tradable. Are those two practices running on seperate theories of value, or can one story cover both? If the rights-based story is the real one, shouldn't cost-benefit analysis be illegitimate in principle?
