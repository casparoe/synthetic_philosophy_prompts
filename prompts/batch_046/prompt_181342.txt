I have been reflecting on whether a rigorous form of tranquilism—according to which the sole constituents of well-being are the absence of craving and the absence of suffering—can accommodate my judgments about a range of marginal cases. Consider the following verdicts, which I present not as settled doctrine but as intuitive pressure points. First, a subject in a persistent vegetative state who retains no conscious experience whatsoever: this strikes me as lacking well-being rather than possessing it maximally, despite the total absence of craving and suffering. Second, an individual maintained on a hypothetical anhedonic compound that eliminates all affective valence, leaving only neutral cognitive processing: this case seems genuinely good in the tranquilist calculus, yet it differs from the vegetative case in ways I struggle to articulate precisely. Third, an ascetic practitioner who endures chronic physical discomfort but reports complete absence of mental resistance or desire for relief: I am inclined to say that suffering is absent, hence well-being is high, but this conflicts with my intuition that the bodily damage itself constitutes a harm. Fourth, an artist who actively seeks out exhausting, painful labor in pursuit of creative achievement and feels no regret: here I want to say that the pain is real suffering and therefore bad, yet the life as a whole seems admirable and not deficient in well-being, which creates tension with the tranquilist axiom. Fifth, a person who suppresses a vengeful craving through sustained, effortful self-monitoring that produces mental fatigue: does the fatigue count as suffering that offsets the eliminated craving, or is the net state superior? And sixth, someone with a stable, mild preference for one climate over another, who experiences no distress in either: this does not seem to involve craving at all.

Given these judgments, is there a unified principle—perhaps one that distinguishes between occurrent and dispositional affective states, or between suffering as phenomenal experience versus suffering as frustrated desire—that captures them without ad hoc adjustments? If not, which verdict should be revised: my reluctance to grant maximal well-being to the vegetative patient, my willingness to approve the anhedonic state, or my insistence that the artist's pain is genuinely bad? I would also welcome a precise statement of what the principle predicts about the self-monitoring suppressor and about whether a mild, non-distressing preference can ever mature into a craving if left unexamined over time.
