Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the philosophy of causation by omission. The citizens of Omelas enjoy prosperity that depends on the continuous suffering of a single imprisoned child; they do not rescue it, and the few who depart do not dismantle the system. Does their collective failure to act cause the child's suffering, or is the cause solely the institutional arrangement that placed the child there? Do those who leave cause further harm by omitting to organize resistance? I want a reading that takes both the story and the metaphysics of omission seriously: does Le Guin presuppose that omissions possess independent causal power, or does she implicitly rely on a prior framework of roles, expectations, and capacities—the kind of normative background that some metaphysicians argue omissions must borrow from positive action? Can the narrative be read as showing that omission is the primary causal structure here, rather than merely parasitic on positive events? Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the story to an illustration of causal theory, and do not distort the theory to accommodate the plot. Conclude by saying what the story contributes to the philosophical debate about omission that pure argument cannot.
