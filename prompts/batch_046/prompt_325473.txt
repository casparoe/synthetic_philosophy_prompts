I have been arguing with a friend in my philosophy-of-science reading group about whether the norms of rational belief should be modeled on investment strategy. We were focusing on geometric rationality—the principle that rational agents maximize expected logarithmic utility rather than linear expectation—which in betting contexts produces the Kelly criterion: stake a fraction of your capital equal to your edge divided by the odds. A growing but still marginal literature extends this idea from finance to epistemology, suggesting that credences should be managed like a portfolio, with extreme confidence treated as a leveraged position that risks epistemic bankruptcy. The question that kept returning was whether this framework applies not merely to individual gamblers, but to collective scientific practice.

Write an essay of no more than 800 words addressing this: should collaborative research communities—grant committees, interdisciplinary consortia, or institutional review boards—adopt Kelly-style leverage rules when distributing resources and attention across competing hypotheses? You should treat scientific belief-formation as a cooperative enterprise with asymmetric costs for error, not as a zero-sum betting pool, and weigh whether geometric rationality's protection against overconfidence justifies its demand that communities never go "all in" on a single paradigm. Reach and defend a clear verdict—either that collective science should institutionalize geometric leverage, or that the analogy between epistemic and financial risk collapses at the collective level—and justify it with reasoning that stays within the conceptual structure of geometric rationality itself, without citing specific authors, papers, or historical case studies.
