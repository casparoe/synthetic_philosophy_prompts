Corporate and university “net-zero” commitments almost universally adopt the GHG Protocol boundary: Scope 1 and 2 emissions are inventoried and targeted for reduction, while Scope 3—supply chains, endowment portfolios, product-use emissions, travel—is reported voluntarily if at all and routinely left outside the neutrality claim. At the same time, these same institutions disclose supply-chain and investment risks in financial filings, and often take credit for indirect benefits such as alumni economic impact or technology adoption. What theory of collective harm and institutional moral responsibility does this accounting boundary presuppose? Does it commit them to the view that liability tracks direct operational control rather than foreseeable causal contribution; that harm diluted across a large supply chain is nullified for each contributor; or that tractability alone can justify a modular cutoff? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and ask whether it is consistent with their willingness to claim indirect positive impacts. In particular, if the boundary is defended by an inefficacy-style argument—that any single institution’s Scope 3 contribution is too small to matter—does that same logic apply to voting, dietary choice, or investment, and can the institution consistently reject it there?
