At a philosophy of mind reading group, two doctoral students—Rosa and Jian—disagreed about how to apply Adam Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator to a specific clinical case. The case concerns a medical ethicist who has congenital analgesia: she experiences almost no physical pain and very little of the emotional affect that usually accompanies it. She must advise whether a patient should undergo a painful but potentially curative procedure.

Rosa argued first: For Smith, moral judgment begins with sympathy, which requires that we actually feel something analogous to what the agent feels. If the ethicist cannot experience pain or fear, she lacks the affective basis for fellow-feeling. She might intellectually understand the patient's situation, but without shared sentiment she cannot genuinely approve or disapprove; she can only apply abstract rules. Therefore she is disqualified from being a genuine moral judge in Smith's framework, and should defer to someone who can sympathize.

Jian replied: That misreads Smith. Sympathy is not emotional contagion but an imaginative projection—placing ourselves in another's situation through imagination. The ethicist can perform this imaginative act precisely because she understands the patient's condition conceptually and can represent its effects. Moreover, Smith's impartial spectator is a rational standpoint that corrects for the distortions of excessive feeling. Someone who is emotionally unaffected might actually approximate the impartial spectator better than someone overwhelmed by empathic distress, because she can weigh long-term consequences without being swayed by immediate horror.

Rosa came back: But Smith insists that we judge the propriety of sentiments by comparing them with our own. If the ethicist's own affective register is empty, there is no internal standard against which to compare the patient's suffering. The internal spectator Smith describes requires that we have sentiments to witness; without them, the ethicist is not an impartial spectator but a detached calculator. Smith explicitly warns against letting reason operate without sentiment, since that produces coldness rather than moral wisdom.

Jian answered: You are right that Smith does not want reason without sentiment, but wrong about what sentiment means here. The ethicist can still have sentiments—she can feel concern, hope, or moral indignation—even if she lacks nociception. The impartial spectator uses these general sentiments, not the specific suffering, to evaluate whether the patient's reaction is proportionate. Furthermore, Smith allows that we sympathize with sentiments we have never personally felt, as when we sympathize with the grief of the bereaved despite never having lost a child. So the ethicist's lack of personal pain does not block sympathy; it merely changes its mode.

Rosa then conceded one point: if the ethicist genuinely lacks all emotional response, not just physical pain, then Jian's defense fails, because Smith's spectator needs some affective engagement to function. But she maintained that in the case as described—where only physical and closely linked emotional sensations are absent—the ethicist is still deficient because she cannot fully grasp the qualitative feel of suffering, and therefore her imaginative projection is necessarily thinner than that of a normally embodied spectator.

Jian accepted that limitation but argued it makes her a partially impaired, not a disqualified, judge. He added that Smith's framework actually predicts this: we trust the judgments of those whose sentiments are calm and well-informed, and we distrust those whose feelings are either absent or excessive. The ethicist falls near the calm end, which is closer to the ideal of the impartial spectator than the empathically overwhelmed observer.

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of Rosa's and Jian's moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating. Then say who is more right overall about whether the ethicist can serve as a Smithian moral judge, and identify what both are missing—particularly regarding how Smith distinguishes sympathy for an agent's situation from sympathy for an agent's actual sentiments, and how that distinction affects the case.
