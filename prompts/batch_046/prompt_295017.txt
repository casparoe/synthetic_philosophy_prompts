I've come to hold a somewhat idiosyncratic finitist view that tries to take Hilbert's hotel seriously as an intuition pump rather than dismiss it, but to read it differently from the standard Cantorian: I think the hotel shows that if actual infinities existed they would have to violate the principle that every occupant can be assigned a unique, non-arbitrary room, because shifting every guest to n+2 leaves room 1 permanently unoccupied without any property distinguishing it from the others — a kind of ontological arbitrariness that a well-founded ontology should not tolerate. At the same time, I think Cantor's diagonal argument is best understood as a result about formal languages or about potential infinity — the unending extendability of any list — rather than as proof that there exists, in any actual world, a set with cardinality strictly greater than aleph-null. To make this coherent, I combine it with a modal-realist gloss: there are infinitely many possible rooms across worlds, but in each world the hotel is finite; the uncountability result just means no single-world finite list can enumerate cross-world possibilities. So my position is that actual infinity is incoherent or at least ontologically extravagant, potential infinity is unproblematic, and Cantor's result is misinterpreted when taken as an existence proof for actual infinite sets.

What would count against this? I want an honest map of its exposure, sorted by how decisive each defeater would be. Specifically: which parts of mathematics or physics genuinely require actual infinities rather than just arbitrarily large finite approximations? Are there physical predictions — in quantum field theory, cosmology, or statistical mechanics — that depend on genuinely infinite-dimensional spaces, and if so, could they be reconstructed with sufficiently large but finite structures without predictive loss? On the philosophical side, does my reading of Hilbert's hotel trade on a confusion between physical occupancy and abstract set membership, and is that confusion fatal? Also, am I insulating myself from refutation by retreating to modal realism whenever an actual infinite is posited — and is that retreat legitimate for an ontological claim of this kind, or does it risk qualifying the view into unfalsifiability in a way that is itself damaging? I'd like to know, for each potential defeater, whether a committed defender of this hybrid view would concede it as genuinely damaging, which ones they would dismiss as a misinterpretation of formalism, and whether the insulation problem is a fatal flaw or merely an awkward feature for a metaphysical position that aims to be substantive rather than merely methodological.
