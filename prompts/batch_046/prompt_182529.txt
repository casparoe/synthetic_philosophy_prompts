I am attempting to work out my own position on when coercive power legitimises, corrupts, or disguises justice, partly in response to the classical pairing—found in Pascal's reflections—that justice without force is impotent while force without justice is tyrannical. Rather than rehearsing familiar formulations, I want to test my intuitions against concrete institutional and interpersonal cases and see whether they converge on a principle.

Here are my gut verdicts.

A democratically enacted zoning ordinance systematically disadvantages one neighbourhood in the allocation of public services; enforcement proceeds through routine inspections and modest fines, with no physical violence. Verdict: not fine—the law's democratic origin does not cleanse its distributive injustice, and the enforcement makes the harm systematic.

A voluntary association upholds an unwritten ethical code—requiring members to contribute labour to communal maintenance—through exclusion and withdrawal of cooperation, with no state backing whatsoever. Verdict: fine, indeed necessary; the coercion is transparent, consensual in origin, and proportionate to the norm's importance.

An individual physically disables a surveillance apparatus that is being used to carry out an unlawful deportation order, knowing this will expose her to criminal liability, but causing no harm to persons. Verdict: fine, provided the disablement is narrowly targeted; the injustice is acute and institutional channels are blocked.

The same individual, having disabled the apparatus, joins others in seizing the local administrative office and issuing new housing directives backed by the threat of further sabotage, allocating resources according to need rather than legal title. Verdict: not fine; the transition from resistance to governance crosses a line I cannot justify, though I struggle to name it precisely.

A legislature retains on the books a statute that criminalises a harmless practice, but applies it through such procedural leniency—automatic deferrals, minimal penalties, widespread prosecutorial discretion—that punishment is rare and easily avoided, though the statute remains available for selective targeting. Verdict: I keep returning to this one. It seems less brutal than strict enforcement, yet more insidious because it preserves injustice while masking it with a veneer of mercy. I am genuinely uncertain whether it is worse.

A private corporation includes in its employment contracts an arbitration clause that applies ethical norms stricter than statutory labour law—protecting whistle-blowers, for instance—and enforces awards through contractual damages rather than state power, with entry into the contract being genuinely voluntary. Verdict: fine, but only if the arbitration body is independent and the contract can be exited without ruinous cost.

Is there a principle, or a small family of candidates, that captures these patterns? I am especially drawn to distinctions involving transparency versus concealment of the coercive mechanism, and consent at the point of entry versus consent to the particular exercise of force. For each principle you propose, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case above, deliver its verdict on a case I have not considered—say, a religious community that excommunicates members for doctrinal deviation, where membership was inherited rather than chosen—and state honestly which of my verdicts, including my wavering one about lenient enforcement, it must overturn in order to remain coherent. If the principles conflict, I want to know which verdict is least defensible.
