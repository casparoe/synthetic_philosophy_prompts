I keep noticing the same distortion in heuristic reasoning — in forecasting tournaments, in clinical intuition, and in strategic analogizing — and I don't have a precise word for it. A reasoner gathers several weak indicators that all point the same direction: perhaps three historical precedents, three symptomatic features, or three cross-domain analogies. They then treat these indicators as probabilistically independent and compound their confidence, as though multiplying separate likelihood ratios. But the indicators were not sampled randomly; each was retained precisely because it felt structurally similar to the target case. That selection process introduces a shared dependency: if the underlying similarity judgment is being driven by surface resemblance rather than deep structure, then all the indicators are likely to fail together. The formal presumption of independence doesn't merely overcount evidence; it conceals a common-mode failure that was baked in during the filtering stage. I know the general territory includes correlation neglect and the reference-class problem, but I'm asking about the specific move — the pairing of an unexamined similarity filter with an explicit independence assumption across the filtered set. Does this have an established name? If it does, distinguish it sharply from ordinary double counting and from the conjunction fallacy. If it doesn't, take a definite stance: is this best understood as a variant of an existing bias, or does it warrant its own category? Should it, define the phenomenon precisely, state its boundary conditions, and explain exactly how it differs from generic overconfidence in analogical reasoning and from simple failure to adjust for correlated evidence. I want a committed carving, not a survey of possibilities.
