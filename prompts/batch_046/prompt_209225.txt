I serve on the governance board of a long-established cooperative that still operates under a founding charter containing an obscure clause: any proposal to alter the cooperative's pricing structure must first be circulated in physical form to every member, then discussed in a mandatory silent-reading session before verbal debate is permitted, and finally approved not by simple majority but by a two-thirds quorum calculated against total membership rather than against those present. The clause was written in an era before digital communication, and modern members find the physical circulation and silent-reading phases redundant and exclusionary for remote participants. A faction argues that retaining the clause is irrational nostalgia—that if the original drafters had access to email and video conferencing, they would never have designed it this way, and that our duty is to modernize according to present needs. Another faction counters that the clause's friction may serve a purpose we do not fully grasp: perhaps it forces members to sit with proposals in isolation before group dynamics take over, or perhaps the high quorum threshold protects minority stakeholders from being outvoted by an active but unrepresentative subset.

I am genuinely divided. Is there a principled philosophical framework for deciding whether an inherited institutional mechanism should be preserved when its original rationale is obscure but its functional effects are unknown? More specifically, how should such a framework weigh the clear present-day costs of an inefficient procedure against the risk that dismantling it might eliminate a hidden safeguard we are not currently measuring, and does that reasoning tend to favor preservation or reform in a case like this?
