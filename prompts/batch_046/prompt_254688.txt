I've been chewing on the Dennett vs Caruso thing about free will and punishment and I think I've landed on a weird middle ground that might actually be unstable. I'm basically with Caruso that there's no deep metaphysical just desert that makes suffering literally owed to someone in a cosmic moral ledger. But I'm also with Dennett that the experience of free will and moral responsibility is doing real evolutionary and social coordination work, and we can't just delete it without breaking norms. So my actual view — let's call it transparent retributivism — is that we should keep retributive institutions and language, but openly treat them as useful fictions. Everyone knows it's not literally deserved; the fiction stabalizes expectations and makes punishment predictable in ways pure consequentialist framing doesn't.

What would actually count against this? Not just Caruso hating fictions — I want the defeaters that could collapse the hybrid. Would empirical moral psychology show that people need to genuinely believe in desert for deterrence to work, so transparency kills the effect? Are there formal results in social choice or game theory showing that common-knowledge fictions collapse once people realize everyone else knows it's fake? What about edge cases like knowingly punishing an innocent person for deterrence — does my view have to bite that bullet, or can I draw a line without breaking consistency? And if Dennett's right that the illusion is unavoidable anyway, why bother making it transparent?

Rank these from fatal to merely awkward. Say which ones Caruso or Dennett themselves would concede count against my hybrid, and which they'd dismiss. Also, does this view actually risk anything, or have I just added enough qualifications to make it unfalsifiable? I'd like to know if I'm holding a genuinely exposed position or just an insulated one.
