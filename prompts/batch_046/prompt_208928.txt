I have come to hold a modified Sartrean view that I am trying to pressure-test before committing to it fully. On this position, human beings possess radical freedom in Sartre’s sense: no prior psychological state, biological constraint, or social structure fully determines the meaning an agent assigns to her situation. Yet I want to go further and claim that bad faith (mauvaise foi) is not merely an occasional self-deception about this freedom, but a structurally necessary feature of diachronic agency. Specifically, to persist as the same agent across time, one must partially “sediment” one’s past choices into a stable character—treating accumulated patterns of action as if they possessed the ontological density of being-in-itself (en-soi). This means that sustained authenticity is not just difficult but conceptually unstable: an agent who refused all sedimentation would fail to satisfy minimal conditions for personal identity over time, since there would be no coherent “project” binding her present choice to her past.

Given this synthesis, I want an honest map of what would genuinely count against it—not the standard determinist objection that neuroscience undermines freedom, but arguments and observations that target specifically the claim that sedimentation is both necessary for identity and a form of bad faith. Which empirical findings in developmental psychology or cognitive science about character formation and decision stability would threaten this view rather than being assimilated as just more “facticity” to be freely interpreted? Which philosophical results—concerning the metaphysics of persistence, the logic of self-deception, or the possibility of choice without prior facticity—would be fatal, and which would only be awkward? I also want to know whether defenders of this position risk insulating it into unfalsifiability by treating every apparent constraint as an object for free reinterpretation, and if so, whether that insulation is a vice or an unavoidable feature of an existential ontology. Please rank the defeaters from decisive to merely uncomfortable, indicate which ones a committed defender should concede as real pressure points rather than dismissing, and tell me whether the view, as formulated, risks anything substantial or has already been qualified into safety.
