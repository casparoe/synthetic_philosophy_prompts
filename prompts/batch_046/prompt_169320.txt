I come from a long career in experimental physics and have only recently begun working through early modern philosophy of science with any seriousness. In reconstructing Johannes Kepler’s simultaneous defense of rigorous celestial mechanics and of a restricted, naturalistic astrology, I have built an argument that seems to prove he could not consistently hold both positions, yet he manifestly did. Here is the chain.

(1) Kepler’s physics of celestial influence requires that every causal action between planets and terrestrial bodies be mediated by a continuous, quantifiable mechanism—whether by magnetic fibers, luminous rays, or harmonic proportions—without occult discontinuities or non-physical jumps.

(2) In his astrological writings, Kepler maintains that specific geometric configurations of the planets at the moment of birth imprint lasting dispositions upon the human soul, not through the conventional zodiacal signs but through the direct spatial-harmonic relation between the celestial bodies and the terrestrial point of origin.

(3) If the causal mechanism is genuinely geometric and continuous, then its effect upon any body present at that point should depend solely on the spatial and temporal coordinates of the interaction, not on the subsequent social, cultural, or volitional history of that body.

(4) Yet Kepler also insists that identical celestial configurations produce markedly different outcomes in different individuals—he allows that a nobleman and a peasant born under precisely the same skies need not share destiny—and he explains this divergence by appealing to the soul’s free response, education, and environment.

(5) But if free response and social context can redirect, amplify, or nullify the celestial imprint, then the geometric mechanism alone is not sufficient to determine the outcome; something that breaks the isotropy of the physical cause—call it an isotropic-breaking mediator—must enter the causal chain.

(6) Kepler explicitly denies that angelic intelligences or other non-physical agents intervene in natural astrology; he insists that astrological influence remain fully within the domain of natural philosophy.

(7) Therefore, either the celestial mechanism is insufficient for the astrological predictions Kepler wishes to defend, which undermines his astrology, or non-physical mediators do exist, which undermines the mechanistic integrity of his celestial physics.

(8) Since Kepler clearly maintains both commitments, one premise must be faulty, but I cannot identify the step without collapsing the coherence of his unified project.

Where does this argument go wrong? Is there a hidden equivocation between the geometric determination of an initial disposition and the geometric determination of a final life outcome? Or can a quantitative physical mechanism be genuinely modulated by higher-order causes without those causes becoming part of the mechanism itself? If I must choose which premises to preserve, I would prefer to keep (1) and (6), since they seem central to his scientific identity; but then I need to understand exactly what is wrong with (3), (4), or (5). Please identify the faulty step or the unstated assumption, explain why it looked plausible to someone trained in isotropic physical reasoning, and price the alternatives: what does rejecting each contested premise cost for our understanding of Kepler’s natural philosophy?
