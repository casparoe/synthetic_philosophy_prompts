I am preparing an essay for my philosophy of mind course on Quine's claim that qualia constitute an incoherent posit. Rather than replaying the familiar physicalism versus dualism debate, I want to examine whether Quine's charge of conceptual instability applies with equal force to so-called cognitive phenomenology — the view that thinking itself possesses a distinctive phenomenal character, such as the felt quality of grasping a mathematical proof. My working argument is that if Quine is correct that private, introspectively accessed qualitative states fail to meet intersubjective conditions for reference, then cognitive phenomenology is not merely empirically false but suffers from the same ontological deficiency he attributes to sensory qualia. I then consider whether accepting this conclusion undermines the phenomenal concept strategy, which presupposes that we have direct, non-descriptive modes of reference to our own conscious states.

I would welcome comments on three points. First, is it legitimate to extend Quine's eliminativist argument from sensory to cognitive domains, or does this conflate distinct categories of phenomenological report? Second, have I captured the essential structure of Quine's scepticism about mentalistic ontology — particularly his emphasis on the absence of behavioural or public criteria for attributing private qualitative states — without distorting his position? Third, I am weighing whether to include a comparison with contemporary debates about artificial consciousness: if Quine-style eliminativism dissolves talk of subjective character altogether, does it also dissolve the question of whether an artificial system could be conscious? Would this comparison illuminate the essay, or does it risk importing a separate debate that distracts from the core philosophical issue?

Please provide your response in British English. I am especially interested in whether my reasoning is clear and whether I have overlooked an important objection that a philosopher of mind would expect me to address.
