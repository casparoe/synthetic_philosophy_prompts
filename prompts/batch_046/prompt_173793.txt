I'm drafting a short piece for my newsletter on philosophy of mind and ethics — I'm a journalist who usually covers science and ideas, so I try not to get too academic, but this one got away from me. I was chatting with a friend about Nozick's expreience machine, and we got stuck on whether the intuition that we'd reject the box is really about reality-contact or about something more like narrative control. So I sketched out this essay idea I'm calling the 'reverse archive problem.' Instead of a machine that pumps you full of fake pleasure, imagine a system where your actual life is hard — real suffering, real work — but every artifact you produce (letters, tools, even your body) gets preserved in a future museum where curators reconstruct your life as one of profound meaning and well-being. Meanwhile, inside the system, you're genuinely miserable. The point is that our judgment about whether your life went well depends on whether we evaluate from the inside (phenomenal experience) or from the outside (archaeological reconstruction).

I'm trying to use this to argue that well-being theories aren't just split between hedonism and objective lists — there's a hidden third axis about temporal authority, i.e., who gets to say whether a life was good: the person living it, or the future interpreters who only have objects and traces. But I worry I'm mixing up epistemology with metaphysics, like saying 'we can't know your well-being without artifacts' and then sliding into 'your well-being just is what the artifacts say.' I also wonder if this is just a convoluted version of adaptive preference, except reversed: instead of people adapting their desires to bad conditions, future observers adapt their interpretations to the conditions they see.

Could you give me comments on the draft concept? Specifically: is the distinction between inside-evaluation and outside-evaluation already named in the literature, and am I misusing it? Does the 'reverse archive' actually add anything to the experience-machine debate, or is it just a flashy metaphor? And is there a glaring logical gap — for instance, does my analogy to how archaeologists infer beliefs from pottery and burial goods actually hold water for well-being, or am I overreaching? I'm not asking for a bibiliography or reading list; I just want to know if the reasoning is shaky and whether there's an obvious counter-argument I'm missing because I'm writing outside my usual beat.
