In AI safety governance and theoretical computer science, there is a recurring argumentative pattern: proponents invoke Gödel's incompleteness results to claim that any formal system powerful enough to represent arithmetic is necessarily incomplete and cannot demonstrate its own consistency, and therefore that any sufficiently complex autonomous system built on formal foundations cannot guarantee its own correctness or safety. This claim is routinely used to justify institutional requirements for mandatory human oversight. At the same time, these same frameworks typically assume that human auditors are not subject to an analogous epistemic ceiling, treating the Gödelian limitation as applying specifically to mechanical symbol-manipulation rather than to rational cognition in general. What philosophical commitments about formalization, the relation between syntax and semantics, and the special epistemic status of human judgment does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the argument strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, assess whether the practice is internally consistent or presupposes inconsistent views about cognition and proof, and describe what an oversight architecture would have to change if it applied the same limitation symmetrically to both artificial and human reasoning.
