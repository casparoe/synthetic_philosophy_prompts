My uncle operates a highly specialized architectural-restoration workshop whose value lies less in its physical equipment than in a dense web of long-standing relationships with heritage-preservation boards, a reputation for judgment that took decades to earn, and a body of craft knowledge—particular mortar-matching techniques, aging assessments, undocumented repair protocols—that has been transmitted through apprenticeship rather than formal documentation. He intends to leave controlling ownership and management authority to my cousin, who has worked in the workshop for ten years and mastered these tacit practices, rather than liquidating the firm and dividing proceeds among all relatives. He defends this on two grounds: first, that if the workshop were sold on the open market, the relational capital—the board's specific trust in this team's discretion—would largely dissolve, meaning outsiders would not actually acquire a meaningful opportunity but merely see a specialized service disappear; second, that he genuinely believes in meritocracy and equality of opportunity, and therefore worries that transmitting this "platform" creates a dynastic advantage for my cousin over other equally talented aspiring restorers who never had access to an uncle's workshop. He asks me whether standard inheritance-tax frameworks address the real moral problem, since taxing the financial assets might leave intact exactly the social and tacit capital that produces unequal opportunity, or whether preserving the workshop intact is itself an indefensible form of family privilege disguised as value preservation.
