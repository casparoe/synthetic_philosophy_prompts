Below is a final-year examination question from a seminar on historical explanation and agency, together with a student’s response reproduced verbatim. You are acting as the external moderator asked to review the grading.

Question (20 marks):
Would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? Assess this by contrasting the Great Man theory of historical causation with structural explanations, and apply your analysis specifically to the origins of the Final Solution, distinguishing intentionalist and functionalist interpretations. In doing so, evaluate whether Ian Kershaw’s idea of officials working towards the Führer successfully mediates between personalist and structural accounts.

Student answer:
The Great Man theory holds that history is shaped by exceptional individuals whose personal qualities redirect collective development; applied to the Third Reich, this implies that Hitler’s unique ideological fanaticism and oratorical skill were necessary conditions for both the regime’s consolidation and its genocidal outcome. Structural explanations, by contrast, stress the instability of Weimar democracy, deep-rooted German authoritarian traditions, and the polycratic bureaucracy of the Nazi state, suggesting that comparable authoritarian and racist policies might have emerged from these underlying conditions even in Hitler’s absence.

Regarding the Final Solution, intentionalists such as Lucy Dawidowicz and Eberhard Jäckel maintain that Hitler formulated a coherent master plan for genocide in the 1920s and executed it systematically after 1933. Functionalists, including Hans Mommsen and Martin Broszat, reject this linear narrative, arguing instead that the Holocaust crystallised incrementally from administrative competition and radical initiative within a fragmented, polycratic machinery, without any single comprehensive directive from the top. Kershaw’s concept of working towards the Führer attempts to reconcile these perspectives: he proposes that Hitler established broad ideological parameters but delegated implementation, allowing subordinates to anticipate and exceed his wishes through competitive radicalisation.

Nevertheless, Kershaw’s synthesis ultimately demonstrates that the bureaucratic dynamics possessed sufficient autonomous momentum. Because officials radicalised policy without awaiting explicit commands, the structural logic of the regime was self-propelling. It therefore follows that Hitler’s personal presence was largely incidental to the escalation of anti-Jewish violence: had he died in 1934, the same polycratic competition and völkisch climate would almost certainly have produced a comparable genocidal programme under an alternative leader. Consequently, Kershaw’s model undermines rather than mediates the Great Man thesis, confirming that structural explanations provide the more complete account of both the Nazi state and the origins of the Holocaust.
