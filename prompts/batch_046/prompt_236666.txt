I just finished a long, circular argument with a close friend about whether Nazi Germany was structurally overdetermined or whether Hitler was genuinely irreplaceable—she kept insisting that without that specific node the network of Weimar crisis, Versailles resentment, and bureaucratic rationalization would still have converged on something functionally identical, while I kept feeling she was treating a counterfactual dependency like an aesthetic preference. To settle my own thoughts, write a satirical museum-catalog entry—about 600 words, deadpan curatorial voice, numbered entries—for an imaginary exhibition titled "Germany, 1933–1945: The Structural Model Only." The premise is that the curators have built a full-scale diorama of the regime using exclusively macro-variables—economic cycles, institutional decay, geopolitical pressure, demographic anxiety—with every reference to Hitler physically excised. The satire comes from the absence becoming a presence: visitors report seeing a mustachioed silhouette in the blank space where the chancellor should stand, propaganda posters display a facial void that viewers instinctively fill in, and economic charts spike in curves that resemble a signature. The catalog copy should stay rigorously committed to structural explanation—no mention of Great Men—while accidentally documenting all the ways a single missing node destabilizes the display. Land the real historiographical problems—counterfactual fragility, overdetermination, the difference between necessary and sufficient conditions—without ever naming them, and make it readable to someone who has never touched the literature. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list; just the catalog entries with brief curatorial annotations.
