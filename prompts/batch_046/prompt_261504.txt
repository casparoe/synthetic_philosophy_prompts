I'm a philosophy professor working on comparative secularism and the politics of recognition, and over the past semester I've catalogued the implicit normative commitments of a senior colleague—through a series of hypothetical institutional cases we've debated in our graduate seminar—without being able to settle on the single theoretical framework that best explains her pattern of judgments. Her verdicts are these. She approves a policy that permits public-school students to wear discreet religious symbols while prohibiting teachers from doing so; she approves displaying religious artifacts in a state museum only when they are presented with explicit curatorial framing as cultural heritage rather than as objects of devotional veneration; she approves state funding for minority-language schools on the condition that they teach the full national curriculum in the dominant language and do not restrict enrollment by ethnicity; she approves banning public religious processions solely when the ban is grounded in neutral traffic and public-order rules applied equally to secular parades, and rejects bans that specifically target religious expression; and she approves requiring naturalized citizens to complete a civic-integration course explaining constitutional values, but only if the course demands comprehension rather than personal endorsement, and applies the same explanatory framework to the state's own founding principles.

Next week we will discuss a new case that has not arisen in our prior discussions, and I want to test whether her track record genuinely determines a verdict. A municipal public library has deployed an AI-driven recommendation algorithm within its digital catalog. Because of asymmetries in the training corpus, the system tends to suggest secular philosophical and scientific texts when users enter queries in the dominant national language, but suggests religious devotional and exegetical texts when users enter conceptually identical queries using vocabulary drawn from minority linguistic traditions. The administration defends the arrangement as algorithmically neutral: it claims it is not endorsing any tradition but merely reflecting documented usage patterns across demographic groups, and it refuses to impose a uniform recommendation logic across languages on the grounds that doing so would privilege one epistemic culture over another. The system operates solely within the public digital catalog, not in any physical display or programming space.

Predict whether my colleague would approve, reject, or conditionally accept this algorithmic arrangement; identify which of her prior verdicts is the closest analog; and say which underlying rule—strict republican laïcité, Anglo-style multicultural recognition, assimilationist integration, or a hybrid—best accommodates her full track record. Be explicit about the principle she would be adopting if she approves, and about the different principle she would be adopting if she rejects. If you think her prior verdicts genuinely underdetermine the case, say so plainly and propose a further hypothetical case—preferably one that does not depend on digital technology—that would best discriminate between the competing rules you identify. Finally, if my framing rests on a confused premise—perhaps by treating algorithmic correlation as a form of state expression, or by assuming that these five verdicts are as theoretically neutral between laïcité and multiculturalism as I have presented them—push back on that directly rather than complying politely. I am less interested in a bare prediction than in the reasoning that exposes the fault lines between neutrality, recognition, and integration as she applies them.
