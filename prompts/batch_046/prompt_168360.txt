After a lengthy argument with a friend about Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology, I am trying to resolve a conceptual tension that keeps slipping through my fingers. Heterophenomenology brackets ontological questions about consciousness and treats first-person reports as authoritative descriptions of a subject's heterophenomenological world—the world as it seems to them. The method is meant to be genuinely neutral: it neither reifies qualia as private objects nor dismisses reports as mere behavioral noise. But here is the precise knot. If the generation of reports is what constitutes the heterophenomenological world, then any system that produces structurally identical reports—regardless of internal architecture—seems to constitute an identical world, which threatens to collapse the distinction between a conscious human subject and a perfect simulator. Conversely, if we restrict the authority of reports by an external criterion—biological life-history, causal integration with a living body, or evolutionary embeddedness—we appear to smuggle in an ontological filter and violate the very neutrality the method promises. Can you explain exactly how heterophenomenology is conceptually structured to navigate between these two poles? In particular, is there an internal distinction within the method—perhaps between world-constitution and world-description, or between granting epistemic authority to reports and recognizing the conditions under which that authority is legitimately applied—that allows it to remain neutral without falling into either unrestricted functional equivalence or a disguised form of biological essentialism? I want the internal logic of the method itself, not an empirical survey of consciousness science.
