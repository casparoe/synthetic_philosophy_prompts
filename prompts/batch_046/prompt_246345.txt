I've just come away from a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend who teaches cognitive science and insists that any behavioral criterion for attributing mind is either empty or secretly smuggles in internal-state checks. We kept returning to the usual suspects—Turing-style interaction, the Chinese Room, philosophical zombies—but I realized the real pressure point isn't in those stock examples; it's in a more tightly constrained case. I'm drafting the final essay question for my graduate seminar in philosophy of mind (upper-level MA, mostly second-year students). The exam is 75 minutes; I want one question worth 20 marks that students cannot answer by summarizing standard objections. Instead, they need to do conceptual work on a scenario I want to call the mirror-locked simulator: a system that entirely lacks phenomenal consciousness but contains a perfect structural isomorph of a conscious agent's cognitive architecture, which it uses solely as an output-calibration mechanism—like an autopilot that reads flight instruments but never feels flight. Because it consults this internal model to generate reports, it behaves identically to the conscious agent in every interactive context, including verbal reports of subjective experience, yet it never introspects; its reports are mechanically derived rather than controlled.

The exam question should ask students to assess whether a behavioral criterion for attributing mind can exclude the mirror-locked simulator without either collapsing into hidden internalism (e.g., inspecting the architecture) or appealing to first-person authority. Specifically, they should examine whether introducing a control principle—that mental-state attributions require the agent to exercise appropriate control over the state—can block the simulator, since the simulator lacks control over its reports even though it behaves as if it has them. They must also address whether applying that same control principle to human agents undermines our ordinary confidence in other minds, and whether adding a design-history condition (that the system was built to model consciousness rather than to be conscious) succeeds in excluding the simulator or merely pushes the problem back one step.

Please draft the exam question itself, then provide a model answer of about 600–800 words and a marking scheme organized around five must-hit analytical moves, with the point value of each (total 20), plus a brief one-line note on the characteristic failure mode for each. Do not ask students for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—this is a closed-book exam, and I do not want answers that guess at sources. Keep the framing conceptual rather than historical, and avoid depending on obscure theoretical literature that might not be in the training data of smaller models. The difficulty should lie in recognizing that saving behavioralism from the simulator either requires giving up its purity or requires a control principle that generates skeptical pressure about other minds.
