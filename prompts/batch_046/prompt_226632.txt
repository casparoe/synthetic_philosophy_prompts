In Arrival, linguist Louise Banks learns an alien script that is circular and non-linear, and she gradually begins to experience future events—especially the life and death of her daughter—as present memories, as if the structure of the language has reconfigured the structure of her cognition. Read the film through LOT, but inject a specific tension: rather than treating Louise’s transformation as simple linguistic determinism, ask whether her case supports Fodor’s claim that cognition requires a compositional, syntactically structured mental representation (the systematicity and productivity arguments), or whether it instead supports a penetrability thesis in which an external linguistic system can directly reshape a non-linguistic cognitive architecture without passing through an intermediate mental language. Focus on the decoding scenes and on the moments when Louise “recalls” the future: do these dramatize a change in her LOT’s syntax and vocabulary, or do they suggest that thought can operate in a map-like, non-sentential medium that still supports systematic reasoning? Be precise about which version of LOT—classical sentential, computational-modular, or representational—is being tested, and say honestly where the film’s depiction of near-instantaneous cognitive transformation risks distorting the philosophical debate by treating mental architecture as far more malleable than any LOT defender allows.
