I've been trying to work out, with a study group, what I actually think about observer-selection reasoning—particularly the tension between self-sampling and self-indication assumptions, and the so-called presumptuous-philosopher objection. Rather than asking for a literature review, I want to test whether my gut verdicts across concrete cases hang together under one principle, or whether some must be sacrificed for coherence.

Here are my intuitions. First, if I wake up as one of a hundred parallel simulation copies with intact memories, alongside one isolated copy with amnesia about being simulated, I feel I should reason that I am probably one of the hundred—this sounds like legitimate self-sampling within a well-defined reference class. Second, if two cosmological theories predict identical observable physical constants but differ only in the total number of observers they generate elsewhere in the multiverse, I do not think my bare existence should favor the high-count theory; that feels like the presumptuous philosopher deriving ontological conclusions from existence alone. Third, if an experimenter will generate ten thousand copies of me only if hypothesis H is true and only ten if H is false, and I then find myself existing with no other distinguishing data, I feel a weak, uneasy pull toward H—though far weaker than a direct Bayesian update on raw observer counts alone would warrant. Fourth, when a theory is retrofitted after the fact specifically to inflate its predicted observer population in order to account for my presence, that adjustment strikes me as disconfirming rather than confirming, despite technically raising the probability of my existence under the theory. Fifth, in a nested case where copies exist across temporal stages with partial memory overlap—so that my present self might be a diachronic cluster rather than a single instantaneous instance—I still feel sampling pressure applies, but only within the cluster that shares my current indexical beliefs, not across all physically similar brains everywhere. Is there a single coherent account—perhaps a restricted reference-class rule, or a principled distinction between evidential and causal observer dependence—that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which one should I overturn, and what is the cost? Please also state precisely what the resulting principle predicts for an unlisted case: I discover I am one of two copies, but the other was created an hour earlier and has already observed an independent experimental result that I have not yet seen. Does the sampling pressure apply symmetrically between us?

Please separate what is well-settled in the philosophical treatment of anthropic reasoning from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding how narrowly the reference class should be drawn and whether the presumptuous-philosopher objection ultimately succeeds—without presenting any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus view.
