I want you to lay out, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—written as a single flowing argument or letter—what a theory of moral learning from biography and hagiography must actually deliver to be adequate. Do not treat this as a literature review or a historical survey; treat it as a specification of conceptual requirements. The theory needs to explain how readers form practical judgment from narrated lives rather than from explicit precepts, and it must handle the particular pressure generated by Plutarch's Parallel Lives, where Greek and Roman figures are paired for comparative moral evaluation without being reduced to a single normative template. It must also account for the mode of learning found in texts like Athanasius's Life of Anthony, which I will call exemplary absence: virtue cultivated through withdrawal, endurance, and the refusal of public recognition rather than through imitable civic action. Give me the desiderata—the cases of biography-to-reader transmission it must cover, including those where the reader's social position makes direct imitation impossible or ethically hazardous; the distinctions it must preserve, such as that between learning from a life and endorsing every choice within it, and between mimetic aspiration and epistemic humility about one's own conditions; the puzzles it must dissolve, for instance why flawed or partially opaque lives often yield deeper moral learning than transparent treatises; and the psychological or narrative-theory results it cannot flatly contradict. Flag which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect preferences of particular camps, and point out any pair you think is jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether the demand for cross-cultural comparability implicit in Plutarch's pairings conflicts with the demand for contextual specificity required by hagiographic exemplary absence. You may briefly note, in passing and without systematic scoring, how a narrative-identity approach or a virtue-ethical reading might fare against one or two of these criteria, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am testing conceptual architecture, not tracking scholarship. At the close, state explicitly how confident you are in the overall specification and identify the one desideratum or tension about which you are least certain, explaining briefly why that particular point gives you pause.
