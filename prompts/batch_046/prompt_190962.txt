A popular essay offers the following account of John Stuart Mill's mature views on socialism: "In his later years Mill had concluded that competitive capitalism was morally and economically obsolete, and that collective ownership of land and capital was the only just arrangement. He believed this transition could occur without coercion, through voluntary cooperative associations; yet he also maintained that, once collective ownership prevailed, the state ought to direct investment, fix wages, and regulate prices centrally, leaving individuals only with consumer choice among collectively produced goods. In this respect he did not merely qualify the liberalism of On Liberty but effectively overturned its commitment to spontaneous order and replaced it with a comprehensive planned economy." The summary sounds authoritative, but I worry it conflates Mill's cooperative sympathies with state socialism. Is this characterisation fair? Please separate what accurately reflects Mill's arguments—especially his distinction between socialism of association and socialism of state, and his insistence that liberty requires protection from bureaucratic power—from the common exaggerations, and clarify what he explicitly rejected regarding central planning and individual initiative. Please provide your answer in British English.
