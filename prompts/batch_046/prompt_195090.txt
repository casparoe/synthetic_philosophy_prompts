I serve as an external technical reviewer—my background is in distributed systems engineering, with no formal philosophy training—on the funding panel for the National Synthetic Biology Initiative, and I have been asked to circulate a draft memo before Thursday’s vote. We must decide whether to approve a three-year, $4.2M grant proposing the construction of de novo metabolic compartments inside mammalian cells. The investigators explicitly reject evolutionary etiology as the criterion for attributing biological function to these synthetic structures; instead, they adopt a causal-role framework, defining a component’s function solely by its present contribution to cellular homeostasis, regardless of any selection history. Several panel biologists object that without an evolutionary lineage these entities possess only engineered behaviors, not genuine functions, and that framing them otherwise risks conceptual incoherence in both the grant’s scientific aims and its downstream regulatory documentation. Others reply that in synthetic biology a causal-role analysis is not merely permissible but necessary, since the proposed entities have no phylogenetic past to invoke.

Please draft the panel’s decision memo, addressed to the initiative’s director, recommending either approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions, including a concrete implementation timeline if conditions are attached. The memo should treat the strongest version of the etiological objection seriously—engaging the worry that function-talk without evolutionary grounding collapses into arbitrary design-labeling—rather than dismissing it as semantic conservatism. At the same time, it should assess whether adopting a causal-role framework in this particular synthetic context actually undermines the scientific validity of the proposed work or instead reflects a legitimate conceptual choice that reviewers can evaluate on empirical grounds. I want the reasoning precise enough that the principal investigators could respond point by point in a revised proposal, yet accessible enough that panel members without philosophy backgrounds can follow the argument. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or suggested readings; the recommendation should stand entirely on its own reasoning.
