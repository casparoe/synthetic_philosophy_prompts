I'm a retired biologist who started reading philosophy of science after I left the lab, and my study group walked me into this. (1) Aumann's agreement theorem: rational agents with a common prior can't agree to disagree once they have common knowledge of thier posteriors. (2) In peer review, reviewers share the same data, methods, and background science — so they share a common prior over whether a paper is sound. (3) After discussion they know each other's verdicts, so they have common knowledge of posteriors. (4) So rational reviewers should always converge on accept or reject. (5) But in my old group, two rational reviewers with identical data often stayed split, citing different auxilliary hypotheses about noise or different weightings of prior studies. (6) So either they aren't rational, or they don't share a common prior, or (1) is false. (7) Denying rationality feels wrong, and denying shared background is absurd, so (1) must be misapplied. I think the slip is between (2) and (3): maybe "posterior about quality" isn't one well-defined proposition but a cluster, or maybe knowing the verdict isn't knowing the full probability distribution. Which step should I actually reject, and what does fixing it cost — do I lose the theorem's bite for science altogether? Keep your answer to one paragraph, max 200 words.
