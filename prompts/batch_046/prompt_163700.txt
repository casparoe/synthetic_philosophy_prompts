Next month I am defending, in an interdisciplinary seminar, a thesis about contingency and chaos in history. The audience will be mostly cliometricians and quantitative historians, together with one complex-systems physicist who understands strange attractors but is deeply suspicious of metaphorical physics in the social sciences, and a philosopher of science with rationalist-blog sympathies who cares about unfalsifiability and hidden-variable explanations. My thesis is that the classic “for want of a nail” narrative misapplies chaos theory by treating macrohistorical outcomes as if they share the same sensitivity to initial conditions as weather systems, without specifying the scale or domain of divergence. I propose that institutional path dependence behaves like a dissipative dynamical system with bounded strange attractors: small perturbations—say, a lost shoe—are usually absorbed within a constitutional basin, but if they cross a critical “counterfactual granularity threshold” they can trigger a basin switch to a qualitatively different institutional equilibrium. Hence contingency is real but structurally constrained; historians should replace the parable with an explicit model of which perturbations are noise inside an attractor and which are genuine bifurcation points.
