I'm drafting an essay for my upper-level philosophy of ethics course on supererogation, and I've developed a framing that I want to stress-test before committing to it. I hold that supererogation is not best captured by the familiar idea of going beyond duty, but should instead be understood as a structural overflow within deontic logic itself: there exist actions that fully discharge every applicable obligation and yet produce a residual moral value that cannot be reduced to either obligation-fulfillment or mere permissibility. On this view, the residual is precisely what distinguishes genuine moral praise—of the kind we direct at saints or heroes—from simple approval of behavior that happens to be good but not required. I worry, however, that this either collapses into redundancy with standard permissibility-based accounts, or that it introduces an unexplained metaphysical commitment to value that sits outside the deontic structure altogether.

What would genuinely count against this position? In doing so, distinguish between what a committed defender of this structural-overflow account would themselves concede as damaging, and what they would dismiss as missing the point. Finally, assess whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability—if any counterexample can be absorbed by redefining the residual—and if so, whether that insulation is a theoretical virtue or a fatal methodological vice for a position of this kind.
