As I understand it, the first theorem shows that any consistent formal system strong enough to express basic arithmetic contains statements that are true but unprovable within that system; the second shows that such a system can never prove its own consistency from inside. If such a physical theory is formalized in a way that embeds arithmetic, perhaps through the encoding of field configurations or measurement outcomes, then wouldn't Gödel guarantee that there are physically true propositions the theory can never derive, and also that the theory can never internally establish its own consistency? I keep encountering the response that this is a category error, because working scientists don't reason inside one fixed formal system. But that seems to miss my point: even if scientific practice is informal, any particular rigorous formalization of our best physics should still satisfy Gödel's conditions. So I'm trying to locate the precise slip in my reasoning. Is it that I'm assuming physical theories need to be "complete" in Gödel's logical sense—where every well-formed statement is either provable or disprovable—when what science actually seeks is empirical coverage, not logical closure? Or is the deeper error that I'm treating "unprovable within the formalization" as equivalent to "beyond all scientific reach," ignoring that scientists might access such truths through observation or through a meta-theoretical framework? I'm also unsure whether Tarski's result on the undefinability of truth is doing hidden work here—whether Gödel's first theorem is essentially just the arithmetic case of a system being unable to contain its own truth predicate, and whether recognizing that changes anything about how we should apply these results to physics. Could you help me identify exactly where the reasoning breaks down, and whether there's any legitimate sense in which Gödel's theorems place bounds on what a scientific theory can achieve?
