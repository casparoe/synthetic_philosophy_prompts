As a retired experimental physicist who spent four decades worrying about calibration curves and whether two observers could ever agree that an instrument had settled on a value, I have recently turned to philosophy and found myself reading Hume’s discussion of taste and judgment. In a local discussion group on aesthetics and objectivity, a fellow member recently ended our debate by invoking the old maxim de gustibus non est disputandum — there is no disputing about taste — as though it settled the question of whether beauty can be objective in any sense analogous to scientific measurement. Because my instinct is that disagreement is precisely what forces us to refine our instruments or our concepts, I want to understand exactly what work that sentence is doing.

Could you lay out, as a precise menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct theses that this proverb might express? I am interested in the full range: the trivial empirical claim that individual preferences differ; the normative claim that there is no correct or incorrect taste; the epistemic claim that rational argument about aesthetic value is impossible; the metaphysical claim that aesthetic properties are not real features of objects; and any intermediate positions, including the possibility that there are standards of taste that depend on cultivated sensibility rather than on universal agreement. For each reading, please indicate whether it amounts to a platitude, whether it is a substantive and defensible claim, and whether it is actually false — particularly in relation to Hume’s own attempt to defend a standard of taste despite recognizing the variability of sentiment. Finally, tell me which of these readings is typically being invoked when someone uses the slogan to dismiss a question about whether beauty is objective, and whether that specific reading actually supports the dismissal, or whether it collapses into one of the other readings once pressed. I do not need a bibliography or a survey of the secondary literature; I need only the conceptual disambiguation, with enough precision that I could bring it back to my group and ask which version they intended.
