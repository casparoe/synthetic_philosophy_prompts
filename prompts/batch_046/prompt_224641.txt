I'm trying to make sense of whether a reason can genuinely motivate someone if it has no connection to what they already care about. I've been thinking about a case that keeps bothering me: a close friend refuses a life-saving medical treatment, not out of fear, but because she says she has no desire to continue living in the condition the treatment would preserve. She doesn't deny that health is valuable in general; she simply says it isn't her reason. Her doctor insists that the bare fact she will die is a reason for her regardless of her psychology, yet she experiences that claim as an outsider deciding what is good for her. I want you to write a dialogue between two equally thoughtful interlocutors—call them Mara and Jonas—who disagree about whether reasons are always internal to an agent's existing motivational set, or whether some are genuinely external and normatively binding even without psychological uptake.

The dialogue should treat the medical case concretely rather than in the abstract. Mara should defend internalism by arguing that without some link to the agent's own concerns, an alleged reason is merely an imposition, and she should respond directly to Jonas's point that death is an objective harm. Jonas should defend externalism by maintaining that certain considerations carry authority independently of an agent's current desires, but he must also address Mara's autonomy worry rather than dismissing it. They should discuss what it means for the doctor to present a reason, whether accepting it requires a change in who she is, and whether internalism inevitably collapses into mere subjectivism. Both should respond to each other's actual arguments—no talking past one another—and neither should be a strawman.
