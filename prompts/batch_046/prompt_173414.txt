Consider the following argument, which I have been developing in a seminar on self-reference in rational agency and which now strikes me as suspicious because it appears to show that any bounded agent with arithmetical self-modeling must either be logically omniscient or abandon self-trust altogether. (1) A rational agent ought to satisfy reflective stability over its idealized epistemic preferences: if, after coherent extrapolation of its own belief-revision norms, the agent judges that its reasoning procedure R is sound with respect to a particular claim P, then it ought to adopt P. (2) For any system S that includes basic arithmetic and can represent its own provability relation, Löb’s theorem applies: if S proves [Prov_S(P) → P] for arbitrary P, then S proves P for arbitrary P. (3) Reflective stability requires the agent to accept, for each P it might derive, the conditional that if R derives P then P holds. (4) Therefore S proves every proposition, committing the agent to omniscience. (5) But omniscience is impossible for a bounded agent, and rational belief should avoid contradiction. (6) Hence no bounded agent can satisfy reflective stability; self-trust is incompatible with rationality.

The premises each seem defensible—especially (1), which looks like a minimal coherence constraint on idealized preference—yet the conclusion is absurd, since we routinely trust specific domains of our reasoning without being able to derive everything. I want you to diagnose the chain precisely. Is there a hidden equivocation between object-level adoption and meta-level endorsement? A scope or quantifier shift between accepting a schema and accepting each instance within the system? Or is the argument valid, in which case we must reject one of the premises—perhaps (1) by distinguishing local reliability judgments from a global reflective policy, or (3) by denying that coherence requires accepting the Löbian conditional inside S rather than in a stronger meta-system? Identify the weakest link, explain why it initially looked plausible, and price exactly what we pay by abandoning it. If you think the flaw is subtle rather than a straightforward false premise, show where the modal or quantifier slide occurs and why Löb’s conditions are not actually satisfied.
