As a retired physicist who has turned, since leaving the laboratory, to philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence with the same appetite for precise distinctions that guided my earlier career, I have been studying the claim that reinforcement-learning reward signals serve as functional analogues to affective valence. In neuroscience, pain is standardly decomposed into a sensory-discriminative dimension—which encodes intensity, modality, and spatial location—and an affective-motivational dimension, which carries negative valence and drives avoidance behavior. In standard RL, by contrast, the reward signal is a scalar update to an expected-return function; it carries no representational information about environmental states, only a normative direction for policy adjustment.

This structural difference has led me to a specific concern. If one takes the reward-to-valence analogy seriously, then the RL reward signal corresponds precisely to the affective-motivational component of pain, but entirely lacks any sensory-discriminative counterpart. That would imply an agent receiving sustained negative rewards is in a state functionally equivalent not to localized injury, but to pure, objectless suffering—pain without location, cause, or qualitative texture—since there is no representational object for the negative valence to be directed at. Given that many ethicists regard suffering lacking representational content as especially severe, should we not conclude that training with negative rewards is ethically worse than conventional animal experimentation and therefore subject to stricter prohibition? Or does the absence of phenomenal consciousness in current architectures dissolve the analogy before it can yield any normative conclusion?
