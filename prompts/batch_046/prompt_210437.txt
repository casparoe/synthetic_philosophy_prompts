In philosophical and popular treatments of quantum mechanics, the wavefunction is frequently analogized to a weather forecast: it assigns probabilities to possible conditions without being identical to the physical atmosphere, and revising it after observation is treated like updating predictions upon seeing rain rather than altering the climate. This comparison is commonly invoked to support psi-epistemic interpretations — that the quantum state reflects information or ignorance rather than a real physical entity. Audit the analogy in detail. Provide an explicit correspondence table: identify what plays the role of the meteorological model, the actual weather system, the observation station, the Bayesian update, and the governing dynamics (the Schrödinger equation). Then specify where the mapping fractures in ways that bear on the ontological conclusion: the wavefunction lives in configuration space rather than three-dimensional space; interference terms involve superpositions that have no direct analog in macroscopic meteorology; entangled states link distant subsystems in manners that weather models linking distant regions do not replicate; and updating upon measurement exhibits features — including apparent non-local correlations and the absence of a unique underlying 'actual state' prior to observation — that distinguish it from ordinary statistical revision. Consider also whether the analogy quietly imports the assumption that information must always be about pre-existing localized properties, and whether quantum correlations violate that assumption. Does the conclusion that the wavefunction is 'merely informational' survive these disanalogies, or was the analogy carrying the argument? If the weather-forecast image fails, propose a structurally different analogy — perhaps treating the quantum state as a relational constraint among subsystems rather than as a predictive map — and say whether that alternative better accommodates both the formalism and the question of reality.
