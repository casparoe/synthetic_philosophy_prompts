I am drafting a scene in a novel in which a philosophy of mind lecturer must introduce functionalism and multiple realizability to a seminar of first-year doctoral students—trained in neuroscience but with no prior exposure to philosophy—within a single fifty-minute session. I do not want the explanation itself; I want the pedagogical architecture. First, establish the dependency order: must the failure of type-identity physicalism precede any discussion of causal-role specification, or can the two be introduced together, and why? Second, identify the single carrying example that should bear the full weight of the distinction—an instance in which a mental kind is realized in a substrate that manifestly lacks the biological properties of the canonical case, but that avoids the well-worn tropes of silicon brains and Chinese rooms, which this particular audience is likely to dismiss as science-fiction conceits. Explain exactly what that example must possess and what it must lack in order to function pedagogically. Third, map the misconceptions these students are likely to form: I anticipate they will conflate functional equivalence with mere behavioral equivalence, and that they will interpret multiple realizability as implying mental properties are purely relational rather than intrinsic. For each misconception, specify the precise moment in the sequence where it should be intercepted and neutralized. Fourth, provide one diagnostic question for the end of the session that distinguishes understanding functionalism as an ontological claim about mental kinds from understanding it as a merely implementational claim about computation. Fifth, state clearly what must be left out at this level—whether teleofunctionalism, Block's liberalism objection, or Putnam's original machine-state formulation—and justify each omission in terms of the audience's conceptual dependencies rather than mere time constraints.
