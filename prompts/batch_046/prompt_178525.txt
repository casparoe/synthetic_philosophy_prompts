A study partner and I have been arguing about whether the relation “all-things-considered better than” must be transitive, especially across multi-dimensional goods. Here is the argument she constructed, and I cannot see the exit.

(1) If x is strictly better than y and y is strictly better than z, then x is strictly better than z; otherwise rational choice could cycle and no optimal option could be identified.

(2) Consider a sequence of environmental policies P0, P1, ..., P500, arranged so that each successive policy Pi+1 permits exactly one additional part-per-billion of a targeted pesticide residue in exchange for exactly one percent higher crop yield, with all other variables held constant.

(3) For each adjacent pair, Pi+1 is strictly better than Pi: the marginal agricultural gain outweighs the marginal ecological loss, given standard cost-benefit analysis.

(4) By repeated application of (1), P500 is strictly better than P0.

(5) P0 is a near-zero-residue regime with moderate yield; P500 permits 500 ppb with maximal yield. At that concentration, the pesticide exhibits a non-linear ecological cascade—specifically, it crosses a toxicity threshold for soil microbiota that undermines long-term soil fertility, a feedback loop not captured in the local, linear trade-off of step (3).

(6) Therefore, under any evaluation framework that includes long-term soil stability, P500 is not better than P0; indeed, P0 is better.

(7) So (4) is false, which means (1) is false: “better than” is not transitive across spectrum-style trade-offs.

(8) But if (1) is false, then rational choice theory collapses for any multi-attribute decision, since every real-world choice involves incremental trade-offs. I would very much like to keep (1).

Where does this argument slip? Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially interested in whether there is an equivocation hidden in the comparison relation between steps, or whether the real problem lies in (3)’s assumption that local linear weights can be aggregated globally without a change in the evaluative framework. If the flaw is in (5) or (6)—say, if long-term effects should have been priced into each local comparison—explain why they were legitimately omitted from (3) and whether adding them back saves transitivity or merely relocates the failure.
