I am writing an essay for a course on rational agency and have become stuck on a conceptual distinction within indirect normativity that the literature rarely addresses head-on. The standard formulation holds that an agent ought to perform the action her fully informed, rational, and coherent counterpart — her idealised self — would recommend from her present position. Yet this formulation seems ambiguous about whether the norm governs only the outcome of practical reasoning (the particular act selected) or also the structure of reasoning itself. Suppose my idealised counterpart employs a decision procedure that systematically discounts present-biased preferences in favour of long-range coherence; if indirect normativity binds me to her recommendations, am I also bound, derivatively, to approximate that very decision procedure in my actual cognition? Or is the indirect norm strictly a bridge between my present situation and the endorsed conclusion, leaving my internal reasoning architecture entirely outside its scope?

The difficulty, as I see it, is that if the answer is the latter — if only the conclusion matters — then indirect normativity appears to permit, or even require, me to arrive at the correct action through reasoning processes that my idealised self would reject as unreliable or incoherent. But if the answer is the former, and I must reshape my reasoning habits to mirror those of the idealised agent, then the distinction between direct norms (which apply immediately to one's mental processes) and indirect norms (which apply via an intermediary deliberative stage) seems to collapse. I would be grateful for an explanation of how this boundary is supposed to be maintained: specifically, whether there is a principled reason why indirect normativity can govern what I do without also governing how I think, and whether attempts to preserve that separation risk making the advice of the idealised self either inaccessible or normatively inert for agents whose reasoning is systematically flawed.
