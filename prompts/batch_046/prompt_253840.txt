I'm delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on democratic ethics, co-hosted by our philosophy department and the municipal election commission. The audience will be split evenly between academic political philosophers and local election officials and community organizers who have no background in normative theory. I want the remarks—about eight minutes, polished but not overly academic—to do real philosophical work rather than decorate a generic welcome. Specifically, please take a definite, argued position on whether voting for the "lesser evil" in a first-past-the-post system should be understood as a moral duty to prevent harm, as an expressive act that corrupts one's own practical judgment, or as a distinct third option that you articulate and defend. This commitment should structure the entire speech: it must determine what I can and cannot say about strategic ranking in the city's new ranked-choice pilot, about whether abstention is ever the cleaner moral choice, and about what ballot design should aim to preserve. The experts should recognize the underlying framework—particularly how you handle the tension between causal efficacy and expressive integrity—but the officials and organizers should leave with a concrete sense of why the framing changes how they ought to think about voter guidance and ballot structure. Do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I want the argument to stand on its own reasoning. Write from a committed stance: choose one view, defend it with clear reasons, and let that choice govern every section of the remarks.
