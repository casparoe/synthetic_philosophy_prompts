I'm trying to pin down what's wrong with this argument my roommate cooked up about assertion norms, but every exit looks worst than the entrance.

(1) The norm of assertion is knowledge: don't say p unless you know it.
(2) Knowledge entails belief, and belief entails a kind of first-person transparency — if you genuinely believe p, you can report that belief without doing any inference.
(3) But there's a whole class of "proxy assertions" where you say p based on an institutional role: a spokesperson announcing a verdict, a doctor repeating a diagnosis from a scan she can't read, me saying "interest rates rose" from a headline I just skimmed.
(4) In proxy assertions, the speaker often can't report the belief transparently — ask them "why do you think that?" and they point outside themselves.
(5) So either proxy assertions violate the belief condition (hence the knowledge norm), or belief doesn't actually require transparency.
(6) If proxy assertions violate the norm, then journalists, doctors, and bureaucrats are constantly asserting illegitimately, which is absurd.
(7) If belief doesn't require transparency, then the belief-condition in knowledge collapses into mere acceptance, and we lose the distinction between knowing and just going along with it.
(8) So the knowledge norm either condemns ordinary social practice or destroys the belief/knowledge distinction.

I really want to keep (1) and (2) — they feel right to me. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Also, is there any connection here to that idea of "epistemic triangulation" where a group holds knowledge no individual in it has?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which step actually breaks — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and don't restate my question back to me.
