I'm preparing an argument for my philosophy and politics course about corruption, clientelism, and gift-giving, and I am concerned that it collapses into an absurd conclusion about democratic government. Here is the chain I have constructed:

(1) Corruption is best defined as allocating public goods according to an indexical obligation—an obligation directed at a specific person or group—when the office is governed by a universal mandate requiring equal consideration of all citizens.

(2) Clientelism institutionalizes exactly this: officials distribute jobs, protection, or resources to clients based on particularistic loyalty rather than on universal entitlement.

(3) Gift-giving to officials creates a competing indexical obligation of reciprocity that displaces the universal mandate; hence it also falls under corruption.

(4) Democratic representation, however, is structured by an indexical mandate of its own: representatives owe specific duties to their constituents—securing local projects, honoring campaign promises, and prioritizing district needs over the interests of citizens elsewhere.

(5) Therefore, if (1) holds, democratic representation is structurally corrupt, since it systematically privileges one group over others on the basis of a particularistic bond.

(6) Yet democratic representation is plainly not corruption; it is a legitimate and necessary form of government.

(7) The usual defense distinguishes authorized particularism (representation) from unauthorized particularism (corruption).

(8) But authorization is merely a social or legal fact; if the universal mandate in (1) is genuinely universal, then any institutional authorization that permits deviation is itself a deviation from universality. This makes corruption either unavoidable (if authorization excuses nothing) or conceptually empty (if authorization excuses everything).

(9) Consequently, either the concept of corruption applies to all representative institutions, or the universal mandate must be replaced by a role-relative standard that permits indexical obligations within defined institutional boundaries.

If I am forced to choose, I would rather preserve (1) and (3) than accept that democratic representation is corrupt, but I want to understand the cost. Which step in this reasoning should be rejected? Please explain why that step initially looks defensible, and then outline what defenders of universal anti-corruption norms, defenders of contextual or role-relative ethics, and institutionalist critics each have to concede in order to reject it.
