In debates about whether the Holocaust should be treated as historically singular or as comparable to other mass atrocities — the Holodomor, Rwanda, colonial extermination — a recurring analogy treats it as a unique painting in a gallery: to hang it beside other works is said either to diminish its particular horror or to deny the very framework that makes comparison meaningful. Proponents of uniqueness use this to resist comparative historiography; opponents reply that treating it as incomparable is like treating an earthquake as having no relation to plate tectonics, which blocks explanation rather than honouring the dead. Separately, in discussions of Zygmunt Bauman's argument that the Holocaust was not a regression but a possibility built into modern rational bureaucracy, a counter-analogy is often deployed: modernity is like a scalpel — a neutral instrument whose moral valence depends entirely on the surgeon's hand — and therefore Bauman wrongly implicates the tool itself.

Audit both analogies in turn. For the painting analogy: provide an explicit correspondence table (the painting, the gallery, the curator, the viewer, the act of hanging, the alleged moral harm). Identify precisely where the mapping fractures — does comparison necessarily diminish, or does uniqueness entail only non-substitutability? Clarify what the analogy smuggles in about moral particularity versus causal explanation, and determine whether the conclusion that comparison is impermissible survives once the disanalogies are visible. For the scalpel analogy: map rational bureaucracy, instrumental neutrality, moral agency, institutional design, and the surgical outcome. Show where it breaks — is bureaucratic rationality genuinely value-neutral like steel, or is its logic of efficiency, categorisation, and distancing already morally loaded? Assess whether the analogy can genuinely exonerate modernity, or whether it conceals the very structural features Bauman identifies. Finally, if either analogy collapses, suggest what a more adequate comparison — perhaps legal precedent, epidemiological modelling, or architectural planning — might look like, and whether it would support or undermine the original conclusion.
