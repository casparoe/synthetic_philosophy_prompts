I'm drafting an essay on Daniel Dennett's taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—and its application to contemporary artificial cognition. Rather than treating the four categories as an evolutionary ladder, I want to argue that Dennett's framework conflates two distinct axes: the phylogenetic or developmental origin of a cognitive capacity, and its present functional architecture. On this view, a system could be developmentally "Darwinian" (shaped by selection pressures) yet functionally "Popperian" (generating and discarding internal hypotheses), or developmentally "Skinnerian" (trained by reward) yet display behaviors that look Gregorian because they are mediated by linguistic tools. To make this concrete, I propose introducing a provisional fifth category—call it "stochastic interpolative"—for systems like large language models, which navigate a compressed statistical manifold of prior outputs without the conjectural risk that defines Popperian cognition, and without the embodied normative correction that defines Gregorian status. I also want to claim that granting Gregorian status to an artificial system should depend not on its ability to manipulate symbols or call APIs, but on its participation in a community that can genuinely correct its misrepresentations, not merely update its weights.

Given these notes, I would appreciate comments on three specific points. First, does the separation of origin and functional architecture actually hold up against Dennett's own treatment, or does he already regard them as inseparable? Second, is my characterization of current language models as non-Popperian defensible—specifically, that they lack an internal mechanism for generating rival hypotheses and letting them die in their stead—or am I overstating the absence of conjectural dynamics in transformer-based reasoning? Third, is the proposed "stochastic interpolative" category philosophically useful, or does it collapse back into a scaled-up Skinnerian account once we examine how reinforcement learning from human feedback shapes the model's outputs? I am particularly interested in whether there is an obvious objection I am missing: for instance, that Gregorian capacities necessarily require biological embodiment or social history, which would make my criterion of normative correctability either too permissive or impossible for artifacts to satisfy. I am not asking for citations or a reading list—just an honest assessment of whether this framework is coherent, interesting, and where its weakest joints are.
