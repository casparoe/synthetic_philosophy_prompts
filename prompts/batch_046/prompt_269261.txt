Hello -- I'm a retired physicist who's only recently started taking philosophy seriously (blame my grandson, he's at uni now). I've been working through Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences, and my book club tried to formalise an objection. I think it proves too much, but I can't spot the slip.

Here is the argument, numbered as best I can:

(1) On the Surfaces/Essences view, every concept is built by analogical mapping from prior experiences; there is no non-analagous cognition.
(2) If (1) holds, then expert knowledge in any field is just a very dense stack of analogies that have become transparent to the expert.
(3) But transparency doesn't change the nature of the representation -- a burnt-out analogy is still an analogical structure, just one we don't notice.
(4) So when a physicist says she literally understands quantum entanglement, not by analogy to something else, she is mistaken; she's using analogies she's forgotten.
(5) If experts are systematically wrong about the structure of their own understanding, then there is no reliable first-person authority about conceptual content.
(6) But we generally accept that experts can correct novices precisely because experts have direct, literal grasp of their domain.
(7) Therefore (1) is false: analogy can't be the core of all cognition, because it undermines expert authority.

I'd quite like to keep (1) -- it chimes with how I used to build mental models in the lab, everything was an analogus leap from the last model. I'd also like to keep (6), since it matches my experience supervising students. So I'm hoping the flaw is in (3) or (4), but I can't see why (3) is wrong. Is there an equivocation between "being analogical in origin" and "being analogical in current use"? Or am I missing something about how analogies can be genuinely transcended?

Sorry if this is muddled. There's probably a typo in there somewhere -- I never was good with spelling.
