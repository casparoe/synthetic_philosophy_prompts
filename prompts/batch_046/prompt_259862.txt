I’ve been trying to formalize Ubuntu-style relational personhood by mapping it onto distributed-systems thinking, and the result is an argument that seems to destroy the view from the inside. Here are the steps I can’t unwind:

(1) If personhood is genuinely relational in the Ubuntu sense—“I am because we are”—then the self is not a persistent object with intrinsic properties, but a dynamic pattern of social edges.
(2) In distributed architecture, if a node’s identity is defined entirely by its current connections, then replacing all connections produces a different node; analogously, if all my constitutive relationships are replaced, I become a different person.
(3) Yet moral and legal practice treats people as continuous subjects over decades despite near-total turnover in relationships—friends, family, colleagues, and communities are replaced, but liability and obligation remain.
(4) So the relationalist needs some invariant “persistence layer”—a core substrate that survives relational updates—to ground continuity of responsibility.
(5) But that substrate is precisely the isolated, intrinsic self that relational personhood was meant to reject.
(6) Therefore, relational personhood either collapses into substance individualism or denies that anyone survives relational change, which would make contracts, criminal liability, and long-term promises impossible.
(7) The conclusion feels far more destructive than the premise warrants, but I cannot name the slip. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? If forced to preserve claims, I would keep (1) and (3)—the relational intuition and the continuity of responsibility both seem non-negotiable—but I want to know what I am trading away.
