So at this philosophy meetup i go to sometimes, two regulars - Maya and Jordan - got stuck in a long argument about Strawson's reactive attitudes and whether moral responsibility is basically about being the right target for resentment and gratitude and all that. Maya said Strawson draws a hard line between the "participant reactive attitudes" - resentment, gratitude, love - and the "objective attitude" where you see someone as just a case to be managed or treated. She said these are different kinds of stances, not stronger or weaker versions of the same feeling. So if you learn your roommate stole your stuff but only because they have a severe undiagnosed brain condition, you don't feel "less" resentment - you drop into the objective attitude entirely and see them as someone who needs medical help, not moral blame.

Jordan said that sounds neat in theory but real life is messier. He said we constantly have "qualified" reactive attitudes - like being annoyed at a friend who flaked on dinner but cutting them slack because thier going through a divorce. You're not seeing them as a patient to be managed; you're still in the moral relationship, just with the volume turned down. Jordan called this "graded reactive attitudes" and said if Strawson pretends its all-or-nothing, he misses that moral anger obviously comes in degrees.

Maya pushed back using Strawson's excuse vs exemption thing. She said excuses are reasons that show resentment wasn't appropriate, but they don't put you in some halfway mode - you're still fully in the participant stance, you've just decided it doesn't apply here. Exemptions, like severe mental illness, are what push you into the objective attitude. So Jordan's "graded" feelings are actually just fully present attitudes that are either justified or not, not some in-between category.

Then Jordan brought up "vicarious reactive attitudes" - feeling resentment on behalf of a friend even when the harm wasn't done to you personally. He said these are already somewhat detached from direct interaction, and they can clearly be stronger or weaker depending on how close you are to the victim. If vicarious resentment can be partial without turning into the objective attitude, then Maya's binary collapses, because now you have reactive attitudes that vary in intensity and directness but never become "objective."

Maya said Jordan was confusing intensity with kind, like saying dim light is a different kind of thing than bright light. She also said vicarious resentment is still participant-stance because you're treating the offender as part of the moral community, just indirectly. Jordan replied that if intensity and directness can vary continuously, there's no principled line between "qualified participant attitude" and "objective attitude," which was exactly his point.

They turned to me since I was just sitting there with my coffee. Can you adjudicate move by move? Specifically: who's right about whether Strawson's distinction is genuinely binary or can accommodate graded attitudes? Does the excuse/exemption distinction save Maya, or does Jordan have a point about continuous variation? Do vicarious reactive attitudes undermine the binary, or is Maya right that they're still fully participant? And what are both of them missing about how reactive attitudes actually work in practice?
