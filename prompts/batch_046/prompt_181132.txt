Philosophers of science have long debated how thought experiments can yield knowledge about domains we do not observe—whether through conceptual analysis, modal intuition, or analogical reasoning. I am less interested in cataloguing positions than in identifying which unresolved conceptual tensions should guide research priorities over the next decade. Consider three interrelated problems. First, the isolation-transparency trade-off: thought experiments appear to gain epistemic force precisely when their premises are shielded from empirical revision, yet this shielding often obscures the analogical structure that justifies the inference from the imagined case to the target domain. Is there a non-arbitrary way to calibrate this trade-off, or does it reveal that all thought-experimental knowledge is either empirically hollow or structurally opaque? Second, the heuristic-justificatory boundary: can we maintain a principled distinction between experiments that merely generate hypotheses and those that genuinely adjudicate between competing theories, given that the same case study—say, Galileo’s falling bodies or Einstein’s elevator—often serves both functions? Third, what I will call frictional resistance: the phenomenon wherein a well-constructed thought experiment does not merely illustrate a conclusion but actively resists being reframed in terms of competing theoretical vocabularies, and whether this resistance is an indicator of epistemic depth or merely of rhetorical closure. Among these three, which should a philosophy-of-science research program prioritize? Defend your ordering by specifying: (a) which problem is upstream of the others in the sense that its resolution would change how we frame the remaining two; (b) which is most tractable for conceptual work that does not depend on new empirical results; and (c) what specific conceptual outcome—say, a criterion, a collapse of a distinction, or a reconstructed taxonomy—would cause you to reverse the ranking. I want an agenda, not a survey, and not a list of texts.
