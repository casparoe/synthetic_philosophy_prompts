I am giving the opening address at an interdisciplinary workshop called "Forecasting the Unsettled," whose audience is split between philosophers of time and practicing forecasters—climate modelers, epidemiologists, and strategic analysts. The workshop's central tension is whether predictive practice presupposes an open future. Write a five-minute speech in which Aristotle's classic problem of tomorrow's sea battle functions as the structural spine, not as decoration: the argument of the address should be shaped by the paradox, so that what I can and cannot say is constrained by it. Specifically, the speech must navigate a narrow path: the philosophers should be able to hear it as taking a coherent stance on the truth-value of future contingent statements, without that stance ever being named; the forecasters should hear it as honoring the reality of their labor, without my committing to a metaphysics of openness they do not require. Do not resolve the paradox—leave the room in the same aporetic position Aristotle leaves his reader—but make the ambiguity do work. Introduce one concrete, invented scenario: a decision whether to evacuate a coastal region in the face of an uncertain storm trajectory, and let that scenario serve as the modern analog to the sea battle, with its own logical and practical ambiguity mirroring the original. The tone should be formal, suitable for a workshop opening, but not like a lecture; avoid all citations, bibliographies, references to modern commentators such as Łukasiewicz or Prior, and any named formal logic. Do not quote Aristotle verbatim—paraphrase any reference to his text. The speech should feel composed for this specific divided room, not generic.
