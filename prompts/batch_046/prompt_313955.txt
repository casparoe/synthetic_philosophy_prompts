I'm preparing material for a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind on whether dreams are experiences, with particular attention to the classic tension between Malcolm-style and Dennett-style positions. Two colleagues—Dr. Chen and Dr. Alvarez—debated this at length after a recent talk. I am reporting their exchange below as an uninvolved bystander and do not signal any preference between them. Please adjudicate their disagreement point by point.

The exchange proceeded as follows.

Dr. Chen: Dreams are not experiences in the philosophically relevant sense. The criterion for an experience being mine is that I possess first-person authority over it that is contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous. During sleep, there is no such authority; upon waking, I am interpreting neural residues, not recounting an episode I actually underwent. The waking self reconstructs a narrative; it does not recall an experience.

Dr. Alvarez: That conflates memory access with phenomenal occurrence. While Dennett's specific cassette model is problematic, the underlying point holds: REM sleep involves perceptual-like neural activation. If experience does not require linguistic reportability—which it clearly cannot, given infants and non-human animals—then the absence of a reporting mechanism during sleep does not entail the absence of phenomenal character. The waking report is evidence of prior phenomenal states, not fabrication ex nihilo.

Dr. Chen: Even granting some phenomenal character, there is a deeper problem about the subject. In waking experience, a continuous self undergoes the event. In ordinary sleep, that self is suspended or fragmented. The waking "I" that reports the dream is not continuous with any subject present during REM. Attributing the dream to "my experience" therefore requires an unjustified identity claim across a gap in consciousness—what we might call the retrospective appropriation problem.

Dr. Alvarez: That assumes personal identity requires continuous consciousness, which is precisely what's contested. More concretely, lucid dreamers report real-time self-presence during REM. If lucid dreaming demonstrates that self-monitoring capacity can operate in sleep, then ordinary dreams may differ only in degree—lacking activated self-monitoring but not lacking a subject altogether. You treat lucid dreams as exceptions that prove the rule, but they may instead reveal an underlying capacity present even in non-lucid REM.

Dr. Chen: Lucid dreaming is precisely the intrusion of waking-level meta-cognitive machinery into sleep. It is not proof that ordinary REM involves a subject; it is proof that when waking mechanisms activate, sleep states become genuinely experience-like. Ordinary dreams lack this machinery, so they lack the structural conditions necessary for being experiences. They are better understood as unowned mental events that the waking self later appropriates through narrative construction.

Dr. Alvarez: You are introducing an ad hoc criterion—"self-monitoring machinery"—that is not necessary for experience. Pain and affective states can occur without meta-cognition. If REM generates perceptual and affective states with phenomenal character, and the brain integrates them upon waking, then the experience occurred and the waking narrative is a possibly distorted report of it, not a creation out of nothing. Your position threatens to make first-person reports about dreams systematically unreliable in a way that undermines first-person authority more broadly.
