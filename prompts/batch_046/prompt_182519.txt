I'm a philosophy undergraduate in a seminar on moral psychology and Kantian ethics. Last week, two of my classmates, Maya and Leo, had a sustained disagreement about whether moral worth requires that the right reason be the sole motivating cause of an action, or whether it is enough for the right reason to be genuinely operative among other motives. They asked me to relay the exchange to someone outside the room and asked for a point-by-point adjudication; I am reporting it here as a bystander without taking sides.

(1) Maya opened with a strict Kantian line: an action possesses moral worth only if it is done from duty, and if self-interest, fear of punishment, or the desire for social approval is what actually explains the agent's behavior, then no moral worth attaches, even if the agent also recognizes the correct moral reason. She illustrated this with the case of returning a lost wallet primarily to avoid legal trouble, arguing that the presence of the moral belief is psychologically inert if it is not the cause that moves the agent.

(2) Leo replied that this standard makes moral worth psychologically unattainable for ordinary people, since human motivation is almost always overdetermined. He proposed that worth should depend on whether the right reason is among the sufficient conditions for the action: if I return the wallet both because it is right and because I fear punishment, and either reason alone would have produced the same behavior, then the moral reason is still genuinely operative, so at least partial worth remains.

(3) Maya countered that Leo's "operative presence" criterion collapses an important distinction between individual agents and collective institutions. She asked us to imagine a corporation that approves a charitable donation solely for tax advantages, but in which one board member genuinely cares about the cause and her vote happens to break a tie. By Leo's logic, she argued, either the corporation or that board member's contribution acquires moral worth, yet this seems wrong: the institutional decision is structurally explained by the wrong reason, and isolating one mental state ignores how reasons actually function in collective agency.

(4) Leo responded that Maya's institutional analogy actually supports his view. We deny moral worth to the corporation, he said, not because presence is insufficient, but because no single agent within it acts for the right reason in a counterfactually robust way. If the board member's care genuinely explains her decisive vote—meaning she would have cast it the same way even without the tax incentive—then she does possess moral worth, and Maya's "sole cause" requirement wrongly excludes her. The proper criterion, Leo suggested, should be counterfactual robustness: would the agent still have performed the right action if the wrong reasons had been absent?

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what important consideration—whether about collective agency, psychological realism, or the conditions for counterfactual testing—both parties are neglecting in their exchange.
