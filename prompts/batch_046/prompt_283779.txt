During a seminar on biological ontology, my colleagues Mara and Eli argued about whether science ever carves nature at its joints. Mara held that bacterial species defined by reproductive isolation—measured, for instance, by average nucleotide identity—track real, mind-independent boundaries, analogous to the chemical elements. Eli proposed that soil microbes are better grouped into what he termed "coupling clusters": stable metabolite-exchange networks that cross-cut genetic lineages but predict ecological dynamics more reliably than phylogenetic trees. He claimed these clusters are the true joints, while genetic taxonomy merely records historical ancestry.

Mara objected that predictive success is not ontology; grouping organisms by metabolic interaction is no more a discovery of natural boundaries than grouping them by size or habitat preference. She insisted that reproductive isolation is a causal mechanism producing discrete entities, whereas coupling clusters are contingent patterns sustained by local environmental conditions.

Eli replied that Mara was privileging "essences" over functional systems without argument. If joint-carving means tracking the divisions that ground successful induction and intervention, then coupling clusters qualify, and the burden is on her to justify why genetic isolation possesses unique metaphysical status. Mara countered that this collapses every useful taxonomy into ontology, erasing the distinction between discovery and invention and yielding an implausible pluralism in which nature has infinitely many equally valid joint-structures.

Can you adjudicate their exchange step by step—assessing whether reproductive isolation genuinely marks mind-independent joints, whether predictive and interventional success alone can establish natural kindhood, and whether Eli's pluralism is fatal or defensible? Also identify what both parties miss regarding interventionist theories of causation and how that omission affects their dispute.
