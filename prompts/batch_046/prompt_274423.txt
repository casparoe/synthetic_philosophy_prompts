Here are notes for a short essay I am drafting on the ethics of memory, using Margalit’s defense of a duty to remember and Rieff’s case for the value of forgetting as points of departure. The essay’s central move is to argue that both positions implicitly treat memory and forgetting as mental or communal acts, and therefore overlook what happens when remembrance is delegated to distributed digital archives. In such systems, data rarely disappear; forgetting becomes not absence but retrieval failure, and honoring a duty to remember shifts from an attitude of moral care to an obligation to maintain infrastructure, metadata, and access protocols. Conversely, Rieff’s claim that societies sometimes need to forget in order to heal assumes that erasure is practically available, yet in redundant, geographically dispersed storage true deletion is technically arduous and institutionally resisted. I propose reconceiving forgetting as “access decay” — the deliberate design of retrieval pathways that become obsolete without destroying the underlying record — and I ask whether this notion can bear the normative weight Rieff assigns to forgetting, or whether it collapses into mere neglect. I want comments specifically on three points: first, whether framing collective memory as an information-management problem commits a category error against the moral phenomenology Margalit describes; second, whether “access decay” is conceptually coherent or only a technical metaphor smuggled into ethics; and third, where the argument is most vulnerable to the objection that I have replaced a normative debate with a description of storage architecture. Please do not suggest readings, bibliographies, or citations; I am interested only in conceptual accuracy and structural weaknesses.
