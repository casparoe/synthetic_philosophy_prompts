I'm taking a comparative institutions course and got into a long argument with my roomate about reservation and quota systems after reading some policy briefs. We kept comparing three specific designs: India's "creamy layer" exclusion for OBC reservations, where if your parents hold certain government posts or earn above a cutoff you lose eligibility even if your caste is still listed as backward; Norway's board gender quota that applies only to public-limited companies (ASA) but not to private-limited ones (AS); and the US university practice of holistic review for affirmative action that considers race but refuses to use explicit quotas.

What puzzles me is that all three treat group membership as a proxy for something else, but they seem to disagree on what that proxy is tracking. The Indian creamy layer looks like it presupposes that caste disadvantage is mainly intergenerational economic/occupational and can be "used up" within a family, which sounds closer to a Rawlsian primary-goods view than to a recognition-based view of caste harm. The Norwegian quota being limited to ASAs seems to presuppose that gender parity matters specifically where corporate authority touches public democratic accountability, not in private economic life generally — so it's not a universal representation principle but something tied to state-adjacent power. And US holistic review avoids quotas but still weights race, which might presuppose that diversity is an educational good for all students rather than pure compensation for past injustice.

But I'm not sure these practices actually need those theories, or if they're just philosophically compatible with them. Can you reconstruct the implicit commitments — about fairness, merit, group identity, and what makes a claim legitimate — that each design strictly presupposes versus merely fits well with? Say where each practice is philosophically underdetermined, so a rival theory could license the exact same institutional rules. And if we replaced the underlying view in each case — say, treating caste as a recognition harm that can't be monetized away, gender parity as a basic right in all firms, or race-conscious admissions as straight reparations — what specific feature of the current practice would have to break or change? I don't need reading lists, just the conceptual reconstruction.
