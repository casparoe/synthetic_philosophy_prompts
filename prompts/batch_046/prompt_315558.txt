Since retiring from experimental physics and turning to philosophy in earnest, I have been working through Milton Friedman’s essay The Methodology of Positive Economics and find myself caught in an argument of my own that seems to collapse into absurdity. I should like to know where, if anywhere, it slips.

(1) Friedman’s so-called F-twist holds that the realism of a model’s assumptions is irrelevant to its scientific merit; predictive accuracy and fruitfulness alone matter.

(2) Every successful scientific model employs idealisations—point masses, frictionless surfaces, discretised grid cells—without which computation would be intractable.

(3) Hence unrealistic assumptions are not, by themselves, scientific defects; indeed they are structurally necessary.

(4) In contemporary practice—climate forecasting, for instance—scientists do not reject a model merely because its parameterisations are unrealistic; instead they run multi-model ensembles that vary those parameterisations across members.

(5) If predictions diverge across the ensemble, the divergence is attributed to the varied auxiliary assumptions, not to the shared unrealistic core, such as the grid discretisation itself.

(6) Therefore any unrealistic feature shared across all ensemble members is insulated from empirical criticism: predictive failure can always be blamed on the varying auxiliaries rather than on the shared idealisation.

(7) By generalising this protective strategy, no core idealisation in any scientific domain can ever be falsified, since one can always embed it in an ensemble and attribute failure elsewhere.

(8) But this makes scientific models unfalsifiable with respect to their foundational assumptions, which contradicts the basic scientific commitment to revising core theories in the face of persistent, systematic anomaly.

(9) So either ensemble methods are fundamentally anti-scientific, or the F-twist—when combined with standard ensemble practice—proves too much.

(10) I am reluctant to abandon (1), since it captures something genuine about modelling practice, and I am equally reluctant to deny (9), since my career was premised on science being genuinely revisable rather than permanently insulated.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? In particular, is there a principled way to preserve Friedman’s insight without rendering core scientific idealisations permanently immune from revision? I should prefer to keep (1) and (4) if I must choose, but I want to understand the price.

I am not asking for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists—only for the reasoning itself.
