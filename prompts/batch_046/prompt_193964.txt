You are serving as an external grader for a graduate seminar in moral psychology. The instructor has provided the exam question and one student response; your job is to produce a point-by-point assessment and a final grade, without signaling whether you personally agree with the answer. The exam asked:

Question — In her framework of institutional repair logic, Elena Vasquez distinguishes guilt from shame not by publicity or privacy, but by the form of social repair each demands. Guilt, she argues, operates through proportional restitution: the offender owes a specific, quantifiable repair to the victim, and the emotion is satisfied when that debt is discharged. Shame operates through status recalibration: the offender must renegotiate their standing within a community, and satisfaction comes from restored recognition rather than from a settled account. Vasquez claims that a culture can rationally choose which logic to institutionalize, but that this is never a choice of which emotion is intrinsically superior; rather, it depends on whether the culture’s legal and social structures can reliably deliver the corresponding repair. She further maintains that if a culture institutionalizes shame-based repair, the normative authority of shame does not disappear but shifts: it derives from communal recognition rather than from individual conscience, and this is a feature, not a deficit.

Evaluate Vasquez’s position. Specifically: (1) Is the restitution/recalibration distinction robust, or does it collapse under counterexamples? (2) Is cultural choice genuinely open, or are there psychological or structural constraints that make one logic unavoidable for a given society? (3) Does institutionalizing one logic undermine the normative authority of the corresponding emotion?

The student’s answer (excerpted):

Vasquez’s repair logic is a useful reframing. Guilt works like a debt contract: I damage your property, I owe you repair costs, and once paid, the moral account is closed. Shame works like a demotion: I violate a norm that defines my place, so I must either be reinstated or expelled. This captures the distinction well.

That said, Vasquez overstates cultural flexibility. The classical account of shame—developed in the tradition associated with Bernard Williams—shows that shame depends on an internalized audience, a psychological structure in which the self is watched by an imagined other. Not all cultures cultivate this structure equally; where it is absent, shame-based repair is not a live option but an alien emotional technology. By contrast, guilt depends only on individual conscience and direct victim interaction, so it is structurally available everywhere. A culture cannot genuinely choose shame if its members lack the requisite internal audience.

On normative authority, Vasquez is half right but misses the danger. If a culture institutionalizes shame, its authority does come from communal recognition, but that makes it conventional. Once an emotion’s force depends on communal agreement, it loses the unconditional, non-negotiable quality that moral obligations are supposed to have. So institutional choice undermines shame’s authority. Guilt avoids this problem: because restitution is objective—what is owed is fixed independently of communal opinion—its authority survives institutionalization intact. In short, Vasquez correctly maps the two logics, but she is wrong that choice is free, and she underestimates the cost to normative authority when a culture picks shame.
