I am preparing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of consciousness and personal identity, and a colleague has circulated the following summary from an interdisciplinary review. I would like your assessment of its accuracy and fairness, since I want the students to see how a competent popularization can sound authoritative while smuggling in interpretive leaps. The summary states, in effect, the following. Roger Sperry's split-brain experiments established that transection of the corpus callosum generates two independent streams of consciousness housed within a single cranium. The left hemisphere governs language and the right hand, the right hemisphere governs spatial cognition and the left hand, and when stimuli are restricted to one hemisphere the other behaves with no cognitive access whatsoever. Consequently, the normal brain does not contain one unified consciousness but rather two sub-personal consciousnesses that are ordinarily fused only by the callosal bridge. Any philosophical defense of consciousness's unity—whether Cartesian, Kantian, or phenomenological—must therefore reconceive the corpus callosum not as a mere neural pathway but as a metaphysical unifier of experience. Furthermore, because split-brain patients can manifest contradictory volitions—say, one hand unbuttoning a garment while the other buttons it—this demonstrates that personal identity can persist through a bifurcation of consciousness, thereby refuting any account that makes numerical identity depend on a single continuous stream of awareness.

Please evaluate this summary as you would an exam answer that mixes empirical reporting with philosophical inference. I want you to distinguish what is experimentally well-established from what is an overreading of the data; to identify any conflation between hemispheric functional independence and the metaphysical claim that normal brains contain dual consciousness; to assess whether the inference about personal identity actually follows from the clinical observations or whether it presupposes a contested theory of identity; and to say whether the characterization of the philosophical tradition is fair or a straw man. In particular: does the summary accurately represent Sperry's own interpretive conclusions, does it correctly describe the contemporary philosophical consensus regarding split-brain cases, and is there an unacknowledged assumption in the move from disconnection pathology to claims about the structure of ordinary consciousness? Please be precise about which sentences a careful philosopher of mind would accept, which they would qualify, and which they would reject outright, and indicate whether any of the conclusions drawn survive once the misrepresentations are corrected.
