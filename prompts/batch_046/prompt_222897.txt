I've been working through some philosophy of mathematics with a study group—mostly people who read rationalist blogs and like to stress-test arguments with toy models—and we keep hitting the same wall when we talk about Hilbert's hotel. The setup is familiar: a hotel with infinitely many occupied rooms, one new guest arrives, everyone shifts up by one, and there's suddenly a vacancy at room one. Most people in the group concede that the shift works. But then I present a second case: instead of shifting, we pair each current guest in room n with room 2n, leaving every odd-numbered room empty, and we move infinitely many new guests into those odd rooms. Logically, the pairing is just as valid. Yet the same people who accepted the shift now object, not by disputing the pairing, but by pointing to the empty rooms and saying that the original hotel was not actually full—that there was always this unused capacity sitting there, so accommodating more guests does not prove anything about the size of an actual infinite set, only about how you distribute people within an open-ended structure.

I see a parallel with reactions to Cantor-style arguments. When shown that a list of real numbers can be extended by constructing a number not on it, people often respond that the list was never finished to begin with, treating the absence as a failure of completion rather than a feature of the set's cardinality. In both cases, the formal step is granted—yes, the shift works; yes, the constructed number differs—but the philosophical takeaway is blocked by an appeal to leftover space or an unfinished process. The people doing this are not classical finitists; they do not deny that infinite collections exist, and they will use infinite sequences in other contexts without complaint. They seem to be applying a specific kind of intuition about residual potential—unused slots, unactualized positions—to neutralize claims about equality or containment among completed infinities.

Is there an established name for this particular move? It feels close to potentialism about infinity, but standard potentialism denies that there is a completed infinite totality at all, whereas here people seem to accept the totality and then invoke unused capacity within it to resist comparative cardinality claims. It also is not quite the fallacy of division or simple equivocation, because the objection targets specifically the interpretation of the formal result, not the logic itself. If there is a recognized concept—perhaps in the literature on Hilbert's hotel pedagogics, or in discussions of finitist resistance to Cantor—I would like to know what it is, how precisely it differs from generic potentialism or from the intuition that infinity is not a number, and where its boundaries lie. If there is no established term, can you name and define the phenomenon in a way that distinguishes it from both finitism and from ordinary confusion about infinite sets, including a boundary case where the intuition is legitimate rather than a misapplication? Please answer in American English, and please do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I am asking because I want to understand the conceptual landscape on its own terms, not to chase down sources I cannot verify without web access.
