I'm putting together a philosophy seminar on free will and I wanna use Groundhog Day as a case study, but I'm worried it'll just turn into "he learns to be nice so he gets free." Read the film through sphexishness—Dennett's wasp that repeats its burrow-check ritual exactly, and if you interupt it, it starts over from scratch. Phil Connors is literally stuck in a loop, but the loop isn't totally rigid: he remembers, he experiments, he eventually learns piano. So where exactly is the sphexish boundary? Is his repetition mechanical in the wasp sense, or is memory already enough to break sphexishness? And when he finally "escapes" by being a good person, is that genuine flexibility—responding to reasons—or just a more complex algorithm that happens to satisfy the loop's exit condition? I want the class to see whether the movie actually dramatizes free will or just dresses up behavioral conditioning in rom-com clothes. Which scenes show real counterfactual openness versus just accumulated skill? And does the film accidentally suggest that being good is just the optimal strategy for escaping repetition, which kinda undermines the moral point?
