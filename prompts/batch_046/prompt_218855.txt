I'm working through my own judgments about the moral distinction between doing harm and allowing it, especially where killing is involved rather than merely letting die, and I want to see whether they cohere around a principle or whether some verdicts have to be revised. Here are my concrete case judgments:

- A physician withdraws life support from a terminally ill patient who has clearly requested it; this feels permissible, and it counts as allowing death rather than causing it.
- A physician administers a lethal injection to a terminally ill patient who has made the same request; this feels impermissible, and the patient's consent does not change that.
- A surgeon kills one healthy patient and harvests his organs to save five dying patients; this is gravely wrong, and the numerical balance is irrelevant.
- A hospital administrator redirects scarce funds away from a cheap vaccine that would save five children at a remote clinic—knowing they will die—in order to fund an experimental therapy for one prominent donor; this is wrong, but my intuition is that it is less gravely wrong than the surgeon case, despite the reversed body count.
- A city engineer chooses not to repair an aging dam she knows will fail and flood a downstream neighborhood, killing dozens, because the repair budget was reallocated to another municipal project; this omission feels closer to actively causing the deaths than to merely failing to prevent them.
- An autonomous vehicle is programmed with a crash-avoidance rule that, in an unavoidable scenario, will steer onto a sidewalk and kill one pedestrian rather than stay course and kill five passengers; I think adopting this programming rule is permissible, though I would not say the same if a human driver made an identical split-second choice in real time.
- A government that had been distributing clean water to a besieged population abruptly stops, knowing dehydration will kill hundreds; this feels like killing, not like allowing.

These verdicts are mostly stable, but I am troubled by the tension between the surgeon and the administrator—both involve an agent making a decision that results in some dying so that others live, yet I treat them very differently. I also keep going back and forth on whether the autonomous-vehicle programming case is genuinely distinct from the human-driver version.

What principle, if any, captures all of these judgments? I want an account that explains why consent matters in the life-support case but not in the lethal-injection case, why the surgeon-administrator comparison comes out the way it does (or forces me to drop one verdict), and why an institutional omission like the dam failure can feel like a doing. Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause handles which case, predict what it says about a case I haven't listed—specifically, a climate-adaptation agency that quietly defunds a coastal-defense project knowing storm-related deaths will increase, without ever directly harming anyone—and be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate if I insist on keeping every clause simple. If no single principle works, present the two best candidates and say exactly what each costs me in terms of overturned verdicts.
