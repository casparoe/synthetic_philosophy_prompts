Question: Distinguish original intentionality from derived intentionality. Explain Dennett’s two-bitser thought experiment and how it relates to the intentional stance. Then discuss whether an autonomous predictive system—rather than a conscious human observer—could serve as the interpreter that assigns derived intentionality to the two-bitser, and evaluate whether this possibility undermines Searle’s claim that derived intentionality necessarily depends on minds with original intentionality.

Student answer:

"Original intentionality is the intrinsic aboutness of mental states like beliefs and desires; derived intentionality is the meaning that symbols, texts, or artifacts possess only because minds with original intentionality assign it to them. Dennett’s two-bitser is a minimal computer with two bits of memory that answers yes or no to questions about its environment, such as whether it is raining or whether there is a fire. It operates through pure syntax without any internal understanding. Dennett argues that once the two-bitser is embedded in a sensorimotor causal network, its internal states acquire functional roles that map onto environmental conditions, and this functional organization is what gives it derived intentionality.

Searle’s Chinese Room argument shows that manipulating symbols by syntax alone never produces understanding. Searle grants that if a computer exactly duplicated a brain’s causal powers, it would possess original intentionality, which is why he insists that only biological substrates can have it. Dennett rejects this: he holds that the intentional stance applies to any system whose behavior is predictively tractable that way, so the two-bitser already has real intentional states, though only derived ones. For Dennett, even human original intentionality is just a more complex case of interpretive assignment—evolution and social practice apply the stance to brains—so there is no metaphysically primitive source of aboutness. Consequently, an autonomous predictive system could indeed assign derived intentionality to the two-bitser, and Searle’s argument fails because it wrongly assumes that derived intentionality must bottom out in a special biological property rather than in interpretive practice."
