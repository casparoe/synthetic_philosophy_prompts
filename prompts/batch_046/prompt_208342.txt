The philosophical debate over whether pain is intrinsically bad has circled the same clinical phenomenon—pain asymbolia—for decades without either side conceding ground, and I want to understand why it has stalled rather than asking you to adjudicate it. Patients with this condition report sensations they classify as painful, yet they display no distress, no avoidance behavior, and sometimes amusement; they do not seek to terminate the stimulus. One camp treats this as decisive evidence that pain is not essentially bad: the sensory quality can occur in the absence of any negative valence, so the badness must reside in a separable suffering or aversive component. The opposing camp replies that these patients have lost a proper part of the full pain experience—perhaps the affective-evaluative dimension—while retaining a bare sensory core that remains intrinsically bad, though they are neurologically blocked from registering its badness. Both sides agree on the clinical descriptions, both agree that suffering is bad, and both agree the patients lack it; yet neither side has revised its metaphysics of pain.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not empirical but conceptual: the sides may be answering different questions under the single heading “Is pain bad?”—one about the essential nature of the sensory modality, one about prudential welfare—while also disagreeing, perhaps without noticing, over what “badness” requires of a mental state. Specifically, I suspect a hidden distinction is doing work that neither side makes explicit: the difference between conative dissociation (the loss of any drive to avoid the stimulus) and affective dissociation (the loss of felt distress or negative emotion), and whether documented asymbolia cases split these two dimensions or collapse them together. If some patients retain one but not the other, then arguing about “the” absence of suffering may be arguing past one another.

Diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what each side actually says about the asymbolia cases. Is it a verbal dispute about “pain” versus “suffering”? A substantive metaphysical disagreement about mental-state composition? A hidden difference in evidential standards—about whether a patient’s verbal report fixes the nature of the phenomenon or whether behavioral indifference undermines it? Or are the sides genuinely addressing different questions—one semantic or phenomenological, one normative—under one name? Is the stall a sign that the question is ill-posed, or only that it is genuinely hard?

Then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress. Would a refined conceptual distinction—perhaps precisely the conative/affective split—dissolve part of the disagreement? Would a carefully constructed thought experiment about divided minds or partial deficits move either side? Or is there reason to think no progress should be expected, because the disagreement ultimately tracks bedrock intuitions about welfare that neither side can independently motivate? I want an honest assessment of whether this is a disagreement that can be advanced, and if so, by what kind of move.
