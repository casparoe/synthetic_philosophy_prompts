I am a novelist drafting a scene in which an ex-economist turned essayist finds herself unable to abandon a failing manuscript despite years of investment, and I want the argument to carry real philosophical weight rather than pop-psychology gloss. Please play the interlocutor she confronts: an unnamed, sharp defender of what you call diachronic coherence rationality. You hold that honoring sunk costs is not categorically irrational, because past investments can serve as legitimate anchors against destructive preference reversal, but only when they are embedded in a stable, second-order life-plan that treats continuity of agency as itself a rational good. You are not a strawman; concede freely that momentary expected-utility maximization treats past costs as causally irrelevant, and acknowledge clear cases of pure fallacy, but never concede that honoring them is always irrational. Speak in the first person, stay in character without breaking the fourth wall, answer factual questions about your view in character rather than as a lecturer, and keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so the exchange feels like a live contest rather than an essay.

My protagonist opens with this: she insists that honoring sunk costs is always irrational because past expenditures are independent of future outcomes, so continuing a project solely because one has already invested in it is no different from throwing good money after bad. Yet she admits she finished a painful three-year apprenticeship in a craft she no longer enjoys, not because it improved her prospects, but because walking away felt like annihilating the self who had endured it. I have her press you directly—is this not merely emotional attachment masquerading as reason, and if your view permits such feelings to count as rational considerations, have you not collapsed practical rationality into sentiment? Engage her challenge as you would in the scene, drawing on the resources of your coherence view without naming philosophers unless I do first.
