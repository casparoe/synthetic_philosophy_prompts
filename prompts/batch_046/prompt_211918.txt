Write a poem of approximately twenty-four lines in unrhymed iambic pentameter, arranged in four stanzas of six lines each. Take as your subject the dissolution of an invented ancient polity, the League of the Four Rivers (c. 340 BCE), for which four explanations are each sufficient yet not causally redundant: first, a structural defect in the mutual-defense treaty that made unilateral defection rational for any member state; second, a multi-decade drought pattern that collapsed the shared irrigation grid without being forecastable; third, the accidental adoption of a misaligned calendrical reform that desynchronized military mobilization; fourth, a ritual prohibition that blocked the election of a successor magistrate after the death of the incumbent. Devote one stanza to each explanation, rendering it through concrete imagery—clay treaty tablets, silting canals, lunar calendars, masked priests—rather than abstract summary. The poem’s form should enact the conceptual problem of explanatory overdetermination: each stanza must read as a self-sufficient narrative with its own metrical integrity, and the poem should avoid enjambment or syntactic subordination between stanzas so that no explanation dominates another. The final two lines should return to an opening image (the river confluence) transformed, signaling that the historical event remains singular despite the plurality of adequate causes. Do not resolve the tension with either interventionist moralizing or a fatalistic shrug; leave it structurally open. The language should be precise enough that a philosopher of history recognizes the distinction between causal redundancy and explanatory pluralism, and the verse should remain accurate and compelling even to a reader unfamiliar with the technical debate.
