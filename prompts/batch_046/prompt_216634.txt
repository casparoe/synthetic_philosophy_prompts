As a novelist accustomed to shaping character through selective detail rather than exhaustive confession, I wonder whether biography can still teach virtue now that hagiography is disreputable. The controversy is whether Plutarch’s practice—pairing Greek and Roman lives as parallel moral experiments in which a single character is tested against different political conditions—offers a defensible model of exemplarity, or whether modern biography’s insistence on flaws and contradictions makes moral learning from lives impossible by destroying the very clarity a reader needs to imitate.

Write an essay that reaches and justifies a conclusion about whether idealized biography remains necessary for genuine moral instruction.

Organize your response in four named sections, in this order:
- The laboratory analogy (about 250 words): explain how paired lives can function as comparative moral experiments rather than mere history, and what they assume about the portability of virtue across regimes.
- The defense of selective illumination (about 300 words): argue that some suppression of flaw is required for exemplarity, using the contrast with modern biography that treats moral failure as fully explanatory.
- The critique of idealization (about 300 words): argue that hagiography produces false psychology, and that readers learn more from recognizing shared failure than from distant perfection.
- Verdict and defense (about 250 words): state your conclusion plainly and reply to the strongest counter-argument.
