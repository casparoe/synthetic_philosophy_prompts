I was sitting in on a graduate seminar on international criminal law last week and overheard two classmates continuing an argument in the hallway afterward. They were debating whether the retroactive charges at Nuremberg and Tokyo can be defended as genuinely legal, rather than as exercises in victors' justice.

(1) The first classmate argued that Nuremberg was not truly retroactive because the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928 had already outlawed aggressive war, giving the defendants legal notice; the tribunal merely added the missing penal sanction. (2) The second replied that the Pact contained no criminal penalties and was widely regarded as a diplomatic aspiration, not a penal statute, so individuals could not have foreseen personal criminal liability—making the charges ex post facto in the meaningful sense. (3) The first then said that Tokyo was actually the harder case: Japan had also signed the Pact, yet the Tokyo Tribunal applied crimes against peace to conflicts in Asia where the Pact's relevance was far more tenuous, and did so without the explicit charter framework that Nuremberg possessed, suggesting Tokyo relied more nakedly on victors' discretion. (4) The second countered that this misses the core problem, which is not retroactivity but structural hypocrisy—no Allied commander was tried for the bombing of civilian populations—so the law applied was really just the victors' moral judgment in judicial robes; he added that in a system with no independent sovereign legislator, selective enforcement does not merely corrupt the law but collapses the distinction between creating a new offense and selecting a new court, since both are acts of the same political will.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. In particular, say who is right about whether the Kellogg-Briand Pact supplied adequate notice, whether Tokyo is distinguishable from Nuremberg on retroactivity grounds, and whether selective enforcement undermines the legal status of the charges or is only a separate moral failing. Also identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—perhaps regarding the distinction between ex post facto jurisdiction and ex post facto offense, or about how customary international law was understood in the 1940s.
