Consider the following argument, which attempts to derive an implausible consequence from Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith. (1) Radical freedom holds that human reality is never determined by its facticity—past choices, social roles, psychological dispositions, or material conditions—because consciousness can always reinterpret and transcend any given situation. (2) Bad faith is the refusal to acknowledge this freedom, treating oneself instead as a fixed being-in-itself, for instance by claiming that one cannot pursue a new career because one is essentially a risk-averse person. (3) If freedom is genuinely total, then the very act of denying it falls within freedom's scope; bad faith is not caused by circumstance but is itself a freely adopted stance. (4) Yet if one freely adopts a stance of self-deception, one must grasp—at least pre-reflectively—the freedom that is being concealed, for one cannot choose to deny X without some awareness of X. (5) This means that the person in bad faith knows, at some level, exactly what they are hiding, which renders the deception structurally unstable and immediately dissolvable by a single act of will. (6) It therefore follows that anyone in bad faith is not only fully responsible for their condition but also able to exit it instantaneously, without need for therapy, gradual transformation, or changes in social conditions. (7) However, Sartre describes bad faith as widespread and deeply entrenched, and he frequently appeals to fear of freedom, social pressure, or the weight of facticity to explain why particular individuals fall into it—appeals that seem to treat these factors as genuine constraints rather than as freely chosen interpretations. (8) We are thus faced with a dilemma: either we must condemn every instance of bad faith as a fully transparent, instantly correctable failure for which the agent bears absolute moral blame—thereby rendering Sartre's explanatory and sympathetic appeals illegitimate—or we must concede that freedom is genuinely limited by conditions that constrain interpretation, which undermines the radicality of the doctrine. I am inclined to preserve (1) and (2), but I am unsure whether the flaw lies in (4) or in (6). Identify the weakest step, explain why it initially seemed sound, and specify what abandoning it costs for Sartre's broader ontology of human reality.
