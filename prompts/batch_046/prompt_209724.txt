I've been trying to understand why unemployment benefits now come with signed "claimant commitments" that require attending appointments, submitting a set number of job applications, and accepting any officially designated "suitable" work, with "sanctions" — weeks or months of suspended payments — for missing a step. The same system treats an accidental failure to declare a partner's income as potential fraud, demanding repayment and investigation, whereas accidental underpayment of income tax is usually handled administratively without moral suspicion. At the same time, employed citizens face no equivalent penalty for failing to optimize their careers, and the criteria for "suitable" work are set by officials, not by the recipient's own judgment about health, skills, or dignity. What view of responsibility and fairness does this institutional bundle presuppose? Does it require that welfare recipients are bound by a special contract with stricter duties of transparency and obedience than ordinary citizenship demands? Is there a single, consistent theory of human choice behind both the sanction for a missed appointment and the denial of benefits to someone who refuses work they consider harmful? Or does the practice quietly combine two incompatible pictures — unemployment as structural misfortune in one office, and as voluntary failure of will in another — applying whichever serves the administrative purpose at hand?
