I have been asked to lead a single fifty-minute session for first-year education students on Locke’s philosophy of education, and the assignment has become personally urgent: my sister, who recently had her first child, asked whether Locke’s empiricism implies that an infant should be treated as an empty vessel waiting for sensory imprint. I want the students to grasp the actual architecture of his pedagogy—how bodily hardening, deferred literacy, and the emulation of manners depend on one another—rather than recycling the popular caricature. Plan the explanatory arc: which concept must be secured before the others can make sense, specifically his distinction between conditioning the body and training the understanding; the one concrete comparison that should carry the entire session, namely the contrast between his program for a gentleman’s education—deferred Latin, practical mathematics, travel—and his lesser-known proposal for working schools for poor children, and why this comparison succeeds where the vulgar “blank slate” image fails; the misconceptions the students will bring or form, including that Locke denies all innate dispositions, that he prescribes cold baths and loose clothing as punitive rather than physiological conditioning, and that his design was exclusively for aristocratic boys with no regard for the poor; and exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted before it hardens. Include one diagnostic question I can ask at the end to distinguish students who have understood the dependency structure from those who are only repeating anecdotes. Explicitly name one element of Locke’s broader political theory that should be set aside as out of scope at this level, and conclude with a line reading Verdict: followed by the first concept that must be introduced.
