I am in the process of redesigning the tagging taxonomy for a digital humanities archive, and the work has forced me to confront how violently the standard epochal labels—“Middle Ages,” “Renaissance,” and “Scientific Revolution”—distort the continuities I observe in manuscript illumination practices, astronomical instrumentation, and legal rhetoric between roughly 1350 and 1650. Next week I am interviewing Dr. A. M. Kowalski, a philosopher of history who has argued that these three categories are not historical discoveries but nineteenth-century sorting regimes that impose a false teleology, though she also maintains that such regimes can possess “archival fidelity” when they track real discontinuities in the conditions for justified belief rather than in events themselves. I need eight to ten interview questions that avoid asking her merely to restate this framework; instead, they should press on its internal tensions, anticipate her standard defenses, and include sharper follow-ups keyed to the answers she is most likely to give.

Specifically, please include the following:

One question on what “archival fidelity” could possibly mean if the periods themselves are invented, together with a follow-up addressed to her likely reply that fidelity is a second-order property of the taxonomy rather than of the past itself.

One question that presents a concrete continuous tradition—lens-grinding and optical theory running from late-medieval spectacle makers through Galileo’s workshop—and asks whether her view forces an artificial split or instead permits recognition of a “slow fracture” that the standard triad obscures; include a brief anticipated answer and a sharper follow-up.

One question on the normative stakes: since funding bodies and museum departments are still organized around these three names, does her constructivism entail that they should be abolished, or can they survive as heuristic fictions with genuine institutional utility? Provide the follow-up for her probable defense that utility and ontology are separate issues.

One question addressing the Middle Ages/Renaissance boundary specifically through humanist rhetoric: if humanism is treated as the defining rupture, how does her framework account for the persistence of scholastic argumentation well into the sixteenth century and its adoption by figures conventionally placed in the Renaissance? Include the follow-up.

A concluding question asking what empirical discovery—say, a securely dated manuscript demonstrating that a particular conceptual shift occurred uniformly across Europe in 1483—would genuinely threaten her view, and what pattern of evidence she would accept as confirming that a discontinuity is real rather than taxonomically projected.

The questions should be suitable for an audience familiar with the broad narrative of early modern science but not with professional historiography. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not include generic biographical questions; and make each question turn on the philosophical substance of her position.
