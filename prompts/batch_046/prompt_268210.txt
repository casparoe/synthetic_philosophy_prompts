At a recent writers' retreat in the countryside, I overheard a prolonged dispute between two fellow novelists—let me call them Elena and Marcus—concerning a character I have been developing for my present manuscript: a provincial archivist named Clara who, upon unexpectedly inheriting sufficient means to relocate to Paris, refuses the possibility with the calm insistence that she is "simply not the kind of person who lives among strangers." Elena argued that this refusal is a paradigmatic instance of Sartrean bad faith: Clara treats her accumulated habits and local attachments as a fixed essence, an en-soi that predetermines her choices, precisely to avoid the vertigo of recognizing that she is radically free and therefore solely responsible for inventing herself without ontological guarantee. Marcus objected that Elena was conflating bad faith with a legitimate acknowledgment of facticity; for Sartre, he noted, freedom is never abstract but always situated, and Clara's history—her embodied rhythms, her network of obligations, her linguistic world—is not an excuse but the very substance through which any authentic choice must be enacted; to dismiss her reluctance as mere self-deception is therefore to reduce Sartre's ethics to a demand for arbitrary caprice. Elena countered that Marcus's appeal to "situation" collapses the critical distinction Sartre laboriously maintains: if every refusal can be recast as respectful attention to facticity, then the concept of bad faith becomes vacuous, and the waiter who believes he is nothing but a waiter becomes indistinguishable from the individual who genuinely understands his role as one possibility among others. Marcus replied that the difference lies not in the refusal itself but in the affective structure behind it—Clara experiences acute anxiety about leaving, which demonstrates that she knows her refusal is a choice for which she is accountable, whereas the waiter in bad faith denies the possibility of being otherwise altogether; hence Clara is not fleeing her freedom but enduring it. They asked me, as the one who invented Clara, whether she is in bad faith, but I found I could not adjudicate their arguments without first knowing where each move succeeds or fails on Sartre's own terms. Would you go through their exchange step by step—rating each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification—and indicate not merely who prevails overall, but precisely what both interlocutors overlook regarding the relationship between anguish, facticity, and the invention of the self?
