Construct a thought experiment that adapts the Mary’s-room scenario to test whether the knowledge argument survives once we distinguish recognitional abilities that depend on constructing an internal sensory map from those that require direct affective calibration. Design a realistic case, set in a cognitive-neuroscience laboratory rather than an isolated room, in which Dr. Elena, a researcher with exhaustive physical knowledge of colour vision, learns to discriminate wavelengths through a brain-computer interface that feeds her visual cortex simulated chromatic signals, thereby gaining a practical ability; contrast this with a parallel training protocol for nociceptive experience, in which the same interface delivers predictive models but no direct affective valence, leaving her unable to acquire the corresponding recognitional capacity. Constraints: present the scenario in a single paragraph; neither case may invoke phenomenal concepts or private-language appeals; and the divergence in verdicts must fall out of the structural difference between map-construction and valence-calibration, not from stipulation. After the scenario, analyse why an ability-hypothesis defender can claim Elena gains no new fact in the colour domain but must concede one in the nociceptive domain; identify precisely which feature of the interface protocol does the explanatory work; and show how a proponent of the knowledge argument could resist by denying that simulated chromatic input genuinely satisfies the internal-map condition. Write your answer in British English.
