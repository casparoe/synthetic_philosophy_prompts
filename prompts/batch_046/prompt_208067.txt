Consider Immanuel Kant being presented, posthumously, with the decision-theoretic problem of diachronic rationality—specifically, the money-pump argument that an agent with cyclic preferences over time can be systematically exploited for guaranteed loss. A contemporary interlocutor proposes extending Kant's framework with what she calls the trans-temporal universalizability condition: a maxim is genuinely universalizable only if an agent can consistently will to act on it across an indefinite sequence of choice points without becoming vulnerable to such exploitation. Would Kant accept this as an implicit requirement of pure practical reason, or reject it as smuggling prudential, instrumental calculation into moral philosophy? In answering, please distinguish carefully between (a) the synchronic consistency demanded by the categorical imperative at a single moment of volition, (b) the diachronic coherence required to avoid being pumped, and (c) whether Kantian autonomy allows an agent to legislate binding constraints on her future selves in order to prevent that exploitation—even when such constraints are not derivable from universal law alone. Your response should reconstruct Kant's likely reasoning from his core commitments rather than appeal to secondary scholarship, and should address whether a purely synchronic ethics is genuinely deficient or merely incomplete in the face of sequential choice.
