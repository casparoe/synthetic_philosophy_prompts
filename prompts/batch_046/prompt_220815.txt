I am a doctoral student in normative ethics and intergenerational moral theory mediating an adversarial collaboration between two senior scholars in population axiology who have been debating the implications of the repugnant conclusion for total-utilitarian aggregation. One scholar, representing the revisionist aggregationist camp, holds that the conclusion reveals only that naive totalism requires structural repair—through critical-level adjustments, range-sensitive aggregation, or constraints on procreative reasons—without abandoning the core commitment that welfare can be summed across persons in different-number cases. The other, representing the person-affecting restrictionist camp, holds that the conclusion demonstrates a fundamental incoherence in any view that permits identity-independent reasons to create lives, and that no repair to the aggregation function can salvage totalism without violating the restriction that reasons must be tied to particular affected individuals.

Please draft the joint statement they should produce. The document should begin with a numbered section—no more than eight items—of agreed claims that are neither vacuous nor slanted. These should cover: (1) the formal structure of the repugnant conclusion, understood as the claim that a sufficiently large population with lives barely worth living can be ethically better than a smaller population with uniformly excellent lives; (2) the shared rejection of simple average-utilitarianism as inadequate for different-number comparisons; (3) the agreement that any adequate axiology must yield determinate verdicts in both same-number and different-number choice situations; (4) the concession that raw intuitions about "better for the world" in different-number cases are unstable, framing-sensitive, and not dispositive on their own; (5) the mutual acknowledgment that neither camp endorses an unconstrained "more lives always add value" principle; and (6) at least two further substantive points of convergence that you identify without collapsing the disagreement.

Following this, present a disagreements table containing exactly three residual disputes. Each dispute must be formulated in neutral language such that each camp can recognize its own position and the opposing position in the wording, without attributing bad faith or conceptual confusion to either side. The disputes should concern substantive normative commitments rather than terminological preferences.

Next, identify three cruxes—sub-questions such that resolution in either direction would lead at least one camp to revise a central commitment. For each crux, provide: (a) the precise question stated neutrally; (b) a concrete analytical case that both sides agree bears on the question—specifically, a closed-system colonization model in which a planner chooses between Plan Alpha (a stable population of 10^9 individuals with uniformly high welfare, defined by an ordinal ranking of life-types shared by both camps) and Plan Beta (a stable population of 10^12 individuals with welfare distributed just above a shared critical threshold defined by that same ordinal ranking), where the empirical parameters are fixed and only the normative evaluation is contested; (c) Camp A's explicit prediction about what the correct evaluation of this model should be and the reasoning behind it; and (d) Camp B's corresponding prediction and reasoning.

Finally, include a brief "tests and predictions" section that records, for each crux, a specific formal argument, hypothetical result, or conceptual analysis that both sides agree would be relevant, together with each camp's explicit prediction of what that argument or result would show. The document should be written in formal academic prose appropriate for a philosophy journal's discussion notes, should avoid all bibliographic references, citations, and reading lists, and should not invoke theoretical frameworks outside standard graduate-level competence in normative ethics. Do not include a verdict adjudicating between the camps; the deliverable is a constructive document both could sign, with the residual disagreement made exact and testable.
