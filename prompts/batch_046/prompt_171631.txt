Since I retired from the physics department I've been poking around in moral philosophy, and I'm trying to make sense of this "wrong kind of reasons" idea. Here are six cases with my gut verdicts — some feel clear, others not, and I'm not sure they all point the same way.

A. A woman gives money to famine relief because she genuinely thinks reducing suffering matters. To me that's the right kind of reason.

B. She gives the same amount because her accountant told her it maximizes her tax write-off. The act is the same, but the reason feels wrong-kind — she wouldn't do it if the write-off disappeared.

C. A grad student accepts a controversial result in quantum optics because she's actually checked the data and it holds up. Right kind for belief, I think.

D. She accepts the exact same result mainly to humiliate her advisor, who bet against it. Even though she's correct, that social-victory reason feels wrong-kind for belief.

E. Now a soldier pulls a child from a burning truck. He knows it's his duty, but he's also thinking hard about the medal he'll recieve. I want to say both reasons are present, and since the duty-reason is right-kind it's okay overall — but maybe the medal-reason contaminates it?

F. A guy files honest taxes not because cheating is wrong, but because he's terrified of prison. Is that a wrong-kind reason in the same moral sense, or just a non-moral reason? I lean toward calling it wrong-kind, but I'm unsure.

My hunch is there's one principel: a reason is right-kind when it's responsive to the very value that makes the action or belief correct — suffering-reduction for charity, truth for belief, moral duty for rescue. But then Case F is tricky, since prison-avoidance responds to self-preservation, not moral value. And Case E is tricky because two values are in play. So does the principle allow mixed motives if the right one is present? Or does it require purity?

Also, I keep wondering if evolutionary stories mess this up. If our moral motives evolved just because they helped tribes survive, is "tribe survival" the real reason and "duty" just a cover story? I don't want to go full biology here, but it makes me wonder if my verdicts are just post-hoc rationalizations.

Could you tell me whether one principle can capture A through F, or if I need separate ones for action and belief? If one principle works, state it precisely and say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, someone who gives anonymously with no tax benefit, no praise, and no warm feeling, purely out of abstract obligation. And be honest: which of my six verdicts does your principle have to overturn, if any?
