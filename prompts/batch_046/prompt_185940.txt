I'm preparing a paper for my AP Ethics seminar, and I've been trying to understand how moral norms that once seemed fixed—such as the acceptance of chattel slavery or the practice of dueling—actually disappear. In our readings we discussed Bernard Williams's claim that some practices are sustained by honor codes that collapse through internal exhaustion rather than through direct philosophical argument, and Kwame Anthony Appiah's idea that an "honor world" can shift when people realize that its norms conflict with values they already accept. I also encountered the suggestion that material interests often drive reform: for instance, that abolition advanced partly because slave economies became less profitable, or that dueling ended partly because states monopolized violence and aristocrats no longer needed private combat to settle disputes.

What I find confusing is a pattern that doesn't fit neatly into either story. Sometimes a practice seems to lose its association with high status—what I would call "prestige decay"—before anyone mounts a serious moral argument against it or before economic interests clearly demand its end. Dueling, for example, came to seem ridiculous and lower-class in some circles before it was formally outlawed or philosophically condemned. So I want to ask: in moral revolutions, is the primary driver rational persuasion, shifting interests, or this kind of honor-based prestige decay? Write an essay that weighs these three explanations, drawing on the Williams and Appiah frameworks where relevant, and reach a justified conclusion about which mechanism is dominant—or how they combine in a specific causal sequence. Do not end with an indecisive summary; defend a specific verdict. The essay should be written in American English, and please do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations.
