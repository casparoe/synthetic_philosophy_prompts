I am a high school senior in an advanced philosophy elective, and I need you to design a rigorous final-exam essay prompt—together with a model answer and a detailed marking rubric—for my instructor to evaluate. The domain is embedded agency, but I want to avoid the standard textbook illustrations of social or institutional embedding. Instead, please construct the question around a distinction I want tested: structural embedding, in which an environment merely constrains an agent’s available options, versus constitutive embedding, in which the environment becomes part of the agent’s reasoning architecture itself. Frame the problem through an original hypothetical case: a real-time climate-policy algorithm that adjusts carbon-tax rates using live satellite data, where human programmers set an initial optimization target but the system autonomously rewrites its internal weightings in response to feedback loops from global agricultural markets. The exam should ask students to determine whether the algorithm is structurally embedded, constitutively embedded, or both; whether either form of embedding eliminates moral responsibility for the original programmers; and whether a human regulator who approves the algorithm’s outputs without understanding its revised weightings counts as a second-order embedded agent. The model answer should argue that structural embedding distributes but does not dissolve responsibility, that constitutive embedding undermines direct agency attribution for specific outputs, that programmers retain responsibility for the initial target but not for unforeseeable weightings, and that the regulator is structurally embedded in the outputs but only constitutively embedded if they fully delegate their judgment. The marking scheme should be calibrated for a 75-minute exam requiring about 900 words and carrying 40 marks out of 100; it must award specific credit for distinguishing the two embedding types, for not conflating causal influence with constitutive dependence, for treating the regulator case separately, and for avoiding the generic conclusion that embedding eliminates all responsibility. Penalize answers that invoke the concept of the banality of evil without applying it specifically to algorithmic delegation, or that treat the algorithm as a full moral agent without justification. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—this assessment requires original analysis only.
