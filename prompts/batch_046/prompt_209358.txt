I am a novelist working on a character who is a computational neuroscientist, and I have become preoccupied with an analogy that circulates in popular and semi-technical writing about predictive processing and the free energy principle: the brain is like a scientist. It generates hypotheses about the causes of its sensory input, tests them against incoming data, revises its beliefs to minimise prediction error, and in doing so constructs a best-guess model of the world rather than receiving one directly. This is used to license several conclusions — that perception is inherently theory-laden, that there is no unmediated 'given' in experience, and that consciousness is best understood as an internally generated narrative continuously corrected by anomalous signals.

Before I put this analogy into my character's mouth, I want it audited with some rigour. Please structure your response in British English, with the following sections in this order and with approximate lengths:

1. The Correspondence Table (about 220 words): lay out explicitly what in predictive processing corresponds to the scientist, the prior hypothesis, the experimental design, the anomalous datum, the theory revision, and — if the analogy is pushed — the scientific community or paradigm.
2. Where the Mapping Fractures (about 260 words): examine where the brain-scientist comparison breaks down. Does active inference genuinely map onto a scientist designing an experiment, or is the 'action' in predictive processing structurally different? Is there a unified 'theory' being revised, or only a distributed hierarchy of local predictions? Address in particular whether precision weighting can be coherently mapped onto a scientist deciding which instruments to trust, or whether that extension collapses.
3. The Smuggled Baggage (about 200 words): identify what features of actual scientific practice the analogy quietly imports — intentionality, rational deliberation, explicit representation of alternatives, a distinction between observer and observed — and ask whether the predictive brain possesses them. Also ask what the analogy suppresses: for instance, the absence of a single subject who 'holds' the beliefs.
4. Do the Conclusions Survive? (about 220 words): take the conclusions usually drawn — that perception is necessarily indirect and narrative-like, that experience is a controlled hallucination, that there is no raw sensory given — and ask whether they still stand once the disanalogies are fully on the table, or whether they were being carried by the analogy itself.
5. A Better Analogy? (about 150 words): if the scientist analogy is irreparably strained, suggest a closer one drawn from craft or art — given my own domain — that captures generative modelling, prediction-error minimisation, and active inference without importing false intentional structure. If no better analogy exists, say so and explain why.

Please keep the analysis disciplined: do not force predictive processing to speak where it is silent, and distinguish disanalogies that genuinely undermine the intended philosophical conclusions from those that are merely decorative. I am particularly interested in whether the analogy can genuinely support the claim that consciousness is narrative-like without already assuming what it sets out to prove.
