I am a junior in an advanced high school philosophy seminar, and for our capstone unit on longtermism and metaphysical risk I have been asked to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between two positions I have been studying with my study group. The debate concerns whether the standard simulation argument dampens what we have been calling far-future fanaticism—the view that astronomical-scale future welfare should dominate present practical reasoning. I need the formal joint document that the two camps could both sign, drafted with enough precision that neither side can claim misrepresentation.

Camp One, which my study group labels the Termination-Dependence position, maintains that if we assign a non-negligible probability to being simulated, then extreme longtermist prioritization is rationally undermined unless we have independent evidence that we inhabit base reality; they grant that simulated suffering and flourishing are phenomenally real but deny that they carry the same moral weight or causal open-endedness as base-reality outcomes, particularly because long causal chains might be truncated at the simulation boundary. Camp Two, the Embedded-Commitment position, holds that substrate status does not diminish far-future urgency: they argue that simulated civilizations may eventually achieve base-reality status or spawn one, that moral status is substrate-independent, and that the simulation argument is too epistemically weak to shift practical reasoning; they also insist on a distinction between causal fanatism—valuing the future only for its unbounded causal reach—and intrinsic fanatism—valuing it regardless of substrate—claiming that only the former is even potentially undermined by simulation worries.

Please draft the joint statement these camps should produce. Structure it precisely: (1) a numbered section of shared commitments, including agreement on the basic simulation-argument structure, the reality of simulated experience, our present inability to verify substrate, the causal versus intrinsic fanatism distinction, and the relevance of what we have termed the boundary-transitivity problem—whether moral and causal properties transfer across a simulation-to-base-reality divide; (2) a disagreements table with neutral formulations each side accepts for its own view, covering whether substrate-dependence is necessary for extreme longtermism, whether simulation probability should update expected-value calculations about the distant future, and whether ecological or technological predictions are altered by boundary-transitivity concerns; (3) the cruxes—specific sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp—such as whether there is a non-arbitrary reason to privilege base-reality causation; and (4) for each crux, one concrete reasoning case or hypothetical scenario both sides agree is relevant, together with each camp's explicit prediction of what that case demonstrates. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; work only from the positions as I have defined them, and ensure the wording is formal enough for an academic collaboration but precise enough that neither camp could claim it slants against them.
