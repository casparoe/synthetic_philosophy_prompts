I have been discussing Leibniz’s claim that this is the best of all possible worlds with colleagues in a philosophy of institutions reading group, and I keep noticing an argumentative pattern for which I do not have a precise term. The pattern arises when a defender of an existing institution, technology, or policy framework treats that framework’s present defects as contingent imperfections—noise that could in principle be corrected without altering the system’s core logic—while treating any proposed alternative’s defects as structurally necessary and irremediable. They then invoke something like a localised Leibnizian calculus, suggesting that any transition introduces a novel evil or an unforeseen compossibility failure that, once entered into a global balance, renders the change unjustified; yet they apply this calculus selectively, never demanding that the status quo survive an equivalent comparison against an idealised version of itself, only that the alternative survive comparison against an idealised status quo.

I have observed this, for example, in debates over central-bank digital currencies, where current inflation-targeting failures are described as correctable by better implementation, whereas a proposed public digital-currency regime’s privacy risks are treated as inherent and inseparable from its design; and in discussions of peer review, where existing gatekeeping biases are framed as removable noise, whereas open post-publication review is assessed against its worst-case scenario of misinformation cascades. In each case the speaker is not engaging in the classic problem of evil—there is no claim that the present arrangement maximises overall good across all worlds—nor is it mere risk aversion, because the status quo’s own risks are systematically discounted. The reasoning feels adjacent to status quo bias and to omission bias, but neither captures the specific modal asymmetry: the status quo is evaluated under a generous, counterfactual-cleaning modal frame, while the alternative is evaluated under a pessimistic, essentialising frame, and both are then wrapped in the language of Leibnizian compossibility.

Is there an established concept—perhaps in political philosophy, decision theory, or institutional design—for this exact configuration? If there is no established term, could you name and define it, with clear boundary conditions that distinguish it from status quo bias, from a motte-and-bailey defence of institutional conservatism, and from a straightforward precautionary-principle argument? I am especially interested in whether the pattern requires an explicit appeal to global optima, or whether it can function with purely local transition-blocking, and where its edges lie when applied to non-institutional cases. Please provide your answer in American English.
