hey, i'm writing a short essay for my philosophy reading group and wanted some feedback before i present it. i spent 30 years studying colony-forming organisms (coral, slime molds, that kind of thing) and now i'm trying to apply that lens to the special composition question — you know, when do parts make up one thing vs just being near each other?

my thesis is basically that philosophers are stuck on static part-whole relations, but in nature composition is really a phase-transition in information-flow density. i call it the 'coupling regime' view: parts don't compose a whole because they touch or are bonded, but because they enter a feedback loop where local rules get overridden by global dynamics. so a coral reef isn't just coral polyps summed up; it's a regime where each polyp's behavior is constrained by the collective metabolic signal. if that signal breaks, you don't have one thing decomposing — you have the regime collapsing, and what's left is just seperate organisms.

i try to map this onto the debate: universalists say everything touches everything through physics, but under my view they miss that most physical contact lacks the right feedback density. restrictionists say only living things or conscious things compose, but that feels arbitrary if you look at slime molds that act like one brain without being one organism in the traditional sense. i think the residue is that special composition might not be a binary yes/no but a gradient of coupling strength, which means the question 'when do parts compose?' is malformed — it's like asking 'when does a swarm become a superorganism?' there's no sharp line, just different regimes.
