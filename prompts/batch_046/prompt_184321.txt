I'm working through my own commitments on the ethics of bringing beings into existence, trying to achieve reflective equilibrium rather than simply adopting a named position. My intuitive verdicts on concrete scenarios are these. A couple who knows they will have a child with a severe genetic disorder guaranteeing chronic suffering should not conceive; that strikes me as clearly wrong. A couple who decides not to conceive a child who would have lived an exceptionally flourishing life—deep relationships, significant creative achievement, and only ordinary disappointment—seems to do nothing wrong; the absence of that happiness is not a tragedy that calls for moral correction. If a society faces a choice between permitting reproduction that yields billions of largely happy lives but also guarantees widespread climate-related suffering, I lean toward endorsing a voluntary reproductive moratorium, though I cannot justify this cleanly without invoking something like a non-identity intuition that feels suspiciously ad hoc when I apply it elsewhere. Finally, a couple who conceives a child expected to have an ordinary life—mostly good, with inevitable setbacks—seems permitted but not required; there is no duty to create that child, yet no obvious wrong in doing so.

The pattern fractures, however, once I move away from biological reproduction to engineered or liminal forms of consciousness. Imagine we could design a synthetic being that experiences rich aesthetic and relational pleasure but is structurally incapable of suffering: no nociception, no grief architecture, no capacity for existential dread. Creating such a being feels permissible, perhaps even laudable, yet I struggle to reconcile this with my reluctance about ordinary procreation, since in both cases we are generating pleasure-capable beings who did not previously exist. Even more disorienting is a gradated potentiality case: suppose we could bring into existence a being whose phenomenology is permanently neutral—no suffering, but also no pleasure, only information processing without valence. Preventing that being's existence feels like neither a moral gain nor a loss, which seems to support an asymmetry between the badness of suffering and the non-badness of absent pleasure. But then I am unsure why the synthetic pleasure-capable being should be treated differently from a biological one, or why neutral existence should be morally weightless rather than a missed opportunity.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about risk distribution, the nature of implicit consent, the moral status of absent states, or the difference between biological and designed origins—that captures my first four verdicts while explaining the fracture in the engineered cases? Please state the principle (or a small set of competing candidates) precisely, identifying which clause is motivated by which scenario above. Apply it to at least two further situations I have not considered—perhaps one involving partial or fragmented consciousness, and one involving intergenerational obligations where the potential beneficiaries never actually come to be—and tell me honestly which of my original judgments it forces me to abandon. I am not asking for a bibliography, a list of authors, or citations; I want to know whether my intuitions hang together, or whether the tension between biological and synthetic cases reveals that one of them is the outlier that should be revised.
