Consider the events of January 6, 2021, at the United States Capitol—when a crowd entered the legislative building during the certification of presidential electoral votes, and when certain legislators had sought to delay or reject state-certified results—as a test case for Spinoza's political philosophy. Spinoza defines democracy not primarily as a constitutional procedure but as the form of sovereignty in which the collective power (potentia) of the multitude is least alienated from itself: citizens obey laws they have collectively authored, and the right of the state equals its actual power to preserve itself. Use this framework to analyze the competing claims of democratic legitimacy made that day. How should Spinoza distinguish between the multitude acting as a democratic subject and a faction usurping the name of the people? Apply his distinction between governance by reason—where freedom of judgment strengthens the state—and governance by passion and fear, to the role of false beliefs about electoral fraud in mobilizing the crowd. Does Spinoza's defense of freedom of expression require him to protect the speech that preceded the breach, or does his view that democratic stability depends on rational agreement allow him to treat that speech as a corruption of democratic judgment rather than its exercise? Finally, assess what the episode shows about Spinoza's theory: does it illustrate his claim that representative institutions tend to separate the multitude from its own power, thereby provoking direct action, or does it demonstrate that direct intervention by an unmediated multitude is incompatible with the preservation of democratic form? Be disciplined about the mapping—identify who counts as the player, what options each faced, and where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
