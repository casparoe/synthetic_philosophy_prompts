Consider the philosophical problem of material constitution, traditionally framed through the relation between a statue and the lump of clay that makes it up—entities that coincide spatially yet differ in persistence conditions and modal properties. Rather than asking for a survey of standard positions, I want a defended prioritization of open research directions, especially where the problem extends to substrates that are not continuously physical—such as functional or informational structures whose parts are distributed, replaceable, or quantized, and whose boundaries may be drawn pragmatically rather than physically.

Imagine a small research group in philosophy of science has about two years of funding. Among the following unresolved issues, which should take priority, in what order, and why?

(A) Whether constitution is inherently one-to-one or can be one-to-many when the substrate lacks physical cohesion—e.g., whether a single functional profile can be simultaneously constituted by different weight configurations, architectures, or token-histories with non-identical survival conditions.

(B) Whether resolving material constitution for physical artifacts is upstream of resolving identity-over-time for non-physical or distributed entities, or whether the two domains should be treated as conceptually independent.

(C) Whether the statue-and-clay intuition implicitly relies on essentialism about artifacts that collapses once we recognize that some entities have conventionally fixed rather than physically fixed boundaries.

(D) Whether there is a stable distinction between "constitution" and "realization" for informational substrates, or whether that distinction collapses and should be replaced by a different ontological framework.

For each, assess tractability for conceptual work rather than empirical modeling, indicate how many downstream questions depend on its answer, and specify what kind of argument, counterexample, or conceptual clarification over the next few years would most shift your ranking. Deliver a ranked agenda with explicit criteria—importance, tractability, dependency, and neglect—not a literature review. Do not provide a bibliography, cite specific authors or papers, or list recommended readings.
