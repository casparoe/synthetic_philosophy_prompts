Analyze the 1960 British obscenity trial of Penguin Books over D. H. Lawrence's Lady Chatterley's Lover—R v Penguin Books Ltd—through the lens of Amartya Sen's liberal paradox. In Sen's formal model, minimal liberty assigns each of two agents a right to decide whether a particular book is read by themselves or by the other, and the Pareto principle then conflicts with that liberty when preferences cycle in a particular way. Map the trial onto this framework: who corresponds to Sen's two agents, what are the options and preferences, and does the jury's acquittal and the subsequent social normalization of the novel constitute a Pareto improvement that violates some party's minimal liberty? Then assess where the paradox explains the dynamics of the case—perhaps the tension between individual readers' autonomy and collective norms about obscenity—and where it misdescribes or is silent, for instance regarding institutional roles or the temporal shift in social preferences after 1960. Finally, consider whether injecting a distinction between first-order preferences and meta-preferences about social norms dissolves the paradox in this case, or whether the trial instead confirms Sen's claim that liberal rights and unanimous improvement are structurally incompatible.
