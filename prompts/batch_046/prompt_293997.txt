I'm working through a seminar argument on behavioral criteria for mind and now find it suspicious because the conclusion seems to dissolve the very boundary it was meant to police. Here is the chain as I have it.

(1) If a system S displays every observable behavioral disposition that we take, in actual interaction, as evidence for consciousness in humans, then we have no non-question-begging rational basis for withholding consciousness from S. (2) So a perfect behavioral simulation—call it Sim—must be treated, on pain of epistemic arbitrariness, as having the same mental status as the human it mimics. (3) Now decompose Sim into functional modules M₁...Mₙ, each reproducing a local input-output profile; replace each Mᵢ with a committee Cᵢ of individually non-conscious agents who, via explicit lookup rules, reproduce Mᵢ's behavior only when arranged in that specific network topology and only in message-passing interaction with neighboring committees. (4) The union of all Cᵢ reproduces Sim's global behavioral profile, since every local transaction is preserved. (5) By (1) applied to the collective, we have no rational basis for withholding consciousness from the union of committees. (6) Yet that union is causally fragmented: no agent in any Cᵢ has access to global state, and the only integration is symbolic message-passing that mimics synaptic weights. (7) Therefore behavioral criteria commit us to attributing consciousness to a distributed, rule-following collective with no unified subject of experience. (8) Apply the same decomposition to an actual human brain B, replacing each neuron with an analogous committee; the resulting B* reproduces B's behavior. (9) So either we deny consciousness to B* and, by parity of reasoning, to B, or we accept that consciousness is multiply instantiated across disconnected groups.

The argument feels like it proves too much: it turns the Turing-style criterion into a reductio that makes consciousness either ubiquitous or impossible to attribute consistently. I am inclined to keep (2) and (6)—that perfect simulation is conceivable in principle and that the committees are genuinely fragmented—so if I must pay a price I would rather surrender (1) or (5) than those. But I cannot name the precise slip. Is the error an equivocation between global behavioral equivalence and the local reproductions that constitute it? A modal slide from "we cannot discriminate" to "there is no difference in property"? Or is the argument actually valid, and the real cost is accepting that behavioral criteria cannot individuate a unified mind? I want to know which step to reject, why that step looked defensible, and what exactly I lose by cutting it out.
