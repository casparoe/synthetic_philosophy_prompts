I'm a journalist who writes about science, philosophy, and the history of ideas, and I've been invited to deliver the commencement address at a university where the graduating class includes both philosophy majors and students in the natural sciences, and where the audience will be split evenly between scholars familiar with Benjamin Franklin's moral perfection project and family members who have never encountered his virtue charts. I need a speech of about ten minutes that treats Franklin's view not as decorative wisdom but as the structural backbone of what I can and cannot say: specifically, I want to build the address around his conviction that perfect moral perfection is unattainable for human beings, that the value lies in the disciplined, sequential attempt—he ordered his virtues deliberately, believing that temperance had to come first because it enabled the clarity needed for the others—and that moral failure should be understood through the lens of his printing trade, as correctable errata in successive editions of oneself rather than as irremediable sin. The speech must comfort a room that expects hope without lying to them about the inevitability of failure; it must not present Franklin's thirteen virtues as a checklist for flawless living; and it must land for the philosophers without losing the family members. Please write it as continuous prose only—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just flowing paragraphs like a letter—without quoting Franklin verbatim (paraphrase any reference to his words without quotation marks), and without asking for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies. Inject something genuinely unexpected into the framing: treat his printer's concept of errata not as a biographical anecdote but as a philosophical model for moral life, showing how he saw each mistake as a misprint to be corrected in the next edition rather than a permanent stain. Make sure the address can be understood fully by someone with no web access and no prior knowledge of Franklin beyond common cultural awareness.
