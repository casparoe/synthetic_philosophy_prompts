I'm preparing the interview guide for an upcoming episode of my philosophy podcast, in which I will interview a philosopher who has recently defended a position she calls the "discontinuity veto with temporal decay" model of advance directives. According to this view, the authority of an earlier self is not a forward-reaching command to mandate specific treatments, but rather a limited veto power: it can block future interventions only when those interventions would sustain a psychological state so radically discontinuous with the earlier self's core values that the resulting individual constitutes a different moral subject. The distinctive twist is that this veto authority decays over time in direct proportion to how long the patient has actually inhabited the disputed state, on the grounds that sustained embodied experience generates a competing, legitimate claim to personal identity that eventually supersedes the original directive. The framework is intended to steer between absolutist autonomy readings, which treat directives as unbreakable intertemporal contracts, and purely presentist accounts, which dismiss the earlier self entirely after severe cognitive change.

I need a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual substance of the view rather than asking for biography or general overviews. Please include at least one question on the boundary between a legitimate veto and a disguised command—specifically, how the model handles refusal of life-sustaining care when that refusal is the only available clinical pathway—followed by a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that the causal structure of the intervention, not its outcome, determines the category; at least one question on how temporal decay is calibrated without smuggling in normative judgments about whether the sustained state is worth living, with a follow-up anticipating her appeal to first-person reports of temporal integration; at least one question on value-inversion cases, where the later self actively embraces the very condition the earlier self feared and vetoed, and whether decay should accelerate, halt, or reverse; and a final question asking what kind of conceptual or empirical finding could genuinely count against the model, rather than merely provide an illustration. The questions should be accessible to an intellectually engaged general audience but technically precise enough for a philosophy podcast, and they should avoid generic prompts, requests for citations, or appeals to obscure secondary literature.
