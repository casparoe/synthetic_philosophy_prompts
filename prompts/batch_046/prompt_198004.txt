Following the recent death of a family member after prolonged illness, I have found myself returning to philosophical questions about whether the past retains any genuine ontological status, or whether presentism's claim that only present entities exist offers an existential clarity that eternalism denies. I am drafting an essay that examines how thought experiments in the philosophy of time persuade through what I propose calling modal framing: they ask us to imagine standing outside temporal flow, or watching a luminous point advance along a static line, and then treat the vividness of that imaginative posture as evidence for a metaphysical thesis.

My notes run as follows. First, presentist arguments often recruit an intuition pump about the moving-now—inviting us to picture time as a line with a spotlight sliding across it. I want to argue that this pump succeeds not because it reveals time's structure, but because it exploits our embodied cognitive architecture for mental time travel, which treats the present as an indexical anchor for planning and memory. The eternalist response—drawing on frame-relative simultaneity in special relativity to deny any privileged present slice—lacks comparable phenomenological vividness; it functions as a structural isomorphism rather than an image. I want to call this second form a structural pump, and to claim that philosophers systematically overvalue phenomenological pumps because they feel like direct insight, when they may only register evolved attentional biases.

Second, I want to distinguish modal framing from straightforward logical error. The moving-spotlight image does not contain a formal contradiction; rather, it quietly assumes that for time to be real, all temporal perspectives must be simultaneously representable within a single frame. I am unsure whether eternalists must reject this framing condition, or whether they can accept it and deny only that the frame requires a privileged slice.

Third, I am considering whether this framework extends to ethical intuition pumps—perhaps the trolley problem activates spatial-proximity biases in moral cognition rather than isolating consequentialist reasoning—but I worry that extending the analysis beyond temporal ontology will diffuse the essay's focus.

Please comment on these notes. I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between phenomenological and structural pumps is coherent and not merely ad hoc; whether my characterization of the eternalist argument from relativity risks misrepresenting its premises; and whether the essay should remain confined to temporal ontology or include the ethical comparison. I do not need bibliographic recommendations; I want conceptual and argumentative feedback.
