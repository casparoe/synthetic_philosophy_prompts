I'm an undergraduate taking a course in comparative religious ethics, and I've noticed a recurring evaluative asymmetry in how Buddhist moral frameworks are discussed alongside consequentialism that I can't quite place within the standard taxonomy of cross-cultural ethical debate. I'd like to know whether it has an established name, and if not, how precisely to carve it out.

The pattern runs as follows. When a Buddhist practice or precept yields outcomes that align neatly with aggregate welfare optimization—widespread vegetarianism in certain Mahāyāna traditions, the social effects of dāna, or monastic healthcare—scholars and students often treat this convergence as evidence that Buddhist ethics can be reconstructed within, or at least maps cleanly onto, a consequentialist framework. But when a practice seems welfare-neutral or even suboptimal by consequentialist lights—strict Vinaya rules about alms-seeking schedules that don't clearly maximize utility, precepts governing celibacy and withdrawal that reduce direct engagement with worldly suffering, or rules prioritizing mental purity over immediate intervention—the justification shifts abruptly to internalist criteria. The practice is then defended not by its outcomes but by its role in cultivating cetanā (wholesome intention), purifying the agent's mind, or preserving the Sangha's disciplinary integrity. What strikes me is that consequentialist metrics function as the default language of success, while Buddhist internalist reasoning is invoked selectively as a defensive exemption rather than being granted equal standing as an independent evaluative framework.

This doesn't seem to be ordinary incommensurability, since both sides in the debate typically acknowledge that mental states and welfare outcomes both matter; nor is it a simple category error, because the asymmetry appears even among interlocutors who accept some pluralism about values. It also differs from standard "imperialism" in moral evaluation, because the problem isn't that one framework is applied blindly to the other, but that the same comparative exercise applies one framework rigorously and the other only opportunistically.

Does this specific configuration—consequentialist metrics claimed for Buddhist successes, internalist exemptions claimed for Buddhist "failures"—have an established label in philosophy of religion, comparative ethics, or the literature on moral incommensurability? If there is no precise term, could you define the phenomenon formally, distinguish it from incommensurability, pluralism, and ordinary double standards in argumentation, and identify its boundary conditions? In your response, please separate what is broadly settled—such as the centrality of intention and mental cultivation in classical Buddhist ethics—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, particularly regarding whether consequentialist reconstruction of Buddhist morality is faithful or distorting, and do not present any contested interpretive claim about Buddhist doctrine or consequentialist theory as if it were the consensus view.
