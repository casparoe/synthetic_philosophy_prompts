Since my appointment three months ago to the procedural review board of an intergovernmental commission on historical accountability, I have been required to evaluate repeated proposals assigning collective responsibility for major conflicts. The mandate has acquired unexpected personal weight: family archives I recently examined place my own great-grandfather within the Austro-Hungarian foreign ministry's alliance-communication apparatus in July 1914, meaning the protocols I design may eventually classify the institutional lineage I inherit as bearing mediated responsibility. The board needs a reusable decision protocol—executable within ninety minutes by a panel of historians and legal advisors without philosophical training—that applies a specific conceptual framework to any proposal attributing war responsibility to a nation-state.

The framework I must operationalize rejects both undifferentiated collective guilt and purely individualist reduction. It holds that mediated responsibility attaches to a nation-state only when three conditions are jointly satisfied: (i) institutional agency—the state's governing apparatus made or sustained discrete decisions that causally contributed to escalation or prolongation; (ii) epistemic culpability—those decision-makers possessed, by the informational norms of their own environment, access to evidence that escalation was probable and that viable alternatives existed; and (iii) continuity of obligation—an unbroken constitutional or institutional lineage links the decision-making regime to the present polity, such that present citizens are not arbitrarily disconnected from the consequences. Crucially, the framework incorporates an attenuation principle drawn from theories of collective action: where decision-making was subject to structural opacity—systematic distortion of information by alliance structures, bureaucratic compartmentalization, or strategic misrepresentation—culpability is distributed across the structural system rather than concentrated in the state as a unitary moral agent. The framework further distinguishes trigger responsibility (the specific decision initiating hostilities) from systemic responsibility (the structural conditions rendering escalation rational for decision-makers), and insists that retrospective repudiation by present citizens does not by itself dissolve continuity of obligation.

Please construct a stepwise decision procedure my panel can apply consistently to any proposal. For each step, specify: the precise question to be answered in order; what evidentiary or argumentative standard counts as a sufficient answer; whether the answer is factual, interpretive, or a value judgment that must be flagged for the legal advisor; and the exact stopping condition—where the procedure yields a negative determination, where it yields a positive determination of mediated responsibility, and where it must suspend judgment pending further deliberation. The procedure should be honest about misfire: identify the cases where reasonable panels applying identical steps will diverge, and mark the points where simplification for non-philosophers necessarily sacrifices a distinction the theory requires.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:
1. The claim that the German Empire bears primary mediated responsibility for the escalation of July 1914, assessing both its trigger and its systemic dimensions.
2. The claim that the Austro-Hungarian Empire bears systemic but not trigger responsibility, specifically because structural opacity in its alliance communications attenuates unitary culpability.
3. A counter-intuitive case in which the procedure assigns mediated responsibility to a democratic polity whose present citizens have formally repudiated the original decision, but whose institutional continuity remains intact—explicitly noting where this result conflicts with ordinary moral intuition and whether the framework requires accepting it nonetheless.

Finally, delineate precisely what this procedure cannot determine: for example, whether it can ground reparative obligations, guide memorial policy, or support individual criminal attribution; and state whether its outputs should be treated as binding findings or as structured inputs to a political judgment that the procedure itself cannot replace.
