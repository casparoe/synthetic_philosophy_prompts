In recent work on Dewey's democratic theory, an interpretive deadlock has persisted between two camps that share almost every textual commitment yet remain unmoved. One camp—call it the social-intelligence reading—holds that Dewey treats democratic institutions primarily as distributed mechanisms for experimental problem-solving: legitimacy flows from a polity's capacity to correct error through broad participation in observation and hypothesis-testing, and democratic norms are ultimately justified by their contribution to reliable social knowledge. The other camp—the associative-ethics reading—insists that Dewey's core claim is ethical and constitutive: democracy is the form of associated living in which individuals achieve full growth through reciprocal interaction, and treating democratic procedures as merely instrumental to epistemic success collapses politics into a technocratic management of outcomes. Both sides cite the same passages concerning the public and inquiry, both reject the reduction of democracy to majority voting, and both acknowledge that Dewey links knowledge and ethics; nevertheless, the disagreement has not shifted in years. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not really about Dewey exegesis but about an unstated clash over whether political justification must be monistic—whether epistemic value can ground normative authority without remainder—or whether inquiry itself must be redefined to carry ethical content. But I am unsure whether this is a verbal dispute about the scope of experimentalism, a substantive disagreement about what counts as a legitimate reason for coercive collective decision, or a bedrock divergence over whether power asymmetries can ever be bracketed inside an inquiry framework. I want a diagnosis, not an adjudication of Dewey's true view: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Do the sides differ about hidden empirical claims about how institutions function, about standards of evidence for normative theory, about which cases count as democratic data, or about starting intuitions—perhaps concerning epistemic sovereignty versus developmental reciprocity—that neither can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move the debate—an analytical distinction, a historical case, a formal result, or a reframing of the question—or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed?
