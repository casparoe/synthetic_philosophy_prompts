In the rationalist-adjacent literature on decision theory, there is a long-running stall around evidential cooperation in large worlds. The evidentialist camp holds that agents running identical decision algorithms should coordinate across causally disconnected universes, simulations, or modal copies because shared reasoning creates correlation that makes cooperation rational; the causal camp holds that without a causal pathway between worlds, defection dominates regardless of correlation, and treating logical copies as strategic partners confuses epistemic dependence with causal efficacy. Both sides accept the same formal apparatus, agree on the structure of Newcomb's problem, and acknowledge the absence of direct causal links across worlds; yet neither has moved in years. My working theory is that the stall is not a substantive disagreement about decision rules but a hidden divergence over how to individuate worlds for indexical reasoning—specifically, whether worlds are separated by causal isolation, by logical independence of physical parameters, or by shared versus distinct computational substrates—with each side implicitly answering a different correlation question under a single heading. Is that right, or is there a genuine formal dispute about applying conditionalization across worlds? And given your diagnosis, what—a precise distinction, a formal result, a reframing of indexical uncertainty, or a reconceptualization of the question—could actually resolve it, or is the impasse permanent because the underlying question is ill-posed?
