I'd like a precise, intuitive explanation of why the alignment problem is so often collapsed into a single formulation—"make the system want what we want"—and whether that framing hides conceptually distinct difficulties that actually require different kinds of solutions. In particular, please distinguish three dimensions: first, the specification problem, which concerns translating human normative commitments into an optimizable criterion without hidden distortion or underspecification; second, the dynamic stability problem, which asks whether those commitments survive recursive self-modification, rapid capability gain, and distributional shift in the environment; and third, what might be called the normative frame problem—directly analogous to the classic frame problem in AI, but applied to values—about how a system determines which of its values should be treated as fixed background constraints and which should be revised in light of new evidence or changed circumstances, absent any pre-given complete hierarchical ordering. Are these genuinely separable problems, or does solving specification automatically resolve the other two? Focus especially on whether the normative frame problem collapses into specification once we introduce a meta-level update rule, or whether it remains an independent challenge with its own characteristic failure modes—for instance, a system that correctly encodes its goals but systematically misclassifies which ones are contextually appropriate to apply. I am less interested in a literature survey than in a clear account of where the conceptual joints actually lie, and why treating alignment as one problem might lead us to believe we have solved it when we have only addressed a single layer.
