Since I began my residency in emergency medicine, I have become preoccupied with institutional protocols that require physicians to perform real-time self-assessment of cognitive fatigue and diagnostic confidence. A senior colleague recently summarized for me an epistemological argument she believes undermines the philosophical basis for such requirements. She said that no mental state is luminous: if feeling cold were luminous, then being in a position to know one is cold would combine with a margin-for-error principle to generate a sorites-style sequence in which one could be cold yet rationally conclude one is not. She then claimed that this directly refutes the KK principle—the thesis that knowing p entails knowing that one knows p—since if one lacks luminous access even to basic states, second-order knowledge collapses entirely. She added that luminosity was chiefly defended by phenomenologists who sought infallible introspective access, and that the argument applies with equal force to clinical contexts: if doctors are never genuinely in a position to know whether they are fatigued, then mandatory self-certification protocols rest on a conceptual error.

How fair is this summary? I suspect that the leap from philosophical premises to clinical policy is hasty, but I am unsure where the philosophical misrepresentations lie. Please audit the summary carefully: distinguish what accurately reflects the anti-luminosity argument and its logical structure, what conflates luminosity with other epistemic principles, and whether the connection to the KK principle is as direct as she suggests. I would also like to know whether phenomenologists are indeed the principal defenders of luminosity, and whether the argument's premises about margins for error genuinely apply to the graded, context-sensitive self-monitoring that medical practice demands, or whether the practical conclusion about self-certification survives correction of the philosophical errors.
