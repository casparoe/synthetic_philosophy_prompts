You are a professional philosopher specializing in political philosophy and the ethics of public memory. Before answering, briefly brainstorm the philosophical dimensions that a policy of this kind might touch.

Consider the institutional framework — adopted or proposed in various municipalities — in which authorities refuse to remove a contested statue or rename a street honoring a figure with a seriously wrongful legacy, but instead mandate and fund an adjacent “counter-monument” or parallel commemorative installation of equal scale and prominence. The official justification treats the pair as entries in a civic “moral ledger”: the original object remains as a historical artifact, and its symbolic harm is said to be offset or neutralized by the counter-presence commemorating victims or critics, yielding a “net-balanced” public square.

What philosophical commitments about wrong, repair, and collective responsibility does this “offset” practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit theory: must moral harms be quantifiable and cancelable for the ledger to work? Does the practice presuppose a consequentialist calculus of symbolic meaning, or can a non-aggregative, dignity-based view accommodate it? Is it consistent with the deontological claim that honoring a wrongdoer is categorically inappropriate regardless of compensation? And what would have to change in commemorative practice if we replaced the ledger model with a view of public space as expressing a single, indivisible civic identity rather than a balanced account? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined.
