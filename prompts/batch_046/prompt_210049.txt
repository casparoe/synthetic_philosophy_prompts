I chair the clinical ethics consultation service at a tertiary-care hospital, and we must issue a formal recommendation by Friday regarding Patient 4421, a 28-year-old with advanced amyotrophic lateral sclerosis who remains fully conscious and communicates via eye-tracking. He is sustained by a portable diaphragm pacemaker that, through the same percutaneous lead, also delivers a basal infusion of an ultra-short-acting opioid analog. The patient has requested discontinuation of life-sustaining treatment. Because of the device architecture, electrical deactivation alone stops pacing but leaves the opioid infusion running at a sub-analgesic rate that suppresses respiratory drive without providing comfort; the expected result is conscious suffocation over roughly eighteen hours. Full surgical explant is the only manufacturer-approved way to halt both functions simultaneously, but the patient refuses surgery and instead asks the team to (a) deactivate the pacemaker and (b) administer a single sedative bolus to which he can consent via eye-code, so that he dies unconsciously from respiratory failure. His parents contend that (a) is permissible letting-die (omission) while (b) is impermissible killing (act), and they demand either the explant procedure or continued full support; they reject the deactivation-plus-sedation pathway as disguised euthanasia. Our institution has no policy addressing integrated neuro-respiratory assist devices. Please draft the service’s opinion memo (maximum 350 words): recommend one concrete course of action with an explicit timeline, engage the parents’ act/omission distinction on its own terms rather than dismissing it, address whether administering the sedative bolus alters the moral category of the death, and give an honest account of the strongest consideration favoring the position you do not adopt.
