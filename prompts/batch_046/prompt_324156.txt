After retiring from experimental physics, I have been working through philosophy of mind and keep returning to a framing I find useful but have not seen systematized: the relation between symbolic structures—call them simmballs, discrete representational tokens embedded in a system—and the physical substrate—call it the careenium—that realizes them. The core question is whether simmballs ever push the careenium around, or whether all causal work is done at the substrate level with symbols serving as mere descriptive gloss.

Please construct a taxonomy, in the form of a branching decision tree, that sorts positions on this question. Do not organize it by author or school; organize it by logical commitments, with each internal node phrased as a concrete claim a thinker could accept or reject without knowing the label of the view that follows.

The tree should begin with two root-level splits:
- Are simmballs ontologically distinct from patterns of careenial activity, or are they reducible to (or identical with) such patterns?
- Is the careenium causally closed at its own physical level?

From there, build out further splits that separate downward-causal interactionism, substrate-level epiphenomenalism, emergent dual-aspect interaction, and eliminative substrate monism. Each leaf should name the position (invent a concise label if the view is unnamed in the literature) and state in one sentence its most awkward commitment—particularly any tension with physical closure or with the intuition that representations guide behavior.

Crucially, include a branch that asks whether symbolic content is individuated by internal syntax alone or requires external causal-historical relations to the substrate; this should split interactionist views into internalist-push and externalist-push variants.

Then, map out the full logical space: for every combination of answers that your tree permits, indicate whether it is occupied. For any empty combination, diagnose briefly why it is unoccupied—whether incoherent (for example, affirming downward causation while denying any ontological distinction), unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate, explaining which shared commitment blurs the boundary.

Please conclude with a brief epistemic note: what specific argument or empirical finding about causal closure in complex physical systems would cause you to revise the placement of one of these positions? Identify the point in your reasoning that is most likely to fail, especially where you have had to make an unstated assumption about how representational content relates to physical dynamics.
