I've been preparing arguments for a graduate seminar on conceptual change in science, and a discussion with colleagues has left me with what I think is a subtle confusion about the limits of Kuhnian incommensurability. I understand Kuhn's claim that rival paradigms operate with different lexical taxonomies—what he later characterized as different lexicons—such that terms like planet or force do not retain stable meanings across revolutions. The standard examples involve wholesale abandonment: celestial spheres disappear, phlogiston is eliminated. But I want to press on cases of what I call asymmetric lexical inheritance, where the vocabulary of a successor paradigm P2 seems to contain every term from its predecessor P1, yet assigns each a systematically altered theoretical role. Newtonian mass becomes a limiting case within relativistic dynamics; classical genetic gene is retained in molecular biology but embedded in a different explanatory architecture. If P2's lexicon strictly contains P1's, there appears to be a shared structural backbone that ought to permit direct translation and rational comparison—seemingly undermining the claim that the paradigms are genuinely incommensurable. Yet Kuhn maintains that even retained vocabulary is semantically transformed, so that overlap is merely homophonic rather than conceptually continuous. So my question is this: does asymmetric lexical inheritance constitute a genuine counterexample to incommensurability, or am I conflating lexical containment with conceptual commensurability? More specifically, is Kuhn committed to denying that true scientific revolutions ever exhibit this kind of inheritance, or does he allow inheritance while insisting that it never provides the neutral ground for comparison that rationalists demand?
