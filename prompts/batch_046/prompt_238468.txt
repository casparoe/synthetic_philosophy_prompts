I'm advising a three-year interdisciplinary program—philosophy and quantitative history—on whether there is an objective fact of the matter about global violence trends, and we can fund about four conceptual projects. The familiar disputes (per capita versus absolute counts, direct versus structural violence, whether psychological harm counts) are already well covered. I want to prioritize four less-examined problems that actually determine whether any dataset can settle the moral-progress claim.

The four issues are: (1) whether "violence reduction" can be metric-independent, or whether selecting a metric (deaths, injuries, DALYs, fear-based surveys) is itself a normative choice that collapses the descriptive thesis; (2) how to account for "prevented violence"—counterfactual harm avoided through deterrence, institutional design, or predictive intervention—since these cases never appear in mortality or crime records but may be essential to a true moral accounting of trends; (3) whether age-adjusted and disability-adjusted adjustments change the directional trend relative to raw death counts, and whether such adjustments smuggle in hidden normative commitments about the value of life-years versus lives saved; and (4) whether the boundary of violence should include slow, distributed ecological harm lacking intentional agency, given arguments that structural violence is continuous with direct violence.

Rank these four for funding priority. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria: upstream dependency (does resolving one make others trivial or moot?), tractability for philosophers rather than statisticians, and relevance to institutions that publish "moral progress" reports. Identify which issue, if resolved negatively, would most undermine the entire enterprise of claiming violence is objectively declining. For each, specify what kind of result—conceptual clarification, formal framework, or normative argument—would actually advance the field rather than restate positions. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Write in American English, in no more than 700 words, and lead with your bottom-line recommendation: the single issue that should receive the largest share of resources and why.
