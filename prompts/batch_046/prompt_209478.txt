In my applied-ethics seminar, discussion of Singer’s drowning-child analogy has stalled for months. One camp takes the argument to establish a continuous, agent-neutral duty: every unnecessary expenditure is morally equivalent to letting another child drown, so rescue obligations scale linearly with disposable income until basic needs are met everywhere. The other camp concedes that failing to save the nearby child is monstrous, but insists that moral agency requires a “project threshold”—once you have contributed a fair share to effective global-health interventions, further demands violate the conditions for having a life plan, not because distance matters, but because obligations must exhibit saturation rather than linear accumulation. Both sides agree on the empirical effectiveness of direct cash-transfer and health interventions, both reject pure egoism, and both treat physical proximity as morally arbitrary; yet neither has moved. Is this impasse substantive—perhaps about whether moral reasons are scalar or binary, or whether ownership generates a non-derivative limit—or is it verbal, with “duty” meaning a binding threshold for one side and a continuous pressure for the other? Or are they answering different questions, one about culpability and one about supererogation, masked by the single framing of “what Singer demands”? Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually commits to, and say what—if anything—would count as progress: a formal model of obligation saturation, an empirical study of donor fatigue as a normative rather than psychological boundary, a distinction that dissolves the shared premise, or an argument that no further resolution should be expected.
