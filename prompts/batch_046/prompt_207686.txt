I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of psychology preparing a workshop paper on doxastic partitioning, and I've built an argument that starts from a constraint on self-deception I find attractive and ends up eliminating implicit bias, repressed memory, and most of the unconscious mental economy that clinical and social psychology presuppose. I want to preserve the strong claim that self-deception is genuinely impossible—I'm drawn to a transparency condition on belief—but I also do not want to be forced into denying that people can hold genuinely unconscious attitudes or avoid forming beliefs they have evidence for.

Here is the chain:

(1) For self-deception to occur, an agent must simultaneously hold p with one doxastic system while another subsystem treats p as false, or at least actively suppresses it.
(2) Belief is transparent in the following minimal sense: if an agent has an occurrent belief that p, and that belief is active in reasoning or action-guidance, then the agent has first-person access to the content p.
(3) If the agent has first-person access to p, then no internal subsystem can treat p as false without the agent recognizing the contradiction; partitioned belief is therefore unstable and collapses upon interaction between subsystems.
(4) So self-deception is impossible: one cannot hide a belief from oneself in a way that permits sustained, contradictory doxastic structures within a single rational agent.
(5) Now consider motivated ignorance: an agent possesses sufficient evidence for p but systematically fails to form the belief that p.
(6) If belief formation is rationally responsive to available evidence, and the evidence for p is present in the agent's cognitive architecture, then the absence of belief that p can be explained only by the absence of cognitive access to that evidence.
(7) Therefore, any case in which an agent has evidence for p but lacks belief in p must be a case in which the agent genuinely lacks access—not a case of hidden, repressed, or partitioned belief.
(8) This applies generally to implicit attitudes, subliminally acquired associations, and clinical phenomena of repression: either the agent has access and believes, or lacks access entirely; there is no stable intermediate state of unconscious belief.
(9) But cognitive science and therapeutic practice routinely depend on precisely such intermediate states.
(10) Hence, accepting the impossibility of self-deception forces the rejection of the entire framework of unconscious mental representation.
(11) That conclusion is unacceptable.

The argument appears valid, but I suspect a scope shift or an equivocation is hiding somewhere between (4) and (8), because I want to keep both (4) and the reality of unconscious cognition. Which premise should I reject? If I weaken (2) by restricting transparency to occurrent beliefs and allowing dispositional ones to remain opaque, do I preserve first-person authority for the cases that matter? If I instead reject (6) by allowing non-rational or subpersonal belief-formation mechanisms, do I lose the normative grip that makes self-deception a rational failure rather than a mechanical glitch? And what do the standard positions—full transparency, partitionism, and the hierarchical predictive processing view—each have to pay to reject the step they target? I would especially like to know whether there is a principled distinction between the kind of access required for self-deception to fail and the kind that unconscious beliefs can lack without collapsing into mere absence of evidence.
