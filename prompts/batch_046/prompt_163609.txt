For the past semester I have been co-leading a graduate seminar on the methodology of philosophical intuition, and we have reached a stall that feels less like an empirical dispute and more like a category error running in a loop. The concrete disagreement concerns cross-cultural variation in responses to standard epistemological thought experiments—specifically the documented divergence between Western and East Asian participants on Gettier-style knowledge attributions and on high-stakes knowledge-denial scenarios in the experimental-philosophy literature. One faction argues that this demographic variability undermines the evidential status of intuition altogether: if the “data” of conceptual analysis shifts with cultural background, then intuition cannot serve as a neutral arbiter in epistemology. The opposing faction accepts the statistical divergence but treats it as noise to be filtered—arguing that philosophical intuition ought to be modeled not as a universal cognitive default but as an expert-trained judgment, analogous to the calibrated responses of trained physicists, and that the apparent split dissolves once you control for pragmatic framing, linguistic implicature, and task-interpretation effects. Both sides agree on the raw effect sizes, both cite the same bodies of work, and neither has moved in months.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal—centered on whether “intuition” denotes a pre-theoretical cognitive reflex or a refined disciplinary competence—but also that a deeper structural issue is hiding in plain sight: the two sides appear to apply different normative architectures to philosophical evidence. One side demands demographic invariance as a necessary condition for evidential weight, while the other treats local coherence within a research tradition as sufficient, effectively applying a Lakatos-style protective-belt strategy to intuition-based methodology itself. Yet I am unsure whether this captures the whole impasse, or whether there is an unresolved empirical question about whether extended philosophical training actually eliminates the divergence, or whether the sides are simply answering different questions—one descriptive (how do people actually judge?) and one normative (how ought a trained philosopher judge?). I want a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says rather than surveying interpretations: is this a substantive disagreement about standards of evidence, a hidden empirical dispute about the trainability of judgment, a case of two groups using “intuition” for different phenomena, or a sign that the framing question is ill-posed? Given your diagnosis, what would count as real progress—an experimental design that isolates conceptual content from framing effects, a conceptual distinction separating first-order judgment from meta-intuitive calibration, a formal result about conditions for evidential neutrality, or an honest argument that the stalemate is permanent because the underlying commitments are bedrock?
