I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of narrative and comparative literature, writing to design a 90-minute graduate workshop for a reading group of ten participants. The session addresses Jorge Luis Borges's denial of temporal succession—especially his argument in the essay translated as "A New Refutation of Time" that time is an illusion generated by consciousness, and his exploration in "The Garden of Forking Paths" of realities in which all outcomes exist simultaneously rather than unfolding in sequence. I want a structured in-class simulation, not a lecture, that makes the group feel the conceptual pressure of this position, and I want them to come away seeing that the coherence of a life-story depends on temporal ordering that Borges denies, so that salvaging personal identity without succession produces contradictions mirroring those in his fictions. To keep the exercise from being a standard discussion, inject a niche framing: center the activity on the problem of narrative identity. If succession is illusory, then the autobiographical story of a life collapses, yet any attempt to communicate that collapse still relies on linear form. Design a game with explicit rules, assigned roles, physical materials (cards, space, or objects), and a non-monetary incentive or scoring structure so that participants must construct and defend a fictional character's life-narrative under constraints that gradually eliminate temporal ordering, causal language, and first-person continuity. The rules should be arranged so that participants run into contradictions—smuggling temporal metaphors into spatial layouts, losing the ability to distinguish memory from anticipation—before the facilitator names what has happened. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, specify exactly what each participant does with cards or spatial positions, and include a debrief that maps specific breakdowns onto Borges's arguments: the Berkeleyan and Humean dissolution of the self, the denial of succession, and the paradox of narrating timelessness. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—most likely participants will reintroduce sequence through spatial adjacency or narrative intuition—and how to correct it without lecturing. Finally, be explicit about which feature of Borges's view the activity cannot reproduce: the metaphysical experience of an eternal, co-present whole can only appear as a failure of coordination, so the facilitator must supply that positive dimension in the closing ten minutes. The deliverable should contain no bibliography, no reading list, and no requests for citations; answer from philosophical knowledge of Borges's texts and narrative theory, writing in a formal register appropriate to a doctoral student addressing a senior colleague.
