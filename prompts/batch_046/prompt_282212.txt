I hold that video-game characters occupy a distinct moral category—let us call it "projected agency"—that is irreducible both to full moral personhood and to mere fictional representation. On this view, when a player engages with a character through sustained volitional projection into the game's diegetic frame, the character becomes a locus of moral consideration not because it possesses consciousness or interests (it does not), but because the player's own practical identity is partially constituted by the pattern of commitments enacted within that frame. Consequently, gratuitous harm directed at such characters can constitute a genuine, if structurally attenuated, moral failing—not instrumentally harmful to the player, but corruptive of the player's capacity for consistent volitional projection across frames. I want an honest map of this position's exposure. Which empirical findings about player psychology, moral development, or the phenomenology of identification would genuinely threaten it, and which would merely be awkward? Which conceptual distinctions—say, between diegetic and non-diegetic action, between identification and sympathy, or between frame-bound and frame-transcendent commitment—would a defender have to concede as damaging, and which could be absorbed by refining the notion of "projected agency"? Are there results in virtue ethics or moral psychology that would either collapse this view into standard accounts of fiction or insulate it into unfalsifiability? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate whether insulation, if achieved, is a philosophical virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind, and say what the view's own defenders should themselves recognize as counting against them rather than only what critics allege.
