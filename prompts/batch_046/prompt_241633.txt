For a dissertation chapter on the ethics of national attachment, I am trying to stabilize the following commitments before I defend them in a workshop. I hold that: (1) patriotism is a virtue only in its critical form—affective attachment to compatriots that remains conditional on the state's basic justice; (2) associative obligations to fellow citizens are genuine but derive their normative force from shared social practices, not from any intrinsic moral standing of the nation-state; (3) cosmopolitan duties take lexical priority over patriotic ones in cases of direct conflict; (4) there is a sharp distinction between patriotic loyalty (directed toward the people) and civic fidelity (directed toward constitutional principles), and the two can come apart when a regime is illegitimate but the populace remains worthy of concern; and (5) the structural vulnerability of patriotic attachment to moral corruption—its tendency to slide into jingoism or complicity—is not a contingent defect but partly constitutive of its value as a virtue, since the depth of commitment required for it to matter is inseparable from the risk of its abuse. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity, and indicate whether abandoning (5) does more damage to the coherence of the framework than weakening (3).
