I have recently assumed trusteeship of a family archive of mid-twentieth-century assemblage sculptures—works constructed from degradable industrial materials such as oxidized rubber, compressed foam, and corroded steel—together with their complete high-resolution 3D-scan datasets. Conservation decisions now force me to confront material constitution in a practical register: if a structurally failed element is replaced by a synthetically identical replica, or if the digital file is used to fabricate a new physical instance after the original decays entirely, do we still hold the original artwork, a restored continuation, or a numerically distinct successor? I am not seeking a bibliography or a survey of positions. I want a committed, defensible prioritization of the open conceptual lines in this area. Rank the following—mereological essentialism versus constitution-without-identity, the role of functional and provenance-tracking properties in artifact persistence, the analog of biological regeneration for inanimate objects, and the metaphysical status of digital-to-physical replication—according to which deserve sustained philosophical attention over the next several years. For each, state your criteria explicitly: tractability for conceptual philosophers rather than empirical scientists, dependency relations among the questions (which answers must be secured before others become answerable), and practical stakes for conservation ethics or legal title. Then take a definite stand: is the digital-archive case a legitimate extension of the statue-and-clay puzzle, or does it reveal a category error in applying material-constitution frameworks to informational artifacts? Do not lay out options neutrally. Identify the single most important crux, state clearly what you would deprioritize, defend your ordering, and explain whether any residue—claims that resist clean translation into your preferred framework—points to a defect in that framework or to an ambiguity in the original problem.
