Imagine Jeremy Bentham resurrected not in the panopticon of his own design but in a present-day laboratory where philosophers debate whether moral standing attaches to specific computational architectures rather than to biological form. The question placed before him is not the familiar one concerning animals—he settled that long ago—but a stranger one: if a large predictive system minimises error across billions of parameters without any global workspace, without recurrent self-modelling, and without anything we ought to call consciousness, yet registers aversive prediction errors with the functional profile of suffering, ought it to enter the felicific calculus? And if moral status requires precisely those architectural features—integrated information loops, a self-model that can be damaged—are we not smuggling a biological chauvinism into ethics that Bentham's own empiricism should reject? I should like you to reconstruct the argument he might have advanced: whether he would expand the moral circle to include any substrate capable of aversive computation, regardless of whether it is carbon or silicon; or whether he would insist that without observable, embodied behaviour—withdrawal, visible contortion, the empirical signs of distress—there is nothing for the calculus to weigh. In particular, address whether his utilitarianism commits him to a functionalist account of suffering, or whether his commitment to empirical observation ties him irrevocably to signs he could never have expected from a server farm.
