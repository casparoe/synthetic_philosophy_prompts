After forty years in experimental physics I have recently turned to philosophy in retirement, and I have been working through Augustine's account of lying, particularly in De mendacio and Contra mendacium. I have constructed an argument that seems to show his absolute prohibition condemns ordinary scientific collaboration, yet I cannot locate the precise misstep and should value a diagnosis.

(1) Augustine holds that lying is always impermissible, for speech was instituted to reveal one's mind to another; to utter what one does not hold in one's mind, with the intention that it be received as true, is to corrupt language's very purpose and to divide the heart.

(2) The essential wrong, on this view, consists not in the falsehood of the proposition uttered in itself, but in the deliberate misalignment between the speaker's internal mental assent and the communicative content presented as assented-to.

(3) Consider a practice I shall call "distributed epistemic authorship": in a large physics collaboration, every named author signs a publication reporting measurements from instruments they have not personally inspected; each genuinely believes the collaboration's verification protocols are sound, yet no individual possesses mental assent corresponding to every specific claim in the paper.

(4) Here there is a systematic divergence between the collective communicative act—presenting all claims as jointly endorsed by each named author—and the actual mental states of the individual signatories.

(5) By the principle articulated in (2), each participant therefore engages in a structural equivalent of lying, irrespective of whether the claims are in fact true and irrespective of any intent to deceive about the data.

(6) Applied consistently, Augustine's view entails that all large-scale collaborative science involves lying, and is therefore sinful.

(7) This conclusion is plainly absurd; I should far rather preserve Augustine's core insight that the soul must be oriented towards truth, and preserve the legitimacy of scientific collaboration, than accept (6).

(8) Where, then, does the argument slip? Is the error in (2)'s abstraction away from Augustine's specific conditions—namely, that lying requires both falsehood and an intent to deceive—so that distributed authorship lacks one or both? Or is there a more subtle equivocation between individual mental assent and collective endorsement? If I must reject one step, I should prefer to keep (1) and (3); but I want to know precisely what rejecting (2) or (4) costs, and whether any other premise can be abandoned more cheaply.
