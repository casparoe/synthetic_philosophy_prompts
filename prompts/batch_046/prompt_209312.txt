I've settled on a strict resemblance nominalism about universals: properties are nothing over and above families of particular objects that stand in resemblance relations, with no shared entity or universal making them alike. My friend pushed back hard, and now I want an honest map of what would actually count against this view—not just the classic regress objection that resemblance itself seems universal, but the deeper pressure points. Specifically, how does the view handle modal properties like being possibly red, or the relation of causation itself, without either fragmenting into realism about those special cases or treating "resemblance" as an unexplained primitive that smuggles in the very universals it denies? Which commitments in philosophy of mathematics or science—say, about structural properties or lawhood—turn out awkward, and which results would a committed nominalist concede as genuinely damaging rather than dismiss as realist prejudice? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the view risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—especially if resemblance gets made primitive—and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a metaphysical position of this kind.
