I was chatting with a friend who's in a phil of mind course and she summed up epiphenomenalism like this: conscious feelings—pain, the redness of red—are totally real, but they're causally inert. Your brain states cause them, but they don't cause anything physical back. So if you yell 'ouch,' that's all neurons firing; the pain itself just comes along for the ride like a shadow. The big problem, she said, is the paradox of phenominal judgment. If pain can't cause anything, then your belief that you're in pain isn't caused by the pain. It's caused by whatever brain state made the pain, but the pain is left out of the loop entirely. So your judgments about your own feelings are basically lucky correlations—two things (the feeling and the belief) both caused by a third factor, with no direct link between them. She said this makes self-knowledge impossible, and since we obviously know our own minds, epiphenomenalism is dead. She also mentioned that defenders try to save it by arguing evolution selected for consciousness because organisms with richer inner lives reproduced more, which she thinks just sneaks causal power in through the back door.

Is this a fair summary? I feel like she's smuggling in a transmission model of phenomenal judgment—like the feeling has to literally push the belief into existence for the belief to be about it—and maybe she's misrepresenting the evolutionary reply, since I think defenders usually treat consciousness as a spandrel, not something selected for. But I'm not totally sure where the errors end and the real paradox begins. Give me a committed answer—don't just lay out options, tell me whether her summary is fair enough to reject epiphenomenalism, or whether the mistakes are big enough that the paradox doesn't actually land.
