You advise a government behavioral-science unit that redesigns public-choice interfaces—pension enrollment, tax-credit selection, and organ-donation registration. For each redesign, your analysts must determine whether observed selection patterns reveal citizens' stable preferences or are artifacts of framing and architecture, because that determination governs whether the unit should honor the pattern or justify overriding it with a default or nudge. Write a reusable audit procedure—executable by an analyst in thirty minutes without specialized philosophical training—that operationalizes revealed-preference theory together with the philosophical distinctions between ordinal utility, revealed utility, and constructed preference. Structure it as an ordered sequence of tests, each specifying: the question to ask, the evidence required to answer yes or no, the point at which the procedure issues a redesign recommendation, and the point at which the issue becomes normative and must be escalated to the policy board. The procedure must explicitly address preference reversals across framing conditions, status-quo bias in opt-in versus opt-out architectures, and social-desirability contamination that makes explicit choice unreliable. Include a brief internal taxonomy that classifies violations as either preference construction—indicating no stable pre-existing preference is present—or preference masking—indicating a stable preference is present but obscured by cognitive noise—and specify how each classification alters the redesign recommendation. Apply the procedure to three interfaces: (a) a pension plan with an opt-out default where 85% enrollment persists across framing variations; (b) a tax-credit form where reversing the presentation order produces a 40-point shift in uptake; and (c) an organ-donation registry where explicit consent is low but family-override authorization is high. In at least one application, the procedure should yield a recommendation to override the observed choice pattern in a manner that conflicts with strict revealed-preference deference. Conclude by identifying which procedural steps are genuinely mechanical and which are judgment calls disguised as checks, and state precisely what the procedure cannot settle about the nature of utility and where philosophical disagreement remains unavoidable regardless of the audit outcome.
