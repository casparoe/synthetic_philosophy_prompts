I am asking you to draft the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop I am convening next month, titled Corruption Beyond the Bribe: Clientelism, Gift Economies, and the Limits of Universal Standards. The audience is deliberately mixed: roughly half are philosophers and legal theorists trained in Western liberal frameworks, and half are anthropologists and regional specialists who study societies in which patron-client reciprocity and ceremonial gift-giving are constitutive social practices rather than deviations from them. I need the remarks—about eight minutes, delivered from the position of a philosophy professor who is not an anthropologist—to do more than acknowledge diversity. They must be structured around a precise conceptual distinction I want to put on the table: between corruption as procedural breach (the violation of an impartial rule that is already accepted within the practice) and corruption as hermeneutic imposition (the misapplication of transactional, contract-based evaluative categories to relational economies governed by non-fungible obligations of status and reciprocity). This distinction should determine what the remarks can and cannot claim: I should be able to condemn genuine exploitation without licensing the dismissal of local normative orders as backward, and I should be able to defend the intelligibility of cross-cultural critique without pretending that all practices are incommensurable. The speech must avoid both moral imperialism and normative quietism, and it should land as an invitation to the workshop's actual work rather than as a settled thesis. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the audience includes non-specialists, and I want the conceptual architecture to carry the weight. Write it in a formal but not stilted register appropriate to a philosophy professor addressing colleagues across disciplines.
