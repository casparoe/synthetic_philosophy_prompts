Write a satirical, deadpan internal policy memo — no more than 650 words, with numbered sections and bureaucratic headers — from a fictional national “Office of Temporal Stratification” to a legislature, proposing that all government action be sorted into Fernand Braudel’s three temporal registers: geographical and climate structures (the longue durée), social and economic conjunctures, and individual events or politics. Apply the framework with absurd literalness: climate adaptation must be routed to the “geographical-time” division and is therefore ineligible for electoral-cycle funding; a recent political scandal is classified as “individual-time noise” and excluded from parliamentary debate; invent one slightly ridiculous intermediate category (for example, “infrastructural deep time”) and insist that it outranks democratic deliberation. The satire should exaggerate real features of Braudel’s hierarchy — especially the subordination of events and individuals to slow-moving structures, and the tension between geographical determinism and human agency — without misrepresenting them, so that a reader familiar with Braudel’s major Mediterranean study and the Annales school recognizes the parody as fair. Maintain earnest administrative rationality throughout; never wink or break character. Conclude with a recommendation that follows the logic to a conclusion the reader recoils from, making visible the real conflict between long-horizon structural analysis and democratic accountability.
