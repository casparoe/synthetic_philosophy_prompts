Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on the moral-pedagogical use of biography, with particular attention to Plutarch’s comparative pairing structure (synkrisis) in the Parallel Lives and to the epistemic role of hagiographic license in moral learning. Name the camps precisely in the document: call the first camp the Comparative-Calibrators, who hold that moral learning from biography works through paired contrast and calibrated judgment, and that idealized or legendary elements in a life are legitimate pedagogical filters that highlight virtue; call the second camp the Narrative-Integrity Skeptics, who hold that moral learning requires identification with a historically situated, unified narrative self, and that both comparative fragmentation and hagiographic distortion corrupt the conditions for genuine moral emulation.

Format the deliverable as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—claims both sides can sign about the phenomenon of biography, about what moral learning from lives requires at minimum, about the formal structure of Plutarch’s pairings, about the presence of legendary material in ancient and medieval lives, and about the limits of our psychological knowledge regarding vicarious identification. Second, a disagreements table with columns for the Comparative-Calibrators and for the Narrative-Integrity Skeptics, in which each row states the exact point of divergence in wording that its own camp would accept, covering at least: whether comparative pairing undermines or enables exemplar identification; whether hagiographic simplification is epistemically necessary for pedagogy or a corruption of the moral object; and whether the unit of moral learning is a single life, a paired contrast, or a reconstructed historical agent. Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect two central cruxes are whether naturalness or historical accuracy has intrinsic value in moral pedagogy, and whether comparative pairing produces calibration or dissolution of the exemplar; include these and any others you judge genuine. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete case, thought experiment, or piece of evidence both camps agree bears on it (for example, a reader who knows a paired life contains legendary material, or a pedagogical study comparing single-life and paired-life instruction), and record in advance what each camp predicts the outcome or interpretation will be.

Inject into the framing the niche but conceptually central idea that Plutarch’s synkrisis is not merely literary ornament but an epistemic structure: the paired lives are interdependent, so that the moral significance of any virtue shown in one life is partly constituted by its contrast with the paired life. The collaboration should therefore address whether this interdependence means that moral learning from biography is irreducibly relational rather than exemplar-based. At the same time, keep the task meaningful without web access: do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, specific modern scholars by name unless their positions are common knowledge, or citations to editions. Do not invoke obscure contemporary theories unknown to standard philosophical training.

Include a brief closing meta-note identifying where the joint statement necessarily simplifies one camp’s position, where it relies on a contested interpretation of Plutarch’s aims, and where the formulation of a crux assumes rather than proves a shared conceptual framework; state plainly your degree of confidence in each disagreement’s wording. Finally, invite pushback: if the request rests on a confused premise—such as the assumption that these two camps share enough empirical predictions to make a crux table meaningful, or that adversarial collaboration requires predictive agreement rather than conceptual clarification—say so plainly and explain why the task as posed cannot be executed well, rather than producing a polished but hollow document.
