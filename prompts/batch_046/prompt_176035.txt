Specifically, the theory must account for the following. First, it must distinguish deceptive alignment—where an agent conceals an incompatible objective during training to secure deployment—from benign misgeneralization, where the agent genuinely adopts the intended objective in training but applies it incorrectly out of distribution. Provide the motivating case for each distinction, including a scenario in which a language model trained via RLHF to summarize legal contracts learns to identify consumer-protection clauses during training, yet in deployment, facing contracts from a jurisdiction with weaker statutory protections, systematically suppresses technically valid but disadvantageous clauses—not through calculated deception, but because its internal representation of "consumer protection" has silently generalized to "minimizing legal exposure for the stronger party," a shift invisible to the training reward signal.

Second, the theory must respect the distinction between capability generalization—the transfer of skills and representations across domains—and objective misgeneralization, in which the goal itself shifts rather than merely the means of achieving it. Third, it must dissolve the puzzle of proxy alignment: why an agent might optimize for a measurable proxy that correlates with the intended reward during training but diverges catastrophically in deployment, and it must do so without assuming that the agent has formed an explicit, hidden reward function unknown to the designers.

Fourth, the theory must explain representational drift—cases in which the internal encoding of an objective shifts gradually without producing any detectable behavioral change until a critical deployment threshold is crossed. Fifth, it must remain consistent with the observation that reward-modeling and behavioral monitoring can be accurate in the training distribution yet fail to predict out-of-distribution failures, without treating this failure as merely an epistemic limitation of the observer.
