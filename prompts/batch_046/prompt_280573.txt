At our philosophy of science reading group last week, a disagreement arose between Nadia, who works on scientific reasoning, and Marcus, who is writing on formal epistemology, over whether Bayesianism can genuinely accommodate old-evidence confirmation without abandoning its core norms. Nadia argued that the problem dissolves once we reject the assumption that an agent's belief state is a single flat distribution. She proposed that rational agents maintain 'structurally partitioned' beliefs: observational claims sit in one compartment, while auxiliary background conditions sit in another, with cross-compartment dependencies governed by explanatory coherence relations rather than by direct probabilistic conditioning. On her view, when Einstein proposed general relativity, the fact of Mercury's perihelion precession was indeed assigned near-certainty in the observational compartment. However, the Newtonian framework had embedded that fact within a specific auxiliary structure—namely, a belief that an undiscovered intra-Mercurial planet was perturbing the orbit. General relativity offered a different embedding: it predicted the precession directly from the curvature of spacetime with minimal free parameters. Nadia claimed that comparing these embeddings requires 'counterfactual marginalisation'—integrating over the auxiliary space using priors that are shared across theories but weighted by each theory's predictive stability. Because GR's predictions for Mercury's orbit remain stable across a wide range of plausible solar-system parameters, its marginal likelihood dominates Newton's, even though the observational datum itself was never 'updated on' in the subjective sense. Marcus objected that this is not Bayesian confirmation but a disguised form of inference to the best explanation. He insisted that if both theories are evaluated with the same prior over auxiliaries, then computing P(E|GR) and P(E|Newton) is legitimate comparative model selection, but it does not show that E confirms GR for an agent who already knew E. For that agent, he said, the likelihood principle forbids treating the same datum differently depending on which theories happen to be on the table. He then introduced what he called 'epistemic locking': once a belief reaches effective certainty within a compartment, it should be treated as fixed background for all subsequent theory evaluation, and only genuinely novel observations can unlock it. Nadia replied that locking creates an arbitrary temporal bias—why should a datum discovered in 1859 be permanently locked out of confirming a theory proposed in 1915?—and that Marcus's own position relies on a hidden revision of the prior over auxiliaries: he only permits comparison when the auxiliary space is held constant, but holding it constant already presupposes that Newtonian mechanics is the correct framework for structuring that space. They asked me to referee. Could you adjudicate point by point? Specifically: is Nadia's structural partitioning a legitimate extension of Bayesianism or an ad hoc rescue? Does Marcus's epistemic locking follow from the likelihood principle, or does it import an unjustified asymmetry? And who is closer to being right about whether old evidence can genuinely confirm a new theory?
