Compare Scanlon's contractualism with a hypothetical variant we might call proportional-rejection contractualism. On the standard view, an act is wrong if it is ruled out by a principle that no affected party could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced agreement. The familiar aggregation problem asks whether saving one person from death can be justified by preventing millions of minor harms, since each minor sufferer might reject being sacrificed for the one, yet the one can reject being sacrificed for them. Scanlon's framework is typically read as blocking simple aggregation, treating each individual's rejection as having decisive force in its own case. Proportional-rejection contractualism modifies this by holding that the strength of rejection is not binary but graded: it scales with both the severity of the burden and the number of people who bear it, allowing diffuse but widespread harms to generate a collectively decisive rejection even when no single instance is severe. In comparing these two approaches, address whether introducing graded, collective rejection collapses contractualism into a form of rule-utilitarianism, and whether Scanlon's binary-rejection framework can adequately handle large-scale collective-action problems—such as climate mitigation or pandemic restrictions—without implicitly relying on some proportional weighting.
