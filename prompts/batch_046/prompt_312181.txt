I'm drafting an essay that tries to dissolve Bradley's regress by treating propositional unity not as an ontological achievement but as a normative status, and I would value comments on whether the move is coherent, whether it genuinely addresses Bradley or merely changes the subject, and whether one analogy I'm using should be kept or dropped.

The familiar regress, as Bradley presents it, runs like this: if a relation R connects terms a and b, then R must itself be connected to a and to b by further relations R' and R'', and so on without end. Bradley takes this to show that relations are unreal and that true unity belongs only to the undifferentiated Absolute. The standard modern response, following Russell, is to accept relations as externally relating—R needs no further glue to bind it to its terms. I want to propose a different path altogether: deny that unity requires a connector of any kind, not because relations are external, but because the demand for a connector arises from treating representation on the model of physical assembly.

Consider the proposition that Socrates is wise. Its unity is not secured by a metaphysical tie that stitches the concept of Socrates to the concept of wisdom. Rather, it is secured by the fact that in asserting or thinking it, one undertakes a specific pattern of commitments: if challenged on whether Socrates is wise, one cannot retreat to the claim that Socrates exists while leaving the wisdom-predication behind. The parts are bound not by an entity but by the normative structure of the representation itself. This is not linguistic inferentialism—I am not claiming that the unity derives from linguistic practice or social scorekeeping—but a claim about representational content prior to language. The brain addresses its own binding problem not by introducing a neural glue that fuses feature maps, but by integrating them into object files through organizational dynamics; analogously, thought integrates concepts into propositions not by adding a relational entity but by organizing them under norms of coherence and challenge.

On this view, the regress dissolves: there is no need for a further relation to bind R to its terms, because R, or the propositional form, just is the normative organization of the terms. The mistake is to ask "what holds them together?" as if togetherness were always a physical relation that requires execution. But I worry that this sidesteps Bradley's actual concern. Bradley was asking about the metaphysics of reality—about how the world itself can contain differentiated unities—not about the structure of thought or representation. I also worry that the analogy to neural binding is a false friend: neuroscience describes causal and functional integration, whereas I am talking about normative integration, and the two may share only a superficial vocabulary.

So my questions are these. First, does this normative dissolution genuinely engage Bradley's argument, or does it change the subject from metaphysics to philosophy of mind? Second, is the claim about pre-linguistic representational normativity coherent, or does it collapse into a form of inferentialism despite my attempt to avoid it? Third, should I retain the analogy to the cognitive binding problem to motivate the idea that integration need not be gluing, or does the disanalogy between causal and normative integration make it more confusing than helpful? And if the normative move is defensible, what does it imply for the unity of false propositions—do they lack unity in the same way that a malfunctioning object file might fail to bind its features? Please comment specifically on the accuracy of my reconstruction of the regress, on the coherence of the normative alternative, and on whether the essay should either deepen the metaphysical response or abandon it entirely in favor of a purely representational account.
