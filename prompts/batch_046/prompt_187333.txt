I've been going through kind of an existential spiral since my cousin asked me whether I'd still donate to malaria nets if I genuinely believed that saving one future life in the far future is worth a thousand present ones. I ended up adopting what I'd call a pretty hardcore version of strong longtermism: not just that the future is important, but that near-term moral obligations are mostly instrumental — they're about keeping the lights on for civilization rather then having intrinsic weight — and that even with heavy moral-uncertainty discounts and risk aversion, the expected value of safeguarding a flourishing long run dominates present-day charity by such a huge margin that the comparison feels almost category-errorish. I'm treating it like my operating system now, but I'm trying to be honest: what would actually count against this?

Not the generic you-don't-care-about-people-now line, which I think I can absorb, but things that defenders of this view would themselves concede are damaging — maybe formal results showing that value lock-in risks are actually low, or philosophical arguments that treating present obligations as merely instrumental collapses into something incoherent, or empirical updates that shrink the expected future population or value by orders of magnitude. I want to know which defeaters are decisive versus just awkward, whether the view has been qualified with so many epistemic hedges that it's now unfalsifiable, and if so whether that's a problem for a practical framework or just good humility. What's the honest exposure map?
