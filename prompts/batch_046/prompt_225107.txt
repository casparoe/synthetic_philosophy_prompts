hey, i've been chewing on a variant of Mary's room that i think exposes a hidden tension in the knowledge argument. imagine Mary knows every physical fact about color vision, but she also has access to a perfect real-time brain scanner that can map her own neural states with zero lag. after she's released and sees red, she gets the phenomenal experience *and* the complete physical readout of that experience simultaneously. now, before her release she could have scanned someone else seeing red and gotten the same kind of physical readout of *their* brain. so after seeing red, she posseses: (1) the phenomenal knowledge, (2) her own brain scan, (3) the other person's brain scan she already had.

the question is whether this trio collapses the argument. the physicalist wants to say (1) is just a mode of presentation of (2). but if (2) and (3) are structurally identical physical descriptions — just applied to different brains — then Mary already knew what the physical readout of 'seeing red' looks like in general. does that mean she already knew what *her own* readout would be like? or is there still an irreducible first-person gap?

please explain this conceptual issue: does the scanner show that phenomenal knowledge is just indexed physical knowledge, or does it actually highlight that the gap is about the *subject* of the knowledge rather than its content? i want the answer somewhat in the weeds — don't just rehearse the classic formulation or list the standard physicalist replies. instead, focus on whether the argument requires an asymmetry in epistemic access that survives even perfect self-monitoring, and whether adding the scanner creates a kind of 'epistemic overdetermination' where Mary has redundant routes to the same fact.
