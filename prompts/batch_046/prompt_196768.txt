I've been treating the Grand Inquisitor's speech as an early preference-utilitarian proof: human cognitive architecture is optimized for security rather than autonomy, so benevolent authority should relieve people of moral choice in exchange for happiness, with coercion made explicit rather than hidden behind "choice architecture." But if surrendering freedom genuinely increases well-being, then the Inquisitor himself—having yielded his personal will to the Church—ought to be serene, yet Dostoevsky depicts him as sleepless and spiritually tormented. Doesn't this internal contradiction falsify the empirical premise? Or should we save the argument with a two-level model, where the masses gain first-order happiness by giving up freedom while the elite suffer precisely because they retain second-order authority over those choices? If the latter holds, doesn't the critique collapse into an ordinary principal-agent problem rather than a profound indictment of freedom itself?
