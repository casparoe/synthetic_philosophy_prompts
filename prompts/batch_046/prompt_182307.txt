Write a dialogue between two equally informed interlocutors—one defending a broadly Dennettian compatibilist stance on moral responsibility and punishment, the other advancing a Caruso-style skepticism about basic desert—set during a closed seminar on institutional justice. Rather than rehearsing the standard determinism-versus-free-will exchange, have them argue over a concrete, somewhat unusual case: a democratically governed municipality has implemented a predictive “social quarantine” protocol, in which citizens who have not committed any crime may be temporarily restricted in movement based on algorithmic risk assessments of future harmful behavior. The Dennettian interlocutor should argue that, even if determinism is true, the protocol can be justified as a form of rational, forward-looking social engineering analogous to quarantine for contagious disease, but she should also defend the idea that the affected citizens’ own characters and prior choices genuinely contribute to their risk profiles in a way that makes the institutional response appropriate—not merely useful—without collapsing into retributivism. The Caruso-style interlocutor should counter that any appeal to the citizen’s “own contribution” smuggles in the illusion of basic desert, and that the only coherent justification is purely consequentialist public-health logic, which demands radical transparency, revocable conditions, and the complete elimination of moralistic language from the board’s rulings; he should press the point that if determinism undermines the kind of control needed for true blameworthiness, then treating risk-assessed individuals differently from those with contagious diseases is unjustified unless the difference is purely administrative. Ensure the dialogue contains genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should address the other’s specific arguments about the boundary between appropriate institutional response and disguised retribution, and neither should be reduced to a strawman. The exchange should conclude without a neat resolution, but with both positions sharpened.
