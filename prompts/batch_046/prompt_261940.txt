I serve on my university's Committee on Institutional Practice and Ethics, and we're deadlocked over a procurement petition that has turned into a philosophy problem rather than a logistics one. A coalition of students and faculty has asked us to ban single-use plastic beverage containers campus-wide; their case isn't mainly about our direct environmental footprint but about institutional complicity in a distributed harm. The opposition—several of whom sit on this same committee—frames this as a clear instance of the inefficacy objection applied to collective bodies: our campus's annual plastic contribution is causally negligible on a global scale, so the ban cannot be justified by harm reduction alone, and treating it as symbolically important risks substituting expressive politics for measurable impact, with real costs to accessibility and procurement budgets. The proponents counter that this misses the structure of collective action—if every institution waits for its own marginal effect to cross a causal threshold, no actor ever moves, and there is a distinct wrong in being a participating node of a harmful practice regardless of whether one's withdrawal tips the global scale.

What makes this harder is a specific wrinkle they've raised that I want the memo to address head-on: they claim the inefficacy objection applies asymmetrically to consumption acts versus institutional signaling, because a university-level ban can shift procurement norms and supplier contracts in ways that individual abstention does not, but they haven't established whether that normative cascade should count as part of the harm being avoided or as an independent benefit that needs separate justification. Meanwhile, the strongest consideration on the other side is administrative consistency—if we accept practice-participation obligations for globally negligible harms, we may be committed to an unmanageable cascade of prohibitions across every supply chain with a non-zero negative externality. Please draft the committee's formal decision memo—approve the ban, reject it, or approve it with explicit conditions. The memo should present an actionable bottom line, lay out reasoning that both camps could recognize as engaging their actual arguments rather than strawmen, and include an honest, non-dismissive treatment of the strongest point on the losing side, specifically the risk that accepting this rationale commits us to an impractical universal regime. Keep the reasoning anchored to our actual institutional decision; don't drift into abstract climate ethics, don't invoke named philosophers or literature as authorities, and don't ask for citations—the committee needs arguments it can defend on their own merits.
