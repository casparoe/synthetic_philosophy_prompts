(2) The Luring Lottery extends this idea: if one agent can restructure the payoff environment so that the other recognizes an isomorphic decision problem, superrational coordination can occur even in the presence of strategic manipulation, provided the structural symmetry is transparent.

(3) If this reasoning is valid, it should apply not only to discrete game-theoretic choices but to any symmetric epistemic situation—such as two philosophers or scientists evaluating the same body of evidence with the same cognitive norms and background knowledge.

(4) Therefore, in any such symmetric epistemic case, fully rational agents must reach identical judgments; sustained disagreement can only arise from a hidden asymmetry or from a failure of rationality in at least one party.

(5) Yet we routinely observe persistent, good-faith disagreement among equally qualified experts—for instance, over the interpretation of quantum foundations or the status of moral realism—without being able to locate any relevant hidden asymmetry.

(6) So either we must conclude that rational peer disagreement is impossible, which seems plainly false, or we must reject the claim that superrational reasoning generalizes from Platonia-style games to epistemic domains.

(7) But if it fails to generalize to epistemic domains, there is no principled boundary that saves its application in the Luring Lottery, since that case also requires extending the reasoning to ambiguous, real-world strategic contexts with imperfect information.
