I'm working through Hofstadter's claim that the self is not a fixed substrate but a high-level pattern of causal loops among symbols—a "strange loop" that generates an "I" wherever it is realized—and I've pushed it into an argument that seems to license an absurd form of distributed identity. Here are the steps:

(1) If the self is constituted by this loop-structure of symbolic causation, then what matters for being "me" is not which particular neurons realize it but that the architecture of feedback and representation is intact.
(2) Patterns are multiply realizable and can be distributed: the same causal architecture can span two brains linked by a continuous, high-bandwidth interface that lets symbols and feedback flow freely between them.
(3) When such coupling occurs, there is not merely cooperation between two selves but a single loop-structure physically distributed across both skulls—so there is one "I" with two bodies.
(4) If the coupling is gradually weakened, continuity of the loop suggests the distributed self persists until divergence is complete, at which point the loop splits.
(5) After the split, each resulting loop is continuous with the original distributed one, so each new self inherits the identity of the pre-split "I."
(6) This means one self can exist simultaneously in two bodies, then become two selves without anyone dying—like fission without destruction.
(7) But this generates incoherent moral and legal consequences: during the distributed phase, one body could act while the other is passive, yet both share the same self and should share culpability; after separation, each self remembers the act but only one body performed it, making attribution impossible to stabilize.
(8) Therefore the pattern view proves too much: either we reject that distributed loops constitute one self, or we accept that personal identity can be spatially extended and fission-prone in ways that collapse our practices of responsibility.

Which premise or inference should I reject? I would prefer to keep (1) and the idea that continuity matters, but I am unsure whether the mistake is in (3) treating coupled loops as one token rather than two tokens of the same type, in (4) applying continuity across a split, or in (7) assuming that shared identity must entail shared culpability. For each option, explain why it initially looked plausible and what giving it up costs for a naturalistic account of the self.
