I serve on the examination committee for our doctoral program in philosophy and rational agency, and I need you to draft one 90-minute essay question for the comprehensive exam in rationality and practical reason. The question should test whether a candidate can move well beyond the standard textbook summary of the sunk cost fallacy—typically framed as a psychological bias or an economic failure to maximize expected utility—and instead navigate a constructed, somewhat unconventional dispute. Frame the prompt around two interlocutors: Party A argues that honoring sunk costs is always a rationality failure because unrecoverable past expenditures are causally and evidentially irrelevant to present options, violating the independence of irrelevant alternatives. Party B responds that in diachronic contexts—where an agent's prior commitments structurally constitute her present practical identity, as in long-term collaborative projects or identity-constituting careers—treating those past investments as reason-giving is not a fallacy but a form of narrative consistency, and that calling it irrational confuses temporal parts of an agent with a static, time-slice maximizer. The exam question you write should ask candidates to adjudicate this disagreement precisely: they must clarify what kind of "cost" (monetary, temporal, psychological, or relational) is required for the fallacy charge to apply in the first place; evaluate whether Party B's narrative-identity defense succeeds or collapses into a distinct failure such as commitment escalation or status-quo bias; and articulate whether there is any non-circular criterion for distinguishing rational diachronic commitment from irrational escalation of commitment. Along with the question itself, provide a model answer that demonstrates the required analytical depth, and provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks allocated out of 20 total and a one-line note for each point describing how candidates typically miss it. Throughout both the model answer and the marking scheme, please clearly separate what is settled in the literature—such as the logical structure of the independence condition and the standard expected-utility framing—from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, such as whether narrative identity genuinely generates present practical reasons, whether diachronic rationality requires trans-temporal utility aggregation, or whether "cost" can be coherently defined in non-monetary terms without circularity. Do not present any contested view as the consensus. Because this is a closed-book assessment, do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requirement in the exam prompt you draft.
