Consider two camps that have debated for several years whether an ant colony can possess a mind of its own, not merely the summed mental states of its members. Camp A, call them the Fugue Integrationists, defend a model they term the Ant Fugue: they hold that colony cognition emerges not from hierarchical command but from contrapuntal temporal interweaving of individual behavioral lines, such that the colony realizes a distributed, non-hierarchical cognitive architecture with its own intentional states. Camp B, call them the Aggregate Reductionists, deny that any such emergence occurs; they maintain that every colony-level regularity is fully explainable by individual ants' local rules and sensory-motor loops, and that attributing mental states to the colony is a category error or at best a misleading metaphor.

These camps have argued past each other, partly because they have not separated genuine disagreement from verbal confusion about terms like "integration," "representation," and "subject." They now wish to produce a joint adversarial-collaboration document.

Write that document. It should contain the following sections in this order:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of propositions about ant neurobiology, colony information flow, and the conditions for mindedness that both Integrationists and Reductionists can sign without reservation. Include claims about what is observed in foraging, thermoregulation, and task allocation; about the absence of a central controller in the colony; and about the fact that individual ants possess nervous systems capable of learning and memory. Do not include claims that depend on accepting either camp's theoretical framework.

2. Disagreements table. A table with three columns: (i) the issue in neutral wording that each side would accept as an accurate statement of its own view; (ii) the Integrationist position; (iii) the Reductionist position. Cover at least: whether temporal interweaving without hierarchical integration can constitute cognitive integration; whether behavioral coherence in an isolated individual (the Aunt Hillary scenario—an ant removed from the colony who nevertheless continues to execute colony-level coordination patterns) indicates a residual colony-level representational program or merely an individual habit; and whether the Ant Fugue architecture requires, for full-mindedness, a single subject of experience or can be satisfied by a distributed subject-less process.

3. Cruxes. Identify the two or three sub-questions such that resolving them in favor of one camp would cause at least one member of the other camp to revise their position substantially. For each crux, state it as a precise empirical or conceptual question.

4. Tests and predictions. For each crux, describe one concrete case, experimental design, or observed phenomenon that both camps agree bears on it. Then record, in separate sub-paragraphs, what the Integrationists predict will be observed or concluded, and what the Reductionists predict. Include a specific reference to the Aunt Hillary scenario and to the temporal-interweaving dynamics of the Ant Fugue model in at least one of these entries.

Keep all formulations neutral: neither camp's view should be described with language that the other camp would reject as loaded. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific scholars or texts. The document should read as a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with residual disagreement made exact.
