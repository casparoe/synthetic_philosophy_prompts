I'm designing a single ninety-minute seminar for clinical-ethics fellows and philosophy graduate students on a problem I'll frame as framework replacement in advance directives: not merely that a patient later prefers what the earlier self refused, but that the injury has replaced the very evaluative categories—what counts as harm, benefit, or self—through which the directive was formulated, leaving no shared metric to adjudicate conflict. I do not want a bibliography, legal survey, or citation list; I want a pedagogical plan for the explanation itself. Specify the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced and why, identifying the dependency that has to be secured before framework replacement can even be distinguished from ordinary preference change. Provide one concrete carrying scenario: specify exactly which clinical and psychological features it must include and which familiar tropes—such as progressive dementia with retained social warmth or a sudden personality inversion—it must avoid, and explain why those exclusions are necessary for the example to make the problem visible rather than collapse into a familiar autonomy-versus-welfare debate. Map the misconceptions the audience will likely generate in sequence—such as treating the directive as an interpersonal contract, assuming that present contentment automatically dissolves prior authority, or conflating psychological discontinuity with mere irrationality—and indicate the precise point in the arc where each misconception should be interrupted rather than corrected retroactively. Propose one closing question or brief exercise that would reveal whether the group has grasped the incommensurability problem rather than simply adopted a side. State explicitly what should be left out at this level—particular neuropsychological taxonomies, jurisdictional variations in directive law, or named theoretical frameworks—and acknowledge that omission openly. Finally, say what argument or clinical observation about personal identity over time or about the authority of prior commitments would lead you to restructure this teaching plan, and identify where your own reasoning is most vulnerable to failure.
