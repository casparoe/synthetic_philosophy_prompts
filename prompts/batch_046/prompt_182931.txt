I am preparing the syllabus for a graduate seminar on normative ethics, and in our recent sessions on the paradox of deontology we have been working through the familiar tension: a deontological constraint forbids killing one innocent person to save five, yet if the stakes are raised—say, to five hundred or five thousand—the constraint does not weaken proportionally, which seems to license far greater aggregate harm than the violation itself would cause. That structural problem is well mapped. But in the discussion I have noticed a more specific argumentative pattern that I do not think is captured by the paradox itself, and I am unsure whether the literature assigns it a distinct name.

Here is the pattern, observed across several student papers and in my own attempts to formalize the debate. A defender of the constraint begins with a victim-centered justification: the single potential victim possesses an inviolable status, and that status does not diminish when more people stand to benefit. So far this is standard deontology. Then, when the interlocutor introduces a variant in which the agent can save the many without actively killing anyone—say, by redirecting an external threat so that it harms neither the one nor the many—the defender often retains the prohibition by appealing not to the victim’s status but to the agent’s causal relation to harm: because nobody is being used as a mere means, the constraint is satisfied. The difficulty is that the defender now seems to ground the very same normative protection in the agent’s causal structure rather than in any property of the victims. Yet in the original high-stakes case, the defender insisted that the protection was independent of outcomes and of numbers, which suggested a victim-centered ontology. So the protected value—let us call it inviolability—remains constant in normative weight across different victim-counts, but its metaphysical or moral basis shifts between victim-centered and agent-centered registers depending on whether the case threatens to collapse the constraint or to preserve it. I have seen this oscillation in discussions of so-called “moral magic” and in some threshold-deontological accounts that allow override only in catastrophic conditions, but neither framework seems to isolate exactly this instability in the grounding of the constraint.

Does this specific pattern—the constant normative weight coupled with a shifting ontological basis for the protected value—have an established name in the literature on deontology? If there is no standard label, how ought we to characterize it so as to distinguish it from ordinary ad hoc reasoning, from the standard paradox of deontology, and from threshold views that explicitly relax the constraint? I am particularly interested in whether we can articulate its boundary conditions: at what point does the shift in grounding become a genuinely different moral principle rather than a rhetorical redescription of the same constraint?
