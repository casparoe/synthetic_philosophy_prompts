Compare the standard theological interpretation of the Death in Damascus paradox—treating it primarily as a tension between omniscient foreknowledge and libertarian freedom—with a decision-theoretic interpretation in which the prophecy functions not as a causal constraint but as a common-knowledge belief equilibrium that restructures the agent's choice set. On this second view, the agent's flight from Damascus is rational only if his belief-state is causally independent of the outcome; yet because the prophecy is public, it enters the equilibrium conditions that determine where death can occur, making the belief itself part of the mechanism that fixes the modal outcome. Which interpretation more accurately diagnoses why the paradox feels philosophically troubling rather than merely puzzling? Specifically, does the belief-equilibrium framing dissolve the problem by reducing it to familiar issues in epistemic logic and counterfactual dependence, or does it leave intact a distinct residual problem about how predictive knowledge can constrain action without being causally efficacious?
