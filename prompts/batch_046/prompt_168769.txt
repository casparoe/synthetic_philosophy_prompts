As a novelist who has long studied how readers assign blame for unintended harms in fiction, I have come to defend a specific interpretation of the Knobe effect: I take the asymmetry—where an agent is said to have acted intentionally when a side-effect is harmful but not when it is beneficial—to show that folk attributions of intentionality are governed not by the structure of mental states but by the moral and narrative salience of the outcome within a story. On my view, the vignette methodology of experimental philosophy does not uncover a concept of intentional action rooted in psychology; rather, it measures how moral weight reshapes descriptive judgment, which means the so-called Knobe asymmetry is best understood as a feature of narrative grammar rather than of folk psychology. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? I want the defeaters mapped with care: which empirical results from non-narrative or cross-cultural attribution studies, which conceptual distinctions between mental-state criteria and evaluative framing, and which findings from adjacent experimental-philosophy domains—say, on moral luck or free will—would genuinely undermine the claim that intentionality attributions track dramatic significance rather than belief-desire structure. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward. Also tell me whether the position risks being insulated by ad hoc qualifications—restricting it to third-person judgment, or to specific literary cultures—such that no observation could count against it, and whether that insulation is itself a defect for a view of this kind. I am especially interested in which objections the defenders of the standard psychological interpretation would themselves accept as serious, not only what critics allege.
