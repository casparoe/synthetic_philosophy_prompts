I am a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, and I am drafting the opening remarks for an interdisciplinary workshop—attended by philosophers, mathematicians, and working physicists—on the principle of indifference and its failures in continuous spaces. Rather than rehearsing the familiar chord, sector, and radius versions of Bertrand's paradox, I want the speech to turn on a formulation I have been developing: assigning a prior over the continuous orientations of a freely rotating symmetric body in three-dimensional space, where rotational invariance appears to require the Haar measure on SO(3), while an intuitive application of indifference over Euler angles pulls toward a different distribution. This tension must do real structural work: it should constrain what I can claim about "objective" priors, and it should show both the specialists—who will recognize the symmetry argument—and the non-specialists that choosing a parameterization is a substantive epistemic commitment, not neutral mathematical bookkeeping. The remarks should run about five minutes, be written in polished American English, and include no bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Please also include, woven through or in a brief closing note, what specific argument or finding would change your mind about whether rotational symmetry alone resolves the paradox, and identify precisely where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
