The Malcolm–Dennett disagreement over whether dreams are experiences has circled for decades without either camp conceding ground: Malcolm treats dreams as narratives invented upon waking, denying any corresponding mental event during sleep, while Dennett maintains that they are genuine experiences, perhaps assembled at the moment of recall but no less real for that. In rationalist forums the same moves still recur—first-person authority versus confabulation, the transparency of introspection, analogies with false memory—yet nobody shifts position. My working theory is that the stall is conceptual rather than empirical: both sides assume that “experience” is binary, either present during sleep or absent, and neither accommodates a third possibility, namely that dream reports involve reconstructed phenomenology in which first-person authority is retained despite the absence of any diachronic phenomenal presence at the time of sleep. Is that diagnosis correct, or is the disagreement genuinely about the ontology of consciousness—one side requiring phenomenal presence, the other allowing functional or representational presence? Please diagnose the impasse concretely: is it verbal, a hidden dispute about what counts as evidence for mental states, or are the two sides answering different questions—one about the metaphysics of sleep consciousness, the other about the cognitive architecture of report? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction between synchronic and diachronic experience, an empirical finding about sleep-state neural replay, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed and dissolve it?
