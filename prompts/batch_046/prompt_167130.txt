A recent popular essay summarizes Benjamin Franklin's moral-perfection project as follows. Franklin arranged his thirteen virtues into a strict hierarchical sequence—temperance first, then silence, then order, and so on—on the theory that each virtue enabled the next, and he conceived the whole scheme as a Protestant adaptation of Stoic askesis aimed at asymptotic self-divinization. To execute it, he kept a daily ledger in which he assigned specific punitive penances for each failure, treating virtue acquisition as a behaviorist conditioning program. The essay claims that Franklin eventually abandoned the ledger because he concluded that monitored, calculated virtue is not genuine virtue at all: true moral excellence must be spontaneous, and therefore any system of self-accounting is self-defeating.

This sounds suspiciously neat. Is it a fair summary? Please disentangle what is accurate about Franklin's ordering of virtues, his actual use of the ledger, his attitude toward Stoicism and Protestant ethics, and his final assessment of the project; identify which parts are anachronistic psychological projections, which conflate Franklin's modest practical aims with metaphysical perfectionism, and whether the essay's conclusion—that Franklin repudiated self-monitoring—follows from the accurate parts. Do not provide a bibliography, but if you appeal to specific passages in the Autobiography, paraphrase them without quotation marks.
