I'm designing a classification instrument for a graduate seminar on democratic theory that focuses on direct democratic mechanisms—Swiss-style facultative and mandatory referendums, the UK advisory referendum on EU membership, and California-style ballot initiatives with bundled provisions. I need a branching taxonomy or decision tree that sorts theoretical positions on the legitimacy and limits of direct democracy, built from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of named philosophers.

The tree should be structured around four diagnostic questions, each anchored in a concrete institutional case, so that a respondent can place themselves by stating their intuition about the case without recognizing any label:

1. Epistemic deference and complex mandates: In a mandatory referendum on a treaty or regulatory package exceeding two thousand pages—modeled on complex Swiss votes—is popular approval legitimate only if a critical mass of voters understand the technical provisions, or can legitimacy rest on deference to a trusted advisory summary?

2. Bundling and preference integrity: If a California-style ballot proposition combines a carbon-tax mechanism with a sentencing-reform measure—policies with no logical or policy connection—does supporting the package as a whole demonstrate a coherent democratic will, or does bundling inherently corrupt the expression of preference?

3. Advisory status and normative authority: When a government holds an advisory referendum (like the 2016 UK vote) and promises to respect the result but faces no legal obligation, does the absence of binding status weaken democratic legitimacy, preserve it by safeguarding parliamentary sovereignty, or transform the vote into a purely expressive act with no normative authority?

4. Scale, harm, and procedural value: If direct democratic mechanisms consistently yield outcomes that increase aggregate suffering—say, by blocking refugee resettlement or public-health measures—does the scale of harm generate an obligation to constrain direct democracy institutionally, or does the procedural value of popular sovereignty remain decisive regardless of outcome?

For each branch, label the splitting claim explicitly. At each leaf, provide either the name of the position that occupies that combination (e.g., pure proceduralism, epistemic elitism, deliberative democratic theory, constitutional constraint view) or mark it "unnamed." Every leaf should include one sentence describing its most awkward commitment—the specific consequence it must accept that its sibling positions reject. For nodes that remain unoccupied, briefly diagnose the emptiness: is the combination incoherent (e.g., demanding both absolute voter sovereignty and unconditional expert deference), unattractive (e.g., treating advisory votes as purely expressive while insisting they be held with high frequency), or merely unexplored?

The taxonomy should explicitly flag any positions it cannot separate. If two distinct views—say, a qualified epistemic deferentialist and a proceduralist with competence conditions—produce identical answers across all four questions, note the collapse and specify what finer distinction (perhaps about the source of deference or the reversibility of the decision) would be needed to split them.

Keep the instrument usable by someone who knows the institutional cases but not the philosophical labels: each branch must be answerable by someone stating what they believe should happen in the case, not by recognizing a philosopher's name. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the deliverable is the tree itself, with branches labeled by the claim that separates them, leaves carrying position names (or "unnamed") plus the awkward commitment, and brief diagnostic notes for empty nodes.
