As a retired physicist who has recently turned to moral philosophy, I have been examining Peter Singer's argument that we bear a stringent obligation to rescue distant strangers at minimal personal cost, illustrated by the analogy of a child drowning in a shallow pond. The argument turns on the claim that spatial distance and institutional mediation do not diminish moral duty: writing a check to an aid organization is, in principle, equivalent to wading into the pond. I am curious how David Hume—whose writings on sympathy, causation, and moral sentiment predate global communications by centuries—would have assessed these specific extensions. Would Hume have regarded the attenuation of sympathy over distance as a cognitive bias to be corrected by reason, as Singer implies, or as an irreducible boundary condition of moral obligation? Moreover, modern applied ethics distinguishes direct rescue, where one's action is causally contiguous with the saved life, from mediated rescue through bureaucratic organizations, probabilistic outcomes, and fungible resources—what I have taken to calling "lever-pulling" rather than "life-pulling." Given Hume's view that causal inference depends on constant conjunction and that moral approval requires an intimate impression of the effect, would he have recognized mediated institutional giving as falling within the same class of duties as direct physical rescue? I seek not a summary of Hume's texts, but a reasoned reconstruction of his likely judgment on whether the drowning-child analogy survives translation into these mediated, probabilistic, and globally distributed forms.
