I have been thinking on my own about a puzzle concerning intention and reward that seems to mirror a well-known philosophical problem, though I do not know its formal name. In my version, a wealthy stranger offers you one million dollars on the condition that, by a specific deadline, you genuinely intend to press a button that will permanently erase one particular harmless but personally meaningful memory. You are not required to press the button after receiving the payment; you need only have formed the sincere intention at the appointed time. Once the money is secured, you may abandon the plan without penalty. The difficulty is that, at the moment of decision, you appear to have every practical reason to form the intention—since that secures the reward—while also having every reason not to carry it out, since the memory is valuable and there is no cost to withdrawal. I have discussed this with a friend and we disagree about whether rationality permits one to cultivate an intention that one already knows one will not execute. Elias should defend the view that rational agency requires a credible continuity between intention and action, and that deliberately adopting an intention one knows will be abandoned amounts to a form of practical self-deception that erodes the coherence of one’s will. They should engage with each other’s actual arguments rather than speaking past one another, and neither should be presented as obviously mistaken.
