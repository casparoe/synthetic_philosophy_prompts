I work in distributed systems and have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been thinking about object identity in terms that map onto what I understand is called the statue and the clay problem. I want a joint adversarial-collaboration document drafted between two camps: the constitution theorists, who hold that a statue and the clay lump that currently makes it up are numerically distinct objects that share all of their parts at a given time but have different persistence conditions and modal properties; and the identity theorists, who hold that there is only one object present, and that talk of a statue versus a lump of clay amounts to different descriptions, properties, or modal perspectives applied to the same thing.

Please produce the document in the following structure.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. List precise statements about physical objects, part-whole relations, spatiotemporal continuity, and persistence conditions that both sides can sign without watering them down to emptiness. Be explicit about what the formal relations imply, and avoid framing anything in a way that silently assumes one camp is correct.

Second, a disagreements table. For each camp, state in wording that the respective camp would accept as accurate: for the constitution side, that there are two objects with different survival conditions; for the identity side, that there is one object with one set of parts and that apparent differences are differences in description or modal attribution, not in object count. Make the entries parallel and neutral.

Third, identify the cruxes—the specific sub-questions such that resolving them would actually move at least one side. I suspect one crux concerns whether an object can survive the complete replacement of its parts if its form or functional organization is continuously maintained, and another concerns whether two objects can occupy exactly the same spatiotemporal region. Confirm, refine, or add to these, and state them precisely.

Fourth, apply both views to a novel scenario intended to stress-test them. Imagine a large bronze statue in a museum maintained by an automated conservation system. Every week, 0.1 percent of its atoms are replaced with chemically identical new atoms, guided by a high-resolution digital twin that tracks position and bonding state in real time. The replaced atoms are catalogued and stored in labeled containers. After ten years, 100 percent of the original atoms have been replaced, but the museum display looks structurally identical and is continuously linked to the digital twin. Meanwhile, a separate team uses the stored original atoms to reassemble a second statue with the same macroscopic shape and internal structure. At the end of the process, the museum displays one statue and the warehouse holds the other. According to each camp, how many objects exist? Is the museum piece identical to the original statue? Is the warehouse piece identical to the original? Could either be identical to both? Record each side's prediction about whether the museum piece has survived the maintenance process, and about what an observer should intuit.

Fifth, for each crux, specify a concrete argument, thought experiment, or empirical observation that both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that case or observation shows.

Finally, state explicitly whether any portion of the dispute is merely verbal—that is, whether the camps agree on all the physical and modal facts and differ only in how they apply labels like object or same thing—and if so, identify exactly which portion and explain why.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the output should stand alone without reference to literature I do not have access to. Avoid highly specialized terminology tied to a particular unpublished system. Keep the reasoning formal and accessible to someone with a background in logic and system design but no academic philosophy background.
