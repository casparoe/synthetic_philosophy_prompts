Question: The French Revolution is often said to devour its own children. Some historians treat Thermidor as a self-correcting mechanism rather than proof of inevitable self-destruction. Is the fall of the Girondins in 1793 best explained by class struggle, revolutionary ideology, or contigency?

Student answer:
The Marxist historians like Soboul say the Girondins were bourgeois representatives who couldn't deliver what the sans-culottes needed, so class conflict pushed them out. The revisionist school, especially Furet, rejects this by saying social class doesn't matter at all and that revolutionary rhetoric alone generated the violence. I think both miss the real driver. The war crisis and prison massacres of September 1792 created a contigency where moderation looked like treason, so the Girondins were executed not by destiny but by circumstance. The old Saturn comparison—that revolutions eat their young—actually supports this, because if history was structurally fixed then radicals wouldn't have turned on each other. So contigency is the best explanation, though class and ideology set the stage.
