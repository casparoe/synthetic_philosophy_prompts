Design a 90-minute workshop activity for about 12 graduate philosophy students on luck egalitarianism, centered on a niche tension: the baseline preferences and judgments of “desert” that participants use to distinguish option luck from brute luck are themselves products of hidden circumstance conditions that the theory treats as morally arbitrary. The deliverable should be a runnable simulation with rules, roles, and small real stakes—for example, distributing a fixed pool of tokens that determine both a collective outcome and individual rankings. Arrange it so participants first negotiate a compensation principle they consider fair, then gradually reveal hidden “circumstance cards” that have systematically shaped each participant’s risk preferences, effort valuation, and sense of entitlement, causing them to experience the collapse of their own distributive framework from the inside. Include a payoff scheme that requires coordination but rewards defection, materials that can be made from ordinary paper, and a structured debrief that maps each stage onto the option-luck/brute-luck distinction, identifies the precise moment adaptive preferences enter, predicts at least two ways the activity will go wrong on first run and how to handle them, and explicitly states which structural feature of luck egalitarianism—such as intergenerational transmission or genuinely unobservable counterfactual baselines—the simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally. No lecture up front; participants should not hear the formal labels until after they have constructed and then watched their scheme unravel.
