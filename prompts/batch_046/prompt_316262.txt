I have been thinking, without any formal training, about why it seems permissible for me to endure a painful surgery today in order to save my own life next year, yet impermissible to kill one healthy stranger in order to harvest organs and save five dying strangers. The pattern suggests that harm and benefit can be traded within the boundaries of a single life, but not pooled across separate lives — as if each person runs their own moral account that cannot be merged with anyone else’s. Before I read further into the philosophy of this, I want the full specification of what a theory that respects this “separateness of persons” must actually deliver. Write the desiderata: the specific cases it must classify correctly, including voluntary self-sacrifice, letting one die versus actively killing one, distributing risk across a group, and cases where one person’s past consent might affect present treatment; the distinctions it must preserve rather than collapse, especially between intrapersonal compensation and interpersonal aggregation; the findings from psychology or legal practice it should remain consistent with; and flag any pair of requirements that appear jointly unsatisfiable. Then, very briefly, indicate how a utilitarian aggregationist view, a rights-based view, and a contractualist view each stand against that checklist. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations — just the criteria themselves, because I want to know what I should be demanding of any answer before I accept it.
