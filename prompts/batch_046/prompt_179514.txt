I'm writing a dissertation chapter in political philosophy on whether moral guilt can be transmitted across generational boundaries within a nation-state, particularly in cases of institutional continuity coupled with complete demographic turnover. Rather than surveying existing answers, I want to establish the full specification—the desiderata—that any adequate theory of intergenerational collective guilt must satisfy before it can claim to solve the problem. The theory must first specify the ontological conditions under which a collective entity can persist as the same moral agent across generations, and it must preserve without collapse the distinctions between criminal liability, political responsibility, moral guilt, and what I will call structural complicity. It must also respect boundary conditions such as the legal principle of non-retroactivity and the phenomenological gap between inherited shame and inherited culpability; it must remain consistent with established results about how collective memory and institutional identity actually operate over time; and it must either generate or explicitly block normative implications—especially whether guilt entails reparative obligations, or whether such obligations can exist independently of any guilt attribution. For each requirement, please provide the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind it, flag which ones are genuinely non-negotiable and which are camp-specific preferences (for instance, of liberal individualists versus communitarians versus institutionalists), and identify whether any subset of desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable. I am especially interested in tensions like maintaining strict institutional continuity while denying collective moral agency, or preserving non-retroactivity while assigning present citizens genuine moral guilt rather than merely derivative responsibility; if a particular combination renders the concept of national guilt incoherent, that is the result I most want to see. Finally, briefly score how Jaspers' well-known fourfold typology and a hypothetical diachronic institutionalist view fare against this list—not with citations, but with attention to which requirements each sacrifices and why that sacrifice matters for the overall coherence of the account. A table is acceptable, but the weight should remain on the criteria themselves and on identifying the single requirement that does the most work in excluding popular theories.
