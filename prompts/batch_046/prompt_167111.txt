I chair a municipal historical-commission board that approves 150-word contextual annotations for monuments and curriculum inserts addressing figures who exercised significant power and caused serious harm—enslavement, colonial violence, suppression of dissent—while also producing widely recognized goods. The board includes historians, educators, and community representatives with no philosophical training; we do not remove monuments, we write the annotations that accompany them.

We have adopted a working principle: do not invoke the temporal-context defense—sometimes described as the man-of-his-time argument—unless it survives a structured test, because uncritical use flattens moral history while uncritical rejection ignores genuine differences in moral epistemology across eras. I need you to turn this principle into an operational, reusable decision procedure that my board can run in a 45-minute meeting for any proposed annotation, executed by staff without philosophical expertise and signed off by the board only at specified nodes.

The deliverable should be a sequential checklist in plain language: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the precise point where the procedure outputs a clear recommendation not to include the defense; the point where it outputs a clear recommendation to include it within specified limits; and the exact stages where the question is irreducibly one of values rather than facts and must be referred back to the board for a vote rather than resolved by staff.

Within the checklist, treat the following distinction as load-bearing. Separate (a) the moral beliefs that were genuinely dominant and largely unchallenged within the figure’s immediate epistemic community—call this the ambient moral consensus—from (b) the moral beliefs that were available to that community through documented dissent, analogical reasoning, or institutional practices in which the agent themselves participated, even if those beliefs were marginal—call this the accessible moral counter-position. The defense should be available only when the harmful conduct was consistent with the ambient consensus and the accessible counter-position was either genuinely absent or structurally blocked from reaching the agent. The procedure must also contain a separate, explicit test for whether the agent actively suppressed, ignored, or punished the counter-position once it was accessible to them.

Then apply the procedure step-by-step to three proposed annotations:

1. Thomas Jefferson—drafting language of universal equality while holding people in slavery, with abolitionist arguments documented in Virginia and known to be within his intellectual circle.
2. Winston Churchill—prioritizing imperial war logistics over famine relief in Bengal in 1943 and maintaining imperial governance in India, despite anti-imperial and humanitarian dissent present within British political, administrative, and parliamentary circles.
3. Christopher Columbus—instituting violent extraction and the encomienda system in the Caribbean, in a context where Spanish clerical critics articulated moral objections to indigenous treatment that reached the crown and were recorded in official proceedings.

For each case, run every checklist stage, state the intermediate findings, show where the procedure yields a staff-level recommendation versus where it triggers a board vote, and identify the final proposed wording for the annotation (including the defense, limited, or excluded). Include at least one case where the procedure produces a result that will be politically uncomfortable for part of the board—specifically, permitting a limited defense for a figure typically condemned and denying it for a figure often defended, or vice versa, cutting across predictable ideological lines.

Finally, specify explicitly what the procedure cannot do: the false positives and false negatives it is structurally prone to; the kinds of evidence that, if missing, should cause staff to halt rather than guess; and the category of historical figures or actions for which the procedure should not be used at all because its premises about epistemic community, accessibility, and structural blockage fail.
