In my bioethics seminar we are working through conditions for valid consent, and two classmates got stuck on a case involving a cognitively normal research participant who agreed to a neuropharmacology trial after receiving a disclosure form that was technically accurate but used dense jargon—referring to a risk of NMDA-receptor-mediated excitotoxic cascade rather than possible damage to brain cells—while omitting any plain-language summary. The participant had no scientific background but was globally competent and uncoerced. One classmate argued that the consent was invalid because competence must be domain-relative: if you cannot comprehend the risk at the appropriate descriptive level, you lack the specific competence required for that decision, regardless of general intelligence. The other replied that this standard is impossible—patients routinely consent to procedures they cannot anatomically understand—so competence should be treated as a binary, global capacity, and the fault lies with the researcher’s poor communication, not with the consent itself. The first then distinguished unavoidable complexity from what she called manipulative informational architecture: deliberately selecting descriptions that predictably exceed a layperson’s baseline literacy when plainer equivalents exist, which she said functions as a form of deception that vitiates consent by exploiting a predictable competence gap rather than by falsehood. The second conceded that such framing is ethically wrong but argued that consent should be analyzed diachronically: there is entry consent and ongoing consent, and because the participant retains full procedural capacity to withdraw upon later understanding, the initial agreement is defective yet remediable rather than void ab initio; treating it as fully invalid, he said, ignores the temporal structure of autonomy and collapses all imperfect disclosure into invalidation. Neither would concede ground. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: assess the claim about domain-relative competence, the reply invoking binary global competence, the counter-distinction involving informational architecture, and the final move about diachronic remediability. For each, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partially right, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then say what, if anything, both are missing—particularly regarding whether deception requires false belief or can operate through framing alone, and whether competence should be evaluated relative to the decision’s informational demands.
