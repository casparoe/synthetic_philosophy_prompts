Here is an argument I constructed for a graduate seminar on moral metaphysics and now find suspicious because its conclusion seems to license moral quietism. (1) Karma, interpreted rigorously as a theory of moral desert, asserts that an agent's present welfare-state is strictly proportional to the net moral quality of that agent's actions across successive lives. (2) If desert is strict proportionality, then the magnitude of present suffering or flourishing is an exact quantitative measure of past moral failure or virtue. (3) Now consider what I will call cross-temporal moral fungibility: the claim that moral desert is inter-temporally commensurable, so that a grave wrong committed in life L1 can be precisely canceled by virtue accumulated in life L2, yielding a neutral present state in L3. (4) Cross-temporal fungibility requires that one unit of present suffering corresponds to one unit of past wrongdoing, regardless of temporal distance or of any psychological continuity between the lives involved. (5) But in standard Abhidharma and Yogacara frameworks, the subject who suffers in L3 bears either no psychological continuity (momentary self) or only a highly attenuated causal-mental link (store-consciousness) to the agents who acted in L1 and L2. (6) Therefore strict proportionality applies to a present subject who stands in no psychologically meaningful relation to the past agents who earned the desert, except through what we might call a bare karmic thread — a metaphysical continuity devoid of memory, character, or first-person identification. (7) If psychological continuity is not necessary for desert to attach, then desert can attach to any entity linked by that bare thread. This includes not only individual rebirth-streams but collective entities — lineages, castes, nations — that share causal-historical connections to past collective actions. (8) Consequently, any present structural inequality, systemic deprivation, or collective harm can be justified as precisely deserved collective karma, and any intervention to remedy it constitutes an interference with the universe's exact moral accounting. (9) This proves too much: karma becomes not a nuanced theory of moral causation but an absolute justification for the status quo, including atrocities that appear intuitively undeserved. (10) So either karma is false, or strict proportionality must be abandoned, or psychological continuity must be required for desert. (11) But if psychological continuity is required, then without memory of past lives there is no basis for attributing desert to present persons, and karma collapses into practical irrelevance. (12) If strict proportionality is abandoned, karma ceases to be a theory of moral desert and becomes merely a loose tendency indistinguishable from general causal optimism. (13) If we accept the conclusion, we embrace moral quietism. The reasoning seems valid, yet I suspect something slips between steps (6) and (7): perhaps an equivocation between individual bare-causal links and collective inheritance, or a scope shift from "desert can attach" to "desert does attach collectively." I would prefer to keep (1) and (2), but I want to know which step to reject and what each rejection costs. Is there a hidden modal slide, or is the argument sound and the real choice among (11), (12), and (13)? Diagnose the flaw precisely, explain why the problematic step looked plausible, and price the alternatives.
