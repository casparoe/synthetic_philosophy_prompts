You are advising a culinary director who is designing a progressive tasting-menu experience around three chili preparations—call them Dish A, Dish B, and Dish C. In repeated paired blind tests, expert tasters consistently fail to distinguish Dish A from Dish B in heat level, and fail to distinguish Dish B from Dish C, yet they reliably and strongly distinguish Dish A from Dish C, judging the latter dramatically more intense. The director must present the dishes in a single linear sequence from least to most intense and assign each a discrete numerical score on a printed scale for guests. She is troubled by a pattern: if A is not more intense than B, and B is not more intense than C, then it seems A should not be less intense than C, but the direct paired comparison clearly shows it is. She asks whether she should enforce a linear ranking and numerical scores despite this tension, whether the relation 'more intense than' is unreliable across small incremental steps, or whether her tasting protocol is at fault. Take a definite position—should she preserve the ordering, reject the idea that intensity can be consistently ranked through such small gradations, or resolve the tension another way?—and defend it with concrete reasoning that addresses both the practical menu design and the underlying conceptual structure, rather than laying out alternatives neutrally and leaving her to choose.
