I'm working out whether my moral judgments fit a general principle or whether particularism is right, and I want a decisive answer rather than an overview of positions.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases:

- A hiring manager gives a job to a slightly less qualified candidate because she was genuinely moved by that candidate's specific, documented history of caring for a disabled sibling—something the formal criteria don't mention. I think this is acceptable, even commendable.
- The same manager gives the job to an equally qualified rival only because they share an obscure hobby. I think this is clearly unfair.
- A trial judge reduces a sentence because the defendant's explanation evokes a trauma the judge herself experienced decades ago. I think she should have recused herself; the judgment is tainted.
- A friend refuses to forgive a calculated, minor betrayal, but forgives a far more serious betrayal that occurred under extreme, idiosyncratic psychological pressure that only she could fully understand. I think this differential response makes sense.
- A physician bumps one patient ahead in triage because that patient resembles her deceased brother, despite no clinical difference. I think this violates her professional duty.
- A community coalition breaks its written agreement to shelter a single family during an emergency that the drafters could not have anticipated. I think breaking the pact is the right call.

My tentative principle is that particular reasons are legitimate only when they are "internally narrated"—accessible and meaningful within the specific relationship or institutional role—whereas arbitrary external resemblances are not. Does this save all six judgments? If not, which ones must I drop, and what is the best repaired principle—generalist or particularist?

Please apply your answer to this further case: a teacher awards a high grade to a weak essay because she recognizes, from private conversation, the student's hidden terror of failure.

Take a committed position: defend a specific principle that tells me exactly which of my verdicts are mistakes, or argue that particularism is correct and that any unifying principle is impossible. Give your reasons, and do not just list competing theories.
