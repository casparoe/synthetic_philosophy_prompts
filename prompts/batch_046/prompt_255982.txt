I'm working through a taxonomy of practical reasoning errors for a course I'm designing, and I've become stuck on whether there is a unified principle behind the sunk-cost fallacy that can accommodate all of my judgments, or whether I need to split the phenomenon into genuinely distinct categories. Here are my verdicts on six concrete cases.

First, if I purchase a non-refundable ticket to a concert and then learn of a better opportunity, I think it is plainly irrational to attend the concert solely because the money is already spent; the past expenditure should carry no weight in comparing the future experiences. Second, if a research team has spent five years pursuing a hypothesis that accumulating evidence has rendered highly implausible, I think it is irrational for them to continue allocating resources purely because those years lie behind them; further investment should depend only on expected future payoff. Third, if a government persists in constructing a bridge that serves no current transportation need, solely because billions have already been expended, that strikes me as a paradigmatic fallacy. But then I encounter cases where honoring past investment seems not only permissible but appropriate. Fourth, if a musician has practiced a technically demanding piece for years and now finds it no longer suits her artistic vision, I think she may rationally finish and perform it—not because the practice hours must be "recovered," but because abandoning it now would rupture a narrative of artistic development in which that labor was genuinely constitutive. Fifth, in a long marriage in which both partners have invested decades, I think it can be rational to work through a serious crisis partly out of fidelity to that shared history, provided the future prospects are not hopeless; here the sunk investment functions as a reason of loyalty rather than as an illegitimate weight on a cost-benefit scale. Sixth, and most puzzling, if a rescue operation has already cost several lives, I think there is genuine moral pressure to continue rather than withdraw, precisely so that those deaths are not rendered "for nothing," even when the objective risk-benefit calculus might favor stopping; this feels structurally like honoring sunk costs, yet calling it a fallacy strikes me as callous.

So I am torn between two ways of organizing these judgments. Is there one precise principle—perhaps that honoring sunk costs is irrational whenever it operates as an independent quantitative weight in a forward-looking calculus, but rational when it expresses fidelity to a narrative identity or to moral commitments made to others—that saves all six verdicts? Or does that distinction collapse upon inspection, with the "narrative fidelity" clause serving only as an ad hoc rescue? If the best principle must overturn one of my judgments, I would most want to preserve the conviction that the rescue case involves a genuine reason rather than a cognitive error, and I am prepared to concede that the musician case might be reinterpreted as masked cost-benefit reasoning after all. But I also want the principle tested against cases I have not listed: for instance, if a philosopher spends a decade developing a metaphysical system she now rejects, is she rationally obligated to publish and defend it for the sake of intellectual continuity, or is that precisely the fallacy in another guise? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which of my cases, deliver its verdicts on the musician, the marriage, and the philosopher examples, and be explicit about which of my six original judgments, if any, it cannot accommodate.
