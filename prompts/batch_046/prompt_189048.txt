Locke holds that education should aim at producing a self-governing adult rather than a merely learned one. Physical health is foundational: he recommends fresh air, plain diet, hardening the body, and avoiding excessive study that weakens the constitution. Morally, virtue is acquired through habituation and the example of the tutor rather than through precept and lecture alone. Intellectually, he distinguishes wit—the quickness and inventiveness of the mind—from judgment, the solid weighing of reasons; he warns that displaying wit is often encouraged at the expense of sound judgment.

On discipline, Locke rejects all corporal punishment as cruel and counterproductive. In terms of curriculum, he advises that formal study in Latin and mathematics should begin rigorously around age three or four, since the mind is most pliable then. Play is permitted, but only as a reward for completed lessons, not as an integral part of instruction.
