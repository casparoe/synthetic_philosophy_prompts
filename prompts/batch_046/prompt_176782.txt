Write a villanelle—nineteen lines arranged in five tercets and a concluding quatrain, with two refrains alternating according to the pattern abA1 abA2 abA1 abA2 abA1 abA2 abA1A2—about Avicenna’s flying man thought experiment. Rather than treating the argument solely as proof of an immaterial soul, frame it as an early form of phenomenological reduction: a deliberate bracketing of body, sensation, and world that leaves a bare self-certainty behind. Keep Avicenna’s own metaphysical conclusion—that awareness survives the total absence of corporeal and sensory conditions—in productive tension with this modern reading. Assign the refrains structural roles that enact this tension: let A1 voice the suspended self in its ungrounded certainty, and let A2 voice the absent world and body that continue to shadow the claim. The rhyme scheme’s circular returns should mirror the argument’s independence from external sensation, and the final quatrain should bring the refrains into conjunction without resolving whether what remains is a soul, a transcendental subject, or only the persistent echo of awareness itself. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in medieval philosophy or phenomenology recognizes the conceptual architecture, yet it should function first as poetry—no citations, no bibliography, and no verbatim quotation from historical sources.
