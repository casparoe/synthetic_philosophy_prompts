I've been working through a position on consent validity that treats deception, coercion, and incompetence not as independent failures but as symptoms of a missing deeper condition I'm calling "framework transparency." The claim is that consent is valid only when the agent possesses "structural literacy"—not merely knowledge of the act's immediate risks and benefits, but an accurate map of the normative architecture (institutional dependencies, hidden exit costs, cascading penalties) that structures the refusal option. Deception vitiates consent mainly when it obscures this architecture—say, hiding that declining a workplace wellness program will trigger cascading penalties through an embedded insurance structure—rather than merely misrepresenting the act itself. Coercion is present not only through explicit threats, but through arrangements that inflate exit costs well above the baseline interdependence that any social role already entails; competence, accordingly, includes the capacity to model how one's refusal ripples through these dependency networks. This seems to capture why some technically informed, uncoerced agreements still feel hollow, but I worry it collapses into familiar relational-autonomy theory with extra vocabulary, or proves too much by invalidating consent in ordinary bureaucratic contexts.
