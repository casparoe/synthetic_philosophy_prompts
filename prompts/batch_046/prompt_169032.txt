Consider the following argument, which I constructed for a seminar on consciousness and self-report and which now strikes me as suspicious because it appears to dissolve empirical consciousness research altogether. (1) Dennett's multiple drafts model rejects the Cartesian theater: there is no central screen at which consciousness occurs, only parallel, continuously revised drafts of experience, none of which enjoys intrinsic authority. (2) If there is no theater, then there is no single inner observer with privileged access to all mental contents; instead, verbal reports are produced by whichever draft happens to be accessed by the speech-production mechanism at the moment of reporting. (3) Now introduce what I will call draft-locking: the claim that once a verbal report is issued, the draft that produced it is temporarily stabilized, and subsequent reasoning treats that draft as the definitive record of the subject's experience, even though other concurrent drafts may have contained contradictory or absent contents. (4) If draft-locking holds, then a subject's sincere report reveals only the content of the locked draft, not the existence or absence of other concurrent drafts. (5) But empirical studies of consciousness—such as those testing awareness of masked or subliminal stimuli—depend on comparing first-person reports against objective measures; they presuppose that a subject can be mistaken about whether she experienced X, and that this error can in principle be detected. (6) Under draft-locking, however, a subject can never be wrong about the content of the locked draft, since that draft is the direct source of the report, and there is no independent access to other drafts against which the report could be checked for accuracy. (7) Therefore, either consciousness studies lack any valid method for detecting error in first-person reports, or draft-locking must be false. (8) If draft-locking is false, then reports are produced without any stabilizing mechanism, which implies that the same subject could issue contradictory reports about an identical stimulus with no principled criterion for determining which draft, if any, constituted her actual conscious experience. (9) So either empirical consciousness research is methodologically impossible, or the multiple drafts model collapses into an indeterminacy that makes the notion of a subject's actual experience incoherent. Where does this reasoning fail? If I must preserve some premises, I would prefer to keep (2) and (5) rather than (7) or (9), but I want to know precisely which step to reject and what that rejection costs for the coherence of the model.
