Here is the exam question I gave my grad seminar (it's a bit in the weeds, but that's teh point):

Question: Historians are routinely warned against presentism—imposing present-day moral and conceptual frameworks on the past. But is total escape even possible, or desirable? Write about 300 words addressing: (1) the distinction between chronological presentism (using present categories to describe past events) and normative presentism (judging past actors by present ethical standards); (2) whether the historian's unavoidable situatedness necessarily undermines objectivity, especially in light of the tension between epistemic humility and value-neutrality; (3) apply this to 19th-century medical experiments conducted without informed consent—how should we evaluate them without falling into either extreme?

Below is one student's answer. I need you to grade it—give it a letter grade or points out of 10, and provide specific point-by-point comments that justify the score. Don't signal your own view of the correct answer; just assess this particular response. Watch for subtle mistakes: misattributed positions, steps that don't actually follow, or distinctions that get collapsed.

Student answer:

Presentism happens when historians let modern ideas distort the past. There are two forms: chronological presentism, where we use current concepts like human rights or scientific method to talk about earlier periods, and normative presentism, where we condemn past behavior using today's ethics. These are really the same underlying problem, because if you're already describing the past with modern language you've smuggled in modern values. So chronological presentism always leads to normative presentism.

Because historians live in their own time, they can never be fully neutral. Butterfield's classic study warned that presentism makes us see history as just a progress story leading to us. The only fix is value-neutrality: historians should suspend all moral judgment and stick to bare facts. Epistemic humility is the opposite—it means admitting we can't be neutral, so we should embrace our bias. So objectivity is impossible if we take humility seriously, and the best we can do is be honest about our present-mindedness.

Consider 19th-century experiments on patients who never consented. We have to call them experiments and patients, which are modern scientific categories, so we're being chronologically presentist right away. That means we're also being normatively presentist when we call them unethical. To avoid this, historians should just describe what happened—who did what, with what results—without saying it was wrong. After all, saying it's wrong requires modern ethics, and we've already shown modern ethics is unavoidable. That's what epistemic humility demands: don't pretend you can judge from outside your own era.
