Here's a trap I think my grad student fell into but I can't see the exit. (1) Maimonides in the Guide says animal sacrifice wasn't commanded because it's good in itself, but as a divine concesion - like a doctor giving a sick patient the food he craves just to keep him alive, slowly weaning him off poison. (2) If a command is purely a concession to human weakness, then once that weakness is cured the command should drop away; otherwise you're treating a healthy person as sick. (3) Maimonides also insists the sacrificial laws are eternal parts of the Torah and can never be abolished, even in the messanic era. (4) So he seems committed to saying God forever mandates a practice that, for a spiritually mature people, is either useless or harmful - like forcing a cured patient to keep drinking medicine. (5) But a perfectly rational God wouldn't do that. (6) So either (1) is false and sacrifice has intrinsic value, or (3) is false and the law can change. (7) He clearly wants to keep (3). So (1) must go, and sacrifice is genuinely good. (8) But wait - if (1) is false, then every other 'concession' Maimonides points out (war laws, slavery rules, dietary codes as health measures) also stops being a concession and becomes intrinsically good, which destroys his whole progressive-historical reading of Torah. So rejecting (1) costs him his entire interpretive framework. Is there a hidden option? Maybe I'm smuggling in the idea that a concession can't be permanent. Where exactly does the slip happen? I'd rather keep (1) and (3) if I can.
