I'm currently working through a unit on moral responsibility in my philosophy of action seminar, and I'm finding the standard Frankfurt-style objections to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities somewhat unsatisfying because they seem to rely on an overly external picture of intervention. I keep thinking about cases where the mechanism that would block an alternative is not an external neuroscientist but an internal, second-order deliberative module that the agent herself cultivated—something like a rigid commitment architecture that she can no longer suspend without undermining her own practical identity. This strikes me as a kind of "nested" Frankfurt case, where the boundary between controller and controlled collapses, but I'm not sure whether defenders of PAP should treat it differently from classic cases, since the blocker is both inside the agent and still functionally coercive.

Could you write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them Mara and Theo—who are trying to work out whether this nested, internal-monitoring version of a Frankfurt case actually undermines the Principle of Alternative Possibilities or whether it instead reveals a hidden ambiguity in what "could have done otherwise" means. Mara should defend the view that PAP survives if we distinguish causal openness (the absence of an external blocker) from modal openness (the genuine metaphysical availability of an alternative path), and she should argue that internalized blockers preserve modal openness in a way external ones do not, because the agent remains the source of the constraint. Theo should push back by arguing that once the blocker is internalized and functions as part of the agent's own reasoning architecture, the distinction between causal and modal openness collapses—if she cannot access the alternative without a kind of practical self-destruction, then in the relevant moral sense she lacks it. They should engage with each other's specific points rather than talk past one another, and the dialogue should include a concrete, invented scenario—perhaps a musician who has installed an irreversible cognitive commitment to never abandon a performance mid-piece, and who completes a difficult concert not because she is externally compelled but because her own second-order system makes deviation literally unthinkable—to test whether the presence or absence of an "outside" intervener makes any moral difference. The tone should be philosophical but conversational, about 800 words, and neither character should be presented as obviously wrong.
