For my graduate seminar on early modern natural philosophy, I am working through Galileo's letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (c. 1615). In the central hermeneutic section, Galileo argues that scripture's purpose is soteriological rather than cosmological: it teaches us how to reach salvation, not the physical mechanisms of the celestial spheres. He then claims that when a demonstrative conclusion about nature conflicts with a literal scriptural reading, the literal reading must be adjusted, because the Holy Spirit accommodated its language to the understanding of common people. I want to present this not as a simple separation of domains, but through a more specific interpretive frame: Galileo treats accommodation not merely as a rhetorical or pedagogical concession, but as an epistemic constraint that prevents scripture from being read as authoritatively asserting physical mechanisms that are accessible to demonstrative reason.

Here is the relevant passage reconstructed from the letter, paraphrased without direct quotation: Galileo notes that the Bible speaks in a manner suited to unlearned people, using language that describes the sun moving and the earth standing still; yet if demonstrative proof establishes the opposite arrangement, one must not condemn the proof on scriptural grounds, since the text was not intended to instruct us in astronomy. He then asks whether it is not more pious to allow that scripture does not speak to these matters at all, rather than to force an interpretation that contradicts demonstrated truth.

My difficulty is parsing the logical architecture of this move. Does Galileo's argument depend on the actual possession of a demonstrative proof—something he believed he held for heliocentrism but which was not yet widely granted—or does it establish a permanent methodological priority for any genuinely demonstrative science over literal exegesis? In other words, is he advancing a universal hermeneutic principle about scientific authority, or is he making a tactical, context-bound defense of Copernicanism?

Please help me understand the structure of the argument in this passage, particularly whether the epistemic-constraint reading I have sketched is supported by the text or whether it imports a modern philosophy-of-science distinction that Galileo would not have recognized. In your response, clearly separate what is well established in Galileo scholarship from what remains genuinely contested—especially regarding the universality of his hermeneutic rule versus its tactical application—and do not present a disputed interpretive view as if it were the consensus position.
