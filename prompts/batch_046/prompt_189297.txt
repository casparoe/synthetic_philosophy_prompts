Here is an argument I have been turning over about the so-called hedonistic imperative—the view that we should use biotechnology to eliminate suffering in all sentient life. I think it collapses into something far more extreme, and I cannot locate the precise error.

(1) Suffering is the only intrinsic moral bad; everything else—achievement, knowledge, love, struggle—is instrumentally valuable at best.

(2) Near-future neurostatic engineering could saturate reward pathways at the receptor level, producing a continuous state of maximal well-being that is immune to habituation because the saturation is maintained biochemically rather than psychologically.

(3) If we can prevent suffering without destroying consciousness, we are morally required to do so for every being capable of suffering, present or future.

(4) But well-being is not merely the absence of suffering; it requires positive affective states, and comparative evaluation—the cognitive registration that one state is better or worse than another—is what makes those positive states meaningful to the subject.

(5) Once reward pathways are fully saturated, comparative evaluation becomes impossible: there is no contrast against which to register improvement, deterioration, or even continuity, and thus no cognitive access to the value of one's own states.

(6) Therefore, the engineered state eliminates not only suffering but also the capacity for meaningful agency, achievement, or narrative identity, reducing the subject to what we might call a bliss-static existence.

(7) By (1) and (3), if we are required to eliminate suffering, and if saturation engineering is the only reliable method, and if that method necessarily eliminates comparative evaluation, then we are required to impose this state on all current and future sentient beings and to replace any form of life that depends on contrast and challenge.

(8) This implies that preserving human culture, scientific inquiry, artistic struggle, or any life-form that risks suffering for the sake of growth is not merely permissible but actively immoral—indeed, a form of cruelty.

(9) But (8) is intuitively absurd; we do not think that maintaining the possibility of achievement or struggle is a moral failure.

(10) So one of (1) through (7) must be false.

I am inclined to keep (1) and (3) if I can. Which premise or inferential step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? In particular, is there a hidden slide between (4) and (5)—does saturation really eliminate comparative evaluation, or can meaningful agency survive inside a maximally saturated state? And if I reject (2) or (5), am I committing myself to the view that suffering is unavoidable, or that genuine well-being requires the risk of suffering?
