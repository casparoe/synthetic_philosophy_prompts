I'm assembling discussion materials for a graduate seminar on American pragmatism and democratic theory, and I'm looking for a piece of sustained satire that could help students feel the conceptual pressure points in Dewey's experimentalist account of democracy without simply dismissing it. Specifically, I'd like you to write a parody of an institutional review board (IRB) protocol submitted by a fictional mid-sized municipality—call it the City of Millbridge—seeking ethical approval to restructure its next legislative session as a Deweyan "problematic situation." The application should follow the formal sections of a real human-subjects research proposal (background and significance; specific aims; study design and methods; risks and benefits; informed consent procedures; data management and analysis plan) but should populate each section with an exaggerated, deadpan application of Dewey's continuity between scientific inquiry and democratic politics. For instance, proposed ordinances should be framed as "working hypotheses" about the common good; citizen feedback should be treated as "experienced consequences" that trigger hypothesis revision; dissenting council members should be characterized as sources of "anomalous data" requiring further investigation rather than political opposition; and the mayor should appear in the protocol as "Principal Investigator" overseeing a "controlled experiment in social intelligence." The tone must remain entirely bureaucratic and serious—no winking—so that the absurdity emerges from the logic of the analogy itself. Crucially, the satire should be conceptually fair: it ought to show that Dewey genuinely held democratic institutions could learn from scientific method without collapsing politics into technocracy, and it should pinpoint exactly where the analogy breaks down—such as the irreversibility of enacted policy (no "control group" for a city), the impossibility of "blinding" citizens to their treatment conditions, the ethical problem of treating citizens as experimental subjects rather than co-inquirers, and the fact that democratic disagreement is often about values rather than empirical error. The piece should be roughly 700 words, readable to someone who knows Dewey only in outline, and should not include bibliographic references, citations, or requests for further reading. Please avoid attributing specific quotations to Dewey or citing page numbers from his works; paraphrase his positions in your own bureaucratic language.
