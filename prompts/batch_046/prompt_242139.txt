I'm writing a dissertation chapter on self-binding mechanisms in democratic theory. Compare Jon Elster's concept of Ulysses contracts—individual rational agents binding their future selves against anticipated preference reversals—with his parallel account of constitutional precommitment, in which a democratic polity binds future majorities through entrenched provisions. Specifically, I want to assess whether a constitution genuinely functions as a collective Ulysses contract, or whether the shift from singular to plural agency fractures the logic of precommitment. To stress-test the analogy, apply both frameworks to intergenerational constitutional clauses: provisions such as environmental or fiscal entrenchments that constrain not only future voters but citizens not yet born. Does the Ulysses-contract model offer any coherent justification for binding agents who do not yet exist, or must constitutional precommitment rest on an altogether different normative foundation—perhaps deliberative legitimacy rather than intertemporal preference consistency? Argue which concept yields the more coherent account of democratic self-binding, and identify the precise point at which the individual-to-collective analogy collapses.
