I'm preparing a long-form feature on whether empirical psychology has made normative ethics obsolete, and during an interview a moral psychologist sketched an argument that seems to collapse not merely virtue ethics but every normative framework that presupposes psychological stability. Before I file the piece, I want to test whether there is an escape route I have missed. Here is the reconstructed argument. (1) Virtue ethics defines a virtue—moral courage, for instance—not as occasional good behavior but as a stable disposition that should manifest across the full range of situations belonging to its domain: physical danger, social ostracism, institutional retaliation, and moral temptation. The psychologist described this not as a rigid global trait but as what she called an ecologically dense response cluster, a pattern that holds within the relevant moral ecology rather than across every arbitrary context. (2) Situationist studies—particularly well-replicated work comparing compassion in emergency versus bureaucratic settings—show that cross-domain behavioral correlations for these virtues are weak, often near r ≈ .20, suggesting that people lack the stability virtue ethics requires. (3) So either virtues do not exist, or virtue ethics mischaracterizes them as portable across domains when they are at best local fragments. (4) If virtues are only local—if one can be courageous in physical risk but not in institutional pressure—then moral education, praise, and blame lose coherence, because we ordinarily treat courage as an evaluation of the agent, not a context-bound label. (5) But the deeper problem is modal: if situationism demonstrates that for any agent and any virtue-domain there exists some situation in which that agent will fail, then possessing the virtue is psychologically impossible, not merely uncommon. (6) Since obligation presupposes ability, virtue ethics issues commands that no human can satisfy, rendering it normatively void. (7) The same reasoning extends to any ethics relying on stable agent-level properties—Kantian autonomy requires a will that remains rational across contexts; long-term consequentialist planning requires stable preference structures; and ordinary moral responsibility assumes continuity of character. (8) Therefore situationism does not merely undermine virtue ethics; it demonstrates that normative ethics as such demands psychological impossibilities. (9) Yet we clearly do not inhabit a world without norms—education, judgment, and deliberation persist—so either the empirical premise in (2) is misinterpreted, or the argument contains a hidden slide. I sense the flaw involves modality, measurement, or what some call trait-state asymmetry—the idea that a disposition can be genuinely present yet masked by overwhelming situational pressure—but I cannot locate the exact joint. Which step should I reject? What makes that step seem plausible, and what does abandoning it cost? I am especially reluctant to deny the empirical robustness of the situationist findings or to accept that moral norms are impossible.
