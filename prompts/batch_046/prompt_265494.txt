I recently moved with my family to a country where the governing party has replaced independent judges, consolidated media ownership, and altered electoral rules, yet multi-party elections still occur and opposition figures continue to hold parliamentary seats. Reading comparative analyses of Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States has left me unsure whether I am witnessing democratic backsliding or only an intense partisan realignment that appears like decay to those on the losing side. Write an essay that asks whether the concept of democratic backsliding can be defended as analytically meaningful rather than as a partisan label, specifically by defining it through the erosion of what I will call procedural reciprocity—the institutional expectation that political opponents retain standing to contest rules—rather than through executive aggrandizement alone or through any particular substantive policy outcome. Weigh the case for this procedural definition against the objection that democracy is ultimately about substantive popular control and that procedural metrics merely encode liberal biases; consider whether the distinction avoids weaponization in the cases of Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the contemporary US; and reach a justified conclusion about which framing best captures genuine institutional decay without collapsing into advocacy for one camp. I am not seeking a bibliography or citations—the reasoning itself must stand alone—because I am trying to form my own judgment about the political environment in which I now live.
