I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or decision theory, though I have read enough papers to recognize when a concept is being used imprecisely. I work on the backend of a clinical decision-support system, and during a recent architecture review my colleagues and I realized that our pipeline implicitly encodes a principle I now understand as Good's theorem — that gathering additional information is always rational when the information is free — despite the fact that in our domain information is never free: each biomarker assay consumes limited therapeutic time, introduces false-positive anxiety that alters patient compliance, and must complete within a hard latency window. I need a reusable decision procedure, derived from the logic of Good's theorem but corrected for non-free, time-bounded, and psychologically costly sampling, that my engineering team could run at this gate without consulting a philosopher or a clinician at every step. The procedure should specify the ordered questions (does the current belief distribution cross the treatment-action threshold? what is the expected value of an additional sample given the prior? does the aggregate cost — temporal, psychological, and clinical — fall below that value?), define what counts as a sufficient quantitative or qualitative answer at each stage, and clearly demarcate where it terminates with 'proceed with current information' versus where it must escalate to a human clinician because the remaining trade-off is normative rather than computable. Please apply the procedure to two concrete cases: first, a borderline troponin result with a forty-five-minute assay turnaround and a narrowing therapeutic window, where the procedure may recommend proceeding without the second test despite engineering intuition that more data is safer; second, a chronic-monitoring protocol where weekly sampling is technically cheap but accumulates patient anxiety and decision fatigue over months, and where the procedure must weigh cumulative burden against diminishing informational returns. Finally, be explicit about which steps are genuine engineering logic and which are judgment calls dressed up as rules, and state clearly what the procedure cannot do — for instance, whether it can resolve conflicts between a patient's explicit preference for certainty and the system's recommendation to act on ambiguous data.
