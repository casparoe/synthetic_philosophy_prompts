Hey — I'm a phil prof who sits on our hospital network's ethics board, and I'm tired of giving residents the same old killing-vs-letting-die lecture that just recycles the trolley problem. I need a reusable decision procedue — like a flowchart in words — that a clinician can actually run in about fifteen minutes when they're reviewing whether to stop, withhold, or disable a life-sustaining intervention. Ground it in the actual philosophy of action and moral responsibility — the doing/allowing distinction, maybe double effect — but translate it into ordered steps: first the physical-causal description (what stops, what moves, what's already running), then the agent's intention and foresight, then their institutional role. At each step say what counts as passing, where it stops with 'this is allowing, not killing,' and where it has to punt to a values call because philosophy doesn't settle the fact. Don't give me citations or a reading list — I want something they can use without searching for Aquinas.

Here's the twist I want baked in: treat 'directed omission' as a seperate category. That's when you don't physically touch the patient but actively disable a sustaining system via a remote command — flipping a software switch to stop an automated ventilator protocol or insulin loop. The standard literature collapses this with ordinary withholding, but I think the agent's direct control over the sustaining mechanism makes the moral structure closer to doing, and the procedue should split them out explicitly rather than smoothing them together.

Run it on three cases:
1. A nurse physically disconnects a ventilator after family consent.
2. A resident declines intubation because a triage algorithm assigns the patient low priority.
3. An administrator remotely disables an automated life-support loop for a palliative patient via system command.

Make sure one comes out counterintuitive — the flowchart classifies it one way, but the moral feel pulls hard the other direction. Flag exactly where the breakdown happens, and be honest about which boxes are just judgment calls dressed up as steps. Wrap up with one sentence on its own line that starts with 'Summary:' and says what this procedue can and can't do.
