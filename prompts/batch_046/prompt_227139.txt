Please take on the role of a senior political philosopher specializing in democratic ethics and the philosophy of collective action. Before drafting the dialogue, spend a few lines brainstorming the strongest, most charitable formulation of both the harm-reduction duty view and the expressive-refusal view in contexts of systemic corruption; make sure neither position is reduced to a caricature.

I have recently been appointed to a citizens’ advisory board for a mid-sized municipality where both leading candidates in the upcoming mayoral race were signatories to the same controversial zoning agreement—an agreement that has since been revealed to involve substantial, undisclosed conflicts of interest. The only distinction is that Candidate A accepted smaller contributions from the development consortium and has proposed modest transparency reforms, while Candidate B took larger contributions and has defended the agreement outright. Several board members are arguing that residents have a clear moral duty to vote for Candidate A as the “lesser evil,” since abstaining or voting for an independent candidate with no chance of winning will likely allow the greater harm to prevail. Others are arguing that casting any vote for either candidate is an expressive act of legitimation: it signals to future political entrepreneurs that corruption of this magnitude still falls within the acceptable range, provided one offers a token reform.

Write a dialogue—approximately 800 to 1,000 words—between two equally competent interlocutors: Dr. Amara Okonkwo, who defends the harm-reduction duty view, and Julian Reyes, who defends the expressive-refusal view. They should not talk past each other. Each must respond directly to the other’s actual points, including Okonkwo’s charge that Reyes neglects the real suffering caused by allowing the worse outcome, and Reyes’s charge that Okonkwo neglects the long-term symbolic damage done by normalizing corruption.

To prevent the discussion from collapsing into generic debate, please inject and rigorously work with the following conceptual distinction I have been developing for the board: the “threshold of symbolic contamination.” This denotes the hypothetical point at which a vote for a partially complicit candidate stops functioning as a harm-reduction measure and instead operates as a structural endorsement that lowers the institutional cost of future corruption. Okonkwo should argue that this threshold is either unrealistically high or impossible to identify with the precision required to justify abstention in a concrete election, and that treating it as decisive amounts to a kind of moral luxury available only to those insulated from the worst outcomes. Reyes should argue that the threshold is not only real but has already been crossed in this specific case, because the zoning agreement involves a breach of public trust so fundamental that any vote within the offered binary legitimizes the underlying framework rather than merely selecting within it. They should debate whether the threshold is determined by the voter’s subjective intentions, by the objective institutional effects of the vote tally, or by some intersubjective standard among the electorate.

Please conclude the dialogue without a false compromise. Instead, each interlocutor should state precisely the empirical or normative condition that would cause them to revise their position: for Okonkwo, what evidence about the long-term systemic effects of “lesser evil” voting would change her mind; for Reyes, what feature of Candidate A’s proposal or of the electoral system itself would make expressive refusal unnecessary or counterproductive.

Use formal, precise language appropriate for a philosophical exchange, but keep it accessible to an educated non-specialist reader on the board. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for further research. Do not quote any specific published text verbatim.
