I've been noticing a pattern in how people defend inductive categories when they eventually break, and I'm not sure if it has a proper name or if it's just a mix of things I already know. You observe a bunch of items that share some visible feature — say, a specific polymer formulation that never cracks under load in testing — and you form a general claim: all batches of this formulation are crack-resistant. Then years later one batch cracks, not because the load changed, but because it was slowly accumulating internal damage that just hadn't reached visability yet. Instead of treating this as a failed induction, people often split the original category: the ones that didn't crack stay "true formulation X," and the cracked one gets reclassifed as "latent-defective formulation X" or "not genuinely X because of hidden contamination." The original rule survives untouched because the anomoly is kicked out of the class after the fact.

I see the same shape elsewhere — like with long-term nutrition studies where a food is "cardioprotective" until decades of data show harm in a subgroup, at which point the harmful cases are explained away as "not the pure form" due to some undetected processing variable. It feels in the neighborhood of Goodman's grue problem, since there's a hidden temporal parameter in the property being projected, but grue is usually about predicting the color of emeralds after a cutoff date. Here the issue isn't future prediction so much as retrospectivly protecting an inductive taxonomy by using the failure itself to redefine membership. Is there an established concept for this exact move? How does it differ from adding an ordinary auxiliary hypothesis, or from the "no true Scotsman" fallacy? And if it doesn't have its own name, what should we call it, and what are its boundary cases — especially since the reclassification can look perfectly reasonable rather than like a pure dodge?
