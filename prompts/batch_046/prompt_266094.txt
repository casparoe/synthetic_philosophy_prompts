Following the recent death of my father, I have been forced to work out whether divine providence can remain coherent with the genuine contingency of human suffering, and I have arrived at a restricted Molinist position I call salient-choice middle knowledge. Next Thursday I present this at our philosophy-of-religion seminar, defending the claim that God knows counterfactuals of creaturely freedom only for morally and soteriologically salient choices—not for every micro-level decision—because those salient counterfactuals are grounded in the agent's stable dispositional character rather than in a complete possible-worlds ontology. This sparse view, I argue, answers the standard grounding objection without collapsing into Open Theism and without requiring the full modal machinery that analytic metaphysicians distrust. The audience is specific: two metaphysicians who treat Molinism as an ad hoc modal inflation; a process theologian committed to dipolar theism who will hear deterministic undertones; a biblical-studies scholar concerned with whether this makes God complicit in the specific evils narrated in texts of lament; and a logic graduate student specializing in modal logic who will test formal coherence of a partial counterfactual domain. I need a speaker's Q&A prep sheet for this audience, not a generic defense of Molinism. Order the objections by likely damage to this precise thesis, phrase each in the vocabulary and commitments of the interlocutor who will raise it, provide a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes with an explicit note on where that reply is soft, state clearly what I should concede at the outset, and identify the single question I am hoping nobody asks—namely, how the boundary between salient and non-salient choices is drawn when moral trauma blurs an agent's dispositional character. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists; I will have no web access during the session. Keep the analysis within philosophical theology, modal metaphysics, and the logic of counterfactuals.
