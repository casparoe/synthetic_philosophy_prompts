I used to spend fourty years tracking bird migration patterns and now in retirement I'm trying to wrap my head around philosophy, which probably explains why I keep seeing measurement issues everywhere. I've been reading about virtue ethics and the situationist critique — Doris, Harman, and responses like Sreenivasan's — and it feels like nobody has actually moved since the early 2000s. The situationists point to experiments where context swamps personality, like that study with seminary students rushing past someone who needed help, and say global charachter traits don't exist. Virtue ethicists reply that those experiments miss practical wisdom, or that traits only show up under the right conditions, or that statistical consistency across contexts is the wrong standard to begin with. But everyone just repeats the same moves.

So my hunch is that maybe this stall isn't about ethics at all but about hidden assumptions: one side is using 'trait' like a psychologist measuring a cross-situational average, and the other is using it like an ecologist describing a dispostion that only activates in specific environmental conditions. Is this a substantive disagreement about what people are actually like, a verbal disagreement about the word 'charachter', or are they just answering different questions — one about prediction, one about normative evaluation — under the same heading? I'd like a real diagnosis that uses what each side actually says, not a survey, and I want to know if there's any study, formal distinction, or conceptual move that could actually break the stallmate, or if I should just accept that philosophers will keep circling this forever.
