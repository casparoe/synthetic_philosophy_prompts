Consider the following paraphrase of an argument from Leibniz's Theodicy. Leibniz holds that God chooses the actual world from an infinite set of possibles by selecting the one with the greatest overall perfection. He concedes that this world includes both moral evil and physical suffering, but denies that these are merely tolerated defects. Instead, they are necessary conditions for greater goods—such as the manifestation of grace, the exercise of freedom, or the emergence of order from disorder—that could not arise without them. He further insists that the perfection of a world is not calculated by minimizing suffering alone, but by balancing two criteria: the simplicity of the laws that govern it, and the richness or variety of the phenomena those laws produce. A world with less suffering might require so many exceptional interventions or convoluted rules that its overall harmony would actually be lower.

I am having trouble seeing exactly how these two criteria—simplicity and richness—are supposed to combine into a single verdict. Does Leibniz treat them as commensurable values that can be summed into one scalar measure of perfection, or does he leave them as independent dimensions that might yield incomparable worlds? More specifically, imagine a counterfactual world with marginally more complex laws but with the complete elimination of one specific category of suffering. Would Leibniz's framework determinately rank the actual world above this alternative, or does the comparison depend on an unstated weighting between law-simplicity and phenomenal variety? And if every monad perceives the entire cosmos with perfect clarity from its own viewpoint, can a locally uncompensated suffering within one monad's perspective still be fully reconciled with global harmony, or must every particular evil be matched by a specific good visible within that same perspective?
