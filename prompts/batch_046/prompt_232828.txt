I am a final-year undergraduate reading Philosophy and currently on placement with my university's residential conduct office, where I must draft preliminary assessment reports on disruptive behaviour in halls of residence. These reports determine whether a case is routed to welfare services, facilities adjustment, or disciplinary proceedings; I face roughly one decision every two days, each report must be completed within thirty minutes, and I have no clinical training, yet my recommendation is signed off by a senior officer who may not share my theoretical background. I want a reusable decision procedure — an ordered sequence of questions with explicit pass and fail conditions — that applies the framework of real patterns and the three explanatory stances (physical, design, and intentional) to these reports. The procedure must help me decide which stance should dominate the explanation of a student's behaviour, and therefore which institutional response is appropriate, without allowing me to pretend I have made a medical diagnosis or a moral verdict I am not qualified to deliver.

Please construct the procedure with the following stages: first, an initial neutral-description stage that strips away stance-laden vocabulary; second, a predictive-divergence test that asks whether the physical, design, and intentional stances generate different forecasts about the student's very next actions; third, a reversibility check that flags when evidence gathered under one stance is being used circularly to justify adoption of another; fourth, a compound-stance clause for cases where the design stance explains a functional failure but residual intentionality directs that failure at a specific target, requiring a layered rather than pure classification; and fifth, explicit stop-points where the procedure hands the decision to a qualified professional because the question is clinical or moral rather than explanatory. For each stage, provide a small set of literal test sentences or questions I can apply in the report, and mark every point where judgement cannot be eliminated and an editor or senior officer must decide.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a student who repeatedly triggers fire alarms at 03:00 despite prior warnings and sanctions; (b) a student whose as-yet-undiagnosed sleep disorder produces loud, destructive episodes that damage shared property; and (c) a student found altering kitchen appliances in ways that cause failures specifically affecting one flatmate — a case where the procedure should yield an intentional-stance disciplinary recommendation that I, being inclined to favour structural explanations, will find unwelcome. In each application, show exactly where the procedure stops, what it outputs, and where it risks misfiring.

Finally, state clearly what this procedure cannot do — for instance, whether it can ever establish blameworthiness, replace clinical assessment, or settle disputes about institutional responsibility — and identify the step in your reasoning that is most exposed if the claim that intentional explanation tracks an irreducible real pattern turns out to be mistaken. Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration — perhaps about the reducibility of intentional explanation to design-level function, or about the ethics of applying predictive stances in punitive institutional contexts — would lead you to reject or substantially revise the procedure, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
