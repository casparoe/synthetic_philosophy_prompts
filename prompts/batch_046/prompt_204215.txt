As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of politics and culture, I find myself stuck on a conceptual puzzle regarding American exceptionalism. The term is typically deployed across three distinct registers: as an empirical historical thesis about the unique constitutional and geographic conditions of the United States, as a normative claim that these conditions generate distinctive moral obligations or privileges in international affairs, and as an ideology that structures collective identity and political imagination. My background in science makes me particularly attentive to how theoretical frameworks can become self-sealing, and I am struggling to understand whether these three registers are conceptually separable or whether they collapse into one another in ways that undermine rational assessment.

More specifically, if exceptionalism functions as an ideology in the strong sense of a pervasive, identity-constituting interpretive framework, it seems capable of operating as a hermeneutic filter: confirming historical episodes are foregrounded as expressions of essential character, while countervailing evidence is treated as aberration or external contamination. This raises a conceptual difficulty. For the empirical thesis to support the normative claim—say, that the United States bears special duties or enjoys special exemptions—the thesis must remain genuinely contestable; otherwise the normative position reduces to a circular justification of group preference. Yet if the ideological dimension protects the historical thesis from refutation by selectively defining what counts as relevant data, then the normative claim appears to rest on an unfalsifiable foundation. Could you explain the precise conceptual architecture linking these three dimensions? In particular, is there a coherent distinction—perhaps between the scope conditions of a descriptive generalization and the evaluative commitments of an ideology—that allows one to maintain exceptionalism as a meaningful normative stance without rendering its empirical basis tautologically protected? I am less interested in historical examples than in clarifying whether the tension is merely apparent and resolvable through conceptual hygiene, or whether it is irreducible and must be managed as a permanent feature of the concept itself.
