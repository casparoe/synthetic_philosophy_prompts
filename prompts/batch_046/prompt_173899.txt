I'm writing my dissertation on temporal ontology and its application to the metaphysics of mind, and at our department's reading group last week two colleagues—let's call them M and K—became stuck in a disagreement about whether endurantism or perdurantism can accommodate what I'm calling the diachronic unity of phenomenal consciousness. M, defending endurantism, argued that only a subject wholly present at each moment can explain the first-personal fact that my past headache belongs to me in the same direct way as my present one; if I were merely a four-dimensional worm composed of momentary slices, no single slice would have the authority to claim ownership of experiences occurring in other slices, which undermines the phenomenological datum that consciousness is unified across time rather than distributed among independent temporal stages. K, a perdurantist, countered that this conflates metaphysical presence with phenomenological ownership, and that endurantism actually faces a more serious problem with temporary intrinsic properties: if the same enduring subject is wholly present both when I'm awake and when I'm unconscious, then that subject literally bears contradictory properties, which forces the endurantist either to relativize properties to times—thereby compromising the alleged simplicity of the enduring object—or to deny that consciousness and unconsciousness are genuinely temporary intrinsics. M replied that temporal indexing is no more ad hoc than spatial indexing and doesn't compromise simplicity, though he then conceded that the analogy might fail because temporal parts are causally and phenomenologically related in ways spatial parts are not. K then pressed a more specific objection involving gradual neural replacement: if an enduring subject is not identical to its material constituents but to its functional organization, then in cases where a brain is replaced neuron-by-neuron with synthetic equivalents, the subject either remains mysteriously identical despite total material turnover or ceases to exist and is replaced—both outcomes seem implausible—whereas perdurantism handles this naturally because different temporal stages can have different material compositions without any single stage undergoing mysterious change. M responded that this misunderstands endurantism, since persistence tracks form or functional organization rather than matter, and that perdurantism actually struggles more because it must explain what binds temporal parts into one four-dimensional worm rather than treating them as independent objects—an explanation that, M claimed, smuggles in precisely the primitive diachronic unity that endurantists accept openly. They then asked whether a stage-theoretic view, treating consciousness as a series of related but non-identical momentary subjects, might dissolve the dispute altogether, and they asked me to referee. Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move—rating the strength of M's spatial-analogy reply, K's neural-replacement argument, M's counter about functional organization, and the appeal to stage theory—indicating who is right about each specific point, what both are missing about the relationship between temporal ontology and phenomenal unity, and whether any part of their dispute is genuinely about words rather than substance?
