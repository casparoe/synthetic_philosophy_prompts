I'm putting together material for a graduate seminar on escalation dynamics and sunk-cost commitment, and a colleague suggested that the Anglo-French Concorde supersonic transport program—running from the 1962 development treaty through the British withdrawal crisis of 1971 to the 1976 entry into service—might serve as a real-world analog to Martin Shubik's dollar auction. Rather than treating it as a generic case of "throwing good money after bad," I want a disciplined structural analysis. Please treat the British and French governments, together with their industrial consortia (the British Aircraft Corporation and Sud Aviation / Aérospatiale), as the bidders; the accumulated development outlays, which exceeded initial estimates by orders of magnitude, as the bids; and the operational fleet, technological prestige, and strategic industrial capability as the contested prize. Lay out the actual decision nodes—particularly the 1971 moment when Britain sought to withdraw but faced treaty penalties and shared sunk costs, and the 1976 decision to continue operating despite predictable commercial losses—specifying for each player what the options, expected payoffs, and costs of concession were.

Then address the mapping's limits with equal care. The Concorde was governed by a cooperative treaty rather than an adversarial auction: both parties shared the prize in the sense that either nation's withdrawal would damage the joint project, yet both also paid regardless of which carriers eventually operated the aircraft. This introduces a structural feature that Shubik's original model does not contain—mutual commitment with shared liability—so ask whether the episode genuinely illustrates the dollar auction's core mechanism (rational escalation driven by the cost of conceding exceeding the cost of continuing) or whether it reveals that cooperative escalation requires a different formal structure altogether. Where does prestige, national identity, or technological spillover enter the payoff matrix in ways that resist monetization? And does the fact that both governments eventually accepted permanent operating losses mean that the "auction" never actually terminated with a winner, but instead collapsed into a jointly sustained loss?

Finally, say what this case shows about the theory rather than just applying the theory to the case. Does the Concorde episode confirm that escalation dynamics operate even in cooperative, high-prestige domains? Does it expose a hidden assumption in Shubik's model—perhaps that bidders are adversarial and that the prize is exclusive—without which the escalation logic changes? And if the model is indeed too adversarial, what minimal modification (for instance, a joint-commitment variant in which both parties pay but share the prize conditionally) would be needed to make it fit, and would that modified model still generate the same pathological escalation? Be honest about where the theory is silent rather than forcing an answer.
