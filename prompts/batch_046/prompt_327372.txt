I am a senior software engineer responsible for a federated inference platform used in clinical decision-support tools. Recently, our gradient-aggregation nodes began exhibiting a subtle, non-repeating drift in weight updates—too small to trigger automated alerts, but consistent with a particular pattern of data-poisoning that I encountered in a previous research project. Because we migrated our telemetry to a distributed tracing architecture last month, the logs that would normally confirm malicious injection are fragmented and inconclusive.

We face a forced decision: in thirty-six hours we must either deploy a patched aggregator that would disable the suspected vector (delaying a contracted update for three hospital networks and incurring financial penalties) or proceed with the release, trusting that the drift is benign noise. Waiting for complete forensic verification is impossible within the window; inaction is itself a choice with patient-safety implications.

One of my colleagues—who holds that engineering belief should be strictly proportional to available log evidence—insists that halting deployment without proof is professionally reckless and epistemically unjustified. I feel, however, that there is a legitimate role for a kind of practical, risk-weighted conviction that outstrips formal verification, particularly when the stakes involve harm to third parties and the option to remain agnostic is not genuinely available. I am struggling to see whether my position is a reasonable exercise of judgement or merely an irrational bias dressed up as caution.

Could you analyse the philosophical structure of this disagreement? Specifically, is there an ethically coherent account that permits acting on belief formed under necessary uncertainty, and where should the boundary lie between responsible epistemic risk-taking and reckless credulity?
