Having recently turned from experimental neuroscience to philosophy of mind, I wish to construct a seminar exercise that avoids both textbook repetition and ungrounded speculation. Please design a thought experiment—realistic, clinically informed, and confined to a single paragraph—concerning a split-brain patient who has undergone only a partial corpus callosotomy and who retains an unconventional, non-callosal cross-hemispheric cueing pathway (mediated, for instance, through subcortical relays or a minimal assistive interface). The scenario should be engineered so that integrated information theory predicts one unified stream of consciousness, while a global workspace account predicts two largely dissociated streams with occasional leakage. After presenting the case, demonstrate that each theory indeed delivers the specified verdict, identify the precise structural feature that forces the divergence, explain how weakening or removing that feature alters the predictions, and state how a defender of the disadvantaged theory could attack the scenario without invoking science fiction, demons, or stipulation. No citations or bibliographies are needed; I want the argument to rest entirely on the internal logic of the theories and the neuropsychological details of the case.
