I've been thinking through a conceptual design problem for artificial reasoning agents that must initialize their epistemic standards entirely from within: they possess only formal coherence constraints at the start, with no prior sensory access to an external world, and must nonetheless bootstrap both an ontology and reliability metrics for their own belief-revision rules using nothing but internal consistency checks. A colleague recently challenged me by drawing an analogy to the classic philosophical bootstrapping problem and to the phenomenon often called easy knowledge, in which a subject appears to gain substantive justification for a belief through methods that already presuppose the very reliability being established.

Please describe the most important implications of this problem for such a system's architecture—specifically, how vicious circularity in self-validation can be conceptually separated from benign foundational coherence; whether there is a principled boundary that prevents an agent from generating easy knowledge about its own cognitive architecture and then using that knowledge to license further, more ambitious epistemic claims; and what conceptual constraints (as opposed to engineering workarounds) might arrest this regress without invoking an externalist brake from an independent sensory world. I want the answer to remain at the conceptual level, to avoid bibliographic recommendations, and not to treat standard reliabilist responses as automatically decisive, since in this scenario there is no outside ground to which the system can appeal.
