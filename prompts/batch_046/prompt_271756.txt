Two interlocutors in a graduate seminar on pragmatism—called A and B—have reached an impasse over whether Rorty’s antirepresentationalism can consistently accommodate what they term local representational checks during belief revision. A began by arguing that if beliefs are not representations of an independent reality but merely tools for coping with the environment, then error collapses into pure instrumental failure: a belief is defective only in the way a broken hammer is defective, not because it misrepresents how things stand. This, A claimed, eliminates any genuinely epistemic normativity and reduces rational revision to pragmatic efficacy. B replied that this conflates causal interaction with justification. On Rorty’s view, B said, the world causally produces beliefs but does not justify them; justification occurs entirely within a vocabulary. Hence there is no need for vocabulary-independent representational checks—causal disruption may prompt revision without carrying semantic content that bypasses linguistic practice. A then pressed a specific challenge regarding perceptual surprises: when a well-entrenched belief is disrupted by an experience that resists assimilation into the current vocabulary, must not Rorty treat that resistance as providing information about how things stand independently of description? Otherwise, A asked, why should one vocabulary revise itself rather than another when both face the same causal shock? B answered that disruption is a reason for change only because a community’s norms of inquiry treat it as relevant; the shock itself is semantically inert until interpreted. B added that A implicitly demands a transcendent guarantee of rationality that Rorty’s framework deliberately abandons. A then introduced what she called the friction problem: if belief revision is fully internal to vocabularies, then cross-cultural persistence of certain conceptual schemes and the disappearance of others cannot be explained without smuggling back in a hidden notion of representational fit—some form of world-resistance that is not merely causal but normatively significant. B conceded that this identifies a real tension—accounting for convergence across cultures without invoking representation—but argued that evolutionary and anthropological explanations of vocabulary survival, together with internal norms of coherence and solidarity, are sufficient. B concluded that A seeks a metaphysical backing for rationality that is both unnecessary and unavailable within an antirepresentationalist picture. The discussion ended without resolution.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point. Specifically: does A successfully show that Rorty’s view requires some vocabulary-independent friction to explain both local belief revision and long-term vocabulary persistence, or does B correctly demonstrate that causal interaction plus immanent norms are fully adequate? Identify precisely which premises each side gets right and which they miss—particularly regarding the distinction between causal explanation and normative justification, and regarding whether antirepresentationalism necessarily collapses epistemic error into mere instrumental failure. Note also anything important that neither party addressed, such as whether there might be a non-representational yet non-arbitrary way to account for the normative force of disruptive experiences without reverting to a mirror-of-nature model.
