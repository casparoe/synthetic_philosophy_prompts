I retired five years ago from a career in experimental physics, and since then have served on the Programming and Ethics Board of the Meridian Archive and Museum. I find myself applying the same rigor I once reserved for data interpretation to questions I never expected to face—particularly those that sit at the boundary between historical fidelity and artistic license, and what obligations survive a person’s death. We are now asked to recommend a decision on a proposal that has split us sharply, and I need a formal opinion to circulate before Thursday’s meeting.

A documentary production company has applied both for grant funding and for access to our restricted oral-history collection—the private interview recordings and unpublished field notes of three recently deceased researchers in early climate science. The filmmakers intend to produce a feature-length dramatization rather than a conventional documentary. Their method includes what they term reconstructed interior monologue: they will invent dialogue and emotional reactions for these scientists, attributing to them sentiments that are consistent with but not documented in the archival record, in order to make the narrative accessible to general audiences. Some board members argue that since the dead cannot consent, and since these reconstructions risk becoming the dominant public memory of real people, we should deny both funding and archive access outright, treating fidelity to the recoverable record as a non-negotiable duty owed to the deceased. Others contend that historical understanding requires emotional engagement, that strict documentary form often fails the public, and that our proper role is not to police artistic method but to set strict conditions—clear on-screen labeling of reconstructed passages, family assent where obtainable, mandatory historian review, and a twelve-month pilot with an evaluation milestone—before approving access.

Please draft the short formal opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve with conditions, deny outright, or approve with a defined trial structure and time frame for review—and it should treat the opposing view with genuine seriousness: acknowledge that dramatization can constitute a form of epistemic harm to the dead by substituting a compelling fiction for a recoverable truth, and explain whether your recommendation accepts that risk under safeguards or judges it unavoidable. The memo should be addressed to the board, written in a tone appropriate to someone with a scientific background who has recently begun reading philosophy of history and ethics, and should avoid requesting bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or further research; this body needs an actionable decision now, not a literature review.
