You are serving as an expert consultant in moral philosophy and institutional ethics, with particular familiarity with the literature on standing to blame and hypocrisy—though you should work entirely from the concepts themselves rather than from any bibliographic record. Before you begin drafting, please take a moment to brainstorm the central distinctions that must survive operationalization: specifically, the difference between being a hypocrite and lacking standing, and the further distinction I am introducing here, which I call structural asymmetry of normative exposure. This asymmetry occurs when a complainant previously committed a wrong of the same normative kind as the one alleged, but did so within an institutional context—such as tenure protections, opaque collaboration structures, or disciplinary silos—that systematically shielded them from the sanctions or scrutiny they now seek to apply to the accused.

I am a philosophy professor serving on our university’s Faculty Ethics Oversight Committee, and we meet biweekly to adjudicate grievances involving student academic misconduct. Our members include administrators and legal counsel without philosophical training, our meetings are time-constrained, and any recommendation requires sign-off by the Dean of Faculty. We need a reusable decision procedure—not a one-off judgment—that the committee can apply whenever a faculty complainant’s own record raises questions about standing. The procedure should take the form of an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where the procedure halts with a determination of adequate standing, where it halts with a determination of inadequate standing, and where it must hand the decision to the committee because the issue is irreducibly one of institutional values rather than factual assessment. You must build in the structural asymmetry condition as a distinct filter, not collapse it into generic hypocrisy. Then apply the procedure to three cases. Case one: Professor M files a plagiarism grievance against an undergraduate; Professor M’s own record includes a co-authored monograph in which a junior scholar’s contribution was omitted, an omission that was never investigated because tenure governance rules effectively blocked student-led complaints against senior faculty. Case two: Professor N, who was formally reprimanded five years ago for self-plagiarizing a conference abstract, now files an identical charge against a graduate student; apply the procedure and show that it produces an answer the committee may find unwelcome. Case three: a scenario in which the wrong alleged and the prior wrong share only a family resemblance—say, data fabrication versus sloppy citation management—such that the procedure correctly flags the case as unsettled and explains what further value judgment is required. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: identify the precise boundary conditions where structural asymmetry shades into mere institutional privilege, and acknowledge the misfire risk when committee members lack full information about prior shielded conduct. Think carefully, proceed step by step, and keep the language precise but accessible to non-philosophers.
