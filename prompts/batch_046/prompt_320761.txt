Below are notes for a short essay challenging the universality of the sunk-cost diagnosis. I distinguish instrumental sunk-cost reasoning—treating unrecoverable past expenditure as a reason for future investment—from what I call diachronic integrity maintenance: honoring a commitment not because the past cost justifies more spending, but because abandoning it now would fracture the agent's narrative identity by rendering her past self an unacknowledged stranger. I sketch three cases: pure instrumental error (staying in a dull film because of the ticket price), legitimate integrity maintenance (continuing to revise a flawed dissertation chapter because years of labor have constituted one's identity as a scholar, not because the labor demands more labor), and a hybrid where agents invoke narrative language to mask instrumental error. I then propose that rational-choice models need a non-reducible parameter for identity-cost. I would welcome comments on whether the distinction between the first two cases is stable, whether integrity maintenance is genuinely rational or merely rationalized commitment, and whether importing a theory of narrative identity here is legitimate without independent defense. I am especially interested in whether there are standard counterexamples I have missed, and in whether my framing wrongly assumes that diachronic consistency is always a virtue rather than sometimes a vice.
