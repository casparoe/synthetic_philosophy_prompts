After forty years in condensed-matter physics I have lately turned to political philosophy, and I have constructed an argument that seems to drive me from scientific common sense straight into universal expert rule. I want to keep the scientific intuition intact and also preserve democracy’s dignity, but I cannot locate the faulty step. (1) In scientific collaboration we do not treat every opinion about a predictive claim as equally weighty; we organize judgment by demonstrated competence and defer to those who have shown predictive success. (2) Much of democratic politics is structurally predictive—policies are chosen because they are expected to cause particular outcomes, such as reduced emissions or stable growth. (3) Therefore, if democratic voting assigns equal weight to every citizen’s predictive judgment regardless of competence, it systematically violates the epistemic norm that governs every other domain in which we seek correct predictions, namely, proportionate deference to expertise. (4) This is not merely instrumental: a decision procedure that we know will be less accurate is, other things equal, irrational to employ. (5) But if this reasoning applies to voting, it applies with equal force to any collective procedure that pools competent and incompetent judges—jury trials, peer-review panels, funding committees, clinical diagnostic boards. (6) Hence consistency demands either that we replace all such procedures with pure expert rule—an epistocracy that extends everywhere—or that we deny democratic equality is justified by epistemic accuracy. (7) The second path looks available: democratic equality might rest on fairness, autonomy, or the expressive value of equal standing, not on getting the answer right. (8) Yet if non-epistemic values can justify ignoring competence differences in politics, they can justify ignoring them in peer review or jury service as well, since fairness and autonomy apply to defendants and grant applicants no less than to voters. (9) So we are pushed back toward universal epistocracy, which proves far too much, unless we can identify a principled distinction that exempts politics alone from the epistemic norms governing science and law—a distinction that now looks ad hoc. I am inclined to defend (1), (3), and (7), but I cannot see which link in the chain to break without paying a steep price. Which step is the error, why did it appear sound, and what does rejecting it commit me to?
