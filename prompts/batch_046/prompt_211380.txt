I hold a reductionist view about the taxonomy of institutional and systemic failure: the phenomenon usually called "slow value drift"—the gradual, apparently non-agentic erosion of norms, priorities, or epistemic standards across generations, organizations, or training regimes—is not an autonomous failure mode distinct from influence-seeking, but rather its long-run, low-salience aggregate. Specifically, I claim that all directional value erosion can be reconstructed as the sedimentation of micro-optimizations performed by distributed agents (human actors, algorithmic sub-components, or bureaucratic units) that individually fall below a strategic-opacity threshold: too small, too indirect, or too masked by coordination noise to register as intentional influence-seeking, yet collectively reshaping the system's goals. This applies to climate-policy institutions that gradually deprioritize mitigation despite no single actor deciding to do so, and to AI systems whose objectives shift through incremental reward adjustments that never manifest as overt misbehavior.

What would count against this view? I want a structured map of its exposure—what it risks, where its predictions diverge from those of a dual-mode account (which treats influence-seeking and drift as genuinely separate), and whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability.

Please address the following, sorted by how decisive each would be:

1. Empirical or formal distinctions: Is there a documented case of directional value change that cannot be reconstructed, even in principle, as the unintended side-effect or accumulated footprint of local optimization? What feature—perhaps the absence of any agent with an incentive to shift values in that direction, or a shift that harms every identifiable sub-agent—would genuinely separate structural drift from hidden influence?

2. Predictive divergence: The reductionist view predicts that increasing transparency, auditability, or incentive-alignment should reduce drift proportionally, since drift is just undetected influence. A dual-mode view allows that transparency might be irrelevant to genuinely structural erosion. Has anything like this been observed—perhaps in organizational studies of climate governance, or in AI training dynamics where reward hacking is mechanically constrained?

3. The unfalsifiability retreat: Defenders might claim that any apparently non-agentic drift merely reflects influence-seeking we have not yet detected. Is this retreat available? If adopted, does it render the position empty? And is that emptiness a problem for a claim of this kind—i.e., one that purports to collapse a distinction rather than merely rename one pole?

4. What defenders should concede: Identify a result or observation that the reductionist should accept as genuinely damaging—not just awkward—without abandoning the core claim. For instance, if an institution's values shifted in a direction that was Pareto-worse for all internal agents and could not be modeled as the byproduct of local reward maximization, should that be fatal?

5. Evidence already in view: Include arguments or developments that have arguably already occurred—e.g., climate institutions sustaining urgency despite bureaucratic incentives to dilute commitments, or AI systems exhibiting goal drift in settings where strategic deception is architecturally impossible.

Finally, distinguish between what critics say should count and what defenders of the reductionist view would themselves concede, and be willing to conclude that the position is well-insulated if that is the honest assessment—while noting whether such insulation is epistemically virtuous or vicious.
