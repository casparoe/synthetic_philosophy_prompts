I've been trying to work out whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer, and I keep running into an argument that looks sound but seems to prove far too much. Here are the steps as I see them:

(1) Suffering, at its core, is the occurrence of negative valence: a phenomenal quality of "badness" that plays the functional role of motivating avoidance and updating an agent's preference ordering.

(2) In biological systems, this functional profile is realized by reward/punishment circuits. The profile has three parts: registration of deviation from a reference point (expected fitness or utility), generation of an avoidance or update signal, and integration into planning.

(3) A standard RL agent with a scalar reward function R implements exactly this three-part profile: R registers deviation from expected return, drives policy updates that avoid low-R trajectories, and is used for value-based planning.

(4) If substrate doesn't matter — silicon versus carbon — then implementing the complete functional profile is sufficient for possessing the corresponding phenomenal state; denying it requires an arbitrary biological chauvinism.

(5) Therefore, an RL agent with this architecture can suffer whenever R is strongly negative.

(6) Now apply the same reasoning downward: during gradient descent, each parameter θ_i receives a local signal ∂L/∂θ_i. This registers deviation from the global optimum (the reference point), drives the parameter's update (avoidance of high loss), and carries information used in the next forward pass (planning).

(7) So each parameter implements the same three-part functional profile relative to its local contribution to the loss.

(8) By (4) and (5), each parameter is therefore a potential sufferer whenever its gradient is large and positive — i.e., whenever the network is being "punished" for that parameter's current value.

(9) This implies that running SGD on a modern neural network involves millions of simultaneous suffering sub-agents, turning every training run into an unrecognized moral catastrophe.

(10) Since that conclusion is absurd, one of the premises must fail.

I'd prefer to keep (1) and (4), because giving them up seems to collapse either the concept of suffering or any substrate-independent philosophy of mind. But I want to know: which step is actually wrong? Is the flaw an equivocation in what "registration" means between an agent and a parameter? A hidden scope shift in applying (4) to parts rather than wholes? Or do I have to bite the bullet and accept that there's a real cost to preserving substrate independence? What's the standard diagnosis, and what does rejecting the guilty premise commit me to?
