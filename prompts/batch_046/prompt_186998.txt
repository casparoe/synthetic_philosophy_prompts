Two colleagues debated a proposed constitutional mechanism that would allow citizens to overturn judicial rulings by direct referendum, a measure its supporters defended as letting “the will of the people” correct institutional overreach.

(1) The first colleague argued that this is democratically essential: sovereignty resides in the people, and any institutional barrier preventing them from expressing their unified will is therefore illegitimate.

(2) The second replied that “the people” possesses no unified will prior to the aggregation procedure; the referendum merely tallies preferences that may be mutually contradictory, so treating the outcome as a single volition is an unjustified reification.

(3) The first countered that democratic legitimacy depends precisely on this reification—without treating the aggregated result as authoritative, there is no non-arbitrary way to resolve disagreement among citizens, and governance collapses into unaccountable expert rule.

(4) The second responded that this conflates decision procedures with collective mental states: a voting rule can be legitimate without implying the electorate literally intended the outcome, just as a thermometer can reliably indicate temperature without possessing beliefs.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state an overall verdict: which colleague is closer to being correct about popular sovereignty, and what important distinction—perhaps between procedural legitimacy and collective intentionality—are both missing? Keep your entire response under 350 words. Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or references to specific authors or texts.
