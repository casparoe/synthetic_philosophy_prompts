I just finished a long and rather heated argument with a close friend about G.E.M. Anscombe's view that President Truman's decision to drop atomic weapons on Hiroshima made him guilty of murder, specifically because he directed his intention towards the death of innocents as a means to ending the war, and whether her doctrine of double effect can actually sustain that charge without collapsing into a form of consequentialism. I want to design a seminar exercise that makes this tension visceral rather than merely textual.

Please design a classroom activity — a structured, rule-bound deliberation with small symbolic stakes — for a group of about twelve philosophy undergraduates, lasting approximately ninety minutes. The setting should be a simulated strategic council faced with a decision that structurally parallels the Hiroshima case: they must choose between several courses of action, each with different casualty profiles and strategic outcomes, and then classify the resulting civilian deaths according to whether they are directed or merely foreseen under constraints they only fully understand as the exercise unfolds. Include defined roles (for example, a strategic advisor, an ethicist, and an observer who scores intentions), a timeline with distinct phases of deliberation and revelation, materials such as decision cards and token-based payoffs, and rules that do not require real money.

Crucially, inject one niche framing to prevent this from being a generic double-effect discussion: include a moral remainder token that every participant accumulates whenever civilian deaths occur, regardless of whether the chosen action is ultimately justified, so that the group experiences the idea that even permissible killing leaves an unredeemed moral trace that cannot be cancelled by strategic success.

The deliverable should include: the rules and payoff structure; the debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto Anscombe's framework (the prohibition on directing evil, the distinction between means and foreseen side-effects, and the absolute claim about innocent life); an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time the activity is run and how to manage it; and a frank note on which feature of Anscombe's position — for instance, the metaphysical claim about the structure of human intention or the non-negotiable status of the innocent — the exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.

Please write the answer in British English. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests. Avoid verbatim quotations from real texts; if you refer to specific claims, paraphrase them without quotation marks.
