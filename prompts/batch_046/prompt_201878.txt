Exam question:
Some philosophers defend what is known, following Derek Parfit, as the Hinge of History Hypothesis: the claim that the present era is uniquely consequential for the long-run trajectory of human civilization. In a prominent reply, William MacAskill argues that, if the future is likely to contain an enormous number of agents and decision points, then our being the uniquely decisive ones is statistically improbable. A recent but non-standard distinction has been proposed between two ways a present choice might matter: a path-dependent hinge, in which small present differences cascade into radically divergent long-run outcomes, and a structurally leveraged hinge, in which present choices become institutionally or technologically locked in, constraining the option spaces of future agents regardless of how numerous those agents are.

How does this distinction bear on assessing the Hinge of History Hypothesis? In particular, does MacAskill's probabilistic challenge apply with equal force to both varieties, or does structural leverage offer a form of present uniqueness that survives the statistical objection? Answer concisely, and illustrate each hinge type with one concrete example.

Student answer:
The Hinge of History Hypothesis holds that our era is special for the future. MacAskill counters that with so many future people, it is unlikely we are the special ones. The distinction between path-dependent and structurally leveraged hinges clarifies things. A path-dependent hinge works like a butterfly effect: a tiny change now produces huge differences later. Because there will be trillions of future decision points, ours is just one branching node among many, so MacAskill is clearly right here—path-dependent uniqueness is implausible. Structural leverage is different: it involves durable lock-in, like establishing a global climate treaty or deploying a pervasive energy grid that later generations cannot easily reverse. Because such constraints persist, the number of future agents is irrelevant—our choice still binds them. Therefore MacAskill's statistical argument only undermines path-dependent hinges, not structurally leveraged ones. This means the Hinge Hypothesis survives in its structural form. Parfit originally conceived the hinge primarily in terms of reducing existential risk, which fits the structural model because preventing extinction permanently eliminates all future possibilities. So MacAskill has not shown the hinge is unlikely; he has only shown that branching hinges are unlikely, while durable institutional hinges could still make us uniquely important.
