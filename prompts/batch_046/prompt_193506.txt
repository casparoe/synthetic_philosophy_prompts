Suppose Thomas Bayes, reasoning only from his 18th-century framework for inverse probability and the principle of indifference, were presented with the Sleeping Beauty problem in a modified form: a coin is tossed with an actual bias of two-thirds toward tails, though the sleeper is assured it is fair; she is awakened on Monday alone if heads, and on Monday and Tuesday with complete inter-awakening amnesia if tails; in addition, an independent observer is awakened every Wednesday, told only the day of the week, and never shown the coin result. How would Bayes likely assign the sleeper's credence in heads upon awakening—treating her awareness that she is awake now as standard conditioning, or as an update over centered worlds weighted by prior objective chance? Then do the same for the Wednesday observer's credence, and identify which part of Bayes's own commitments—objective chance versus epistemic probability, or his use of indifference—would most determine whether he adopts the halfers' or the thirders' position in the original unbiased problem.
