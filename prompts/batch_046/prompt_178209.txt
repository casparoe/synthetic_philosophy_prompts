I spent thirty years as a physicist working on non-equilibrium dynamics before retiring, and over the past year I have joined a local reading group that is working through the classics of political philosophy with the same patience I once applied to long experimental runs. Last month we turned to Machiavelli’s account of fortune and virtù, and I found myself struck by a structural parallel that I have not encountered in the introductory literature—though I freely admit that my exposure to the scholarly tradition remains limited. I have drafted an essay arguing that Machiavelli’s image of fortune as a river that can be partly channeled but never fully subdued corresponds, not to mere randomness, but to the distinction in dynamical systems theory between endogenous control parameters and exogenous forcing that pushes a system toward a bifurcation point. On this reading, virtù is less a catalog of moral or tactical excellences than an adaptive capacity—something like structural resilience—that allows a political order to preserve its identity across parameter shifts, even as it experiences the phenomenon one might call critical slowing down near a threshold. I then ask whether Machiavelli’s well-known claim that fortune governs roughly half of human affairs is best interpreted as an early, intuitive acknowledgment of ergodicity breaking: the idea that long-run statistical averages fail to determine the path-dependent trajectory of any particular state.

I am writing to request your comments on these notes before I attempt a fuller draft. Specifically, I would value an assessment of whether this analogy genuinely illuminates Machiavelli’s text or whether it imposes a modern scientific framework onto concepts that operated in an entirely different register. I am particularly concerned that I may have conflated Machiavelli’s practical, almost tactical notion of virtù—centered on the prince’s capacity to act—with a systems-level property that properly belongs to institutions rather than to individuals. If there are subtle misrepresentations in how I distinguish fortune from pure chance, or in how I characterize the scope of human will against external forcing, please identify them directly. I do not need a bibliography, a reading list, or suggestions for further sources; my aim is to refine the argument on its own terms and to determine whether the comparison holds philosophical water. Any observations on accuracy, coherence, or the value of the parallel itself would be deeply appreciated.
