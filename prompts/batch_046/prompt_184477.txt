I'm taking an upper-level seminar on value theory this term, and we've been working through classical and contemporary hedonism. A problem that has come up repeatedly—one that seems to survive even Mill's qualitative distinctions—is the heterogeneity objection: if pleasure is the one intrinsic good, then every pleasure must share a common qualitative feature, yet the pleasure of solving a chess problem, the pleasure of warm water, and the pleasure of recognizing a familiar melody seem phenomenologically incommensurable. For our final meeting, I want to present a short poem that captures this tension structurally rather than resolving it.

Please write a sonnet—either in strict iambic pentameter or in a clearly marked loose variation, with a definite volta around line 9—about the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. In the octave, present the hedonist's aspiration: that beneath the diversity of experiences there is a single, immediately recognizable pleasantness that makes them all good in the same way, using concrete, sharply different images of pleasure (for instance, intellectual discovery, bodily warmth, aesthetic recognition). At the volta, let the objection land: the comparison of these experiences reveals no shared felt quality, only a projected normative judgment or, at best, a family resemblance. In the sestet, explore the consequence—either the common feature collapses into an empty abstraction, or hedonism must retreat to a formal claim about preference-satisfaction that abandons phenomenological immediacy. The closing couplet should state exactly what must be given up: either the unity of intrinsic value or the claim that pleasure is a homogeneous felt quality. Crucially, let the form do philosophical work: use the rhyme scheme to yoke together words that denote phenomenologically distant pleasures, so that the sonic harmony of the rhyme stands in tension with the semantic disparity of the experiences it links, making the reader feel the very pairing-problem the hedonist needs to solve. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist recognizes the debate, including the distinction between quantitative and qualitative hedonism and the modern formulation of the heterogeneity objection, rather than merely naming pleasure and happiness.

After the poem, include a brief separate note that distinguishes what is generally settled about the heterogeneity problem (for example, that quantitative hedonism faces a serious challenge in accounting for qualitative differences) from what remains genuinely contested (for instance, whether Mill's qualitative move answers the objection or merely relocates it, and whether pleasantness can be defended as a determinable rather than a determinate property). In that same note, indicate precisely where the poem takes liberties—whether in attributing a particular phenomenological claim to Mill, in treating family resemblance as the only alternative, or in suggesting that hedonism must retreat to preference-satisfaction—so that the audience can see which parts of the argument are poetic compression and which are live philosophical positions.
