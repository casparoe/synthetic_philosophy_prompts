Read Albert Camus’s The Stranger (L’Étranger) through the specific political-philosophical lens of his opposition to capital punishment, particularly the argument developed in his essay “Reflections on the Guillotine” that modern execution is a concealed, bureaucratic form of premeditated murder sustained by social hypocrisy and by the state’s claim to metaphysical certainty over human life. Rather than treating Meursault’s trial and impending death solely as an existentialist parable about the absurd, consider how the novel structurally withholds the execution itself—ending before the guillotine falls—while constructing Meursault’s conviction less around the killing of the Arab man than around his failure to perform conventional grief at his mother’s funeral and his refusal, in the prison scenes, to adopt the chaplain’s redemptive narrative.

How does this absence of the execution scene dramatize Camus’s claim that societies hide capital punishment in order to avoid direct moral reckoning with state violence? In what ways does the prosecution’s theatrical reconstruction of Meursault as an emotional monster—condemning him for an epistemic failure to display the “correct” interior states—mirror the bureaucratic substitution of procedural certainty for human judgment that Camus critiques? Examine specific moments: the trial’s narrative-building, Meursault’s sensory immediacy in the cell, and his refusal to confess or seek forgiveness. Where does the novel resist being reduced to an anti-death-penalty tract—perhaps in its sympathy for Meursault’s physical, pre-reflective existence, which seems to transcend the state’s moral categories altogether? And what does the work add to philosophical debates about state violence and concealed violence that a direct argument alone cannot provide?
