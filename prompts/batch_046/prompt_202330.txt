A friend summarized Amartya Sen's liberal paradox like this. Sen considers a small society in which each person holds an unconditional right over one purely personal choice — for instance, whether they themselves read a specific book. He also requires that if both people agree on a social outcome, that agreement must prevail. From just these two conditions, Sen derives a contradiction: there exist preference configurations in which respecting both individuals' personal rights forces society into an outcome that both actually reject, thereby violating the agreement condition. My friend concluded that this shows liberal rights are incompatible with any collective decision rule that respects unanimous preference, essentially reducing Sen's result to Arrow's impossibility theorem expressed in terms of freedom. They added that in more recent treatments people do not take the rights as given but assign them through an initial contractual stage, yet claimed the contradiction survives unchanged. I am suspicious of the jump from a two-person example to all collective decision-making, and I do not know whether that contractual stage belongs to Sen's original argument or is an outside addition. Is this a fair summary? Please distinguish the parts that accurately reflect Sen's reasoning, the parts that overstate or conflate it with other results, and whether introducing a preliminary contract for assigning rights actually alters the logical structure of the paradox.
