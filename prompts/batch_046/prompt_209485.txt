I am a novelist preparing an application for a six-month interdisciplinary writing fellowship. My current project is a historical novel set in the decline of dueling culture in nineteenth-century Europe, and I need a philosophical research proposal—roughly two pages—to serve as the conceptual backbone for the narrative. The problem I want to investigate belongs to the debate on moral revolutions: do norms like slavery and dueling end primarily through moral argument, through shifting interests and power structures, or through transformations in honor? I am particularly engaged with Bernard Williams’s emphasis on honor-worlds and Kwame Anthony Appiah’s account of how moral revolutions unfold, but I want to avoid the standard comparative framing that treats slavery and dueling as parallel cases. Instead, I wish to inject a conceptual distinction I have not seen developed in the existing literature: splitting “honor” into “vertical honor” (status recognition granted or withheld by institutional superiors) and “lateral honor” (peer-to-peer esteem maintained within a closed, self-governing group). The research question must be stated precisely: using this bifurcation, can we explain why dueling persisted for decades after state prohibition and widespread moral condemnation, whereas slavery collapsed more rapidly once its economic and political supports were undermined, even though defenders of both practices appealed to honor? The proposal should explain why existing answers—both the Williams thesis and Appiah’s framework—leave this particular asymmetry open, and it should propose an approach based on conceptual analysis of primary historical narratives rather than empirical sociology. Please specify what a successful result would look like—perhaps a refined typology of honor-based resistance to moral change—and what would count as failure, for instance if the vertical/lateral distinction collapses or adds no explanatory power beyond a straightforward interest-based account. Be realistic about scope: six months, one researcher, no fieldwork, deliverable being a polished philosophical essay of approximately eight thousand words that I can use in the fellowship report and as background for the novel. Do not include a bibliography or citation list; I will supply all references myself. The tone should be formal and polished, suitable for a fellowship committee with members outside philosophy. Please conclude with a line beginning “Verdict:” giving a one-sentence assessment of whether the proposed framing successfully opens a tractable question that is neither too broad for six months nor reducible to the standard debate.
