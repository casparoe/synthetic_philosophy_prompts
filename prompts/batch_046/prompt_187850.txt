I'm a senior philosophy major currently enrolled in our seminar on existentialism, public life, and modern media. I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about four minutes, roughly 550 words—at tomorrow's interdisciplinary workshop, "The Named Voice and the Anonymous Feed," co-hosted by our philosophy department and our school of journalism. Before you draft anything, please take a moment to think carefully, in the role of both a scholar of Kierkegaard's social thought and an experienced academic speechwriter, about how his critique of the press and "the public" can do real structural work here rather than serve as decoration. The audience is split: half are philosophy faculty and graduate students who know Kierkegaard well; the other half are journalists and media-studies undergraduates who do not. The occasion also marks our student newspaper's launch of a strict real-byline policy that ends all pseudonymous and anonymous commenting. I want to honor that decision, but I must avoid praising "the public" or "the crowd" as an autonomous source of wisdom, and I must not treat the removal of anonymity as a simple triumph of transparency or authenticity. Instead, use Kierkegaard's idea that the press creates an abstract, leveling entity in which responsibility dissolves into the mass, and frame the byline rule as a risky, necessary discipline that forces every speaker back into singular, accountable existence—not as a window onto a truer self. The speech should be formal and polished, accessible to non-philosophers without diluting the concept, and it must not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, invented page numbers, or specific edition details. Please begin with brief brainstorming of the philosophical structure, then draft the remarks from my perspective as the student speaker.
