In seminars on Bertrand's paradox, one often hears the maxim 'no reason to prefer one, so treat them all the same' invoked as though it settles the problem. At a recent meeting, a speaker used exactly this line—calling it a principle of indifference under ignorance—to defend the 1/3 answer derived from parameterizing chord midpoints by distance from the center, and to dismiss the 1/2 and 1/4 answers as artifacts of arbitrary coordinate choices. I am convinced the maxim is concealing several distinct commitments, but I cannot yet separate them cleanly. Could you lay out the precise theses this slogan could be expressing, presented as a menu rather than a single reading? In particular, distinguish: (i) a purely epistemic claim about how rational agents ought to assign credences given total ignorance of a generating mechanism; (ii) a physical-symmetry claim about which parameterization correctly captures the random process that produces the chord; (iii) a mathematical claim about the existence or uniqueness of invariant measures (translation-invariant, rotation-invariant, or otherwise) on the relevant sample spaces; and (iv) any other substantive interpretation that does not collapse into one of the above. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, whether it implicitly selects a specific parameterization (endpoint pairs, radius, midpoint distance, or angular orientation), and whether it genuinely supports the 1/3 result without begging the question against the rival answers. I want to bring this disambiguation back to the seminar to test whether the speaker's dismissal of the other parameterizations holds up.
