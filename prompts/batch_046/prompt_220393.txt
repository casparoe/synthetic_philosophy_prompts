Question: Daniel Dennett's intentional stance lets us predict behavior by treating entities as rational agents with beliefs and desires. Recently philosophers have asked whether LLMs can be interpreted this way—treating them as "believing" Paris is the capital when they consistently say so, and "desiring" coherence. Others say this is just anthropomorphism. In about 300 words, evaluate whether Dennett's framework licenses attributing beliefs to LLMs, addressing (a) what he means by the reality of belief patterns, (b) whether lacking biological desires undermines the stance, and (c) whether hallucinating a false citation is a false belief, a malfunction, or something else.

Student answer: Dennett's intentional stance is basically a predicitive strategy: instead of analyzing weights, we treat the system as rational with beliefs and desires. For Dennett beliefs aren't hidden mental objects but real patterns in behavior, so if an LLM consistently outputs that Paris is the capital, we can genuinely say it believes that. The objection about biological desires misses his point—he doesn't require biological urges, just that attributing a goal makes behavior intelligible, so an LLM can "desire" coherence like a thermostat desires temperature. Where it gets tricky is hallucination. When the model invents a citation, it's expressing a false belief. Dennett, following Davidson, holds that beliefs must form a rational web; widespread hallucination shows the web is broken. So we should restrict the stance to reliable domains and deny it applies where hallucinations dominate. Also, Dennett argues the design stance is always inferior for complex systems, which is why engineers never use it for LLMs—this shows the intentional stance is the only practical one for AI.
