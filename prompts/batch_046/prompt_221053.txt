I have been tracking the determinations of a particular institutional bioethics board with which I collaborate, and I am trying to predict how it would rule on a new protocol. Its recent track record is as follows.

First, in a psychiatric trial where clinicians described an off-label ketamine infusion as promising and well-tolerated, knowing that remission rates were modest and that transient dissociation was common, but where all quantitative risks were disclosed in writing, the board held that consent was valid: optimistic verbal framing does not invalidate consent if the material risks are documented.

Second, in a residential study offering substantial compensation to unemployed participants for a six-week withdrawal from psychiatric medication, the board upheld consent, ruling that economic vulnerability does not amount to coercion provided the compensation is not contingent upon a particular response and withdrawal is clinically monitored.

Third, in a case where a fifteen-year-old with high scores on standardised competence assessments requested enrolment in an experimental gene-therapy trial that her parents opposed, the board affirmed the adolescent's consent, holding that demonstrated decision-specific competence can override parental veto in non-emergency research.

Fourth, where participants in a behavioural study were told it concerned stress responses to urban noise but were in fact being assessed for implicit racial bias through hidden auditory primes, the board invalidated consent, ruling that deception about the object of study undermines autonomous choice irrespective of physical risk.

Fifth, in a surgical comparison where patients were informed that both techniques were in use but were not told that one was still classified as experimental by the hospital's own surgical committee, the board again invalidated consent, holding that institutional-status judgments can be material to a reasonable decision.

The board now faces a protocol in which competent adult participants explicitly agree to a study in which they will be deceived about its precise purpose for the first fortnight, after which full debriefing and data-withdrawal rights apply. The deception is methodologically necessary because awareness of the true aim—measuring implicit social-cognitive bias—would invalidate measurement. Participants are told that deception will occur and that it concerns social cognition, but not the specific form. There is no direct coercion, and compensation is modest.

What verdict should the board reach for consistency? I am less interested in the bare answer than in the competing rules that fit the earlier five determinations equally well, what each predicts here, and which further case would best distinguish them. In particular, I want to know whether the board is running a material-transparency rule, an autonomy-preservation rule, or a competence-and-coercion filter, and whether its earlier invalidation of hidden deception was driven by informational omission or by the structural impossibility of autonomous participation during deception.

Please write your answer in British English, and include a hypothetical sixth case that would force a choice between the two leading interpretations.
