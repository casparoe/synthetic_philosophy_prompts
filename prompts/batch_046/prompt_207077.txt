The examination question asked: "The paradox of 'Buridan's ass'—a donkey placed equidistant between two identical bales of hay and allegedly starving from indecision—is often discussed in connection with Jean Buridan's theory of volition. First, clarify whether Buridan himself actually formulated this specific animal scenario in his surviving works, and explain his actual account of what the will does when the intellect presents two goods as exactly equal. Second, recent interdisciplinary commentary has drawn an analogy between the donkey's paralysis and spontaneous symmetry breaking in physical systems (for instance, a ferromagnetic domain cooling below the Curie point with no external field to determine its magnetization direction). Assess whether this comparison genuinely illuminates Buridan's position or whether it instead imports a category mistake between physical causation and volitional agency. Limit your response to approximately four hundred words."

The student submitted the following answer:

"Buridan introduces the ass example in his commentary on Aristotle's ethics to prove that determinism is false: if the will always followed the greater good, a perfectly rational agent could be paralyzed. His resolution is that the will can generate an arbitrary internal perturbation—analogous to quantum fluctuations—that breaks the tie, allowing it to choose one bale. This is essentially the same mechanism as spontaneous symmetry breaking in physics, where a system randomly selects one state from a symmetric potential. Therefore, the analogy is not a category error but a deep structural insight. However, Buridan also allows that if the will fails to do this, the agent might simply die, which Spinoza later classified as irrational behavior comparable to suicide or madness. Leibniz criticized this view by arguing that hidden petites perceptions always tip the balance, but Buridan rejected this because he believed in genuine indifference."
