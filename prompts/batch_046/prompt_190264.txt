I sit on the institutional review board of a university neuropsychiatric center. A team proposes studying patients emerging from disorders of consciousness—minimally conscious and unresponsive wakefulness states—using real-time multivariate fMRI paired with structured first-person reports. Their protocol is explicitly framed around isolating a Cartesian theater moment: a single neural event where distributed inputs bind into one conscious scene, which they propose using as the clinical criterion for when a patient has regained consciousness sufficiently to participate in end-of-life decisions.

The board is split. Proponents say clinical medicine needs an operational biomarker of conscious presence, and that identifying a central integration point—however philosophically contested—offers reproducibility. Critics invoke Dennett's multiple drafts model, arguing that consciousness may lack any central stage; that apparent unity is a temporally dispersed, revisable process with no authoritative version; and that grounding withdrawal protocols on a presumed theater risks treating a measurement artifact as the seat of personhood.

Draft the board's decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the investigators and the clinical-ethics liaison. Recommend a concrete course of action with a clear time frame, treat the proponents' need for clinical operationalization seriously, and address the critics' concern as a potential source of patient harm rather than a theoretical quibble. Do not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
