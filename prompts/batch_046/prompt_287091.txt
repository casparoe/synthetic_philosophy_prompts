You are an expert in moral philosophy and deadpan bureaucratic satire. I just had a long, exhausting argument with a friend about tranquilism—the view that only the absence of craving and suffering has intrinsic value, and that achievement, pleasure, knowledge, or positive states carry no weight unless they reduce some prior deficit. To think through where this actually leads, please write a satirical, deadpan “Municipal Implementation Manual” (around 600 words, in flat technical prose with section headings, bullet points, and invented but plausible metrics such as a “Craving-Abatement Index” or “Suffering-Neutral Zoning Score”) for a mid-sized city that has adopted tranquilism as its sole governing criterion for urban policy. Before drafting, briefly list the three core commitments of tranquilism and the two most serious practical tensions they generate when applied to governance—do this as internal planning notes, not part of the manual itself. Then produce the manual, covering at least education policy, cultural programming, public-health intervention, and end-of-life administration. The humor and critique must arise from rigorously following the view’s own logic, not from inventing straw-man objections: for example, that stimulating culture is administratively harmful because it generates ambition; that education should avoid producing new competencies that create future cravings; that mild chronic dissatisfaction is a higher policy priority than flourishing; and that measuring suffering requires surveillance mechanisms that themselves risk producing distress. Keep the tone consistently deadpan—no winking, no explicit moralizing, no named living philosophers—so that a reader familiar with well-being theory recognizes the satire as fair. Do not request or include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
