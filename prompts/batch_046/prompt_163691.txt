The paradox of deontology is that it forbids actions like killing even to prevent greater harm, yet also demands we save lives, so in Voss's case both distributing the drug (which kills some) and witholding it (which lets thousands die) seem morally wrong. The doing/allowing distinction is key: distributing actively causes death, while witholding is merely letting people die, so deontology permits witholding. Double effect applies only when the harm is physically indirect, not a direct result of the act, so it can't justify distributing here since the allergic reaction is caused directly by the injection. Michael Walzer's idea of supreme emergency shows that moral rules have a catastrophic threshold—if over ten thousand lives are at risk, the constraint can be broken—so Voss should only violate her oath if deaths reach that scale. Otherwise the oath functions as an absolute side-constraint. Even though witholding is the right choice, Voss should feel a moral remainder for the lives lost, which shows deontology isn't heartless but still respects agent-centered limits.
