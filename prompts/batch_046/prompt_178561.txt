A colleague sent me an argument about hinge epistemology that seems to collapse Wittgenstein’s framework into either irrationalism or self-contradiction, and I cannot locate the weak joint. Here is the chain, reconstructed from her notes:

(1) For Wittgenstein in *On Certainty*, hinge propositions are not empirical hypotheses but fixed points around which inquiry turns; they are not justified by evidence but are conditions for evidence to count within a practice.

(2) If a proposition is a structural condition for evidence to count within practice P, then within P there is no evidential route to its rejection; any apparent counter-evidence can be absorbed by adjusting auxiliary commitments, because the hinge itself determines what adjustments are legitimate.

(3) So a true hinge is rationally unrevisable within the practice it structures.

(4) Now consider the commitment that “measurement reveals pre-existing values,” which functioned as a hinge in classical experimental physics: instruments were designed and calibrated on the assumption that they registered values already possessed by the system, rather than participating in producing them.

(5) In quantum mechanics, this commitment was not merely adjusted but rejected; measurement is now treated as participatory, and experimental design is built around that rejection.

(6) If (3) holds, this rejection could not have been rational within classical practice; it must have been a non-rational conversion, analogous to a change in religious framework, not compelled by reasons available inside the old system.

(7) But the shift was driven by specific anomalies—black-body radiation, the photoelectric effect, spectral line discreteness—that classical physics handled only through increasingly ad hoc modifications, until the quantum framework resolved them with greater elegance and predictive power.

(8) Therefore the shift was rationally compelled by evidence and explanatory virtue, not a non-rational conversion.

(9) So (3) is false: hinges can be rationally revised after all.

(10) But if hinges are rationally revisable, they are not structurally distinct from well-confirmed empirical claims; they are merely claims with very high confirmation.

(11) That dissolves the hinge/empirical distinction entirely, leaving the framework of *On Certainty* with nothing distinctive to say.

She concludes that hinge epistemology proves too much: either scientific change is never rational, or there are no hinges. I want to preserve both the structural distinction and the rationality of scientific progress. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, must saving the distinction require treating quantum mechanics as an entirely different practice with its own hinges rather than a rational revision of the old ones? And if so, does that make cross-paradigm evaluation impossible in a way that undermines our judgment that quantum mechanics represents genuine improvement? Please diagnose the argument precisely: name the step, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives for someone who wants to keep both Wittgenstein’s distinction and the rationality of theory change.
