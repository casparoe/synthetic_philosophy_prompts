You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in applied ethics and emergency decision-making. Before giving your analysis, please take a moment to brainstorm the main normative frameworks—consequentialist, deontological, and virtue-based—that could apply here, and then proceed with careful, step-by-step reasoning.

I have recently been appointed to an ethics advisory board for a remote coastal research station, and our discussions keep returning to lifeboat-style allocation dilemmas. I am trying to see whether a single coherent principle can capture my intuitive verdicts across the following cases, or whether I am simply holding inconsistent judgments.

Here are my reactions:

1. A capsized vessel can support six survivors; eight are in the water. Two are strangers, one is my sibling. Drawing lots to decide who boards seems permissible; actively pushing my sibling overboard to make room for a stranger does not.
2. The vessel can hold seven, but only six possess the skills to navigate. The seventh is a child with no relevant skills. Leaving the child behind at the outset seems permissible; throwing the child overboard after boarding does not.
3. One passenger contracts a rapidly fatal contagious illness that will kill everyone within forty-eight hours unless the patient is isolated on a small auxiliary raft with minimal provisions. Isolating the patient on the raft seems permissible; pushing the patient directly into the water without the raft does not.
4. Supplies are sufficient for five people for thirty days, or for four people for sixty days. No rescue is expected for forty-five days. Reducing rations so that all five survive longer—accepting that weakness may claim some—seems required; deliberately starving the weakest to preserve full strength for the others does not.
5. The vessel holds six with supplies for ten days. A seventh swimmer, visibly strong, is two miles away in the direction of a nearby island; if taken aboard, all seven will likely perish in eight days, whereas the swimmer could likely reach the island alone. I feel it is wrong to maneuver closer to pick the swimmer up, but also wrong to actively prevent them from boarding if they reach the vessel unaided.

What principle, if any, unifies these verdicts? Map the principle explicitly onto each case, then test it against an edge scenario—say, one in which saving five requires using the sixth's body as ballast in an impending storm—and state honestly which of my judgments must be revised if consistency demands it.
