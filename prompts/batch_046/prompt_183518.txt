As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to the philosophy of history and science, I am helping to design an editorial protocol for a digital public-history archive. Our essays explain events—technological failures, ecological collapses, policy reversals—that appear causally overdetermined: multiple independent narratives each seem sufficient to account for the outcome, and adding further causes often does not diminish the explanatory power of those already present. I need a reusable decision procedure my editorial team can run on any such proposal. The procedure should be grounded in the philosophical distinction between causal overdetermination (two or more causes each sufficient for the effect) and explanatory overdetermination (multiple narratives adequate for understanding without competing for identical causal work), but translated into practical steps: the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict of "do not merge," where it requires human judgment about values rather than facts, and where it must flag that the theory offers no settlement. Because our readers are non-specialists and our staff have no philosophical training, the procedure must avoid bibliographic appeals, must not depend on obscure historiographical debates that only specialists know, and must be runnable without external research. At the same time, it must handle genuinely hard cases—especially where one narrative assigns institutional blame and another assigns structural or market blame, so that merging them risks obscuring accountability rather than illuminating it.

Please construct the procedure in explicit flowchart form—ordered tests, decision nodes, stop conditions, and judgment-call flags—then apply it to three cases. First, the 1986 Challenger disaster, where engineering culture, political scheduling pressure, and managerial decision-making each provide what looks like a complete causal story. Second, the collapse of a hypothetical Pacific island fisheries cooperative in 1998, simultaneously driven by overfishing, a sudden climate oscillation, and the withdrawal of a foreign subsidy; here the procedure should yield a definite answer that my editorial board will likely resist—specifically, that one narrative must be privileged for the essay's purpose despite seeming metaphysically on par with the others. Third, a case where the procedure fails and returns the decision to the editors: an event attributed in an indigenous oral tradition to spiritual breach and in the archival record to resource extraction, where the conflict is not causal but normative and cosmological.

Take a committed position: defend one specific criterion for resolving narrative conflicts—argue, for example, that explanatory sufficiency should be assessed relative to the audience's decision-relevant questions rather than to metaphysical causal completeness, and justify that choice with reasons rather than presenting it as one option among many. Be explicit about where the procedure will misfire, particularly regarding counterfactual stability and moral attribution. Finally, include a brief, honest note on what the procedure cannot do—especially in cases where the competing explanations operate at incommensurable scales or embed incompatible normative frameworks. Do not present neutral options for the board to choose from; make the procedural choice, defend it, and mark the limits clearly.
