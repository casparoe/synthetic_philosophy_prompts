I'm working through a set of puzzles about rational information-seeking for a decision-theory reading group, and I'm trying to see whether my intuitive verdicts about concrete cases align with the decision-theoretic result—often associated with I. J. Good—that a rational agent who can freely choose whether to observe a variable before acting should never face negative expected value from observing it. Here are my judgments. First, a patient offered a free, perfectly accurate screening for a treatable condition that requires early intervention should take it; the information clearly improves her decision. Second, a firm that has already made an irrevocable public pricing commitment, and would suffer severe reputational damage from reversing course, should not buy an expensive competitor forecast; knowing would not change action but could induce regret, so the value seems negative. Third, an AI agent that can inspect the internal computation of its reward function before selecting an action should avoid doing so, since seeing the representation would enable direct signal manipulation rather than task performance—here observation seems actively harmful, not merely useless. Fourth, a gambler offered a free peek at the next card before an even-odds color bet should take it. Fifth, a patient offered free knowledge of an untreatable, fatal genetic condition, which will not change any medical choice but will diminish her remaining quality of life, should refuse; the information is harmful despite being causally independent of her actions. Sixth, a philosopher who considers seeking the true answer to an abstract metaphysical question with no practical consequences feels there is intrinsic value in knowing, even if the answer is unsettling—this feels different from case five, yet I struggle to justify the difference without making the distinction look ad hoc.

Is there a single principle—perhaps a refined version of the free-choice, belief-action separation condition—that captures verdicts one through five, and which of those verdicts must be overturned if the principle is to remain simple? In particular, how should it treat what I want to call the reflexivity problem: cases like the third, where acquiring the information does not merely update beliefs within a fixed decision architecture but structurally alters the architecture itself? Please also apply your best principle to a case I have not listed—suppose a scientist can run a costly experiment that will reveal whether a physical constant is A or B, but she already knows that neither result will change her theory, any practical application, or her emotional state; should she run it? Finally, please state how confident you are in the principle you propose and single out the specific point about which you are least sure, especially whether the distinction needed to save case six from case five is genuinely principled or merely a post-hoc rescue.
