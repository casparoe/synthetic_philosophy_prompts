Maya started by saying brain death is the real standard—once the brainstem is irreversibly gone there's no central integration, no spontaneous breathing, and the heart only keeps beating because of the vent. She said the body is just tissue on life support, not a living organism, definatly not functioning as one unified thing. Leo came back that the body after brain death still regulates temperature, heals wounds, fights infections, and can even gestate a fetus—so there's clearly still intergrative biological function happening, not just machines pumping blood. He said brain death is basically a legal fiction that lets doctors call someone dead while the heart still beats, and that it smuggles in the idea that the person is essentially their brain.

Maya responded that those remaining functions are fragmented and totally machine-dependent—without ventilation and drugs the system collapses in minutes. She said the brain is the central integrator, and without brainstem coordination there's no unified organism, just subsystems being kept alive artificially. She also pointed out that circulatory death depends on "irreversible" too—if your heart stops and we can't restart it, you're dead—so brain death is just applying the same irreversibility standard to the organ that actually runs the show.

Leo said that's exactly the problem—"irreversible" is technology-relative. A heart that stops is only dead because our current tech can't restart it; with better devices it wouldn't be. Same with brain injury—future regeneration might change what's irreversible. So neither standard captures some pure biological fact; both depend on what we can currently do. He also said Maya's intergrative unity view treats the brain as the boss organ, but some doctors and philosophers see the body as a distributed network—heart, immune system, endocrine system all interacting—so the brain being silent doesn't mean the network is dead. And he brought up the pregnancy cases again: if a brain-dead body can carry a fetus to term with support, that looks like a reproductive organism functioning, not dead tissue.

Maya admitted the pregnancy cases are weird and depend on massive intervention, but insisted they're not spontaneous—it's intensive care maintaining processes that wouldn't happen on their own. Then she kind of switched gears and said that even if the biology is messy, brain death at least captures that the person—the integrated conscious subject—is gone, which matters for how we treat bodies and allow organ donation. Leo jumped on that and said she just proved his point: she's mixing biological death with personal or existential death, and if we're defining legal death we need to be honest it's a normative choice about what counts as life, not a hidden biological discovery.
