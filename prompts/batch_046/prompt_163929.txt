Consider the fictional state of Veridia, whose Constitution includes Article 99. Article 99 reads: "This article may be amended only by the procedure described in this article; and any valid amendement to Article 99 immediately updates the general amendment procedure for all other articles to match the procedure now contained in Article 99." The exam asks: Is this a version of Alf Ross's well-known paradox about self-amending legal rules? And if a legislature tries to amend Article 99 using a different, older procedure—call this a "meta-amendement"—would Hart's idea of a rule of recognition treat that meta-amendement as valid?

Student answer:
"Article 99 clearly creates Ross's paradox because it tries to change the rules for changing rules. Ross showed that a constitutional provision that regulates its own amendment is logically impossible, like the liar paradox, because if you change the rule using the old rule you violate the new one, and if you use the new one you never had authority to invoke it. So Veridia's Article 99 is self-contradictory and can't exist in a consistent legal system.

As for Hart, he said the rule of recognition is just one fixed master rule that lists all valid sources of law. Because Article 99 makes the master rule depend on itself, Hart's framework can't handle it—there's no stable social practice to recognize. Therefore any meta-amendement that bypasses Article 99 is automatically invalid, since the rule of recognition never allows self-reference in the first place. The legislature should stick to the procedure inside Article 99, but since that procedure updates itself, they're stuck in an infinite loop with no legal exit."
