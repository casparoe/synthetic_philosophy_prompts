I'm working through the backward induction paradox in my philosophy of game theory seminar, and I'm trying to see whether my intuitions about rational play in sequential games actually cohere. Here are four cases with my verdicts.

First, the standard two-player centipede game with common knowledge of perfect rationality: I think the first player should stop immediately, since backward induction seems to erase every subsequent node.

Second, the same structure, but where it is common knowledge that both players are level-k reasoners with a known, shared bound k: I think rational play should continue precisely to node k and then stop—not at node one. So a publicly known bound on reasoning depth seems to suspend backward induction in a calibrated way.

Third, a single-agent analog where I can take $1 now or wait for payoffs that double each round, but each round carries an independent 1% risk of total mechanism failure: I think there is a specific optimal stopping round determined by expected value, not an immediate stop. The absence of a strategic opponent means backward induction doesn't apply, yet risk introduces a principled cutoff.

Fourth, a two-player version with the usual payoffs, but where before each move I observe a "confidence score" indicating how certain my opponent is that I am perfectly rational. If the score is very high, I feel I should stop immediately, anticipating that they anticipate me. But if the score is high and they have just continued, I feel I should continue, taking their move as evidence that they don't actually trust my rationality. These two reactions feel hard to reconcile.

Is there a single principle—about common knowledge, recursive belief, or strategic risk—that captures all four verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and why? Also, apply your best principle to a case I haven't listed: the same two-player game, but the confidence score is hidden and I can only infer it from my opponent's deliberation speed. What should I do at node 2?
