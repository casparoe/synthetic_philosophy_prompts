Consider the one-liner that captures the so-called paradox of tolerance: that if a society extends unlimited tolerance even to the intolerant, the intolerant will eventually destroy the tolerant society, and tolerance with them. In university governance debates, this is routinely invoked to justify institutional refusal—denying platforms, disinviting speakers, or sanctioning student groups—when those targeted advocate positions that reject democratic deliberation itself. Without requesting bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, disambiguate the precise theses this slogan could be advancing. Present your analysis as a menu of at least four distinct readings: a descriptive historical-sociological claim about the collapse of liberal orders; a normative claim about the moral incoherence of unconditional tolerance; a strategic claim about institutional survival under adversarial conditions; and a conceptual claim about the self-defeating structure of a principle that includes its own negation. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or ambiguous, and specify which reading—if any—would actually license the specific administrative act of preemptively excluding a speaker whose platform includes the abolition of the forum in which they speak. Then, introduce a distinction rarely made in standard treatments: separate first-order intolerance (the intolerant speech or position being tolerated) from second-order intolerance (the institutional decision not to tolerate that speech). Examine whether the paradox applies symmetrically across both orders, or whether there is a hidden asymmetry—perhaps involving the agency of the institution versus the agency of the speaker—that makes the slogan misleading when used to justify institutional exclusion. Conclude by saying which precisification of the slogan best fits the administrative context, which ones are red herrings, and what feature of the idea remains unaddressed by the slogan regardless of reading.
