I am serving as a grader for an advanced undergraduate exam in evolutionary biology and philosophy of science. The exam question asked students to: "Explain Gould and Lewontin's critique of the adaptationist program, with particular attention to the San Marco spandrel analogy, to the conceptual distinction between spandrels and exaptations in Gould and Vrba's later formulation, and to what this implies about the proper burden of proof when testing evolutionary hypotheses—specifically whether structural constraint or genetic drift should serve as a null baseline."

One student submitted the following answer:

"Gould and Lewontin draw on the architectural spandrels of San Marco—triangular spaces that necessarily appear when a dome is placed on rounded arches—to argue that many biological traits are not direct adaptations but unavoidable structural byproducts. A spandrel carries no adaptive design of its own; it arises solely as a side effect of selection acting on other features. They contrast spandrels with exaptations, which are traits originally shaped for one function and later co-opted for another. Crucially, spandrels differ from exaptations in that they can never become the target of selection, even secondarily: once a spandrel is co-opted, it is no longer a spandrel but a different kind of feature altogether. The broader methodological point is that adaptationists erroneously treat selection as the default explanation. To correct this, Gould and Lewontin propose that evolutionary biologists should adopt spandrel formation and drift as the null hypotheses, requiring that any selectionist claim be tested against and conclusively distinguished from these non-adaptive baselines. Their position is therefore best described as anti-adaptationist: they maintain that natural selection is rarely the primary driver of morphological traits, and that pluralistic explanations should be relegated to secondary status only after spandrel and drift accounts have failed."
