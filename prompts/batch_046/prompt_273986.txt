I'm running a single fifty-minute session for second-year philosophy students focused narrowly on Descartes's cogito and the precise scope of what it establishes. The students arrive with the popular-culture version: that it proves a soul, or that it is obviously circular, or that it is a tautology. Plan the explanatory arc: what conceptual distinction must be secured before they can see the argument as anything other than an inference with a hidden premise (and why the syllogism reconstruction should be deferred, not introduced first), which carrying example—perhaps comparing the certainty of a particular mental occurrence to the certainty of a bodily presence—should carry the distinction between the occurrence of thinking and the existence of a substantial thinker, the predictable misconceptions in the order they will arise (substance dualism, circularity, tautology) and the exact point in the sequence where each should be intercepted, one diagnostic question that distinguishes understanding the scope from mere repetition, and what must be deliberately omitted at this level (for instance, the full Meditations architecture or the later proofs of God) with a brief justification for leaving each out.
