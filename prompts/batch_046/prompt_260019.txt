Consider Hannah Arendt's implicit rulings across her discussions of political falsehood—especially in her essays on factual truth and the Pentagon Papers—regarding four cases.

First, she treats the traditional concealment of specific military or diplomatic intentions from an enemy state, the classic arcana imperii directed outward, as belonging to a category distinct from modern political lying; she does not direct her central moral critique at this form of secrecy.

Second, she condemns the Pentagon Papers pattern: government officials systematically deceiving their own citizens, representatives, and allies about the progress of a war in order to preserve an image of inevitable victory and to shield decision-makers from accountability. She regards this as the paradigmatic modern lie because it substitutes a constructed image for factual reality within the shared public realm on which democratic judgment depends.

Third, she condemns the suppression or reclassification of internal historical records documenting a past failed policy, where the concealment serves not to protect active operational secrets but to defend an institutional narrative of competence. She treats this as a corruption of the historical record that destroys the ground for informed opinion, structurally akin to the Pentagon deception.

Fourth, she examines a wartime administration that reports false casualty figures to the domestic press during an active rescue operation, but issues fully corrected figures immediately upon the operation's conclusion. She treats this as closer to tactical concealment than to modern image-lying, though she warns that it collapses into the latter category if the correction is withheld or if the deception serves to protect the administration's reputation rather than the operation itself.

Now consider a new case. A democratically elected government, convinced that a severe ecological threat requires immediate carbon-reduction legislation, deliberately publishes a single, discrete falsified scientific assessment—exaggerating the immediate causation of one specific weather event—to its own citizens and press. The purpose is neither to conceal state secrets nor to defend the administration's image or decision-making process, but solely to generate rapid democratic support for a narrowly tailored, temporary tax measure. The government never hides its authorship of the report; it fully corrects the scientific record in a public audit within one year; and the falsehood concerns only an external physical fact, not the government's own actions or competence.

What do you think Arendt would say about this ecological falsehood—would she condemn it as a modern political lie, treat it as an acceptable tactical deviation, or place it in a separate category altogether? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the rule she seems to be running across the four earlier cases, the other rules that fit those verdicts just as well, and which of the earlier cases best distinguishes them. Identify the closest analog among the four precedents, and state honestly what principle Arendt would be adopting whichever way she rules. Finally, name one further hypothetical case that would most clearly separate the leading rules.
