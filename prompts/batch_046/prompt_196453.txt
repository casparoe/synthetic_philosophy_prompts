I am preparing a seminar on vagueness and would like to test my own framing of the interaction between epistemicism and supervaluationism. As I understand it, Williamson's epistemicist account rejects semantic indeterminacy altogether: the predicate 'bald' possesses a precise boundary—say, at exactly 3,247 hairs—but our epistemic limitations prevent us from knowing its location. Supervaluationists, by contrast, deny that any single boundary is correct; they preserve the tolerance principle by distributing sharp boundaries across admissible precisifications, none of which is uniquely authoritative. My question concerns a formal extension of this contrast. Suppose we model the tolerance principle not as a bare logical axiom but as a continuity condition on a metric space of precisifications, so that incremental changes in hair-count yield incremental shifts in the set of admissible precisifications. On this model, epistemicism appears to violate continuity: a single hair's removal can cause an abrupt, globally uniform flip in the unknown sharp boundary, with no intermediate precisificational state. Supervaluationism, conversely, respects continuity because no one precisification is privileged. Given this framing, must the epistemicist reject the topological structure of the precisification space, or can she argue that the unknown boundary itself varies continuously across epistemic possibilities? Furthermore, if vagueness is ultimately a feature of our representational apparatus rather than of the world, what justification remains for imposing any metric or topological structure upon the space of precisifications in the first place?
