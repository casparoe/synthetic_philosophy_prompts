Since I recently began volunteering to catalog the contents of a decommissioned community workshop—where every tool bore marks of improvised repair and several machines had been deliberately disabled before abandonment—I have become preoccupied with how archaeologists move from physical objects to the beliefs and institutions that produced them. I am drafting an essay that challenges the standard "symbolic correspondence" model, according to which grave goods, ritual deposits, and modified artifacts are read as direct expressions of cosmological belief. Instead, I propose a framework I call "frictional inference": treating wear patterns, anomalous repairs, and ritual "killing" of objects not as symbols to be decoded but as side-channel traces—physical residues that reveal where an institution failed to fully determine behavior. Just as a cryptographer infers a system's internal logic from timing leaks and power fluctuations rather than from the message itself, I suggest archaeologists should read institutional tension from material friction: broken tools left in working spaces, over-repaired items that outlived their users' needs, or deliberately destroyed goods that signal social dissent rather than piety. The essay focuses on two comparative cases: the ritual destruction of weapons in Scandinavian bog deposits and the anomalous repair histories of grinding stones at certain Neolithic settlement sites. I want to argue that these patterns do not primarily attest to shared belief in an afterlife or in a sacred economy, but rather index points where social roles exceeded or conflicted with the material regimes meant to stabilize them.
