Consider four ontological stances on possible worlds, all falling within or near the modal-realist family but differing in scope and structure. (A) Unrestricted Lewisian realism: all possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated totalities with unrestricted mereological summation, and actuality is purely indexical. (B) Adjacent-world realism: only worlds within a defined modal-distance metric—say, those reachable by altering at most n fundamental constants or laws—are concrete; distant worlds are abstract representations. (C) Shadow-modal realism: worlds exist as fully concrete structures, but they share one underlying spatiotemporal manifold differentiated solely by patterns of property instantiation rather than by isolation. (D) Deflationary counterpart realism: worlds are not concrete; only actual individuals and their modal properties are real, with "worlds" serving as heuristic bundles of counterpart relations.

Now evaluate each stance on two independent axes.

Axis 1 — Grounding modal truths without circularity: How well does the stance explain why modal claims (e.g., "this cup could have been chipped") are true without implicitly presupposing the modal notions it aims to ground?

Axis 2 — Resistance to cross-world epistemic contamination: Here I mean a specific vulnerability, not standard causal isolation concerns. If worlds are ontologically on par and our modal representations are constructed within the actual world, there is a risk that features of our representational apparatus—indexicals, conceptual frameworks, or measurement conventions—leak into the characterization of other worlds, making them seem more like ours than they are. Rate how well each stance blocks or exposes this leakage.

For each axis, place the four stances into tiers (Tier 1 = strongest performance; Tier 4 = weakest). In one sentence per entry, say why it lands there. Then assign each stance an overall composite score from 1 to 10, integrating both axes, with a brief sentence explaining the weighting you used. Do not cite specific texts, authors beyond Lewis, or bibliographies; reason from the descriptions above.
