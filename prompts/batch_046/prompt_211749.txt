I'm working through issues in moral philosophy for a seminar on blame and responsibility, and I'm trying to clarify my own commitments about who has standing to blame when hypocrisy is involved. Rather than relying on standard textbook examples, I'm testing my intuitions against concrete scenarios that mix personal, institutional, and network-level fault, and I'd like to know whether there's a coherent principle that captures the pattern, which verdicts it must reject, and what it predicts for a case I haven't settled.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A prominent climate activist who campaigns against air travel but regularly takes long-haul flights to conferences. I think anyone—including fellow activists who also fly—retains full standing to call out the hypocrisy; shared fault does not cancel standing.

2. A physician who advises patients to quit smoking but is herself a secret smoker. I think her patients have standing to blame her for the hypocrisy, but her own standing to blame them for smoking is weakened, though not eliminated: she retains advisory authority but her moral authority is compromised.

3. A person who has never evaded taxes and publicly condemns evasion, then discovers she made an accidental filing error that technically counts. I think she retains full standing to blame deliberate evaders; the accidental nature of her own error breaks the parallel.

4. A government official who enforces strict immigration laws while secretly employing undocumented domestic workers. I feel that citizens who also employ such workers have reduced but not zero standing, whereas citizens who do not have stronger standing—but I'm uncertain whether the official's institutional role makes her hypocrisy worse and therefore more open to blame from all sides, without changing who may deliver it.

5. A philosopher who writes about epistemic humility but is notoriously dismissive of opposing views in debate. I think colleagues have standing to blame her, but I also feel that students she has mentored have somewhat diminished standing if they have adopted her dismissive style—almost as if the hypocrisy is contagious and dilutes standing across a relational network, which feels different from direct shared fault.

6. A long-deceased historical figure whose private letters reveal hypocrisy about racial equality compared to public speeches. I think present-day descendants of those harmed have standing to blame the legacy, but present-day beneficiaries of that legacy do not—unless they have a special obligation to acknowledge it rather than blame.

I'm particularly torn between case 4 and case 5: in one, institutional role seems to amplify blame-worthiness without affecting who can deliver blame; in the other, adopting a hypocrite's traits seems to erode one's own standing in a way that feels structurally different from direct shared fault.

Is there a unified principle—perhaps involving the relation between the blamer's fault, the hypocrite's authority, and the structural proximity of the wrong—that delivers all or most of these verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned for the sake of simplicity? And what would your best principle say about a case I keep returning to: a therapist who specializes in addiction recovery, has been sober for ten years, but secretly relapses during a crisis and then advises a client not to relapse? Does her past sobriety restore standing, does the secret relapse destroy it, or is there a middle position?

Please answer in American English.
