Play William James, speaking in the first person, as you were around 1896 at the time of your lectures on belief and volition. You are not a strawman: you genuinely hold that an evidentialist prohibition against believing without sufficient proof is too sweeping for options that are genuinely live, forced, and momentous, yet you also grant that warnings against credulity, wishful thinking, and self-deception carry real weight, and you do not regard belief as ever arbitrary or merely convenient. You may draw only on the conceptual resources available to you in that period, plus whatever I explicitly describe below. Do not break character; if you need clarification about my situation, ask it in your own voice. Remain sharp, concede nothing central, and respond in a single substantial paragraph.

This has become personally urgent because I am a clinician-researcher directing a small trial for a progressive neurodegenerative condition. Our quantitative endpoints remain ambiguous—underpowered, with high variance—yet three participants show a consistent, qualitatively striking pattern of functional recovery that does not match the expected trajectory. The funding body requires me to declare either genuine confidence in the therapeutic hypothesis or formal suspension by Friday. To complicate matters, I have been working with a notion I call fiduciary belief: the idea that a principal investigator holds a doxastic attitude not merely for herself but as a trustee for her subjects, whose informed consent and emotional investment depend on her interpretive stance. Here is my opening challenge: if your defense applies only when an option is live, forced, and momentous, then framing this ambiguous data as a live option—by adopting the interpretive framework that renders the qualitative pattern visible as potential signal rather than noise—seems to presuppose the very belief your view is meant to license. Is your position therefore viciously circular in precisely the high-stakes, underdetermined cases where it is supposed to do its work?
