A colleague in a law-and-philosophy reading group summarized the Hart–Devlin debate like this: Devlin held that every society is bound together by a shared moral fabric, and that private acts like homosexuality or adultery, even if consensual and hidden, erode that fabric and therefore threaten social disintegration; consequently, the criminal law should enforce the community’s dominant morality whenever it reaches a critical level of collective intolerance, indignation, and disgust. Hart, in reply, invoked Mill’s harm principle: the state may coerce individuals only to prevent harm to others, not to protect them from themselves or to enforce popular morality; since private immorality produces no tangible injury, moral revulsion alone can never justify punishment. From this, Hart concluded that legal moralism is not merely empirically unsupported but conceptually authoritarian, and that a liberal state must reject all moral legislation as illegitimate interference with liberty.

The summary sounds mostly right to me, but I worry it flattens both thinkers. Is this a fair account? Please distinguish what is essentially accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what subtly misrepresents Hart or Devlin—especially regarding Hart’s actual attitude toward moral legislation and whether Devlin’s argument depends on a claim about tangible harm. Finally, does the summary’s conclusion that Hart “showed legal moralism collapses into authoritarianism” actually follow from the accurate parts?
