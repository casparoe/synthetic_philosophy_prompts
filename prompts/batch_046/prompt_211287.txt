I am a cognitive psychologist who, over the past eighteen months, restructured my research program away from a representational-computational framework toward an embodied-enactive approach. The transition has been intellectually disorienting: experiments I once designed now seem to presuppose a vocabulary of internal representations that my current collaborators treat as category mistakes, and I am unsure whether we are disagreeing about evidence or speaking past one another. This experience has made the problem of Kuhnian incommensurability urgent for me in a concrete, professional way, not merely as a historical thesis about physics.

With that context in mind, draft the joint statement that would result from an adversarial collaboration between two camps regarding this specific case. The first camp, call them the Strong Incommensurabilists, holds that the representationalist and embodied-enactive paradigms in cognitive science lack any paradigm-neutral observation language: descriptions of behavioral data, neural recordings, or subjective reports are already theory-laden in ways that prevent direct comparison, so rational adjudication requires something closer to conversion than to argument. The second camp, call them the Local Translationists, holds that incommensurability is always local and partial: for any specific dispute—say, about the role of working memory in visual search—one can construct a temporary bridge vocabulary that preserves the empirical content of both sides, allowing piecemeal rational comparison through shared predictive successes and anomaly resolution.

The deliverable should be a single formal document structured as follows.

First, a numbered section of Agreed Claims—statements both camps can sign without reservation. These should cover: (a) what Kuhn originally claimed about paradigm shifts and incommensurability, correctly characterized without straw-manning either side's reading; (b) the actual differences between the representationalist and embodied-enactive research programs in contemporary cognitive psychology, stated in terms both sides recognize; (c) what incommensurability is not—for instance, it is not mere difficulty in translation, not mere theoretical disagreement within a shared framework, and not irrationality; and (d) the existence of genuine conceptual change in the history of cognitive science.

Second, a Disagreements Table. For each residual disagreement, provide: (i) the precise statement of the Strong Incommensurabilist position, phrased in language they would accept; (ii) the precise statement of the Local Translationist position, phrased in language they would accept; and (iii) a brief diagnosis of whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal—if verbal, say exactly which terms are being used differently and how.

Third, a section of Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would move at least one camp significantly closer to the other. For each crux, specify: (a) the crux itself, stated neutrally; (b) a concrete case, experiment, or historical episode both camps agree is relevant—for example, a specific debate about whether mirror-neuron data support direct perception or internal simulation, or about whether predictive-processing models can accommodate enactive claims; and (c) what each camp predicts about that case—what outcome or interpretation they would expect, recorded in advance so that the predictions are genuinely risky for each side.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not invoke obscure or highly specialized theories that a well-informed generalist in philosophy of science would not recognize; keep the conceptual apparatus within the reach of standard Kuhnian scholarship and mainstream cognitive science. Where technical vocabulary is needed—such as theory-ladenness, bridge manual, or anomaly resolution—define it briefly within the document so that the statement remains self-contained.

The tone should be that of a collaborative text both parties could put their names to: neither polemical nor adjudicating, but making the remaining disagreement as exact as possible, and separating disagreements that are genuinely substantive from those that were about words.
