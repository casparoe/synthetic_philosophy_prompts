In my philosophy-of-mind reading group, a debate about Douglas Hofstadter's claim that the self is a strange loop—a self-referential pattern rather than a substance—has stalled for months. One side holds that if such a loop were sustained continuously across two brains via a direct bidirectional neural bridge maintaining a single integrated self-model, there would be one distributed 'I', not two copies. The other side holds that the first-person perspective is necessarily indexed to a single spatiotemporal trajectory; distributing the loop would merely create two partial self-models, each with its own 'I', regardless of causal integration. Both sides reject Cartesian egos, both accept the neuroscience of self-modeling, and both agree that no actual human brains are linked this way. The disagreement persists unchanged.

What kind of stall is this? Is it a hidden empirical question about which continuity conditions are necessary for personal identity, masked by agreement on the metaphysics of patterns? A verbal dispute about whether 'distributed self' means 'shared loop' versus 'fragmented loop'? A clash of bedrock intuitions about the irreducibility of the first-person point of view that neither side can argue for? Or are the camps answering different questions—one about metaphysical identity over time, the other about phenomenological ownership—under the single heading 'persistence of the self in others'? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement with reference to what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal model of loop continuity, a refined thought experiment, a conceptual distinction that dissolves the conflict, or an argument that the impasse is principled and unresolvable.
