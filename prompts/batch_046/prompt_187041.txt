I am grading take-home exams for a graduate seminar on strategic reasoning and epistemic logic. One problem presented a three-player perfect-information game with a public-announcement mechanism, in which each player possesses both substantive rationality (maximizing expected utility given her beliefs) and procedural rationality (updating beliefs correctly upon new information). The scenario specified that a public announcement reveals Player 2 remains substantively rational—she maximizes given her beliefs—but fails procedural rationality, since she does not revise her beliefs about Player 3's preferences. Students were asked to determine whether Player 3 can still apply backward induction at her own node, whether the failure is localized or global, and how common knowledge of rationality operates separately across these two dimensions.

The student submitted the following answer: Backward induction requires common knowledge of rationality at every node. In this game, rationality has two parts: substantive rationality, which means maximizing given your beliefs, and procedural rationality, which means updating beliefs correctly. The announcement reveals that Player 2 lacks procedural rationality—she doesn't update correctly—but she is still substantively rational, since she maximizes given her wrong beliefs. Because CKR requires that all players are fully rational in both dimensions, the failure of Player 2's procedural rationality destroys CKR globally. Once one link in the chain is broken, the infinite hierarchy of mutual belief collapses for the entire tree. Therefore, Player 3 can no longer apply backward induction. Even though Player 2 is still substantively rational, Player 3 cannot predict her move, because procedural failure means Player 2's beliefs about Player 3 are arbitrary. In fact, without full CKR, Player 3 lacks any basis for predicting Player 2's choice. This also shows that finite-order mutual beliefs are insufficient. Even if Player 3 has second- or third-order beliefs about Player 2's substantive rationality, without the full common knowledge including procedural rationality, backward induction is impossible. This aligns with Aumann's agreement result: rational coordination requires common priors not just over outcomes but over belief-revision procedures, and without them players can rationally disagree about the path. Thus, the announcement eliminates the unique backward-induction solution for all players, not just Player 2.
