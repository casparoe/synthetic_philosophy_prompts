In philosophy of mind and epistemology, G. E. Moore's proof of an external world — holding up his hands and declaring that here is one hand and here is another, therefore the external world exists — is frequently compared to an engineer performing a ground-truth injection: rather than debugging a system through internal diagnostics, one introduces a verified baseline datum from outside to reset all downstream inference. The comparison is used to argue that scepticism can be answered not by inferential repair but by direct presentation of an undeniable reference point, and that the sceptic, like a miscalibrated instrument, owes an explanation for the discrepancy rather than the demonstrator owing a proof. In particular, does the analogy smuggle in an assumption that the sceptic's belief-set is malfunctioning in a measurable way, and does it quietly depend on a shared framework for what counts as a valid reference point that Moore's audience may not share?
