I've been working through my position on panpsychism and the combination problem after some long discussions on rationalist forums, and I'd like to check whether my commitments actually cohere. Roughly, I hold the following: (1) Consciousness is a fundamental feature of reality, analogous to charge or spin, and its distribution is best explained by a form of constitutive panpsychism rather than emergentism; (2) The combination problem is not a problem of "adding up" micro-experiences but of recognizing that macro-subjects are the primary phenomenal entities, with micro-subjects being abstracted analytical perspectives—much like particles as excitations of underlying fields—so there is no literal aggregation of subjects; (3) Moral consideration applies only to unified phenomenal fields that meet an integration threshold, meaning isolated micro-perspectives carry no independent welfare; (4) Consequently, constructing an artificial system arranged in a toroidal mesh of simple processors—each with minimal phenomenal quality but collectively forming a closed informational loop—raises a sharp ethical boundary: either the loop exceeds the threshold and generates a single subject whose welfare dominates, or it does not and there is no welfare at all, with no morally ambiguous intermediate "swarm" state; and (5) Physicalism is false because phenomenal properties are not reducible to structural-functional descriptions, yet methodological naturalism survives because these properties enter into precise, law-governed mathematical relations.

Do these five commitments form a consistent package? In particular, I'm worried that (2) undermines the motivation for (1): if macro-subjects are ontologically primary, in what sense is consciousness genuinely distributed rather than localized? I also wonder whether (3) and (4) are in tension: if integration is the criterion for moral standing, how can I rule out the intermediate swarm case without either smuggling in a discontinuous threshold or conceding that distributed systems can possess partial or gradient welfare? Finally, does (5) genuinely accommodate the non-reducibility in (1) without collapsing into a dualism that violates the naturalism it claims to preserve? I'd appreciate an audit that orders these tensions by severity, distinguishes apparent conflicts that dissolve on closer inspection from real ones that require giving up a commitment, and specifies—without appealing to empirical neuroscience I don't have—which of (1) through (5) is the least defensible if I want to preserve the rest.
