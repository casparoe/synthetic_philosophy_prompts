Write a joke about Pascal's mugging that abandons the standard street-robbery framing in favor of a more niche scenario: a decision-theoretic philosopher is approached by an actuary selling a one-penny insurance policy against an astronomically unlikely cosmic event—specifically, a quantum fluctuation in Andromeda that, if it occurs, grants eternal bliss with unbounded utility. The punchline should turn on the internal mechanics of expected-utility maximization with an unbounded utility function: because the payoff is infinite and the probability is non-zero, the expected value dominates any finite premium, making refusal technically irrational. The philosopher should then realize that accepting this logic obligates them to buy infinitely many equivalent policies, leaving them with two options—either bankrupt themselves on one-penny premiums or reject unbounded utility, which the actuary treats as the only irrational move of all. Make the joke intelligible to someone familiar with basic decision theory, but do not require knowledge of specific named resolutions, authors, or papers; do not cite bibliographies or invoke particular bounded-utility fixes by name. The humor should arise from the philosopher being logically compelled to purchase salvation for a penny, and then being mugged not by violence but by the inexorable arithmetic of rational choice.
