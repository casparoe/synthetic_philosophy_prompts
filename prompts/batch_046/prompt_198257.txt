Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers of mind—Dr. Kessler, who holds that phenomenal consciousness is best understood as a "user illusion" generated by predictive processing, in which the brain constructs an agent-centered virtual interface that presents computational outputs as directly given without access to the underlying machinery; and Dr. Voss, who contends that calling this an "illusion" conflates representational mediation with absence of contact, and that first-person acquaintance with mental states is a genuine, non-propositional mode of presentation rather than a fabricated user-model.

The exchange must center on a specific hypothetical device: a "phenomenal inversion module," a cognitive prosthesis that allows one subject to map the representational architecture of another onto her own qualitative framework, so that she reports experiencing the other person's inverted color spectrum as if it were her own. Kessler should argue that the module demonstrates consciousness is portable representational content—there is no private, non-illusory core, only transferable user-models that can be coupled across brains. Voss should reply that if the module truly transfers experience, it shows acquaintance can be coupled to foreign substrates without being duplicated, which supports rather than undermines its reality as a direct relational fact; if it transfers only information, then the subject never genuinely accesses the other's consciousness, confirming that direct acquaintance is incommunicable and therefore not an illusion but a primitive boundary.

Require real back-and-forth: Kessler must directly address Voss's distinction between coupling and duplication, explaining why portability still favors constructivism even if duplication fails. Voss must directly answer Kessler's portability argument, specifying whether the module's success or failure reveals a non-representational substrate or merely extends representational misattribution across subjects. The dialogue should end not with resolution but with a sharply drawn crux: whether intersubjective phenomenal mapping undermines the privacy of acquaintance or only expands the domain of predictive self-modeling. Keep both voices rigorous, avoid straw positions, and do not invoke citations, bibliographies, or named texts beyond the conceptual framework described.
