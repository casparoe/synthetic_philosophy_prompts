As a novelist working on a manuscript about grief, memory, and whether a person can survive inside those who loved them, I have assembled the following philosophical commitments and need a consistency audit before I build further plot around them. Please approach this as an expert in philosophy of mind and personal identity, with particular attention to the problem of distributed selves and symbolic persistence along Hofstadterian lines. Begin with a brief brainstorming phase in which you map the logical relations among the commitments; then proceed to the structured audit.

Here are my commitments:

(1) I am a physicalist about minds: mental properties depend on physical organization, and there is no non-physical ego or soul.

(2) The self is fundamentally a narrative construct — the "I" is the protagonist of an ongoing story that is partly authored by others, not a private substance discovered within.

(3) I accept a view in the spirit of Hofstadter that a self can be distributed or copied across brains: if another person genuinely internalizes my patterns of judgment, memory, emotional response, and symbolic associations — carrying what one might call my careenium — then in a meaningful sense I can persist in that other brain after my death, and possibly across multiple brains.

(4) At the same time, I hold that first-person phenomenal consciousness is essentially private and non-transferable: there is no coherent sense in which my subjective what-it-is-likeness could be duplicated or shared in another body.

(5) I take psychological continuity to be the correct criterion for personal identity over time, but I allow that continuity can branch: if my patterns are copied into two different people, both can be partial continuers of me, with no metaphysical fact of the matter about which is the real successor.

(6) I believe social recognition and intersubjective acknowledgment are constitutive of selfhood: without being mirrored, named, and responded to by others, there is no robust "I" to speak of.

(7) I maintain that if my closest companion, after my death, came to embody my patterns so thoroughly that she finished my sentences, adopted my evaluative framework, and carried forward my unfinished projects, this would be not merely a tribute but a genuine continuation of my self — singular, not one branch among equals.

Beyond these, I have been developing a more niche conceptual tool for the novel: the idea of mnemonic resonance. By this I mean a cross-brain synchronization of emotional tone and associative structure that is stronger than ordinary empathy but weaker than full pattern-copying. I think this resonance can occur between living people — long-married couples, for instance — without either losing their distinct "I"s; yet I also think that when it reaches a certain intensity after one person's death, it becomes identity-preserving rather than merely empathetic.

Please audit this set. Identify which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled with minimal adjustment, and which are mutually reinforcing. For each genuine tension, rank its severity and specify the cheapest revision — exactly which commitment must be modified and at what conceptual cost. In particular, examine whether my acceptance of branching continuity in (5) undermines my singular-continuation claim in (7); whether physicalism in (1) can comfortably accommodate both distributed patterns in (3) and the privacy of consciousness in (4); and whether mnemonic resonance introduces new tensions with either social recognition (6) or the non-transferability claim (4). Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most tension across the entire package.
