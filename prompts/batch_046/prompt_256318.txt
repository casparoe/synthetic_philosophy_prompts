In our philosophy of history and ethics course we have been examining a long-standing stall in debates about moral revolutions: specifically, whether norms such as Atlantic slavery and duelling collapsed because moral argument revealed them as wrong, or because shifts in honour codes and economic interest made them unsustainable. The literature pits rationalist explanations against Bernard Williams’s scepticism about moral argument’s causal efficacy and Kwame Anthony Appiah’s honour-based account, yet both sides cite the same abolitionist texts and the chronology of duelling’s decline without convergence. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not archival but conceptual — it concerns explanatory exclusion, namely whether demonstrating that honour incentives changed is compatible with demonstrating that people came to see slavery as wrong, or whether one explanation necessarily crowds out the other, structurally analogous to debates about mental causation. Is this diagnosis correct? Or is the disagreement hidden in empirical details about private motivations, a verbal dispute over what counts as a genuine moral revolution rather than a fashion, or bedrock intuitions about human agency that neither side can argue for? I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is, supported by what each side actually claims, together with an honest assessment of whether a conceptual distinction, a reframing of the question, or nothing at all could move the debate. Please answer in British English, and state clearly what consideration would change your mind and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
