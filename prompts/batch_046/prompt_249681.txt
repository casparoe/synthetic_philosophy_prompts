There's a well-established practice in philosophy of mind—across textbooks, exam questions, and journal articles—of treating Mary's room as the canonical stress-test for physicalism. The setup is standardized: Mary is stipulated to possess complete physical knowledge about color vision (neuroscience, physics, functional roles), she is released and sees red for the first time, and we ask whether she learns something new. The practice treats this as a direct probe: if she learns a new fact, physicalism is threatened; if she only gains an ability or acquaintance, physicalism survives.

What strikes me as philosophically loaded is not the case itself, but the institutional selectivity with which it is deployed. We do not run structurally parallel "room" arguments for other domains. For instance, we never present "Georgina," a mathematician who has memorized every theorem and proof in chess endgame theory—including all computational properties of optimal play—but who has never actually played or watched a game, and ask whether she learns something genuinely new when she first executes a winning move. Similarly, we do not present an interoceptive analog—say, a physician with exhaustive physiological knowledge of nausea who has never experienced it—to test whether physicalism about bodily states fails. Meanwhile, within the very same syllabi, knowledge-how is cordoned off via Ryle's distinction and explicitly protected from Mary's-style reasoning.

What implicit theory of epistemic completeness and of phenomenal uniqueness does this selective deployment presuppose? Must the practice assume that phenomenal knowledge is the only kind susceptible to a "complete knowledge plus first exposure" gap, or is it compatible with the view that Mary's case works only because "physical information" is tacitly defined to exclude first-person functional execution?
