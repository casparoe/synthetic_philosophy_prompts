Just-war theorists have been deadlocked for decades over the moral equality of combatants: traditionalists, following Walzer, hold that soldiers on both sides possess equal rights to kill in battle so long as they observe jus in bello, since individuals are not responsible for the justice of the cause; revisionists, following McMahan, deny this, arguing that unjust combatants are liable to be killed while just combatants are not, so there is no moral symmetry in the right to fight. Despite extensive refinements regarding collective responsibility, epistemic excuses, and the separation of jus ad bellum from jus in bello, the core disagreement remains intact. My hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about liability, but about whether moral permissions can be institutionally delegated—whether a state or military structure can confer upon individuals a permission to kill that those individuals would not possess acting alone, analogous to how judicial authority derives from office rather than personal desert. Is this diagnosis accurate, or is the deadlock driven instead by a hidden disagreement about the metaphysics of collective agency, about the empirical conditions of soldiers' epistemic access to the justice of their cause, or about what counts as a valid excuse? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually claims, together with an account of what kind of progress—whether a conceptual distinction, an institutional analysis, or a change of question—would be appropriate given that diagnosis, or a reasoned argument that the dispute is ill-posed and should not be expected to resolve.
