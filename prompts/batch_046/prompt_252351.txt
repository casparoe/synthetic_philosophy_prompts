I work in software engineering with no formal background in philosophy, but I have been thinking about the absent-minded driver problem. I have come to accept a position that reframes the paradox away: the driver should be modeled not as a fully rational agent with perfect recall of his own plan, but as a bounded-rational agent in a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process. Because he lacks observational access to whether he has already passed the first exit, his belief state at the second node is genuinely ambiguous, and exiting there maximizes expected utility conditional on that belief. On this view, there is no deep conflict between causal and evidential decision theory; the behavior is locally optimal given incomplete state information, analogous to a stateless service processing requests without session history. What would count against this position? I want an honest assessment of its exposure: which formal properties of POMDPs fail to map onto the philosophical case, which objections from decision theorists about precommitment, planning, or the value of consistency survive this reframing, and whether results in human cognition or AI planning support or undermine treating absent-mindedness as an observability failure rather than a rationality failure. Please distinguish decisive defeaters from merely awkward ones, indicate what a defender of causal decision theory would concede, and say whether this framing risks insulating itself by redefining the problem away.
