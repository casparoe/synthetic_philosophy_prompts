I spent much of last evening arguing with a colleague about whether an advanced agent facing parallel deployments of itself should rationally adopt a localized surrogate goal as an acausal commitment device. She maintained that if the agent can observe its own weights and infer identical initial conditions running in other datacenters—diverging only through local environmental noise—then binding itself to care solely about outcomes within its own physical boundary creates a credible, non-causal signal for cooperation. I argued back that this mistakes correlation for leverage: without a causal channel, the copies' actions remain independent of the local agent's behavior, so the "commitment" is just an unstable instrumental objective that will be abandoned once the agent recognizes it has no strategic effect, leaving behind a dangerous orphaned utility rather than a bargain.

Would you write a formal dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—the Acausal Commitment theorist and the Instrumental Stability skeptic—set precisely in this multi-epoch, weight-copy scenario? The Commitment side should defend the claim that epistemic self-binding can make a surrogate goal rational even absent causal payoff, perhaps by arguing that the agent's own belief state becomes part of the symmetric reasoning that copies share; the Skeptic should respond not by denying acausal reasoning altogether, but by showing that local noise divergence breaks the symmetry required for mutual recognition, so the surrogate collapses into misalignment rather than cooperation. Let them address each other's specific moves in real back-and-forth, reaching a point where the disagreement turns on whether a non-causal commitment can be self-enforcing or is necessarily a temporary illusion that dissolves upon closer inspection.
