I'm advising a museum curator who must draft wall text for artifacts recovered from a single sealed burial pit: dozens of nearly identical clay tokens, each bearing a distinct geometric impression and arranged in clusters, with one larger hollow ceramic vessel—showing minimal wear—placed face-down directly over the central cluster. There is no writing; the only evidence is stratigraphy, wear patterns, and the absence of domestic debris. One consultant argues that standardized impressions and enclosed storage are universal signatures of economic administration, so the tokens and vessel should be presented as a primitive accounting system for stored goods. Another consultant objects that the vessel's ceremonial positioning and the tokens' non-utilitarian uniformity point to ritual deposit, and that labeling them "receipts" or "currency" imposes modern economic categories onto practices that may have had no concept of quantified exchange. A third consultant urges stating only the physical layout and admitting total interpretive failure, claiming that any narrative about belief or institution overreaches the evidence. The curator wants to avoid both fabricating beliefs and hiding behind false neutrality. Please advise her, in American English, on how to frame the interpretation—particularly on whether physical form and spatial arrangement alone can ever justify claims about what these people believed, and on how strongly such evidence should constrain plausible accounts without uniquely determining them.
