I was at this philosophy meetup with my friends Ravi and Nina after we watched some documentary about people who bet everything on one idea. Ravi said something like, if there's even a tiny chance of an outcome that's massively good — maybe not literally infinite, but like curing aging or saving a million people — then expected value says you should chase it, even if you'll probably fail and lose everything. Nina called that EV fanaticism and said it's how you justify doing stupid things. They went back and forth for a while and I couldn't follow all of it. Here's roughly what they said:

Ravi: Look, decision theory is clear. You multiply value by probability. If saving a million lives is the prize and the chance is 0.001%, the expected value is still huge — bigger than saving ten lives for sure. So if you're being rational, you pick the longshot.

Nina: But that's exactly the fanatacism people talk about. You're treating a near-certain disaster as if it's just a rounding error. For the people who actually suffer in the 99.999% world where it fails, it's not a rounding error — it's their actual lives ruined.

Ravi: You're smuggling in risk aversion and calling it rationality. Risk aversion is just a preference for smoother outcomes, not a reason against the math. If the math says do it, and you don't, you're leaving value on the table.

Nina: Okay, but there's a difference between a casino running a thousand bets and me making one decision with my life. The law of large numbers doesn't apply to me. I live in one branch, not an average of branches. So "expected value" for me isn't the same as for an institution that can spread risk.

Ravi: That doesn't change what's correct. It just explains why people are biased. Plus, if you really think about it, every decision you make is part of a larger pattern — you want your decision procedure to be EV-maximizing across your whole life, not just one roll.

Nina: But then you end up never committing to anything stable. You'd always jump to the next longshot. There's a kind of process value in sticking with something that builds real goods, not just chasing probabilities. I don't have the fancy word for it, but it feels like there's "decision capital" — if you take the longshot and fail, you don't just lose, you destroy your ability to make good choices later. So EV has to account for that, and once you do, the longshot looks way worse.

Ravi: Fine, add that to the calculation. If the failure state includes losing decision capital, the expected value drops. But that just means you do the calculation more carefully — it doesn't mean EV is wrong. It means you were using naive EV. So my point stands.

Nina: So then we're not really disagreeing about the principle, we're disagreeing about what's in the box? That seems like a cop-out. If you can always save EV by adding hidden costs after the fact, it's not really guiding anything.
