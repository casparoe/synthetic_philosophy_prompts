Construct a thought experiment that isolates a structural form of consequentialist cluelessness—call it “intermediate causal opacity”—in which an agent can observe all immediate local outcomes of an action yet cannot compute the global utility of that action because nonlinear feedback loops make the long-range mapping structurally intractable, even for a predictor with perfect access to local data. Set the scenario in a realistic public-health or urban-planning context (ordinary workplaces or institutions, no science fiction, no demons, no exotic physics, and no predictor better than a highly informed human expert). Engineer the case so that an act consequentialist is forced either into decision-paralysis or into committing to a high-stakes gamble whose global payoff is permanently unresolvable, while a rule-consequentialist or deontological agent can act on a principle that does not require resolving the opacity. Present the scenario in a single paragraph. Then demonstrate that the design works: state precisely what each framework recommends and why; identify the structural feature that generates the divergence, distinguishing it sharply from mere epistemic ignorance or standard risk; explain how a defender of act consequentialism could attack the case without denying the opacity, specifically by appealing to bounded expected-value reasoning with explicit confidence intervals; and say what happens if that structural feature is removed—whether the divergence collapses into a familiar risk-management problem or into a standard predictor-style dilemma. The purpose of the case is to test whether the paralysis is genuinely compelled by consequentialist logic itself, or only by an auxiliary assumption about the required precision of utility computation.
