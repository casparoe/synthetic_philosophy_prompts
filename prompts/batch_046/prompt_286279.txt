I've been working through a somewhat heterodox framing of the doctrine of double effect in trolley-style cases, and I want to pressure-test it rather than defend it by default. The view is this: DDE does not ultimately rest on a metaphysical or psychological divide between "intended" and "merely foreseen" harm, but can instead be reconstructed as a structural constraint on practical reasoning—specifically, a prohibition against treating a foreseeable harm as part of the evidential basis or counterfactual weighting scheme that selects an action. On this reading, diverting the trolley to kill one and save five is permissible not because the death is "outside" my intention in some mental-state sense, but because the harm is not part of the reasoning pathway that makes the diversion the best available option; by contrast, pushing the fat man involves using his body, and therefore his death, as a causal and evidential premise embedded in the plan itself. I take this to explain why DDE tracks our intuitive verdicts without invoking spooky mental causation, and I think it predicts that in structurally isomorphic cases—say, a variant in which the one dies not as a side-effect but as a necessary evidential node within a more complex decision tree—the moral verdict should flip, even if the agent's inner mental state is held constant.

What would count against this reconstruction? I want the arguments, formal results, or carefully constructed counter-cases that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they would be. In particular: are there decision-theoretic formalisms that successfully model DDE-permissible actions without any constraint on evidential weighting, and if so, does that show the constraint is redundant rather than genuinely explanatory? Are there trolley variants—perhaps involving probabilistic outcomes, distributed agency, or indirect causation—where our intuitive verdicts remain stable but the evidential-weighting structure is identical to the case my view treats as prohibited? Would defenders of the standard psychological or intention-based reading concede that my framing captures part of the truth but collapses into a rival account, say a purely consequentialist framing with an arbitrary side-constraint, and if so, is that collapse a fatal problem for the view? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by allowing the "evidential pathway" to be defined post hoc to match whatever verdict we want—and whether that kind of insulation is a virtue or a vice for a principle of this kind. Finally, include anything that has arguably already happened in the design of thought experiments or in the relevant philosophical literature that should have updated me by now but hasn't, and distinguish clearly between evidence that would be merely awkward and evidence that should be treated as genuinely fatal.
