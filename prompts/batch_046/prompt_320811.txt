Construct a diagnostic taxonomy—structured as a decision tree with labeled branch points—for metaethical positions that respond to the Euthyphro dilemma, designed for an audience that understands basic moral philosophy but should not need to recall specific authors or texts. Rather than sorting people by which label they prefer, the tree should sort them by how they answer a sequence of concrete, interlocking questions.

Begin with this initial case: suppose that, in every possible world, no being capable of rational thought—including any divine being—could form attitudes of approval or disapproval toward cruelty; ask whether, in that scenario, there would still be a fact of the matter about cruelty's wrongness. From that root, branch through the following distinctions: (i) whether moral properties depend on actual divine mental states, on counterfactual ones, or on none at all; (ii) whether a divine nature that is essentially good can ground moral norms without making those norms response-dependent on attitudes; (iii) whether the correct explanation of a moral fact, if there is one, runs downward from theology or upward from features of the world that theology merely recognizes; and (iv) whether a theist is committed to treating moral knowledge as necessarily mediated by revelation, or as in principle accessible through unaided rational inquiry.

At each internal node, phrase the split as a single question that a respondent can answer without knowing which philosophical position it corresponds to. At each leaf, place either a named position (for example, classical divine command theory, nature-based modified command theory, theistic moral realism, secular moral realism, secular anti-realism, or unnamed combinations) or mark the combination as 'unnamed,' followed by one sentence describing its most awkward or counterintuitive commitment. Identify at least two logical combinations that remain unoccupied in your tree, and for each offer a brief diagnosis: is the gap due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere underexploration? Explicitly flag any pair of leaves that the branching logic cannot cleanly separate, explaining why they collapse onto the same answer pattern.

Organize the response in the following sections, in this order:

1. 'Root and branch logic' (approximately 350 words): present the tree structure, state each node's diagnostic question, and show how the answers combine.
2. 'Leaves: positions and commitments' (approximately 400 words): list each occupied leaf with its name (or 'unnamed') and the one-sentence awkward commitment.
3. 'Gaps and indistinguishable pairs' (approximately 300 words): cover the empty combinations and the separation failures.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citation. Do not ask for specific quotations from texts; paraphrase any reference to standard arguments without quotation marks. The prompt should remain answerable by someone relying on general philosophical training rather than specialized archival knowledge.
