I have just concluded a long and somewhat exasperating argument with a friend over whether Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy of education can be distinguished in any practically meaningful way from modern constructivist learning theory. She insisted that Whitehead's well-known attack on inert ideas is nothing more than the standard progressive objection to rote memorization, and that his three rhythms of mental growth—romance, precision, and generalization—merely rename the familiar sequence of exploration, practice, and synthesis found in any contemporary curriculum guide. I tried to argue that Whitehead means something far more specific and metaphysically grounded: an idea becomes inert not simply when a student fails to apply it, but when it is acquired in isolation from the living process of becoming that constitutes every actual occasion, including the occasion of learning itself. For Whitehead, knowledge is not mental furniture accumulated in a static mind but an event of integration within an ongoing process, which is why education must be understood as the discipline for the adventure of living rather than the transmission of finished truths. My friend challenged me to show exactly how this ontological commitment changes the evaluation of a concrete curriculum, and she proposed a test case: a secondary mathematics program that spends ten weeks on decontextualized calculus drills followed by a single capstone modeling project in the final days. She claimed that any good constructivist would object to this design on the same grounds Whitehead would, and therefore that invoking process metaphysics adds nothing but decorative language. I need a formal assessment instrument that proves otherwise.

Please draft an exam question suitable for an upper-level philosophy of education seminar, designed to be answered in approximately ninety minutes with an expected length of about eight hundred words. The question should present the mathematics curriculum scenario I described and ask students to evaluate it through Whitehead's framework, but with a specific constraint: they must identify the precise point at which a constructivist critique and a genuinely Whiteheadian critique diverge, particularly with respect to the temporal structure of learning and the ontological status of precisely acquired but never generalized knowledge. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; a model without web access should be able to answer from conceptual understanding alone. Along with the question, provide a model answer in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or other formatting devices, written as one might write a formal letter—of roughly six hundred words. The model answer should demonstrate the specific moves that separate a top response from a mediocre one: clearly distinguishing Whitehead's process metaphysics from generic progressive pedagogy, treating romance, precision, and generalization not as linear stages but as cyclic phases that must interpenetrate within any genuine learning event, explaining why the late capstone project cannot retroactively animate the earlier drills, and proposing at least one concrete curricular revision that follows specifically from viewing knowledge as an event of becoming rather than a possession. Finally, provide a marking scheme, also in uninterrupted continuous prose with no formatting, structured around five must-hit points totaling twenty marks. For each point, specify what the point is worth, the specific conceptual move required, and the typical error that causes students to lose marks—such as treating inertness as mere student boredom, sequencing the three rhythms chronologically rather than cyclically, or offering a generic pedagogical fix that does not depend on Whitehead's metaphysics of actual occasions.
