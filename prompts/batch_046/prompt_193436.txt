You are delivering the retirement toast at a dinner honoring Dr. Elena Vasquez, a physicist who spent four decades developing statistical methods for causal discovery—particularly interventionist frameworks that map how correlations shift under experimental manipulation rather than assuming hidden mechanisms. Roughly half the audience are her colleagues in physics and philosophy of science, who will catch every philosophical move you make; the other half are family members with no technical background. You are a philosopher who holds an occasionalist view inspired by Al-Ghazālī: you believe that observed regularities—fire burning cotton, quantum correlations holding across measurements—are not necessary connections intrinsic to nature, but stable habits (ʿādāt) of direct divine causation, such that objects possess no causal power of their own. You want to honor Vasquez’s rigor and the practical power of her models without attributing to her the discovery of “necessary causal laws woven into nature,” language you regard as metaphysically false, and without turning a warm toast into a theology lecture that loses her relatives. The philosophical commitment should shape the speech structurally: you must refuse standard realist praise about “uncovering nature’s hidden mechanisms,” yet you must still celebrate her patience with ambiguity, her refusal to overclaim, and the extraordinary robustness of the patterns she mapped—patterns you see as remarkably stable habits rather than metaphysical necessities. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts. Write a four-to-five-minute toast that lands as affectionate and precise for the experts—who should recognize that you are deliberately withholding realist causal language—while remaining fully accessible and moving for the family, and that centers Vasquez’s character and achievement rather than using her as a philosophical prop.
