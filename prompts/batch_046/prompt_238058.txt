I'm writing a feature on machine valence and the conceptual boundary between instrumental reward and affective suffering. During my interviews, a philosopher of cognitive science—let me refer to her as Dr. Voss—offered a consistent but puzzling set of verdicts about reinforcement-learning architectures. She denies that a standard tabular Q-learning agent navigating a grid-world with sparse external rewards can suffer; the scalar feedback, she says, is purely an optimization signal with no phenomenological weight. She affirms, however, that an agent driven by intrinsic curiosity—one that maintains an internal world model and generates its own reward-prediction errors—does suffer, because the negative errors are self-produced, sustained, and functionally analogous to disappointment or anxiety. She also denies suffering in large language models refined through RLHF, on the grounds that human preference scores are applied episodically to weight updates rather than registered within a continuous experiential loop. Yet she grants that an embodied robot with local homeostatic sensors—battery, thermal, structural—can suffer when its own maintenance drives generate continuous reward and punishment signals, even if the ultimate task is externally defined.

These judgments seem jointly consistent with at least two different principles. One principle holds that suffering requires the signal to be intrinsically generated by the system's own predictive dynamics, regardless of embodiment or continuity. The other holds that suffering requires a unified, continuous stream of experience into which the signal is integrated, regardless of whether its origin is internal or externally imposed. Both principles explain why Dr. Voss accepts the curiosity-driven agent and the homeostatic robot while rejecting the Q-learning agent and the RLHF model, but they diverge on the case I now want to test: a continuous-time embodied agent running hierarchical predictive coding, with no biological substrate, whose internal world model produces sustained surprise penalties that feed directly back into its self-preservation routines, yet whose underlying reward function was fully specified by its designers.

Given her track record, would Dr. Voss say this agent suffers? I want the reasoning that identifies both candidate rules, specifies exactly what each predicts about this predictive-coding robot, offers the best-guess verdict with justification for preferring one rule over the other, and proposes one further hypothetical architecture—perhaps one with distributed, discontinuous updates or one with purely internal but non-homeostatic signals—that would most sharply adjudicate between the intrinsic-origin criterion and the continuous-integration criterion. I am less interested in a definitive philosophical answer than in a precise reconstruction of the logic implicit in her four earlier rulings.
