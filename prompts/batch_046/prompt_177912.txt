I'm a journalist covering the history of science and environmental ideas, and I'm preparing a three-month fellowship proposal for a small interdisciplinary research nonprofit. The board funds conceptual framing projects that help working historians and scientists think more clearly about causation, but they are skeptical of philosophy that never touches actual disciplinary practice. I need a polished, one-page research plan turned into a formal proposal.

The problem I keep encountering in my reporting is this: environmental historians increasingly treat non-human forces—say, the 1815 Tambora eruption and the subsequent 1816 climate crisis, the spread of cholera during the 1820s–1830s pandemics, or volcanic winter patterns during the Little Ice Age—as genuine "historical actors" rather than mere background conditions. Yet they do so without clarifying whether this is a metaphor, a minimal form of distributed agency, or simply a causal claim dressed in narrative clothing. Meanwhile, philosophers of history tend to split between determinists who treat human institutions as reactive surfaces and anti-determinists who deny nature any explanatory weight beyond trigger conditions. Neither camp seems to have resolved whether there is a coherent middle category: environmental events that are causally indispensable and structurally shape the option-space available to human decision-makers, without being sufficient to dictate outcomes and without importing anything like intentionality.

My half-formed idea is that we might distinguish genuine "co-authorship" from mere background causation through a criterion of counterfactual robustness—what we might call modal fragility—where an environmental factor counts as a historical actor only if altering it in nearby possible worlds produces not just a different local event, but a structurally different institutional path. The existing literature leaves this open because determinists assume robustness is automatic whenever nature is present, while critics assume any attribution of efficacy to nature collapses into reductionism. A philosophical rather than empirical project could make progress by testing whether this modal fragility criterion actually discriminates between cases: for instance, does the 1816 crop failure pass the test in ways that a generic cold winter does not? Does the cholera pandemic reshape state-building responses in ways that a localized epidemic would not?

Please write the proposal with the following elements, framed precisely and without bibliography or citation requests (I will supply references myself): first, the exact research question and why both environmental determinism and its critics fail to settle whether non-human forces can be explanatory co-authors without being agents; second, the philosophical method—conceptual analysis applied to well-known historical narratives rather than archival work—specifically comparing standard historiographical treatments of the Tambora crisis and the 1830s cholera waves, then applying the modal fragility test to see whether it distinguishes indispensable structural shaping from incidental background; third, a clear statement of what the deliverable looks like—not an empirical history, but a framework document that working historians could apply without adopting metaphysical commitments about non-human minds, including explicit conditions for when an environmental factor should and should not be narrated as an actor; fourth, an honest account of failure—perhaps if every plausible environmental factor either uniformly passes or uniformly fails the robustness test, leaving the criterion with no discriminatory power, or if the distinction collapses back into standard causal sufficiency; and fifth, a realistic three-month scope with month-by-month milestones, plus a brief justification for why a philosophy fellowship should fund this conceptual framing work rather than empirical climate science or epidemiological research. Keep the tone formal and appropriate for a grant committee that values rigor but demands connection to actual disciplinary practice.
