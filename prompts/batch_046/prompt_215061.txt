A close friend is considering leaving a secure career to spend five years establishing an independent network of remote coastal observation posts for rare atmospheric optical phenomena—superior mirages, green flashes—using a homemade instrument she developed during a year of solitary fieldwork. She lacks formal meteorological credentials or institutional backing, but she has documented dozens of events she believes conventional stations miss. She is aware of the outside statistics: nearly every amateur who attempts to build a parallel scientific observation infrastructure without peer review or funding fails within two years, typically through isolation, resource depletion, and seeing patterns in noise. She argues these figures do not apply to her—her device yields reproducible measurements, she understands her own resilience and motivation better than any external observer, and she has already produced results where others have not. Yet she also recognizes that she fits the psychological profile—high self-confidence, preference for solitude, attraction to explanations outside mainstream science—most prevalent in the failure group. She asks whether she should treat her private assessment of her own uniqueness as stronger evidence than the statistical base rate, and whether the fact that she can identify her own susceptibility to self-deception should make her more or less trusting of that private assessment. How should she reason about the decision, and what does it mean for her confidence if she can clearly see the flaws in her own reasoning but still believes she possesses genuinely exceptional, unshared information?
