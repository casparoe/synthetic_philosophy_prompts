After a long argument with a friend last night about whether tranquility is a genuinely positive condition or merely the privation of suffering and craving, I want to test how consistently different theories of well-being treat a precisely defined edge case. Our dispute turned on whether a being that has permanently eliminated all craving and all suffering, yet retains intact reflective consciousness with no first-order desires whatsoever, occupies the best possible state or something closer to neutral non-existence.

Consider five accounts: (A) positive hedonism, which values the presence of pleasure; (B) desire-satisfactionism, which values the fulfillment of actual preferences; (C) Schopenhauerian will-negation, which values the cessation of striving; (D) strict tranquilism, which holds that only the absence of craving and suffering carries value; and (E) reflective void-positivism, a position my friend sketched, according to which absence is valuable only when a conscious subject remains to register the lack of disturbance, though that registration itself adds no positive value.

Please rank A through E by assigning each a score from 0 to 10 representing how highly that account evaluates the welfare of a being in “stable non-craving awareness”—defined precisely as complete absence of craving and suffering, intact reflective consciousness, zero first-order desires, and no emotional disturbance. For each score, provide one sentence explaining whether the evaluation requires smuggling in a positive good that strict tranquilism would reject. Keep the reasoning internal to the logic of each view; do not cite texts, provide bibliographic references, or appeal to empirical studies.
