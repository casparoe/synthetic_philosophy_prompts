In my reporting on debates at the intersection of science policy and religious conviction, I have repeatedly noticed an argumentative configuration that I cannot map cleanly onto the standard categories of public reason. I want to know whether it has an established name, but more importantly I want its boundaries drawn precisely—what it is, what it is not, and where it shades into nearby patterns—so that I can identify it reliably in coverage.

The phenomenon is this. A speaker with a religious commitment advances a policy claim—say, about genetic technology or environmental preservation—and supports it with two apparently independent lines of reasoning: one framed in secular, publicly accessible language, and one rooted in theological premises. The distinctive feature is not that both lines exist, but that they are structurally interdependent in a way that is rarely acknowledged. The secular line relies on normative concepts—human dignity, natural ends, moral status, intergenerational obligation, intrinsic worth—that carry far more prohibitory or prescriptive force than their secular justifications can bear. When secular interlocutors press for reasons that should bind citizens who do not share the faith, the speaker either implicitly or explicitly appeals to the theological framework that gives those concepts their weight. Yet when challenged that this framework should not determine public policy, the speaker does not retreat to a genuinely autonomous secular position; instead, they shift to a different secular formulation—species integrity, ecosystem rights, potentiality-based dignity—that is equally hollow without the same theological backing. The two lines do not stand in a core-and-extension relation; each serves as hidden epistemic scaffolding for the other, and neither can stand alone without collapsing into either a bare assertion of doctrine or a non sequitur.

Consider two concrete shapes. In debates over germline genetic editing, some opponents argue that the practice violates the inherent dignity of the human organism, presenting this as a philosophical or biological observation about identity and potentiality. When asked why this dignity prohibits editing but permits corrective surgery or vaccination, the response often invokes a theological anthropology—human beings bearing a fixed, sacred status—without admitting that this anthropology is doing the normative work. Once that theological appeal is challenged as inappropriate for public justification, the speaker returns to secular language, but now interprets dignity through natural norms or species integrity that only acquire prohibitory force if one already accepts the theological view of human nature as inviolable. The secular argument is not a lossy translation of the religious one; it is functionally incomplete, yet it denies its dependence.

In climate and conservation debates, certain religious advocates press for radical preservation using ecosystem science and intergenerational justice. The data might support moderate adaptation; the urgency and comprehensiveness advocated, however, align with a theology of creation stewardship in which nature has intrinsic value derived from divine origin. When secular critics observe that the science does not mandate the radical scope, the advocates do not scale back. They shift instead to a rights-of-nature framework that borrows normative authority from the very stewardship theology they have ostensibly set aside. Again, the secular reasoning is not an autonomous parallel track; it is epistemically parasitic, relying on the theological framework it claims to have replaced, while the theological framework relies on the secular framing to avoid the charge of imposing doctrine.

This is not, I think, simple equivocation, because the speaker typically holds both lines sincerely rather than switching meanings for deception. Nor is it precisely the motte-and-bailey pattern, since neither line is a defensible core and the other an indefensible extension; both are contentious, and both are necessary to sustain the conclusion. It also differs from standard accounts of translation failure in public reason—where religious content is rendered in secular terms with acceptable loss—because here the secular rendering is structurally hollow without the religious backing, yet actively denies that backing is required.

If this configuration has an established name in political philosophy, rhetoric, or the study of religious argumentation, I would like to know it, together with an explanation of how the concept distinguishes genuine secular autonomy from hidden dependency of this kind. If it does not have a name, I would welcome a proposed term, a precise definition, and clear boundary conditions—specifically, how to distinguish this mutual-scaffolding pattern from cases where secular and religious reasoning genuinely converge without parasitism, and how to identify the point at which a secular argument crosses from being coincidentally aligned with theology into being epistemically dependent on it. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of authors; I want the conceptual carving itself—what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
