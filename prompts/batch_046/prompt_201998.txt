Grade the following exam response. The question asked students to explain Dennett’s notion of the self as a center of narrative gravity—paraphrased, not as a metaphysical substance but as a theoretical construct analogous to a center of mass—then apply it to a deliberately constructed case: a hypothetical lesion produces bilateral narrative isolation in which each hemisphere maintains continuous first-person pronoun use and disjoint episodic memory, yet both remain embedded in one living human organism with unified motor control and global speech; the subject can sincerely utter both “I remember X” and “I do not remember X” in different contexts, and reports feeling “both and neither” when queried holistically. The student was asked whether this yields one self, two selves, or none within Dennett’s framework, and to flag precisely where that framework is genuinely contested rather than settled.

Please assign a letter grade and provide point-by-point comments that justify it. In your assessment, separate what is broadly settled—Dennett’s rejection of a Cartesian, simple-substance ego and his fictionalist, narrative-based orientation—from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of personal identity: for instance, whether narrative discontinuity alone can split selfhood, whether biological or organism-level unity constrains narrative gravity in any way, and whether first-person reports of being “both and neither” should be treated as data or as noise to be explained away. Do not present any contested interpretation of Dennett’s view, or of the split-brain implications, as the consensus position.

Student answer:
The center of narrative gravity is Dennett’s metaphor for the self: just as the center of gravity of a physical object is a useful theoretical point with no parts of its own, the self is a fictional character we construct by weaving experiences into an autobiographical story. In the bilateral isolation case, because there are two separate narrative streams with no shared episodic access, there are necessarily two distinct centers of narrative gravity, hence two selves inside one body. This follows directly once we accept that the self is purely narrative. Animalists reject this by appealing to the persistence of the human organism, but Dennett’s framework is settled in denying that biological continuity has any weight for selfhood. What remains genuinely contested is only whether we ought to accept fictionalism in the first place; once we do, the multiplication of selves here is unavoidable. The patient’s report of feeling “both and neither” is best explained as an illusion produced by the body’s single motor output system, not as evidence that narrative gravity might be distributed or partially unified at a higher level. Therefore, there are exactly two selves, and the feeling of ambiguity is merely an artifact of unified speech.

Grade this response with specific attention to its conceptual moves, its handling of the hypothetical case, and its characterization of what is settled versus contested.
