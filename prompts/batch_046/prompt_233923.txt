I serve on the editorial board of an interdisciplinary journal that publishes predictive modeling studies in climate adaptation, public health, and social policy. Every month we receive submissions that forecast specific outcomes—regional crop failures, vaccination-campaign coverage, migration flows—often blending mechanistic causal narratives with statistical reference classes. Our reviewers have domain expertise but limited formal training in epistemic calibration, and the editor-in-chief must sign off on any decision to accept, reject, or downgrade a predictive claim. Each evaluation must be completed within ten working days. I need a reusable, written decision procedure that operationalizes the distinction between inside-view reasoning (case-specific causal and structural analysis) and outside-view reasoning (reference-class base rates and historical frequencies), together with an explicit epistemic-modesty check. The procedure should take the form of ordered gates or questions, with clear pass, revise, or reject criteria at each stage, explicit stop points where the confidence claim is downgraded or rejected, and explicit hand-off points where the remaining judgment is irreducibly about values—such as what level of false-positive risk is acceptable for policy—rather than about epistemic calibration. Please design this procedure so that a reviewer without theoretical background can apply it consistently, and specify which distinctions must survive simplification and which can be dropped without corrupting the logic. Then run the procedure on three submissions: first, a climate-adaptation study that predicts regional crop failure within five years using only mechanistic soil-and-weather simulations, with no comparison to historical adaptation-failure reference classes; second, a public-health forecast that combines a reference-class base rate for vaccine-hesitancy campaigns with a detailed inside-view narrative about local religious leadership structures, but asserts ninety percent confidence without adjusting for the documented overconfidence of hybrid predictive models; third, a meticulously constructed inside-view forecast for a genuinely unprecedented intervention—say, the first regional deployment of a gene-drive mosquito—where no reference class exists, internal coherence checks are transparent, and the authors cautiously propose sixty percent confidence based solely on structural reasoning, yet a government agency wishes to treat the forecast as calibrated for binding budget allocation. For each case, show exactly where the procedure stops, where it demands revision, where it hands the decision to a value judgment, and identify the case in which the procedure produces a recommendation you, as the practitioner, will find unwelcome. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do—especially for predictions lacking both a reference class and any outside-view anchor—and explain how a practitioner should recognize that boundary rather than forcing a spurious numerical calibration.
