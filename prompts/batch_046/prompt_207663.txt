You are a senior lecturer in political philosophy with extensive experience in ceremonial rhetoric and interdisciplinary workshop design. Before composing, please take a moment to think carefully about the architectural constraints: the central philosophical idea must shape what can and cannot be said in the address, rather than serving as decorative ornament, and you should map out the speech's three movements before drafting any prose.

I am delivering the opening remarks at this year's interdisciplinary workshop on Institutional Resilience and Historical Contingency, a gathering of roughly thirty participants—half academic political theorists, half senior civil servants who think regularly about organizational failure but have never read The Prince. The occasion carries personal weight: eighteen months ago, an abrupt and arbitrary funding restructuring nearly dissolved my own research center, and only a rapid, improvised reconfiguration of partnerships and internal roles saved it. That experience compelled me to revisit Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù not as a quaint humanist trope, but as a living framework for understanding how institutions survive shocks they did not cause.

Please write the speech, approximately six minutes when spoken, observing the following constraints. First, the philosophical framework must do structural work: I must not claim that my mentor, my institution, or I myself mastered fate through pure rational planning, since Machiavelli assigns roughly half our actions to fortune; yet I must equally avoid fatalism, since he assigns the remaining half to virtù—understood not as moral virtue but as the adaptive capacity to reshape one's own nature to match changing circumstances. Second, please use the hydraulic metaphor—fortune as a river that can be channeled by dikes and embankments when calm, but that overwhelms them in flood—to organize three movements of the address: the unexpected shock (the flood), the preparatory labor that made survival possible (the dikes built in calm weather), and the honest acknowledgment that even well-built banks are sometimes destroyed. Third, inject a somewhat niche conceptual framing: distinguish Machiavelli's gendered, almost theatrical conception of fortune as an active force that shows its power precisely where no adaptive readiness has been prepared, from the modern statistical notion of risk as neutral, quantifiable variance; use that distinction to argue that institutional resilience requires not only data-driven prediction but a kind of performative institutional plasticity—an organizational virtù capable of altering its own form under pressure. The audience should include scholars who will recognize the reference to the half-half division in chapter 25 of The Prince and practitioners who will grasp the dike metaphor without knowing its source. Do not provide citations, a bibliography, or suggested readings. Do not use verbatim quotation marks around any passage from Machiavelli's text; if you wish to reference his language, paraphrase without quotation marks. The tone should be formal, reflective, and appropriate for an interdisciplinary workshop opening—serious about contingency, but not despairing.
