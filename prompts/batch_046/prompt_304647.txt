I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar focused on motivation and artificial reward, and I built this argument, but its conclusion seems too extreme to accept. (1) Wireheading is wrong because it severs the causal link between reward signals and the external achievements they represent: you feel successful without having succeeded. (2) If causal decoupling is the core problem, then any practice that suppresses the brain's error-monitoring circuits to generate satisfaction without corresponding external change—what I want to call second-order wireheading—should be equally wrong. (3) But ordinary cognitive-behavioral therapy, some therapeutic uses of antidepressants, and even benign self-deception can suppress error-monitoring and produce satisfaction without altering the world. (4) So, by (1) and (2), these everyday practices are objectionable reward-tampering. (5) That is implausible; we routinely encourage them. (6) Therefore the causal-decoupling account either fails or needs a principled distinction that separates direct neural stimulation from these other cases, but I cannot see one that does not collapse into question-begging. I would prefer to keep (1) and reject (6), but I am unsure which intermediate step bears the cost. Where exactly does the argument slip, and what does fixing it require me to give up?
