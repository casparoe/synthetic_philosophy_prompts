I write for a small science-and-ideas magazine, and I'm prepping an interveiw with a philosopher who's just published a book defending indirect normativity through what she terms 'sequential deliberation with retained friction'. Her core claim is that practical norms don't apply to us straight away; instead they operate through a three-stage idealisation—first epistemic correction, then affective calibration, then institutional embedding—and crucially she keeps some of the 'noise' or friction from each prior stage inside the next one, so the final ideal agent isn't some purified alien but carries traces of real-world messiness. I need eight to ten sharp questions that go well beyond her restating the view. I'd like at least one pressing why indirectness is needed at all when direct normative accounts seem more parsimonious, one asking whether retaining all that friction just turns the idealisation into a dressed-up description of ordinary cognitive and behavioural bias, and one on what happens when two agents running the protocol reach incompatible normative outputs—how does she analyse the disagreement without smuggling in direct norms? For each, please include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give—she often deflects objections about applicability by saying they're 'engineering issues for applied ethics, not for the programme'. So please build a sequence that makes that deflection definately harder to use, ending with one question about what kind of evidence or failure could actually count against the framework itself. Could you also write the reply in American English? Our US edition needs it. And just to check—am I right that on her view a small language model trained only on normative texts, if run through the same three layers, might generate binding outputs, or is that a misreading?
