I've been trying to work out why I can genuinely grieve over a character's death while knowing full well that the character never existed. The explanation I keep running into is that fiction works just like children's make-believe: you agree to treat the story as if it were real, the way a child treats a stick as a horse, and any feelings you have are part of the game — pretend-sadness for pretend-people. People use this to say there's no real paradox, because once you see that the emotion belongs to the pretend frame, it doesn't conflict with your belief that the object is unreal.

But I'm not sure that holds up. If my sadness is genuinely part of the game, why does it spill over? I still feel a kind of lingering weight after I close the book, and it sometimes changes how I respond to real people in ways that don't feel like I'm still playing. Meanwhile, with a stick-horse, I don't actually feel protective toward the stick once the game is over.

Can you lay out the correspondence explicitly — what in fiction maps onto the stick, the child, the rules of play, the emotional response — and then show exactly where the mapping breaks? In particular, does the make-believe analogy quietly import the idea that fictional emotions are simulated rather than real, and if so, is that import justified? Does it smuggle in a view about the purpose of the activity — that pretend-play is purely self-contained entertainment — that might not fit fiction, which often seems designed to train or calibrate responses I later use outside the frame? I also want to know whether a different analogy — say, fiction as a kind of emotional cross-training, where I practice compassion on safe, predictable targets to build capacity for messier real-world relationships — survives the same audit better, or whether it introduces its own hidden commitments about the value of the whole versus the parts. Ultimately, I want to know whether the make-believe analogy actually dissolves the tension, or whether it just renames it.
