Argue that treating germs, climate anomalies, and volcanic events as historical actors—a framing adopted in parts of environmental historiography—commits a specific category error that undermines both explanatory rigor and normative accountability. Defend the position that these forces should not be analogized to agents with minimal intentionality, but should instead be reconceived as counterfactual fragility conditions: structural properties of human societies that do not initiate historical change themselves, but expose the brittleness of institutions when they intersect with biological or geophysical processes. Your argument should explain precisely why attributing agency to pathogens or volcanic winters collapses the philosophy-of-action distinction between initiating a change and being a background condition, and why this collapse makes it impossible to maintain coherent moral and political responsibility for outcomes that are genuinely co-produced by environmental forces. At the same time, show that analyzing these forces through the lens of fragility—rather than treating them as passive background—preserves their substantial causal weight without reverting to environmental determinism. Address the objection that your view secretly restores human exceptionalism, and respond to the charge that fragility is merely determinism redescribed in modal language. Be precise about how this reconception changes what it means to write a causal narrative in which non-human forces play an indispensable but non-agential role.
