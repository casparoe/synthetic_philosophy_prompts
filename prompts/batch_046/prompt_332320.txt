I run a graduate philosophy seminar of about fifteen students, ninety minutes, on moral metaphysics and collective action. Design a runnable simulation—using only pen, paper, and a small shared pool of real stakes (for example, each round’s token transfers determine whether a minor charitable donation is withheld or a brief unpleasant task is assigned)—that forces participants to feel the pull of a distributed-permission variant of the paradox of deontology. The rules should work as follows: divide the group into five sequential roles, each holding a single “enabling token.” Each role-player may, without penalty, pass the token to the next role; passing is framed as mere permission, not direct harm, and opting out is always individually available but requires destroying the token, which each player is told they do not own. Only the final role can “activate” harm, but activation is mechanically impossible unless all prior tokens have been passed. Arrange payoffs so that every individual move feels deontologically permissible—no one directly causes harm, each can exit, and collective blocking requires someone to violate the permission-structure by unilaterally destroying another’s token—yet the system, if left to individual choice, deterministically produces the harmful outcome. Participants should build the chain themselves, notice only at the end that they have constructed an unavoidable harm-machine, and then experience the second-order frustration that a collective agreement to stop it seems to demand exactly the kind of unauthorized interference that deontology forbids. After the simulation, provide a debrief that maps each stage onto parts of the idea: the non-aggregation of individual permissions, the gap between doing and allowing when agency is distributed across roles, and the asymmetry between collective responsibility and individual constraint. Predict what will go wrong in actual runs—likely attempts to renegotiate rules mid-game, or one participant refusing to play the final role—and say how to handle them without lecturing. Be explicit about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce—most plausibly, the metaphysical question of whether moral reasons genuinely aggregate across distinct agents—and indicate what the facilitator must supply verbally to close that gap. Do not ask for bibliographies or citations; the prompt should stand on its conceptual architecture alone.
