I am preparing an essay for a metaphysics seminar that applies the endurantism versus perdurantism debate to institutional entities rather than to familiar physical cases like statues or persons. My central claim is that a constitutional framework—say, a founding document that accumulates amendments—seems to endure through time (it is referred to as "the same constitution" across centuries), yet each amendment looks like a temporal part that alters its content, which pulls toward a perdurantist analysis. To resist this, I sketch an endurantist reply that distinguishes the enduring "constitutional office" or normative authority structure from the "body of constitutional law" that grows and changes. To move the discussion away from well-worn examples, I introduce a hypothetical polity with a "living constitution" in which amendments do not merely append new clauses but recursively rewrite prior ones, so that the textual content at time t and at time t+1 share no literal overlap, while citizens and courts nevertheless continue to invoke a single, continuous constitutional authority. I argue that this "recursive endurance" case creates pressure on both standard views: pure endurantism struggles to explain what remains wholly present when the textual substrate is entirely replaced, while pure perdurantism seems to dissolve the institutional continuity that practical reasoning depends upon. From this I tentatively propose a hybrid "functional-persistence" view, according to which what persists is neither a three-dimensional substance nor a four-dimensional worm, but a continuously maintained normative authority relation that can survive complete turnover in its material and textual realization.

The essay is still in note form, and I want to be sure I am not mischaracterizing either metaphysical position when I transpose them into this institutional register. Specifically, I worry that the perdurantist might simply treat the constitutional authority as itself a four-dimensional object composed of authority-stages, in which case my "recursive" scenario collapses into a standard temporal-parts analysis. I also want to know whether the distinction I draw between an enduring office and an accumulating body of law is genuinely available to the endurantist, or whether it implicitly smuggles in perdurantist machinery by treating amendments as distinct parts that stand in relation to a whole. Furthermore, I am unsure whether my "functional-persistence" proposal is conceptually substantive or merely a relabeling of one of the standard positions with institutional vocabulary.

Could you comment on the conceptual coherence of this framing? In particular, please assess whether my characterization of endurantism and perdurantism in this non-physical domain is accurate, whether the recursive-amendment thought experiment introduces a genuinely distinct puzzle or only reproduces familiar Ship-of-Theseus-style tensions, and whether the hybrid view I am sketching is philosophically interesting or collapses into one of the two camps. I am working without library access, so I ask for conceptual and logical feedback only—no requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. I am especially interested in objections from the perdurantist side about whether normative authority can be coherently analyzed as having temporal parts at all.
