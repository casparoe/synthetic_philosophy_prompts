As a novelist who works primarily with archival lives and who has long been troubled by the ethics of dramatizing real deceased persons, I want you to compose a sustained deadpan parody—approximately 600 words, formatted as the "Ethical Compliance, Counterfactual Audit, and Acknowledgments" appendix to a serious work of historical fiction. The piece should be written in the voice of an author who has internalized, to an absurd degree, the anxiety that fictionalizing the dead imposes a single narrative trajectory on lives that contained unrealized possibilities. Invent a pseudo-bureaucratic framework complete with technical-sounding protocols: for instance, a Board for Posthumous Narrative Consent, a Counterfactual Non-Interference Clause, a Dignity of Unchosen Lives assessment, and a Fidelity-to-Possibility index. The author-protagonist should sincerely apologize for taking liberties with minor characters, list the counterfactual refunds owed to history—compensating for paths not taken, careers abandoned, marriages that might have occurred—and treat the dead as stakeholders with legitimate claims against misrepresentation. The satire must come from extending genuinely debated concerns—our obligations to real people we dramatize, the violence of reducing a life to plot—into an institutional reductio ad absurdum, never breaking character to wink. Do not name living persons, cite specific scholars, quote real texts, or ask for bibliographies; instead, allude generally to the tension between narrative license and representational ethics in historical fiction and biography. The result should reward readers familiar with debates about what we owe the dead, yet remain legible and amusing to someone encountering the concept for the first time. Keep the irony sustained, the prose polished, and the form strictly that of a mock-legal ethical appendix.
