As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy systematically, I tend to treat novels as extended thought experiments with observable variables rather than as mere entertainment. I am preparing a discussion for a philosophy reading group on Ursula K. Le Guin's The Dispossessed, and I want to read it through the problem of immigration, open borders, integration, and the welfare state—not as allegory, but as a structured comparison between two societies that differ precisely on these variables.

Le Guin gives us Anarres, an anarchist society without a formal state, private property, or legal borders, sustained by a system of mutual aid that functions like a welfare state without a treasury; and Urras, a capitalist state with strict border controls, stark inequality, and institutional welfare only for those within its citizenship. Shevek, a physicist from Anarres, crosses to Urras to pursue his theory of simultaneity, and his experience of arrival, labor, intellectual collaboration, and eventual return can be read as an empirical case study in migration, integration failure, and the compatibility of open movement with collective provision.

Read the novel with this framing. Where does Shevek's passage illuminate the standard philosophical arguments—say, the claim that a welfare state requires closure to protect its goods, or that open borders depend on shared moral premises? Does Anarres's isolationism, despite its anarchist rejection of the state, constitute a de facto border regime designed to protect its mutual-aid economy? When Shevek is received in Urras, he is simultaneously welcomed as a prestigious intellectual and confined by the structures of state power: does this dramatize the idea that integration without assimilation is impossible when the receiving society is organized around citizenship and property?

I want the reading to be precise: point to specific scenes—the arrival at the port, Shevek's interaction with the Ioti underclass, his discovery that the university's openness depends on state patronage, his growing awareness that he cannot belong—and explain what each shows about integration costs or the brain-drain dynamics of migration. But do not reduce the novel to an illustration. Where does Le Guin's narrative resist the philosophical framework? For instance, does the novel suggest that the real obstacle to open borders is not economic scarcity but ideological homogeneity—something the standard welfare-state argument misses?

Finally, I want to inject a conceptual distinction that the philosophical literature often collapses: the difference between legal exclusion, in which a border functions as a gate controlling entry, and social closure, in which a society achieves belonging only through assimilation into a shared ideology. Anarres has no legal borders but practices intense social closure; Urras has legal borders but allows selective entry based on utility. Does the novel imply that any sustainable system of collective provision requires one form of closure or the other? And if so, what does that mean for the aspiration to genuinely open borders?

Give me a reading that takes both Le Guin's fiction and the philosophical debate seriously, and conclude with what the novel adds—perhaps about the lived experience of being a border-crosser in a society without a state—that argument alone cannot supply.
