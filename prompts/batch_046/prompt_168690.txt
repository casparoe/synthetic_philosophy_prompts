I work as a senior strategic-planning officer in a defence ministry, and several times a month I must review briefing papers that invoke historical analogies—Munich-style appeasement, Vietnam-style quagmire dynamics, or Thucydides-trap power-transition scenarios—to justify or oppose live policy options. I need a reusable decision procedure that my team can run in roughly thirty minutes using only the briefing materials in hand, with no access to archives or external databases, that produces a clear recommendation or a clear rejection, marks every point where values rather than facts must decide, and states honestly where it will misfire.

Please construct an ordered procedural checklist—questions to ask in sequence, criteria for a sufficient answer at each stage, hard-stop points where the analogy must be rejected, and hand-off points where judgment is irreducible—grounded in what philosophy and historiography actually know about analogical reasoning in policy contexts. In translating the underlying theory into practice, you must preserve a distinction between diachronic causal-homology (whether the mechanisms that produced the historical outcome are present and similarly configured in the present case) and synchronic indexical-resonance (whether surface features trigger emotional or rhetorical recognition without preserving the causal dependencies that drove the original outcome). Build in a three-part stability test for counterfactual dependence—mechanism preservation, preference-order invariance, and institutional-constraint parity—that a non-specialist can apply with only the documents on the desk.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) using the Munich analogy to justify preemptive strikes against a regional nuclear aspirant; (b) using the Vietnam analogy to reject a limited training-and-advisory mission in an ongoing civil conflict; and (c) using the Thucydides trap to mandate economic containment of a rising peer competitor. One of these applications must yield an answer that I am likely to find unwelcome, and you must flag it explicitly. For each case, show the step where the procedure stops, the step where it hands the decision to a person, and the specific feature of the analogy that does the work.

Finally, specify exactly what the procedure cannot do—where analogical reasoning is structurally incapable of delivering a policy verdict regardless of how rigorously the steps are executed—and identify every irreducible judgment call. Write in British English, state how confident you are in the procedure’s central claims, and single out the one point about which you are least certain.
