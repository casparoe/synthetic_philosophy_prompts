I am preparing a course module on the justification of deductive inference, and in discussing applied contexts—particularly legal reasoning assisted by formal models and debates about automated theorem-proving—I have kept noticing a pattern that I cannot place precisely within the literature on Carroll’s tortoise. The pattern runs as follows. A reasoner applies a specific inference rule, say modus ponens, to a concrete case and presents the result as rationally compelled. When challenged to justify the rule itself, she does not invoke a further logical principle (which would invite Carroll’s familiar infinite regress), nor does she claim that the rule is self-evident in a foundationalist sense. Instead, she treats the meta-level licence as pragmatically adopted—grounded in institutional convention, cognitive economy, or practical efficacy—and explicitly suspends the demand for rational justification there. Crucially, the object-level deduction is still regarded as genuinely justified, not as a merely mechanical output, while the meta-level is acknowledged as rationally opaque. This produces a bifurcation: deduction is compelled at one level and conventionally halted at another.

If there is an established concept for this exact configuration—where the justification of deduction is split between a rational object-level and a pragmatically suspended meta-level—I should like to know its name, together with precise boundary conditions that distinguish it from neighbouring positions. How exactly does it differ from Carroll’s original regress, in which Achilles is dragged upward rather than choosing to stop? How does it differ from standard foundationalism or coherentism, and from the ordinary strategy of adding auxiliary hypotheses to protect a theory? If no established term exists, could you coin one, with a definition that marks its edges clearly, including what it is not?
