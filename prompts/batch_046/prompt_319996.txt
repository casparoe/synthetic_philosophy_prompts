here's jordan's answer (they're usually solid but i think they slipped up in a couple spots):

Zhuangzi uses the butterfly dream to show that we can never really know if we're awake or dreaming, which is basically the same point Descartes makes in the Meditations. Both argue that sensory experience is unreliable because dreams can perfectly mimic waking life. The main difference is that Descartes is worried about the foundation of scientific knowledge and uses the dream argument as a stepping stone to hyperbolic doubt, while Zhuangzi is more interested in the transformation of things (wuhua). However, Zhuangzi still ends up with radical skepticism: since he offers no criterion to tell dream from reality, he must think all our beliefs about the world are equally unfounded. In fact, the passage proves that personal identity is an illusion because if you can't tell whether you're the butterfly or Zhuangzi, then there's no stable self. This makes Zhuangzi's view closer to modern eliminativism about the self than to Descartes's rationalism, which at least saves the thinking subject with the cogito.
