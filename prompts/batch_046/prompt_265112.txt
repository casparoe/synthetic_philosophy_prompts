Here is an argument I built for a seminar and now find suspicious because the conclusion is far broader than I intended.

(1) A discipline makes progress only when it produces answers that survive every possible reconceptualization of the problem-space; call this framework-invariant progress.
(2) Philosophy never achieves framework-invariant progress: any answer about free will, knowledge, or justice depends on prior commitments—about causation, normativity, or ontology—that a successor framework can reject root and branch.
(3) So philosophy makes no progress.
(4) By the same token, mathematics and empirical science also lack framework-invariant answers—non-Euclidean geometry reopened the parallel postulate; quantum mechanics reopened determinism.
(5) So mathematics and science also make no progress.
(6) That is absurd.
(7) Therefore (1) is false, and philosophy must be capable of progress after all.

I want to keep (2): philosophical answers really are vulnerable to root-and-branch reconceptualization in a way that, say, the existence of electrons is not. I think the slip is somewhere between (2) and (5), but I cannot name it. Where does this go wrong? If I reject (1), do I have to accept that progress is just local sedimentation—the embedding of answers in practical and disciplinary structures that makes them costly to abandon, rather than any immunity to revision? What does that cost?
