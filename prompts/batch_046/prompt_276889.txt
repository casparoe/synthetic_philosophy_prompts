I'm working through Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater in a philosophy of mind reading group, and I formalized an objection that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to make the multiple drafts model collapse into self-refutation. Here is the chain.

(1) The Cartesian theater requires a single functional point in the brain where distributed processing converges and becomes conscious experience. (2) Neuroscience reveals no such convergence point; consciousness-related processing is distributed across temporal, parietal, and prefrontal networks without a central homunculus. (3) So the theater is a fiction, and we should adopt Dennett's multiple drafts model, on which there are only parallel streams of content being edited and revised, with no authoritative "final draft" that gets displayed for a subject. (4) But if there is no authoritative final draft, then for any given mental content there is no determinate fact about whether it is currently conscious or still being edited at an unconscious stage. (5) Now consider a subject who sincerely reports, "I am now consciously experiencing a red afterimage." On the multiple drafts model, that report is itself just one more draft being edited. (6) If there is no authoritative version against which to measure it, then the report cannot be correct or incorrect about her consciousness; there is simply no standard of verification left. (7) Therefore, on this model, first-person reports about consciousness never carry epistemic authority; they are at best further drafts among drafts. (8) Yet Dennett's own heterophenomenological method treats first-person reports as constraining data that any adequate theory of consciousness must accommodate. (9) So the multiple drafts model undermines the evidential basis of the very theory that proposes it. (10) The only escape is to restore some version of the Cartesian theater as the place where reports are verified and granted authority. (11) Hence rejecting the theater forces us either to abandon heterophenomenology or to concede that consciousness is first-personally unknowable.

I want to keep (2) and (3) because the distributed neuroscience and the rejection of a central homunculus seem right, and I also want to keep (8) because heterophenomenology looks indispensable. That leaves me looking hard at (4) or (6). Which step is the slip, and what does giving it up cost? Does it require Dennett to admit that "publication" is still a real event even without a theater, or does it show that first-person authority needs something like a verification point after all? I feel like something subtle is being smuggled between the absence of a convergence point and the total loss of determinacy, but I cannot name the exact move.
