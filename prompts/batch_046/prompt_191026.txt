Analyze the 1981 strike by the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization (PATCO) and the Reagan administration's mass firing of the striking workers through the lens of Nietzsche's genealogy of morality, specifically the typology of noble and slave morality, the mechanism of ressentiment, and the inversion of values. Do not treat this as a standard labor-history case study; instead, examine the moral rhetoric on both sides as competing frameworks of value creation. First, characterize the controllers' self-justification—centered on contractual fidelity, skilled autonomy, and the honor of keeping one's word—within Nietzsche's framework: does this align with noble morality's self-affirming hierarchy and active determination of the good, or does it conceal a reactive, resentful comparison with other federal workers and management? Second, analyze the administration's framing, which emphasized the illegality of federal strikes under the Taft-Hartley Act, the breach of public duty, and the protection of travelers, asking whether this represents a sovereign, noble-moral ordering that establishes value through decisive action or a slave-moral inversion in which "good" is defined as whatever safeguards the weak (the public) from the powerful (the controllers). Third, identify what Nietzsche's genealogy fails to capture in this episode: for example, does the institutional structure of federal employment, the specific cognitive and safety demands of air-traffic control, or the legal prohibition on strikes reveal that the theory treats morality as primarily psychological affect rather than embedded in bureaucratic and statutory constraint? Fourth, assess whether Reagan's decisive, unapologetic dismissal—executed without conciliation and largely endorsed by a public that had initially sympathized with the workers—confirms Nietzsche's suspicion that modern democratic states operate through slave morality, or whether it demonstrates that state power can still express the active, hierarchical character of noble morality. Conclude by stating which features of the episode the genealogy genuinely illuminates, which it distorts by forcing them into its typology, and what the case suggests about the limits of applying a pre-institutional moral psychology to conflicts within modern administrative states.
