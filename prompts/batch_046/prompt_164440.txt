I am preparing arguments for my metaphysics seminar on the special composition question and have adopted a specific restricted view: parts compose a whole if and only if they are integrated into a single causal-dynamical system that displays downward causal efficacy irreducible to the interactions of the parts taken separately. I reject spatial contiguity and conventional kind-membership as sufficient conditions, and I take this criterion to avoid the ontological excess of universalism and the eliminativism of nihilism without collapsing into mere conventionalism. I want a rigorous assessment of what would actually count against this position. Which concrete cases—such as spatially dispersed but functionally unified processes (e.g. distributed ecological networks), quantum-entangled pairs that lack a shared spatiotemporal framework, or artefacts whose functional coherence is externally imposed—would challenge the downward-causation condition? Are there formal results in mereology or in causal modelling that demonstrate this criterion is either too permissive, licensing arbitrary scattered objects, or too restrictive, excluding entities we ordinarily take to exist? Furthermore, what does my view predict that its rivals do not—say, about the persistence conditions of organisms or social institutions—and could those predictions be assessed without begging the question? I also want to know whether defenders of this approach can insulate it by qualifying ‘integration’ as a gradable or context-sensitive notion, and if so, whether that insulation comes at the cost of unfalsifiability. Please distinguish between defeaters that would be fatal and those that are merely awkward, say which ones I should concede as genuinely counting against the view rather than dismissing, and assess whether the position risks collapsing into ontological pluralism or a disguised form of universalism once such qualifications are added.
