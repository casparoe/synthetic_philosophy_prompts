I'm auditing notes from an advanced philosophy of language seminar in which the instructor summarized two-dimensional semantics as a framework designed primarily to defend modal rationalism. According to her gloss, every expression carries two distinct intensions: a primary intension that fixes the extension in each world considered as actual by the speaker, determined solely by rational and linguistic competence without any empirical investigation, and a secondary intension that fixes the extension in worlds considered as counterfactual, which is rigid and tracks the actual-world referent. She applied this to the standard case of "water is H2O": the primary intension assigns to "water" whatever substance plays the water-role in that world, so in an epistemically possible scenario where the clear liquid in the lakes is XYZ, "water" applies to XYZ; but once the speaker knows that water is actually H2O, the secondary intension makes H2O essential to water, so in every counterfactual world water is H2O. From this she concluded that two-dimensionalism ultimately collapses the a priori/a posteriori distinction, because learning an empirical fact about the actual world rationally obligates the speaker to treat that fact as holding in all epistemically accessible worlds, turning an a posteriori discovery into an a priori constraint on the primary intension. She added that this shows the framework fails to solve Kripke's puzzle about necessary a posteriori truths, since it merely relocates the explanatory burden onto an unexplained rational competence that fixes the primary intension, and therefore defenders of 2D semantics are committed to the implausible view that scientific discovery can directly reshape our conceptual contents.

I found the summary compelling but worry that it imports commitments that actual proponents of two-dimensional semantics—particularly those working in the Chalmers-Jackson tradition—would reject.
