Write an essay about the Grand Inquisitor scene in The Brothers Karamazov and whether his trade-off between freedom and happiness actually holds up. Most people read it as people are too weak for freedom so they sell it for bread and security, but I think there's a sneaky equivocation in there between happiness as contentment and happiness as the absence of moral vertigo. The Inquisitor says Christ overestimated human strength, but he never really argues that people are happier under his system—he just argues they're less terrified. So the controversy is: is he offering happiness, or is he offering a kind of anaesthetic for existential dread? And if it's the latter, does that mean freedom isnt being traded for happiness but for something more like cognitive closure?

Weigh both sides. Maybe defend the Inquisitor by saying that for finite, embodied creatures, the elimination of radical choice IS a form of well-being, not just a numbing agent. Or attack him by arguing that Dostoevsky frames the happy masses as actually hollow—like the people in the crystal palace dream he mocks elsewhere—so the Inquisitor's happiness is parasitic on suppressing the very consciousness that makes happiness meaningful.

Reach a conclusion and justify it. Dont just summarize the scene; actually take a position on whether the freedom/happiness trade-off is coherant or if the Inquisitor smuggles in a false dichotomy. You can mention the crystal palace bit if it helps, but no need to cite page numbers or anything.
