I've been retired from field biology for about three years now and recently started hanging out with a local philosphy group—mostly people half my age who read way too much. Last Tuesday we got sidetracked on something that sounds silly but got pretty intense: what actually makes every 'a' an 'a', no matter the font or handwriting? Two people there—one's a graphic designer, the other just finished a masters in cog sci—went back and forth for like twenty minutes while I just listened and drank my coffee. I'm still not sure who was closer to right, so I want a outside opinion.

Here is how I remember it going.

(1) The designer said there's definetely a core geometric invariant: every 'a' has exactly one enclosed counter and one open stroke that connects back. Even the weird blackletter ones or double-decker handwritten ones keep that relational skeleton. She said that's why your brain can parse a totally new font instantly—it's matching a topological template, not memorizing shapes.

(2) The grad student pushed back that if you actually catalog letterforms across scripts and handwriting, there's no single invariant that survives. Single-story handwritten a's drop the enclosed counter almost entirely, and some decorative fonts turn the tail into a loop. So he said it's family resemblance, not an essence—Wittgenstein-style, no shared feature, just overlapping similarities.

(3) She came back with: okay, but the brain doesn't do family-resemblance matching in real time; visual recognition studies show we lock onto boundary relations first. Even when the counter is distorted, we still parse it as an a because the entry-exit stroke maintains its adjacency to the enclosed region. The style varies, but that adjacency relation is constant.

(4) He said that's just pushing the problem up a level—if the invariant is that abstract, then a circle with a line through it should count, and we both know it doesn't. He argued the real anchor is motor-program equivalence: we recognize an a because the stroke dynamics—pen down, loop, up, tail—match the production program we've internalized since childhood. He mentioned some sensory-substitution work where people learn to perceive patterns through tactile feedback, and they only recognize letters after learning the production kinematics, not the pure geometry.

Neither of them budged. The designer said motor programs can't explain recognizing printed text you've never written yourself. The student said geometric invariants can't explain why reversed-stroke a's feel wrong even when they're visually identical.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Rate each of the four points from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each. Also tell me who is right about which specific claim—geometry vs family resemblance vs motor dynamics—and what both of them are missing about how letter categories actually work. Don't worry about giving me a reading list, I just want to know where the argument stands.
