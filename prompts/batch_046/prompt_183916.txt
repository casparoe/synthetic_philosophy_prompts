I came across a summary of the scale argument about insects, framed in a way I had not seen before. The writer claimed that with perhaps 10^18 insects alive at any moment, even a prior of only 0.1% that a given insect has phenomenal consciousness yields an expected population of conscious subjects in the billions — exceeding humanity. They then introduced what they called a "moral risk asymmetry": if we mistakenly deny consciousness to insects, the harm of neglect is vast, whereas if we mistakenly grant it, the cost of minimal consideration is trivial, so risk-sensitive ethics should favour protection. They added that recent behavioural work — bees showing anxiety-like navigation errors after stress, and fruit flies displaying nociceptive sensitisation — should update that 0.1% prior upward, perhaps to 10% or higher. From this they concluded that consistent expected-value reasoning treats widespread insecticide use as among the worst moral harms, and that rejecting this conclusion amounts to applying risk logic selectively to avoid an uncomfortable result. I feel there are hidden leaps — from behaviour to phenomenal consciousness, and from risk asymmetry to a direct moral catastrophe — but I cannot tell which steps are fair representations of the argument and which are overreach. Could you audit this summary: which parts accurately capture the scale argument or risk-sensitive ethics, which parts conflate distinct claims, and does the conclusion survive once the misrepresentations are corrected?
