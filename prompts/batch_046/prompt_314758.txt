Please argue that the St. Petersburg paradox is best dissolved not by invoking diminishing marginal utility or standard risk aversion, but by adopting what we might call an epistemic-horizon truncation in rational evaluation. On this view, when an agent faces a gamble with unbounded potential payouts—such as the classic coin-toss game with exponentially increasing rewards—she should not integrate over the full infinite sample space. Instead, she truncates her evaluation at the boundary where successive outcomes exceed her actual cognitive and temporal capacity to coherently represent them, treating the tail beyond that boundary as decision-theoretically inert rather than as carrying further probabilistic weight. Defend the claim that this truncation is not an ad hoc psychological limitation but a normative constraint on rational expectation: show how the horizon can be fixed by the agent's epistemic resources without importing hidden risk preferences. Then address the objection that such truncation undermines expected utility theory generally—specifically, argue why it should be confined to paradoxical cases with unbounded payoff structures and need not distort rational choice in ordinary, bounded decision problems.
