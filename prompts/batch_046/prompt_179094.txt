I am preparing a graduate seminar module on the metaphysics of cognition and need a diagnostic decision tree that sorts competing accounts of intelligence by what they actually commit to about edge cases, rather than by which label a respondent prefers. The tree should begin from a shared starting point—granting that we want to distinguish genuinely intelligent performance from mere adaptive behavior or random success—and then branch through a sequence of concrete forced-choice judgments. At each node, present one specific scenario and ask which verdict it supports: for instance, an artificial system that solves previously unseen mathematical proofs but fails every commonsense physical-reasoning benchmark; a slime mold that optimizes a transport network without any central controller; a human savant with an isolated hyper-ability and pervasive deficits in other domains; and a hypothetical collective in which thousands of uncoordinated agents each perform a simple task, yet the aggregate produces solutions that no individual designed. From these cases, the branches should ask whether intelligence must be domain-general or can be fully modular, whether it requires a single bounded agent or can be genuinely distributed across a group, and whether successful output is sufficient or the system must also maintain an internal model of its own representational failures.

The leaves should locate named positions where they are well established—psychometric general-factor accounts, pluralist or modular views in the Gardner tradition, extended-cognition functionalism, and strict biological-naturalist views—while leaving explicit gaps for unoccupied combinations of answers, each briefly diagnosed (for example, whether a combination is incoherent because it demands both total domain-generality and the complete absence of any representational substrate, or whether it is merely unexplored). To keep the instrument from collapsing into a quiz about familiar labels, inject one less familiar branching distinction: whether intelligence requires what we might call autonomous representational correction, meaning the system can revise its own internal mappings when they misfire without relying on an external programmer, evolutionary selection, or social scaffolding. This should split positions that otherwise sound identical on standard criteria. The tree should contain no more than eight leaves, should never ask a respondent simply whether they think intelligence equals IQ, and should flag any pair of leaves that the tree genuinely cannot separate, explaining whether the ambiguity stems from shared commitments or from an underdetermined logical space. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the point is to map the conceptual terrain, not to attribute sources.
