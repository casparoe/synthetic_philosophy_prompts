Two grad students in my dept got into it over beers about panpsychism and the combination problem. (1) The first said panpsychism is dead on arrival: you can't derive one macro-subject from billions of micro-subjects, it's like expecting a table to emerge from atoms that are each a little bit table-y. (2) The second replied that combination happens everywhere in science—quarks combine into protons—so why is mental combination specially mysterious? (3) The first fired back that physics combines quantitative properties, but consciousness is qualitative and intrinsic; there's no "phenomenal addition" rule that turns micro-redness into macro-blueness, so the analogy fails. (4) The second said they're mixing up two different problems: the palette problem (how qualities combine) isn't the real killer, it's the subject-summing problem—how many subjects become one. They argued split-brain patients already show partial unity can break, so we just need a merging principle for subjects, not a law of phenomenal addition. They accused the first of treating both problems as one.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say which specific point each side actually gets right, and identify what both are missing—especialy whether the split-brain analogy really does the work the second student thinks it does.
