I've been kind of obsessed lately with why human extinction is bad beyond just the deaths involved. I'm not religious or anything, but I read about Samuel Scheffler's idea that people care about humanity continuing after thier own death — he calls it an afterlife conjecture, I think — and it clicked with me because I've been having these anxiety dreams about climate collapse lately.

So I was trying to explain to my friend that extinction isn't just bad for the people who die, it's bad for us now because all our projects depend on there being a future. But then she hit me with the non-identity problem: if humanity goes extinct, those future people never exist, so you can't say we've harmed them specifically. At first I thought Scheffler was saying those future generations have moral standing, but now I'm wondering if he's actually relying on some kind of impersonal value — like there's value in culture or knowledge continuing regardless of who experiences it? And if he is, doesn't that mean humanity is being treated like a super-agent with its own interests? I feel like I'm mixing up the afterlife idea with Benatar's asymmetry or maybe with collective immortality arguments, but I can't tell if that's a real problem for his view or if I'm just conflating present disappointment with harm to non-existent people. Can someone untangle whether the argument works without attributing rights to people who never get born, or does it secretly need an impersonal good that can't actually be harmed?
