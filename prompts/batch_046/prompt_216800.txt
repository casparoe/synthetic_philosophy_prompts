I'm coordinating the final assessment for our MA-level epistemology seminar on testimony and social knowledge, and I need a 90-minute essay question—aimed at about 1,200 to 1,500 words—that tests whether students can do more than summarize the standard Humean reductionist story about inductive inference or Coady’s anti-reductionist appeal to irreducible social dependence. To keep the question out of pure taxonomy-recitation, please frame it around a synthetic testimonial chain: a claim that a particular synthetic compound is carcinogenic at trace doses is transmitted through a multi-link chain—field technician collects samples, an algorithm flags statistical correlations, a peer-review filter endorses publication, a policy document summarizes the finding, and an end-user citizen acts on the warning—where no single human link possesses either direct perceptual access to the compound’s biological effects or independent reasoning sufficient to justify the carcinogenicity claim on their own, yet the chain as a whole appears to transmit justification or knowledge. Ask the candidate to evaluate whether reductionism can accommodate this chain without collapsing into anti-reductionism: specifically, they must identify exactly where in the chain a reductionist would locate the requisite perceptual bases and inductive inferences, whether that location forces the reductionist to concede an irreducibly institutional or collective entitlement, and whether an anti-reductionist can consistently deny that the chain’s justification is ultimately decomposable into individual cognitive achievements. Require them to address at least one concrete tension—such as whether the algorithmic link counts as a speaker, whether peer-review reliability can be treated as a basic source, or whether the end-user’s belief is justified only if every intermediate link is—rather than treating the chain as a black box.

Please provide the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words that makes the specific argumentative moves a top response needs, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five must-hit points with assigned weights (for example, 4 marks each). For each point, include a one-line note on the characteristic mistake that should cost marks—such as conflating algorithmic processing with human testimony, treating institutional reliability as automatically basic without argument, or describing the reductionist/anti-reductionist debate in generic terms without applying it to the synthetic chain. The difficulty should come from choosing the crux: a student who merely defines reductionism and anti-reductionism and lists familiar objections should not be able to earn more than about half the marks.
