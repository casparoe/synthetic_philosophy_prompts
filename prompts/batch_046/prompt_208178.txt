I sit on my city's Heritage and Public Interpretation Board and we're meeting next tues to vote on a $200k grant for an outdoor instalation called "The Affluence Trap" — it walks visitors backwards through the neolithic transition and argues that agriculture wasn't just a tech shift but a kind of social lock-in to hierarchy and labor surplus. The designers lean on hunter-gatherer affluence, but they inject this niche distinction: "caloric affluence" (more food) versus "relational affluence" (flat status distance), and they claim modern urban welfare should be judged by the second metric rather than GDP-style growth. Half the board thinks this is a philosophically defensible framing of archaeological consensus; the other half thinks it's using public money to push a romanticized normative baseline — like, even if foraging societies had low inequality, we're not going back, so why fund an exibit that treats flat social distance as a live policy benchmark for a city of two million? I need a short memo circulated by monday that recommends approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with a concrete time-frame for any design revisions if we go conditional. It should take the designers' strongest point seriously — especially that storage and surplus have structural roots in inequality — but also honestly say what's wrong with treating a foraging social structure as a practical benchmark today. Don't give me a bibliography; just work through the conceptual tension and tell me what the board should actually do.
