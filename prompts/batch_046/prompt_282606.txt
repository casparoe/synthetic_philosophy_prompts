In historical Jesus research, scholars have applied the standard criteria of authenticity—multiple independent attestation, embarrassment, dissimilarity from both Judaism and early Christianity, and coherence—for several generations, working with substantially the same textual corpus and archaeological backdrop. The result is not convergence but a stable pluralism: one reconstructed Jesus is an eschatological prophet announcing imminent divine judgment, another a Cynic-like wisdom teacher, another a non-violent resistance organizer, and the arguments for each seem to recycle the same evidence with different weightings. What kind of stall is this? Is there a hidden empirical disagreement—say, about the dating of Q or the independence of Thomas—that, if resolved, would collapse the divergence? Or is the disagreement methodological: do the sides differ about what counts as data (say, whether the Gospel of Thomas can serve as an independent witness), about what the criteria actually prove (probability versus plausibility), or about whether biography is even the right goal? I have a hypothesis I want tested: that the impasse is driven by an unacknowledged ambiguity in authenticity itself, where one camp treats it as source-layer recovery (what Jesus actually said) and another treats it as event-layer explanation (which traditions best account for the shape of the movement after his death), so that both sides apply the same criteria but to different questions. If that is right, is the debate ill-posed, or can a conceptual distinction—perhaps separating historical-probabilistic authenticity from causally-explanatory significance—actually move things? And if it is not right, what is the real structure of the disagreement: bedrock intuitions about the nature of first-century Galilee, a hidden normative commitment about what a founder-figure must be like, or something else? I want a diagnosis that engages the actual positions—apocalyptic versus wisdom versus social-reform portraits—not a survey of the field, together with an honest assessment of whether any new criterion, archaeological find, or formal historiographical result could end the stall, or whether the divergence is permanent given the nature of the sources.
