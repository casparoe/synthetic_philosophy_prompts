I've been drafting an essay on the architecture of valid consent, spurred partly by recent forum discussions about whether epistemic humility ought to be treated as a precondition for autonomy rather than merely an intellectual virtue. The conventional bioethical taxonomy partitions invalid consent into three failures: deception (informational deficit), coercion (volitional interference), and incompetence (cognitive incapacity). I want to propose a fourth, orthogonal failure mode that I am calling calibration failure—cases in which a subject receives fully transparent disclosure, faces no external pressure, and possesses adequate domain-general reasoning, yet holds a systematically distorted model of her own competence to integrate that information into an accurate personal risk assessment. To fix ideas, I sketch a scenario involving a research subject with advanced quantitative training—say, a theoretical physicist—who enrolls in an early-phase gene-therapy trial. She parses the statistical disclosures correctly; what she misjudges is the gap between her fluency with formal notation and her capacity to weight clinical base rates, long-tail adverse events, and embodied bodily risk. Her consent is procedurally impeccable, but I want to argue it is substantively hollow because her autonomy operates on a miscalibrated self-model.

The essay then asks whether calibration failure should be recognized as a distinct vitiating condition, or whether it collapses into an expansive reading of incompetence on one side, or into a subtle duty-to-correct that shades into deception on the other. I also examine whether institutional remedies—structured reflection protocols or third-party calibration probes—would protect autonomy or erode it through paternalism, and I draw an analogy to financial risk disclosure, where regulators treat investor overconfidence as a systemic externality rather than an individual cognitive failure. I am not asking for a bibliography or citation check; the framing is deliberately speculative. I want structural and conceptual feedback: does the four-part taxonomy hold together? Is calibration failure empirically distinguishable from incompetence in clinical populations? Where does the financial-analogy break down when bodily integrity rather than capital is at stake? And does the conclusion—that we should treat meta-cognitive calibration as an independent axis of consent validity—survive once the disanalogies are on the table?
