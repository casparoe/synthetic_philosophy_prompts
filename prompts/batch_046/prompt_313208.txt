In my school's philosophy seminar, we recently read the White Horse dialogue associated with the School of Names, particularly Gongsun Long's well-known argument that a white horse is not a horse. Afterward, two of my classmates—Maya and Leo—got into a sustained disagreement about what the dialogue is actually demonstrating, and they asked me to help settle it, though I genuinely could not decide.

Maya took the position that the dialogue is primarily a logical and linguistic exercise about reference: 'horse' is a simple predicate that picks out a species, while 'white horse' is a compound predicate that picks out the intersection of being a horse and being white; since the extensions differ, the terms do not denote the same thing, and denying their identity is just rigorous taxonomy rather than any real paradox. Leo responded that this interpretation makes Gongsun Long into a trivial logician and ignores the metaphysical and epistemological concerns of the Mingjia school—specifically, that the dialogue is about whether accidental properties like color can be conceptually separated from a substance without altering the substance's essential nature, and that Gongsun Long is probing whether compound descriptions ever truly capture the reality of the thing named. Maya then argued that Leo is anachronistically importing modern essentialist metaphysics into a text concerned with the rectification of names (zhengming), where the point is precisely that language can mislead us into treating compound descriptions as if they named unified realities. Leo countered that separating language from ontology is itself anachronistic for the School of Names, since for them the correctness of names was inseparable from the structure of reality, and that Maya's taxonomic reading fails to explain why the dialogue was historically treated as philosophically puzzling rather than merely obvious.

Could you adjudicate their exchange step by step? For each move—Maya's initial logical interpretation, Leo's metaphysical counter, Maya's charge of anachronism about zhengming, and Leo's reply about the inseparability of language and ontology—say who makes the stronger point and briefly justify your assessment. Then give an overall verdict: is the White Horse dialogue best understood as a problem of logical reference, metaphysical essentialism, or the relationship between naming and reality? Also address whether, on the most charitable reading, Gongsun Long is genuinely denying that white horses belong to the category of horse, or whether his conclusion is compatible with ordinary common sense once properly interpreted.
