Hey, I'm trying to understand this bit from Plutarch's Life of Alexander (the part where he explains why he writes lives not histories). He writes:

"For it is not Histories that I am writing, but Lives; and in the most illustrious deeds there is not always a manifestation of virtue or vice, nay, a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles when thousands fall, or the greatest armaments, or sieges of cities."

Then he compares painters who capture likeness from the face and eyes (where character shows) but mostly ignore the rest of the body.

I'm a bit stuck on a wierd tension here. If Plutarch thinks the real character-revealing stuff is these tiny micro-signals—a phrase, a jest—then doesn't that make biography a pretty shaky tool for moral learning? I mean, hagiography usually picks big heroic deeds to imitate, but he's saying the big deeds might not even show virtue or vice. So is he warning us not to learn from grand narratives, or is he saying biographers should hunt for those small revealing moments?

Also, the painter analogy feels off to me. Painters focus on face/eyes and neglect the body; is he saying biography should do the same—focus on the "face" of the soul and ignore the "body" of political events? That seems like it undercuts the whole point of writing about famous people, since their fame comes from those big events he's telling us to mostly ignore.

Can you help me parse what he's actually arguing? Is there a hidden contradiction between using lives for moral education and admitting that the important stuff is these uncurated, tiny details?
