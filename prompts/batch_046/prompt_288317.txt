You are a professional philosopher of decision theory and institutional ethics with deep familiarity with the normative foundations of risk, intertemporal rationality, and fiduciary obligation. Before drafting, please take a moment to brainstorm the core criteria an investment mandate for a multi-generational institution should satisfy—growth sustainability, protection against absorbing failure, distributive fairness across cohorts, and fidelity to charitable purpose—and identify precisely where these criteria come into conflict.

I serve on my university's Long-Term Endowment Governance Committee, and I need a formal written opinion for circulation before Thursday's meeting. We must decide the investment mandate for the new Perpetual Scholarship Fund, a century-scale endowment intended to provide need-based aid across successive, discrete generations of students. The investment subcommittee proposes adopting a strict Kelly-criterion strategy: maximize the geometric growth rate of the fund's real value, arguing that geometric rationality is the uniquely correct normative standard for any agent facing an indefinite sequence of independent investment opportunities. The dissenting bloc—drawing on non-ergodic economics and intergenerational ethics—objects that this framework implicitly treats the institution as a single continuous agent with multiplicative utility, whereas our fund actually serves distinct cohorts of students whose welfare is not fungible across time; they further contend that geometric optimization tolerates risk profiles in which a single catastrophic drawdown functions as an absorbing barrier that permanently eliminates all future beneficiaries, making ensemble-average reasoning inappropriate for fiduciary duty. Half the committee regards this as a straightforward application of rational-choice theory to long horizons; the other half regards it as a category error that conflates personal gambling strategy with moral obligations to discrete future persons.

Please draft the committee's decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, including an explicit implementation or review timeline. The memo should present a clear, actionable bottom line; it should engage the strongest version of the geometric-rationality claim, explaining whether and why it succeeds or fails in this specific institutional context, and it should treat the non-ergodic and intergenerational-justice objection as a substantive philosophical challenge rather than as mere risk aversion. Include reasoning that members of either camp could recognize as fair, and if your recommendation is qualified, specify concrete monitoring conditions, leverage caps, or safeguard mechanisms. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the reasoning should stand on its own.
