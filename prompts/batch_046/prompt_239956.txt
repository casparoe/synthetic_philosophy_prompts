Aquinas distinguishes two kinds of tyranny. The first is the usurper, who seizes power without legitimate title; the second is the abusive ruler, who originally governed justly but now violates the common good. Against an usurper, Aquinas holds that subjects owe no obedience, and because no legitimate authority is being overthrown, private citizens as well as public bodies may resist and, if necessary, remove him—provided that the action does not produce a greater disorder than the tyranny itself. Against an abusive ruler, however, the situation is different: since he originally possessed legitimate authority, private individuals are strictly forbidden from acting on their own initiative, both because such assassination violates natural law and because it invites anarchy. Here, only the corporate body of the people, acting through its legitimate representatives, may undertake deposition, and only when the corruption is severe and irreparable. Aquinas applies the greater-evil principle symmetrically: in both cases, one must weigh the probable harm of resistance against the harm of continued tyranny. Thus, while he never endorses private tyrannicide against a legitimate ruler, he leaves open legitimate resistance—whether private or public—against an usurper, with the key distinction being the presence or absence of an original rightful claim to obedience.
