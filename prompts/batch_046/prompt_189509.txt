I'm giving the retirement toast for my old advisor Dr. Elena Vasquez this friday. She taught philosophy of mind for about thirty years and also ran the university's wooden boat restoration workshop, which is actually how I met her—I was a philosophy undergrad who accidentally signed up for her boatbuilding elective instead of the logic seminar. The audience is going to be split right down the middle: half the philosophy department who know her papers on skill and propositional knowledge, and half the boatyard crew who just know her as the person who taught them to steam-bend oak frames and read the grain. I want the toast to actually do something with the knowing-how versus knowing-that distinction, not just decorate the speech with a philosophy reference. Specifically, I recently tried to repair my own little skiff using only youtube videos and a manual—pure knowing-that—and I totally messed up the planking because I didn't have the feel for how the wood moves under the plane. So I need the speech to navigate whether her legacy is best described as transmitting "knowledge" in some general sense, or whether there's something structurally seperate about what she passed on in the workshop versus the seminar room. The philosophers will catch references to Ryle's regress and probably the intellectualist challenge, but I don't want it to turn into a lecture for the boat people. It should be warm, about four minutes, and avoid saying anything false about her—she'd hate it if I implied she was in some mystical realm of ineffable skill, but she also wouldn't want me to reduce the craft to a set of instructions. Can you write it? I keep drafting versions and they either sound too academic or too sentimental, and I need it by tomorrow.
