Analyze the 2000 Illinois moratorium on executions and Governor George Ryan’s 2003 mass commutation of all remaining death sentences as an episode that tests the standard tripartite justification of capital punishment—retributive proportionality, deterrence, and the risk of executing the innocent. Rather than treating the risk of innocence as a uniform probabilistic cost, frame it as an asymmetric institutional error that specifically undermines the state’s moral authority to administer retributive punishment, and distinguish ex ante deterrence (the effect on potential offenders) from ex post institutional credibility (the effect on public trust after wrongful convictions are revealed). First, map the episode onto this framework with discipline: identify the institutional actors, the options available to the state before and after the series of death-row exonerations, and how each pillar—retribution, deterrence, and error-risk—bears on the decision to halt executions and then to commute every sentence. Second, say which features the framework explains well and which it misdescribes or leaves silent; in particular, address whether the blanket commutation of sentences for inmates whose individual guilt was not challenged shows that the error-risk had migrated from an evidentiary problem to a systemic legitimacy problem. Third, assess whether the Illinois case is best read as confirmation that the risk of innocence is an unavoidable constraint on capital punishment, or as an anomaly revealing the standard triad to be structurally incomplete—especially regarding the distinction between individual-level desert and system-level legitimacy. Be explicit about where the mapping breaks down and do not force the theory to speak where it offers no clear verdict.
