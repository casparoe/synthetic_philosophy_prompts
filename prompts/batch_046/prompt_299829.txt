I am drafting my philosophical profile on democratic membership for a reading group, and I want the others to stress-test the consistency of my commitments. Here is where I stand.

First, I take membership in the demos to be grounded primarily in subjection: you belong if you live under the coercive basic structure for a non-trivial period, such that you can be expected to comply with its laws and are shaped by them over time. From this I infer that long-term resident non-citizens should hold full national voting rights, and that citizens who have permanently relocated abroad should eventually forfeit theirs.

Second, I also accept an affected-interests principle: being profoundly and irreversibly shaped by a collective decision without any say in it is a form of domination that damages democratic legitimacy, and this applies even across territorial borders.

From these two principles together, I support binding institutional representation for future generations—say, an ombuds office with veto power over long-range ecological and fiscal commitments—even though those representatives are not electorally accountable to the people they stand for.

Third, I deny that temporary visitors, seasonal laborers, or short-term contract workers should have local voting rights, even when municipal zoning or labor ordinances directly determine the conditions of their stay.

Fourth, I hold that people with severe, permanent cognitive impairments should retain full voting rights if they can express a stable preference, because withholding the franchise on grounds of cognitive capacity inflicts a status injury incompatible with equal citizenship. At the same time, I maintain that legitimate democratic deliberation requires participants to be capable of revising their preferences in response to public reasons.

Fifth, I reject extending formal membership to sentient non-human animals, but I support mandatory legal advocates for them in legislative committees.

Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which conflicts are only apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and how severe each real tension is. For each, specify what the cheapest fix would be—whether revising a principle, overturning a derivation, or adjusting a case verdict—and what it costs. Order the tensions by severity. Then tell me which single revision to my set would eliminate the most conflicts, and what intuitions I would have to sacrifice to make it. Do not treat this as a lecture on democratic theory; treat it as an audit of my particular position.
