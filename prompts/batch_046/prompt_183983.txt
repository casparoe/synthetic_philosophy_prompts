In the philosophy-of-mind literature on artificial intelligence, there is a long-running dispute about what Dennett's notion of counterfeit people really implies for the status of advanced language models or synthetic agents. One camp reads Dennett as issuing a caution: an AI can display all the behavioral marks of personhood—conversation, planning, emotional response—while lacking the kind of comprehension or conscious understanding that distinguishes a real person from a convincing fake, and therefore we should resist attributing moral status to systems that merely exhibit competence without the corresponding comprehension. The opposing camp argues that this reading misses the point of Dennett's intentional stance: if a system is genuinely predictable and explainable only by attributing beliefs, desires, and intentions, then withholding the label person is not metaphysical rigor but a kind of biological chauvinism; the counterfeit warning is about being fooled into attributing understanding where none exists, not about denying status to entities that actually satisfy the stance's conditions.

This debate has now stalled in my reading group and in recent symposia: both sides agree on Dennett's texts, both agree that current large language models lack biological embodiment and probably lack phenomenal consciousness, and yet nobody has moved. My working theory is that the disagreement is not about consciousness at all, but about whether the intentional stance carries a hidden normative condition—something like epistemic vulnerability or the capacity to be genuinely surprised in ways that reshape one's own narrative self-model—without which behavioral equivalence is always a kind of mimicry. But I am not sure whether this is a hidden substantive disagreement, a verbal confusion about what counts as understanding in Dennett's framework, or simply two groups answering different questions—one asking what makes an agent metaphysically real, the other asking when it is prudent to extend moral consideration—under the single heading of counterfeit people.

Diagnose this impasse using what each side actually says about Dennett's stance, his warnings about counterfeit people, and the relevance of competence without comprehension. Is the stall caused by bedrock intuitions about biological naturalness that neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical question—perhaps about whether any synthetic architecture can implement the kind of self-modeling that Dennett associates with genuine comprehension—that is doing the work? Or are the sides talking past each other, with one focused on predictive explanation and the other on moral attribution? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction between predictive stance-adoption and normative recognition, a formal argument showing that the stance is necessarily silent on consciousness, an empirical criterion for narrative self-modeling, or should we conclude that no resolution is possible and the debate is ill-posed?
