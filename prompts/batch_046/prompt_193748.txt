I'm taking this course on poltical philosophy and we got into this huge debate about Norway's Halden prison vs American mass incarceration, and now I'm kind of stuck on what actually matters here. Like, everyone knows Halden is this weirdly humane prison where guards don't carry weapons and inmates cook together, and the US has this massive punitive system built on retribution and incapacitation. But people jump straight to "we should copy Norway" or "that'd never work here" without figuring out what the actual philosophical disagreement is about. Is it about whether punishment has to involve suffering to count as punishment? Is it about whether the state has a positive duty to rehabilitate people, or just a negative right to separate dangerous ones? Or is it something deeper about whether you can treat prisoners with dignity while still depriving them of liberty — like, is dignity compatible with coercion in this specific context?

I'm trying to figure out where the actual philosophical work needs to happen if we want to compare these systems honestly. There's this cluster of open questions I'm seeing. First, whether rehabilitation can be coerced without being self-defeating — if you're forced into therapy or education, does that undermine the autonomy it's supposed to restore? Second, whether the normalization principle in Halden, where prison life is designed to mirror outside life as closely as possible, makes sense in societies with totally different levels of social trust, economic equality, and informal social control. Third, whether recidivism rates tell us anything about justice at all, or if that's just a utilitarian side-issue that distracts from whether punishment is morally deserved regardless of outcomes. Fourth, whether comparing these two systems requires first solving some problem about collective responsibility — like, can you blame a nation for its prison conditions in the same way you'd blame an individual, or is that just a metaphor that confuses institutional design with personal guilt?

If I'm going to direct limited attention — say, a research grant or a semester's work — which of these are actually upstream? Which ones, if settled, would change how you'd answer the others? And which are probably dead ends, either because they're not tractable with current philosophical tools, or because answering them wouldn't change what a policymaker should actually do? I'm especially curious if there's a hidden dependency I'm missing, like maybe you can't resolve the coercion question without first deciding something about the legitimacy of state authority over citizens, or whether the recidivisim debate is totally independent and people just conflate it with the dignity question for rhetorical effect. Give me a defended agenda, not just a survey, and don't just list books I should read.
