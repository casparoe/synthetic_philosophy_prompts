Its advocates argue that scale finally overcomes the archival invisibility of ordinary people; its critics observe that algorithmic aggregation substitutes predictive categorization for interpretive reconstruction.

How might he respond to the argument that processing vastly more documents solves the problem of historical silence without requiring the historian's empathetic interpretation? Can statistical regularities across mass archival data stand in for, or even surpass, the reconstruction of lived experience and conscious agency? Finally, identify an internal tension in Thompson's commitments—between his democratic impulse to make ordinary lives visible and his insistence that such visibility requires humanistic interpretation rather than structural abstraction—that this scenario renders especially acute.
