I've been thinking about the boundary problem—who actually belongs to "the demos"—partly because I work remotely for a firm based in another country. I pay taxes there, follow its labor regulations, and am bound by its contracts, yet I have no vote in its elections, while someone born there who has never contributed does. From this I built an argument that seems to prove too much: either democratic boundaries dissolve into a single global electorate, or the principle that those affected by collective decisions should have a say in them is wrong. Here is the chain: (1) Democratic legitimacy requires that anyone genuinely bound by a group's coercive laws can participate in making them. (2) Fairness then demands that anyone in a relevantly identical position—same exposure to the laws, same practical stakes—must also be included, or else membership is just an accident of birthplace or passport. (3) So if Paris residents vote in French elections, a Brussels commuter who works under French labor law and pays French taxes should vote there too. (4) By the same token, anyone whose livelihood is coercively shaped by French trade policy—for instance, a farmer overseas whose income depends entirely on those rules—is also affected and should belong to the French demos. (5) Once that farmer is included, the reasoning applies recursively to everyone tied to him: local trading partners, descendants affected by long-term policy, and so on. (6) The only consistent stopping points are a single world democracy for every local decision, which seems impossible to operate, or an admission that democratic membership is purely conventional with no moral basis. (7) Yet abandoning the idea that being affected matters feels deeply wrong. Where exactly does this chain break? Is there a hidden equivocation between being "subject to law" and being "affected by policy"? Which premise should I reject, and what does each rejection cost? Please answer in American English, and treat me as an intelligent non-specialist—no assumed familiarity with academic literature, just clear reasoning about the steps and where the slope actually gets slippery.
