I am working through a philosophical fiction exercise and would like you to write a short story—roughly 800 to 1,200 words—that reimagines Avicenna's flying man thought experiment in a contemporary clinical setting. In the classic formulation, a fully formed person is suddenly suspended in empty space, limbs spread so that no part of the body touches another, with all external senses and internal bodily awareness completely absent; despite this total deprivation, the subject is said to retain an immediate, undeniable awareness that he exists, though he would have no awareness of possessing a body. For your narrative, place the protagonist inside a real, physical near-future isolation chamber engineered to reproduce precisely this condition: weightless, dark, silent, and pharmacologically stripped of interoception and proprioception, leaving only what remains of bare self-consciousness.

Inject the following niche framing into the scenario: the protagonist is not a single continuous consciousness but rather two synchronized cognitive streams—Stream A and Stream B—occupying the same suspended body but experiencing the void with incompatible temporal structures. Stream A registers existence as a sequence of discrete, countable now-pulses, while Stream B experiences an unbroken, timeless continuity without intervals. The story should explore, through internal reasoning rather than external observation or recovered memory, whether the certainty of "I exist" that survives sensory deprivation can still be coherently asserted when the "I" is fractured along these temporal lines—whether Stream A and Stream B constitute one self, two selves, or a grammatical fiction. Avoid standard virtual-reality or mind-upload tropes; treat the chamber as tangible medical apparatus, and let the philosophical tension emerge from the protagonist's attempts to determine whether his self-knowledge proves an immaterial soul, reveals selfhood as a linguistic habit, or indicates that some unnoticed sensory residue has escaped suppression. Conclude not with a resolution, but with a final moment that forces the reader to decide between these three interpretations.
