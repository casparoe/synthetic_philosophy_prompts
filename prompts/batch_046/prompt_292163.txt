I'm writing from the perspective of a student in a graduate seminar on distributive justice. Last week my study group—Elena and Marcus—got stuck in a disagreement about whether prioritarianism genuinely avoids the leveling-down objection, and I haven't been able to resolve it for them.

Elena started with the standard defense: prioritarianism weights benefits by how badly off the recipient is, but it assigns no intrinsic value to equality itself. So if we compare a distribution where Alice is at welfare 20 and Bob is at 10 against one where Alice is leveled down to 10 and Bob stays at 10, prioritarianism clearly prefers the first. The concave weighting function only makes benefits to the worse off more valuable; it never makes harming the better off valuable in its own right. Therefore, she said, the leveling-down objection—properly understood—only sticks to pure egalitarianism, not to prioritarianism.

Marcus agreed that prioritarianism doesn't value equality intrinsically, but he introduced a structural twist. He asked us to imagine an indivisible positional good—say, access to a highly visible elite credential—that can only be awarded to one person and that, by its nature, produces status-comparison effects. If the credential goes to someone already well off, the worse off don't just stay at 10; they suffer a measurable welfare loss from relative deprivation, falling to 8. If the credential is withheld entirely, everyone remains at 10. Marcus argued that in this case prioritarianism should recommend withholding the credential—effectively leveling down the well-off person's opportunity—even though no one is made absolutely better off. Elena objected that this isn't leveling down in the objectionable sense; it's just preventing a harm to the worse off, so prioritarianism is still tracking absolute welfare, not equality.

Marcus then pushed further. He said that if comparison effects are built into the well-being metric this way, then any time reducing the best-off prevents a relative-deprivation harm to the worst-off, prioritarianism will endorse harming the best-off without direct redistribution. That makes the view structurally indistinguishable from egalitarianism in practice, because the "priority" weighting is doing no independent work—the outcome is driven entirely by the equality-preserving move. Elena replied that there's still a conceptual difference: egalitarianism cares about the gap itself, whereas prioritarianism cares about the gap only instrumentally, through its causal impact on absolute welfare. She also noted that if comparison effects are weak or absent, prioritarianism will happily allow large inequalities, which pure egalitarianism wouldn't.

Neither of them would concede the core point. Marcus insisted that once you allow relative welfare effects into the calculus, the distinction between "priority to the worse off" and "equality of welfare" collapses into semantics. Elena insisted that the causal pathway matters: harming the well-off is only justified when it actually protects the worse off, not when it merely makes the distribution flatter.

I'd like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange. Specifically: is Marcus right that prioritarianism, once it accommodates comparison effects, is vulnerable to a form of the leveling-down objection? Or is Elena correct that the causal structure preserves a meaningful distinction? And what, if anything, are both of them missing—perhaps about how prioritarian weighting should treat non-transferable goods, or about whether the concavity of the weighting function itself introduces an implicit egalitarian commitment independent of comparison effects? Please treat this as an assessment of the arguments as actually made, not as generic advice.
