I'm grading papers for an advanced seminar in comparative metaphysics and epistemology. The exam presented the following question:

"Jain anekāntavāda holds that reality (sat) possesses infinite attributes (ananta-dharma) and that any single cognitive grasp captures only one partial standpoint, or naya. The syādvāda method expresses this through conditional predication marked by syāt ('perhaps' or 'in some respect'). Consider a case of peer disagreement in normative ethics: two equally rational agents, A and B, judge the very same action to be morally right and morally wrong, respectively, with no available higher-order arbitration.

(a) Explain how anekāntavāda addresses such contradictory judgments without collapsing into subjective relativism, making specific reference to syādvāda and the doctrine of naya.

(b) A critic argues that applying the sapta-bhaṅgī (sevenfold predication) to this case—asserting that the action 'is right,' 'is not right,' 'is both right and not right,' 'is inexpressible,' and the corresponding combinations, each qualified by syāt—merely redescribes the disagreement rather than resolving it, and that normative ethics requires decisive commitment rather than perpetual qualification. Evaluate this criticism and identify one limitation internal to using anekāntavāda in normative ethical reasoning."

One student submitted this answer:

"Jain anekāntavāda denies that reality can be captured in a single description because every substance has infinite properties. Each judgment is a naya—a viewpoint that highlights one aspect while suppressing others. Syādvāda formalizes this by prefixing every assertion with syāt, signaling that the predicate applies only under the conditions of that particular naya.

In the peer disagreement case, A's judgment expresses one naya and B's judgment expresses another. Because anekāntavāda denies absolute exclusivity, both judgments capture real aspects of the action. The contradiction is resolved not by finding a hidden fact, but by recognizing that rightness and wrongness are not contradictory in the absolute sense: they belong to different descriptive registers of the same complex object.

The sapta-bhaṅgī extends this logic by enumerating all seven modal combinations—'it is,' 'it is not,' 'it is and is not,' 'it is inexpressible,' and the rest—each introduced by syāt. Applying this to moral disagreement means the action can bear all seven descriptions simultaneously, provided each is qualified. This dissolves the conflict, so the critic is wrong: anekāntavāda does not merely redescribe disagreement, but shows that 'right' and 'wrong' are not mutually exclusive properties.

There is an internal limitation, however. Because every judgment must carry the syāt qualification, normative ethics can never deliver an unqualified prescription; one can never say 'do X' without implicitly adding 'from one standpoint,' which undermines action-guiding force. Furthermore, the doctrine requires that contradictory properties literally inhere in the same object at the same time. Jain philosophers defend this by distinguishing substance (dravya) from its modes (paryāya), though applying that distinction consistently in dynamic moral contexts is difficult."
