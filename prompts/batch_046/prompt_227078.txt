Having spent thirty-five years in experimental condensed-matter physics and only recently turned to philosophy of science in retirement, I find myself facing an epistemic puzzle in my own past work that I suspect mirrors a well-known problem—though I prefer not to name it and risk framing the answer. In the mid-1990s, my laboratory compiled an extensive archive of thermal-fluctuation spectra in amorphous thin films, all interpreted at the time within the standard equilibrium fluctuation-dissipation framework. Several years ago, I independently adopted—on theoretical grounds unrelated to those old measurements—a modified non-equilibrium extension that predicts a specific, previously unremarked asymmetry in the high-frequency tail of the spectrum.

Returning to our 1994 notebooks, I find that three of the runs do display that asymmetry, though we never recorded it as significant then. I am now drafting a review in which I wish to cite these archival measurements as confirmatory support for the modified framework. My unease is not statistical but philosophical: I have known these data for decades; they played no causal role in my adopting the new theory, and I only examined them for the asymmetry after my theoretical commitment was formed. Still, the fit is genuine.

Can there be a principled distinction—perhaps turning on whether the framework genuinely predicted the asymmetry in advance rather than accommodating it post hoc—between using long-familiar evidence to bolster a newly held view and using genuinely novel evidence? More specifically, should my prior acquaintance with these measurements diminish, leave unchanged, or even enhance their confirmatory weight, and does the answer depend on whether I treat my prior knowledge of the data as part of my background belief state or as part of the evidential base?

Please address this with analytical precision, and in doing so clearly separate what is settled among those who work on these questions from what remains genuinely contested; do not present any disputed position as though it were the consensus. I ask for no citations, bibliographies, or lists of further reading—only the reasoning itself, laid out as rigorously as one might in a formal treatment.
