I am defending the position that clinical triage algorithms should treat the vagueness of categories like "critically ill" through an epistemicist lens—holding that there exists a precise, unknown physiological boundary—rather than through supervaluationism, which tolerates genuine indeterminacy and thereby permits arbitrary tie-breaking in resource allocation. I argue that this epistemicist commitment is necessary for distributive justice in emergency medicine, not merely a semantic preference.

Next week I present this to an interdisciplinary seminar with the following composition: two philosophers of language who are committed supervaluationists and regard vagueness as a purely semantic phenomenon without practical stakes; one contextualist about medical language who believes category boundaries shift with institutional context; two medical ethicists concerned with algorithmic fairness but unfamiliar with formal semantics; one health-law scholar skeptical that philosophical vagueness debates have any bearing on regulatory drafting; and one biostatistician who will ask whether unknown sharp boundaries are empirically tractable. The philosophers care about preserving flexible clinical judgment; the ethicists care about transparency and non-discrimination; the lawyer cares about administrability; the statistician cares about measurability.

My argument sketch: (1) Supervaluationism about "critically ill" implies that for borderline patients there is no fact of the matter about correct triage priority, which licenses arbitrary ordering; (2) Arbitrary ordering in life-saving resource allocation violates a minimal condition of distributive justice (non-arbitrariness); (3) Epistemicism denies this indeterminacy—there is a fact, though unknown—which forces investment in better biomarkers rather than acceptance of conventional cutoffs; (4) Therefore, epistemicism is the appropriate framework for algorithmic triage design.
