Student answer:
Malcolm argues that dreams are not experiences because the criteria for saying someone is having an experience—being awake, able to report the experience, and behaving consistently with it—are absent during sleep. Following Wittgenstein, he holds that mental-state language requires public criteria, so without the possibility of verification in sleep, we have no grounds for attributing inner experiences to a sleeper. Dennett, however, proposes the cassette theory: the brain records a narrative during REM sleep that is played back upon awakening, like a cassette inserted into memory. This explains dream reports without positing actual conscious experience during sleep. Therefore Dennett rejects Malcolm's conclusion, maintaining that the cassette mechanism proves dreams are real experiences that happen during sleep but are immediately forgotten. Both philosophers nevertheless agree that any genuine experience must in principle be reportable at the time it occurs, and both appeal partly to empirical sleep science—Malcolm by citing the lack of REM-related cortical activation for conscious experience, Dennett by invoking neurocognitive studies of memory consolidation—to ground their respective positions.
