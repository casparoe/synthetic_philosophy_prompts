Since a recent experience with general anesthesia left me unsettled by the apparent discontinuity of my consciousness despite the uninterrupted physical presence of my body, I have been trying to determine what actually makes a physical system a single conscious subject rather than none, many, or an unrecognizable cosmic one. Here are my verdicts on seven cases, and I want to know whether any coherent principle captures them all or whether I must abandon some.

Case 1: A healthy, awake human brain with normal functional connectivity. Verdict: One unified phenomenal subject is present.

Case 2: The very same brain under deep propofol anesthesia, with global functional disconnectivity but intact anatomy and no structural damage. Verdict: No unified subject is present; consciousness is absent, not merely hidden.

Case 3: A split-brain patient with complete corpus callosotomy, awake and behaving through a single body. Verdict: Ambiguous—probably not one fully unified subject, yet not clearly two full subjects either; bodily unity seems to preserve some singular personhood despite the internal division.

Case 4: Two fully intact human brains in separate bodies linked by a hypothetical perfect bidirectional neural interface that allows instantaneous, lossless sharing of all conscious contents. Verdict: Still two subjects, not one merged consciousness; perfect synchronization is not phenomenal identity.

Case 5: A distributed artificial system with an architecture isomorphic to a human brain's functional connectivity graph, running globally across thousands of non-biological servers with no shared evolutionary or developmental history. Verdict: Not a conscious subject, despite the functional isomorphism.

Case 6: The universe considered as one system under cosmopsychism, with all physical processes contributing micro-experiential properties that combine into a single cosmic experience. Verdict: Even if this metaphysical picture is true, there is no reason to think it produces one subject analogous to human consciousness; if a cosmic subject exists, it is phenomenally unrecognizable and not "one of us" in any meaningful sense.

Case 7: A single human brain in which forty percent of cortical processing has been gradually offloaded over a decade to an external wireless device that maintains real-time dynamical coupling with the remaining biological tissue, preserving behavioral and cognitive continuity throughout. Verdict: Still one subject, though the spatial and material boundary of that subject has become genuinely unclear.

The pattern I think I see involves both functional integration and spatiotemporal-historical continuity within a single organizational origin, but I cannot make it precise. Case 2 shows that anatomy without function is insufficient. Case 4 shows that function without a shared bodily-historical origin is insufficient. Case 5 shows that function without biological development is insufficient. Yet Case 7 shows that biological substrate can be partially replaced and spatially distributed without destroying unity. Meanwhile Case 3 is puzzling: internal functional division within one origin produces ambiguity rather than clear multiplicity, whereas Case 4's perfect integration across separate origins produces clear multiplicity. And Case 6 threatens to show that a single origin with universal integration still does not yield the kind of subject I recognize.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving a specific threshold of integrated information within a causally continuous boundary that traces back to a single developmental origin—that captures all seven verdicts? Or must I drop my judgment about Case 4, or about Case 5, or about Case 7? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, apply it to a further case I have not considered (for instance, a hive of social insects with global chemical coordination achieving colony-level optimization, or a human brain that has been divided and each hemisphere placed in a separate robotic body with independent sensory input), and be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. Also, please say what argument or observation would most likely change your assessment—whether empirical evidence about split-brain patients, a formal result about information integration in distributed non-biological systems, or a conceptual point about the transparency of phenomenal unity.
