The debate over extending Dennett’s intentional stance to large language models has, in my view, settled into a predictable rhythm: one side argues that attributing beliefs is justified whenever it improves predictive accuracy, regardless of internal architecture, while the other side maintains that without representational vehicles organized by satisfaction conditions, such attributions are merely convenient fictions. Despite years of exchange, neither camp has altered its core commitments, and the discussion repeatedly circles the same two test cases—whether an LLM believes that Paris is the capital of France, and whether it wants to complete a prompt—without clarifying whether the disagreement is about ontology, explanation, or semantic convention.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is partly verbal, with belief functioning as a stance-relative predicate for one group and as a natural-kind term for the other, but I am unsure whether there is also a hidden substantive question about what counts as an internal representational architecture in a distributed, gradient-based system. To probe this, consider a hypothetical synthetic agent, System V, trained entirely in a continuous sensorimotor loop rather than on linguistic data. System V develops a stable, context-invariant cluster of activations in response to a specific obstacle class, and this cluster can be selectively modified by an experimenter in a counter-belief intervention that changes the agent’s obstacle-avoidance behavior without disrupting its broader predictive dynamics. One philosopher might take this localized isolability as evidence of a genuine representational state; another might treat it as proof only that a predictive heuristic can be surgically targeted, which is still compatible with a purely instrumental stance.

Given this framing, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the broader debate about AI belief-attribution stalled because the sides are answering different questions—one about pragmatic norms of explanation, one about metaphysical conditions for mentality—under a single heading? Or is there a real, unresolved empirical or formal disagreement about whether gradient-based systems can host representations with satisfaction conditions? What do the actual positions in the literature say that reveals this structure? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal criterion for when stance-adoption is more than instrumental, an empirical result about internal clustering in LLMs, or a reconceptualization that separates the predictive and ontological issues? If the disagreement is bedrock—rooted in incompatible intuitions about the naturalness of mental representation—explain why no further argument should be expected to resolve it.
