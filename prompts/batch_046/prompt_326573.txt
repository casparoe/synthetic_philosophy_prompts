I am not an academic—I'm a local attorney who grew up along Marrow Creek and now sit on the board of the Preservation Collective—but I have been turning over in my mind the address I must deliver at the collective's fiftieth-anniversary gathering next month. The room will be mixed: the original founders, now in their eighties, who spent decades quietly buying watershed land to keep it intact for people they will never meet; the young ecologists and volunteers they have recently recruited; and the county officials who control whether our tax exemptions survive the next budget cycle. My uncle, who helped draft our charter, used to insist that a forest standing in 2150 owes us nothing less than a forest standing next door, that temporal distance is morally irrelevant. Recently I have tried to understand whether that conviction can survive the argument for pure time preference—the view that it is rational to value an identical future good less than a present one solely because it lies farther away in time, with no appeal to risk, ignorance, or changing circumstances. If pure time preference is defensible, then the collective's long horizon looks less like moral consistency and more like an irrational refusal to discount, and my uncle's claim collapses into sentiment. If it is indefensible, I need to say so plainly, but I also need to avoid the easy rhetoric of "our children will thank us," which smuggles in a preference for the near future that the argument is supposed to justify.

Please write this opening address. The philosophical position you take on pure time preference should do real structural work: it must determine which appeals are available—whether I can invoke obligations to distant strangers, whether patience is a virtue or a cognitive bias, and whether the founders' half-century wait was rational endurance or a kind of category mistake. The speech should honor people who have given their lives to slow, invisible work without telling them false things about posterity's gratitude, and it should land for listeners who have never studied philosophy but who understand that money and time are not the same thing. Take a definite, committed stand—do not lay out options and leave me to choose—but show the reasoning that forces the choice, and be explicit about where the argument might break if a listener rejects one of your premises. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want the argument to stand on its own for an audience that cannot look anything up.
