As a journalist covering conceptual foundations in physics and philosophy, I have adopted a particular extension of Thomson's lamp: I take the paradox not as a curiosity about toggling lights, but as showing that any system completing an infinite discrete sequence in finite time lacks a determinate state at the limit point. From this I argue that genuinely continuous physical change, if analyzed with infinite precision, would be vulnerable to the same limit-indeterminacy, and therefore that physical continuity is best understood as an emergent, coarse-grained approximation rather than a fundamental feature. What would count decisively against this position? I want the objections ranked by severity: which results in real analysis, in the physics of motion, or in the ontology of time would be fatal, which merely awkward, and which a defender could concede without abandoning the core claim. In particular, does the standard reply—that physical switches have inertia and therefore cannot toggle infinitely—genuinely dissolve the argument, or does it miss the structural point about limits? I am not looking for a bibliography; I want an honest map of where the view is exposed and where it is well insulated.
