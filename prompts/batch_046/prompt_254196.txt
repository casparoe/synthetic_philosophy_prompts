I am a high school student preparing an independent essay for an advanced philosophy course on reasoning without reasoners, and I would value conceptual feedback on the argument I am developing. I want to examine a parallel between Darwin's explanation of complex biological competence—such as the functional precision of the eye—through natural selection without any guiding comprehension, and Turing's proposal that a computing system might display linguistic and problem-solving competence in the imitation game without possessing internal semantic understanding or intentional purpose. In both cases, it seems that ordinary explanatory order is inverted: rather than comprehension being the primitive source from which competence flows, competence emerges first through blind mechanisms—evolutionary variation and algorithmic symbol manipulation—while comprehension, if it appears at all, is a late and localized derivative. I am particularly interested in whether this inversion is genuinely structurally identical across the two domains, or whether evolutionary competence (functional adaptation without mental representation) and computational competence (formal processing without meaning) differ in a way that breaks the analogy I am drawing.

For the essay, I have begun extending this comparison to ask whether accepting Darwin's inversion should make us more philosophically receptive to Turing's, since if human cognitive competence itself arose through selection processes that understand nothing, then demanding semantic comprehension as a necessary prerequisite for machine reasoning might rest on an unexamined anthropocentrism. At the same time, I worry that I may be conflating two distinct senses of understanding—functional design in biology versus intentional aboutness in cognition—and that there may be an objection, perhaps from the philosophy of biology or from recent philosophy of mind, showing that the inversion is not as strange or as unified as I suggest. Could you comment specifically on whether the parallel holds, where I am likely eliding important distinctions between evolutionary and computational processes, and whether there is an obvious conceptual objection that undermines the claim that competence can be genuinely primitive in both cases? I am not asking for bibliographic suggestions or a reading list, but rather for sharpening of the conceptual architecture itself.
