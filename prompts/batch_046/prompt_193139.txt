While I was revising a chapter in a borrowed apartment in the East Village, I overheard a disagreement between two guests—Elena, a cognitive linguist, and Marcus, a philosopher of mind—who had drifted into the kitchen and begun arguing about linguistic relativity. I listened without joining in, and I am reporting their exchange exactly as I recall it.

Elena began by saying that the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis is often caricatured as the claim that language makes thought impossible, when what it really asserts is that obligatory grammatical categories train habitual patterns of attention. She pointed to evidential marking—the grammatical requirement in some languages to specify whether information was seen, inferred, or heard—as a clear case. Speakers of such languages, she said, do not merely learn a vocabulary; they develop a reflexive habit of tracking epistemic sources.

Marcus answered that this is unremarkable. Any specialized practice, he said, produces cognitive habits. Learning chess improves spatial reasoning; learning medicine improves diagnostic pattern-matching. The fact that speakers of evidential languages become good at tracking sources no more proves linguistic determinism than chess proves that the knight's move reshapes the brain.

Elena conceded the analogy but pushed further with an idea she called structural residue. Even when fluent bilinguals switch to a language without evidentials, she claimed, they retain a meta-cognitive tagging of information sources in working memory—an organizational pattern that shows up in non-linguistic sorting tasks. The grammatical obligation, she argued, has not merely supplied a skill but has reorganized the architecture of working memory itself.

Marcus replied that this confuses correlation with causation. The cultures that maintain evidential languages also train children in rigorous source-monitoring through storytelling, ritual, and pedagogy. Language is the transmission vehicle, he said, but the causal work is cultural. To credit the grammar specifically is to mistake the messenger for the message.

Elena responded that language and culture are indeed inseparable, but the specific grammatical form—the evidential morpheme—acts as a scaffold that stabilizes the practice across generations. Without that structural scaffold, she argued, the cognitive practice would diffuse. She added that she was not claiming total determinism, only channeling: language does not build the river, but it digs the bed.

Marcus granted that this was a more modest claim, but he said it diluted the Sapir–Whorf thesis into triviality. If all the hypothesis amounts to is that language can act as a cultural scaffold for domain-specific habits, then every social institution qualifies, and the distinctive role of grammar disappears.

They turned to me then—not for my opinion, which I withheld—but to ask whether I could adjudicate their exchange point by point. I could not, and I am asking you to do so.

Say where, if at all, each party is missing something important, and indicate who is more right overall. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate to the subject.
