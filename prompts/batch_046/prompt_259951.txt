As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am commissioning a poem to accompany a feature on the limits of moral aggregation. Write a Shakespearean sonnet—strict iambic pentameter, rhyme scheme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG—addressing the comparison between the torture of one person and the presence of a dust speck in the eye distributed across an astronomically large population. The octet should present the aggregationist intuition that sufficient quantitative multiplication of an imperceptible harm eventually outweighs a single catastrophic harm. The volta must fall precisely at line nine and should enact the philosophical objection: that accumulating dust specks does not synthesize a torture-level injury but merely replicates the negligible, thereby exposing a category error disguised as arithmetic. The couplet should not resolve the tension but should hold both claims in suspension—acknowledging that the aggregate sum is numerically real while insisting it remains qualitatively distinct from the single severe harm. The poem should be accessible to a general reader, yet precise enough that a specialist in population ethics recognizes the embedded distinction between total and average utility framing without any explicit labeling. Ensure the rhyme pairs subtly track the qualitative-quantitative divide—so that, for instance, language of counting and scaling rhymes with language of kind and nature—so the form itself performs the conceptual work.
