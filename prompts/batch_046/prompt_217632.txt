I've been thinking about why philosophers treat it as obvious that if you know one thing, you must also know everything that logically follows from it—the issue they call logical omniscience. To make sense of it on my own, I invented a distinction: "latent entailment" is just the logical relation sitting quietly inside the structure of what I believe, while "active entailment" is the actual mental work or computation required to bring that consequence out into conscious awareness. So if I know it's raining and I know that rain makes the ground wet, the wetness of the ground is latently entailed, but I might not actively know it until I actually run the inference—even though nothing new was added to the world, only to my attention. Could you explain the conceptual issue of logical omniscience in formal epistemic logic—specifically, why standard models build in closure under logical consequence, and where exactly a distinction like mine fits? I want to understand whether the problem is that these models are unrealistically demanding about mental computation, or whether they are capturing something real about belief-structure that my distinction merely renames. Please don't just catalog standard fixes; I want a gut-level sense of why the closure principle is embedded in the formalism and whether separating latent from active entailment actually undermines it or just pushes the difficulty one step back.
