I serve on the Collections Loan Appeals Board of the university museum, and by next Friday we must issue a written opinion recommending whether to approve a six-month loan of an 1870s collodion glass-plate negative to a traveling exhibition. The plate’s emulsion carries a documented chemical disposition to silver out and crack upon exposure to atmospheric oxygen; for the past decade it has been stored in an argon-filled enclosure that suppresses this reaction. The lending institution now contends that the enclosure does not merely mask the instability but has eliminated the underlying disposition, so the object should travel under standard rather than fragile-object handling protocols. The borrowing institution’s chief conservator replies that the chemical structure retains the disposition intact, the enclosure functions only as a mask, and any seal breach—whether accidental or during routine maintenance—would operate as a fink that removes protection precisely when the manifestation conditions obtain, producing rapid irreversible damage. Our recommendation must specify a concrete course of action—approve with enhanced fragile-object protocols, approve under standard protocols, or deny the loan—together with a clear time frame for implementation, and it must treat the lender’s position seriously rather than merely restating institutional policy. In the reasoning, please separate what is settled about the metaphysics of dispositions, masks, and finks from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers who specialize in this area, and do not present any contested interpretation—such as whether a sustained mask can ever amount to a genuine removal of a disposition, or whether risk assessment should treat masked dispositions as present for policy purposes—as if it were the consensus view. The opinion should also include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration favoring the side that ultimately loses.
