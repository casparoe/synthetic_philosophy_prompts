I'm taking this philosophy of practical reason course and we keep circling back to whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. The prof laid out the standard view - that reasons should be foward-looking and past investment shouldn't count - and mentioned how behavioral economists treat sticking with a bad project as a cognitive bias. But then a few of us in the study group pushed back with cases where quitting seems to violate some kind of diachronic commitment, like when a collective has built shared practices around a long-term goal. We even tried to formalize a distinction in class between cost-sensitive reasoning and reason-sensitive reasoning, but now the debate has totally stalled. The rationalists say we're just dressing up loss aversion in fancy language, and we say they're assuming a weirdly atomized decision-maker who never has to maintain identity over time. Is this disagreement actually about hidden empirical predictions - like whether people who honor sunk costs end up with lower well-being - or is it that "irrational" is being used in two different registers, one normative-decision-theoretic and one virtue-ethical? Could it be that both sides are answering different questions about practical reason under one heading? If that's the diagnosis, what would actually move it - a formal model of diachronic agency, an experiment on collective commitment, or just accepting that the question is ill-posed? I keep thinking there's a real gap around identity-constituting commitments that neither side addresses, but maybe I'm just conceeding to my own confusion.
