I am a high school junior taking both AP United States History and AP Biology, and I have become interested in whether the conceptual vocabulary from one course can illuminate debates in the other without distorting them. In history, we examined the critique of so-called Whig history — the tendency to interpret past political and social conflicts as inevitable steps toward present-day liberal democratic institutions, treating defeated movements and obsolete practices as if they were merely maladapted predecessors rather than coherent systems that made sense on their own terms. In biology, we have studied Sewall Wright's adaptive landscapes, Gould and Lewontin's idea of spandrels, evolutionary exaptation, genetic drift, and the distinction between teleonomy (functional design that looks directed) and teleology (genuine directedness toward a predetermined endpoint).

Could you restate the Whig historian's interpretive method — particularly the habit of reading modern norms backward into past agents' intentions and of treating historical "winners" as occupants of a uniquely high adaptive peak — using only the conceptual machinery of evolutionary biology: selection pressures, fitness peaks, spandrels, drift, exaptation, and teleonomy? Be precise about which historiographical moves map onto which biological concepts. Then provide a residue report: which parts of the standard critique of Whig history survive translation intact (for instance, the problem of contingency versus determinism, or the error of treating institutional byproducts as primary adaptations); which parts become distorted because evolutionary vocabulary lacks analogues for human intentionality, normative judgment, or the possibility that a society might reject a "fitter" institution for principled reasons; and whether anything in the Whig narrative actually becomes clearer under the biological framing than it was in its original form. I am especially interested in whether describing past societies as "failing" because they were maladapted to an inevitable modern environment is a faithful evolutionary analogy or a category mistake — and whether the residue reveals a weakness in the biological metaphor or in Whig history itself.
