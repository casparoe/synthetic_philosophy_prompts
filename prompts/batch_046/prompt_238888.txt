Having recently experienced a prolonged estrangement from my family and the community in which I was raised, I have turned to philosophical accounts of personhood to understand whether my identity can persist independently of those bonds. In particular, I have encountered the Southern African concept of Ubuntu and its associated relational conception of personhood, according to which individual being is mediated through communal belonging. This leads me to a concern that I cannot resolve. If personhood is genuinely constituted by one's relations to others, rather than being an intrinsic property that relations merely shape, then it seems to follow that a human being who is permanently isolated—for example, through indefinite solitary confinement or irreversible exile—ceases to be a person in any morally significant sense. But this conclusion appears not only counterintuitive but also dangerous, since it would seem to license the denial of rights precisely to those who are most vulnerable. Is there a coherent reading of Ubuntu on which relational constitution does not entail that isolation annihilates personhood, or is the concept ultimately incompatible with the idea that every human being possesses an inviolable dignity regardless of social connection? In your answer, please indicate what specific argument or empirical consideration would cause you to abandon this interpretation, and identify the point in your reasoning that is most susceptible to error.
