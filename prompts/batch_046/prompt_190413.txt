I'm preparing a position paper for a seminar on semantic paradox, and before circulating it I want a rigorous consistency audit of the view I have settled into. Here is the profile, as precisely as I can state it.

(1) I defend a modified Tarskian hierarchy in which truth is stratified, but I deny that the stratification is syntactically rigid. Instead, I hold that levels are assigned pragmatically through what I term contextual ascent rules—procedures embedded in the object-language that determine, for any given utterance, the appropriate level of the truth-predicate being deployed, and these rules can be iterated.

(2) On this account, the classic liar sentence is well-formed but expresses a grounding failure rather than a contradiction. This failure is not a defect to be eliminated; it is a structural feature that reveals truth to be an essentially open-ended predicate, incapable of being fully captured at any fixed level.

(3) I reject deflationism, on the grounds that a purely disquotational account cannot accommodate these open-ended grounding failures without implicitly reintroducing a substantive correspondence relation.

(4) I maintain that natural language is not essentially inconsistent—Tarski was wrong on this point—but that it contains a systematic ambiguity in its truth-predicate that functions analogously to the hierarchy. Ordinary speakers navigate this ambiguity without explicit awareness of the level they are operating on.

(5) I further hold that the strengthened liar—formulated, roughly, as the claim that it is not true at any level—is genuinely false rather than paradoxical. Its universal negative is defeated by the very existence of the utterance that expresses it.

(6) Finally, I take all of this to support a modest realism: there are mind-independent facts about whether a given claim is grounded at a specific level n, even though our access to which n applies may be fallible and context-dependent.

My question is not for a lecture on the liar paradox in general, but for an audit of this specific package. Which pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, and which only appear so? For each genuine tension, specify the severity, identify the cheapest revision that would restore consistency, and indicate what that revision costs in terms of the other commitments. In particular, I am concerned that my pragmatic, context-sensitive assignment mechanism in (1) and (4) may undermine the mind-independent realism in (6); that my treatment of the strengthened liar in (5) may sit uneasily with my characterization of the classic liar in (2); and that my rejection of deflationism in (3) may not actually be supported by the arguments I have given. Rank the tensions by severity, and indicate whether there is a single revision—giving up one commitment or refining one distinction—that resolves the largest number of conflicts without collapsing the view into either a rigid hierarchy or a deflationary account.
