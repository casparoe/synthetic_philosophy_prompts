Construct a thought experiment, no longer than a paragraph, set in a municipal water-board office that operates under "administrative kinship": hiring and contracting are embedded in rotating-credit kin obligations, and refusing a substantial ceremonial gift (for example, livestock or temporary water-rights) from an applicant's family is itself treated as a corrupt breach of social reciprocity. Engineer the scenario so that three theoretical frameworks yield precisely these verdicts on whether the manager acts corruptly by accepting the gift and hiring the applicant: (1) a Western proceduralist theory of corruption, defining corruption as the abuse of public office for private gain, judges the hiring corrupt; (2) a relational clientelist theory judges the refusal to accept the gift and hire the kin member as the genuinely corrupt act; and (3) a universalist impartial-duty theory also judges the hiring corrupt, but rejects both the proceduralist's institution-specific framing and the clientelist's culture-bound framing, grounding the verdict instead in a cross-cultural duty of impartiality.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate the reasoning for each framework; identify the structural feature—the embedded reciprocity norm—that forces the clientelist view to invert the Western verdict; explain what happens to the case if that feature is stripped away; and show how a defender of the clientelist position would attack the scenario as culturally imperialist rather than analytically flawed. Write the answer in American English.
