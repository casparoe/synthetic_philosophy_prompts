Hey, quick question from a seminar I'm prepping. There's this move I keep seeing in normative ethics—probly not original, but I can't find the standard diagnosis. The setup is: you want to know what agents ought to do, so you construct an idealized deliberator—fully informed, unbiased, rational, whatever—and ask what they'd pick. Classic stuff. But the version that actually comes up in papers is weirder: the problem we're solving is constituted by the very imperfections we're idealizing away.

Take distributive justice. We ask what should we agree to if we didn't know our social position—but if you genuinely lacked any particular position or relationship, the question of distribution might not even arise, because there's no us with distinct interests to distribute among. Or take obligations to distant strangers: the theory asks what a perfectly impartial agent would do, but perfect impartiality seems to dissolve the boundary between self and other that makes obligation a meaningful category in the first place. Even addiction ethics—what should a fully rational agent do about dependency?—when dependency partly just is a failure of rational agency.

So the pattern is applying an ideal-deliberation framework to a problem-space that only exists for non-ideal agents. Critics say it's like asking a healthy person for medical advice about being sick—there's no there there. Defenders say the point is normative aspiration, not diagnosis.

Is there an established name for this specific failure mode? I know it's near indirect normativity and constructivist ethics, and maybe touches on the non-identity problem, but I don't want the generic label. I want to know if philosophers have specifically named the case where idealization doesn't just distort the answer but eliminates the problem-space altogether. If not, whats the right way to distinguish it from ordinary idealization objections? And is there a canonical case study—maybe in Rawls or in ideal observer theory—that captures it exactly?
