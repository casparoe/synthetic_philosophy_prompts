okay so me and my roomate have been arguing about constitutional monarchy and democratic theory and we built this argument that now feels like a trap. i want to keep the democratic legitimacy premise but i also think constitutional monarchy in the uk, japan, and scandanavia is actually stable, so the conclusion can't be right. here's the chain:

(1) in a democracy every binding exercise of state authority must be traceable to popular authorization, either direct or through representatives.
(2) in the uk, japan, sweden, norway, and denmark the head of state formally appoints the prime minister, dissolves parliament (or in japan's case promulgates constitutional amendments via kokuji koi), and grants assent to legislation.
(3) these formal powers are not traceable to popular authorization because the monarch/emperor is hereditary.
(4) now either these powers are genuinely available, or convention has made them impossible to exercise.
(5) if they're genuinely available, then there is a path where state power is exercised without democratic authorization.
(6) if they're impossible to exercise—bound by convention—then they aren't real powers, they're just theater.
(7) but a theatrical act that is legally required for government formation or law enactment isn't "no power"; it's a dormant veto point built into the constitutional machinery.
(8) a dormant veto point is still a structurally present, unauthorized source of potential state power.
(9) therefore every parliamentary democracy with a hereditary head of state contains a hidden, democratically unauthorized veto point in its constitutional machinery.
(10) that means either we accept that the uk, japan, and scandinavia are all partially illegitimate democracies, or we deny that a dormant but legally necessary role counts as a veto point.
(11) but denying that is like saying a loaded gun with the safety on isn't dangerous—structural capacity matters even when unused.
(12) so constitutional monarchy is incompatible with full democratic sovereignty, and the only consistent democratic position is abolition or complete stripping of legal formality.

where's the slip? i feel like something moves between (6) and (7), maybe an equivocation on "power", or maybe (11) is a false analogy. i want to keep (1) and i actually want to keep (9) if i can, but i'm willing to pay whatever's least costly. can you name the step, explain why it looked okay, and price out the alternatives? also, if the argument is actually valid, which premise should a defender of constitutional monarchy bite the bullet on?
