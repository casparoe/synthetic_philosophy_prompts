I'm trying to work out what I actually think about the ethics of voting, going by my gut on concrete cases rather than from theory.

My verdicts: in a race with one corrupt-but-competent candidate and one honest-but-harmful one, voting for the corrupt one is okay, probably a duty. In a safe-seat election, voting for a fringe candidate just to boost their official tally feels like fine expression. In a toss-up race, doing the exact same thing feels negligent, not brave. Casting a blank ballot because I genuinely reject every option is okay, that's honest. Refusing to vote because I think the system is illegitimate is okay, but then I shouldn't complain about the result. Refusing because I'm lazy is not okay, that's just free-riding. Voting in an open primary for the other party's weakest candidate is okay, strategic voting isn't wrong. Voting in a shareholder meeting for stock I inherited but understand nothing about -- I think I should abstain rather than add random noise. Voting in a referendum on long-term climate policy that won't affect me but will affect people not yet born: I think I should still vote for what seems right, maybe even have a duty to.

There's one pair I can't reconcile: strategic primary voting feels clever and acceptable, but voting for a protest candidate in a swing state feels irresponsible, even though both are using your vote to shape outcomes you don't directly prefer. Is there a principel -- maybe one about causal efficacy, expressive fidelity, or epistemic humility -- that captures all these, or do I have to drop one? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, predict my verdict on writing in a fictional character's name in a system where write-ins are never counted, and be honest about which of my judgments it can't save.

Please separate what's settled in this area from what's genuinely contested among philosophers who work on it, and don't present a contested view like it's the consensus.
