I have been reading various accounts of the French Revolution, particularly the years between 1789 and 1794, and I keep noticing a structural repetition that I am not sure whether historians recognize as a distinct mechanism or treat as an illusion of narrative order.

The repetition runs as follows. A group takes power by arguing that the revolution has failed to realize its own founding principle—popular sovereignty, equality, or the general will. Once in authority, they govern by applying that principle with greater literalness and severity than their predecessors did. They are then removed—often executed—not by counter-revolutionaries but by successors who accuse them of the very same failure: an insufficient, corrupted, or hypocritical commitment to the principle in question. The Girondins fall to those who claim a more authentic democracy; the faction around Danton falls to those who demand more rigorous republican virtue; Robespierre falls to those who invoke the same language of popular will and moral purity. What strikes me is that the accused rarely defend themselves by rejecting the principle. Instead they insist that they were its truest executors. The mechanism looks less like betrayal and more like a kind of reflexive escalation: each stage generates a stricter interpretation that retroactively disqualifies the previous stage for impurity rather than for opposition.

People often say that revolutions devour their own children, which sounds like a broad claim that revolutionary violence eventually turns inward. But what I am trying to isolate feels narrower: an internal logic in which an ideology applies its own standard to its own advocates with increasing rigor, producing successive purges justified as deeper fidelity rather than as punishment for apostasy. Does this specific dynamic—this self-cannibalizing progression driven by literal consistency—have an established name in the study of revolutions? If not, which of the familiar explanatory frameworks—class struggle, ideological extremism, or historical contingency—best approximates it, and where exactly does my description overreach? I am particularly unsure whether I am imposing a false pattern onto events that might just as well be explained by shifting coalitions or by the pressures of war and scarcity.
