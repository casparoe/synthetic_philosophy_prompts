Here's an argument my study group built about causal overdetermination in macrohistory, and I can't locate the exit even though the conclusion would dissolve the discipline. (1) Call a factor F genuinely explanatory of event E only if F makes an explanatory difference: had F not occurred, E would have lacked an explanation altogether. (2) In macrohistorical cases — the collapse of the Western Roman Empire, the outbreak of the First World War — structural conditions S and contingent decisions D are each causally sufficient; absent S, D still fully explains the outcome, and vice versa. (3) So for any single factor, its counterfactual absence does not leave the event unexplained. (4) By (1), therefore, neither S nor D genuinely explains these events. (5) Every macrohistorical event is causally saturated in this way: economic, political, environmental, and agential conditions each form sufficient subsets. (6) Hence no individual factor genuinely explains any macrohistorical event, and historiography offers only pseudo-explanations. (7) But historiography plainly succeeds, so (6) is false. (8) Therefore (1) is false, and explanatory status does not require counterfactual exclusivity. I would prefer to keep (1) — it captures the intuition that explanations must do real work rather than ride along on redundant causes — but I want to know the precise cost of keeping or abandoning it. Is the flaw an equivocation between token and type explanation, a quantifier shift from "no single factor" to "no factor at all," or a hidden assumption that explanations must be individually necessary rather than jointly sufficient? Identify the step that actually fails, explain why it looked plausible, and say what a defensible version of the Difference Condition would have to look like.
