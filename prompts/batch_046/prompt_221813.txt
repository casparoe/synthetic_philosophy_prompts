As a PhD student working on consciousness and metaphysics, I keep returning to the stall in the philosophical-zombie debate: anti-physicalists claim that a complete physical duplicate lacking phenomenal experience is clearly conceivable and therefore metaphysically possible, while physicalists reply either that the conception is covertly inconsistent or that phenomenal concepts block the modal inference. Decades of refinement—phenomenal-concepts strategies, transparency arguments, two-dimensional appeals—have not moved either camp. My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about modal epistemology or the ontology of qualia, but about incompatible standards for what counts as an adequate explanation of experience: one side demands that a successful account entail the phenomenal facts a priori, the other insists that empirical identity coupled with functional explanation is sufficient, and they talk past each other by using "explanatory gap" for both demands. Is this diagnosis right? Or is the dispute substantive, with both sides genuinely disagreeing about whether modal imagination tracks possibility in the phenomenal domain? Diagnose the structure: do they differ over hidden empirical claims, over evidentiary standards, over which cases count as data, over bedrock intuitions about first-person authority, or are they answering different questions—one semantic, one metaphysical—under the single heading "Is physicalism true"? And specify what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result about the logic of phenomenal concepts, an empirical finding about the cognitive mechanisms underlying modal judgment about experience, a conceptual distinction that separates explanatory adequacy from metaphysical entailment, or should we conclude the question is ill-posed because "conceivability" fragments into incommensurable capacities? Support your diagnosis with the actual commitments each side makes, not a survey of interpretations.
