At a philosophy discussion group, Maya and Leo argued about Plato's Meno problem — specifically why knowledge is superior to true belief. Maya defended the classic interpretation: knowledge is true belief anchored by an account or reason (logos), and that anchoring gives it stability and justifies holding it across contexts, whereas true belief is fragile and accidental. Leo objected that this merely relocates the problem: if the account is itself a belief, then knowledge is just true belief stacked on more true beliefs, with no clear explanation of why the stack is intrinsically more valuable. He added that the practical advantage — being able to navigate similar situations — explains the value without any mysterious anchoring. Maya replied that Leo ignores the normative aspect: possessing an account makes one epistemically responsible for the belief in a way that accidental true belief does not, and that responsibility is part of what makes knowledge valuable. Leo then proposed a thought experiment: imagine a neural prosthetic that reliably generates true beliefs about immediate weather conditions and can produce, on demand, an opaque reasoning trace that the user cannot understand but can cite. He asked whether a user relying on this device knows the weather, or merely possesses outsourced true belief with a borrowed justification. Maya said yes, it counts as knowledge, because the account exists in the system the user depends on; Leo said no, because the anchoring must be accessible to the believer, otherwise the stability belongs to the machine, not to the person's cognition. They asked me to referee. Please explain which specific claims are correct, who is more right overall, and what both are missing about the value of knowledge over true belief.
