I'm trying to map out my actual judgments about when a human life counts as meaningful versus absurd, partly because I'm preparing a presentation for a course on philosophy of value and partly because I keep returning to the same tension in discussions with friends. Rather than asking for the standard taxonomy—objectivist lists, subjectivist fulfillment conditions, or hybrid accounts—I want to know whether there is a single principle behind my intuitive verdicts, even if it forces me to abandon one or two of them.

Here are my gut judgments on concrete cases. First, consider the Camus-style figure of Sisyphus: condemned to roll a boulder up a hill forever, with no cumulative progress and no recognition from anyone outside the cycle. I think this life is genuinely absurd—there is a stark cosmic mismatch between effort and result—yet I also think it can be meaningful if the agent actively chooses to embrace the struggle itself rather than merely endure it. Second, take a researcher who spends thirty years developing a cure, dies the day before publication, and is never posthumously credited; her work eventually saves thousands, though she never learns of it. I think her life is clearly meaningful and not absurd at all, despite her total ignorance of the outcome. Third, imagine a solitary builder who spends decades constructing an intricate architectural replica out of toothpicks inside a locked room, with no audience, no practical function, and full awareness that it will eventually be discarded; he values the activity intensely. I think his life is meaningful, though I am genuinely unsure whether it is also absurd. Fourth, consider a superintelligent artificial being that experiences profound aesthetic and intellectual fulfillment, fully aware that the universe is heading toward heat death and that no trace of its achievements will survive. Here my judgment flips: despite its rich inner life, I think its existence is absurd and not genuinely meaningful. Fifth, take an average person who lives comfortably, works a routine job, raises a family, and never once reflects on whether any of it matters; I think this life is not absurd—there is no cosmic mismatch—but it is also not particularly meaningful, merely lived. Sixth, there is the case of a public-health official who implements a preventive measure so effectively that a potential pandemic never materializes, leaving the population unaware that any danger existed, and she dies in complete anonymity. I think this life is meaningful, yet I cannot tell whether it differs in kind from the researcher's case or only in degree.

The pattern is difficult to reconcile. Subjective fulfillment seems sufficient for meaning in the toothpick builder but not in the AI; cosmic futility undermines meaning for the AI but not for Sisyphus, whose rebellion apparently saves him; objective contribution saves both the researcher and the official, yet the builder lacks it entirely and still seems meaningful to me. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving what I tentatively call "narrative friction," the idea that meaning requires not just value or awareness but a resistant structure that shapes one's efforts against some background of dissolution—that can accommodate all six verdicts? Or must I drop at least one? Specifically, what clause explains why the builder's subjective investment counts while the AI's does not, and why Sisyphus's attitude transforms absurdity into meaning rather than merely masking it? And what should the resulting account predict for a case I have not listed: a being that generates complex mathematical proofs inside a black hole that evaporates before any information escapes, knowing with certainty that nothing will ever observe its work, but experiencing deep satisfaction in the act of discovery itself?
