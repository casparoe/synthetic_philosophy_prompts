I'm a PhD student in history of medicine advising on an oral-archive exhibition. We have recorded retired psychiatric nurses describing clinical practices they administered between 1960 and 1980—insulin coma therapy, prolonged mechanical restraint, referrals for compulsory sterilization—that were then standard of care and that several interviewees still defend as ethically appropriate given the institutional pressures and clinical knowledge of their era. Our museum partner proposes framing each recording with present-day clinical-ethics annotations that classify these practices as violations of patient autonomy and bodily integrity. The nurses themselves, invited to the opening as honored participants, have asked that we not apply "today's rules" to their working lives. Meanwhile, a patient-advocacy coalition argues that presenting the testimony solely within its original ethical vocabulary amounts to sanitizing systemic harm.

As the exhibition's historical consultant, I must recommend a curatorial strategy: preserve the nurses' accounts in their own normative and clinical language, overlay explicit modern moral commentary, or construct some integrative framing. The decision will shape whether visitors understand these women as competent professionals navigating severe material and epistemic constraints, or as agents within a system now recognized as abusive. Is there a coherent philosophical position between judging the past by present moral standards and suspending judgment entirely? Or does the very architecture of the archive—who is interviewed, who authors the annotations, whose vocabulary governs the display—make any claim to neutrality conceptually impossible?
