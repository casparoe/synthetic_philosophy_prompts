I am preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club on Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments, and I have reconstructed the Lucas-Penrose line, then extended it to a modular architecture. I want to check whether the extension reveals a hidden flaw in the original or whether it genuinely proves too much.

Here is the argument, with the extension included.

(1) Any consistent formal system F rich enough to represent elementary arithmetic contains a Gödel sentence G_F that F itself cannot derive, though a rational being who grasps the rules of F can recognize G_F as true.

(2) Suppose, for contradiction, that the reasoning of a particular human mind is fully captured by some consistent formal system H.

(3) That human can understand H—at least in principle, since H is meant to model her own reasoning.

(4) By (1), she can then recognize the truth of G_H, even though H cannot derive it.

(5) But if her reasoning is exactly H, then whatever she can establish should be derivable in H. Hence she should not be able to establish G_H.

(6) This contradiction shows that no human mind is identical to any consistent formal system H.

So far this is the familiar anti-mechanist claim. Now here is the step that worries me.

(7) The brain is not a monolith; it contains distinct reasoning modules—say, a perceptual-inference module P and a meta-cognitive module M that evaluates P's outputs.

(8) If P is mechanizable, there is a formal system F_P capturing it. By (1)-(6), M—if it is also mechanizable—should be unable to capture P's Gödel sentence within F_P, yet M apparently recognizes it.

(9) Applying the same reasoning to M itself, there must be a meta-meta-module M' that escapes formalization, and so on ad infinitum.

(10) Therefore the argument, if sound, does not merely show that minds are not machines; it shows that any finite modular architecture capable of self-reference requires an infinite tower of non-mechanizable levels.

(11) Since actual brains and artificial neural networks are finite and lack such infinite towers, the argument proves too much: it undermines not only mechanism but also any plausible finite model of cognition, biological or artificial.

(12) So one of the premises must be wrong.

My intuition is that the slide occurs between (3) and (4): grasping a formal system in the abstract does not automatically grant one the practical or constructive ability to build and recognize its specific Gödel sentence, especially when that system is as complex as a model of one's own brain. But if I weaken (3) or (4) to save mechanization, I seem to concede that humans have a kind of necessary cognitive opacity to themselves, which feels like a high price to pay.

Could you diagnose the argument? Specifically, which step is the weakest link, and does rejecting it save mechanization or just relocate the mystery? Also, if the recursive modular extension in (7)-(10) is illegitimate, what exactly blocks it—is it a misapplication of Gödel's result to empirical subsystems, or something deeper about self-reference? I would prefer to keep (1) intact, since Gödel's theorem is a mathematical fact, and I am reluctant to deny that minds can understand formal systems they design. I am more willing to weaken (2) or (6) if necessary.
