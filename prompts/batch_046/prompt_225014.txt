Since I recently assumed oversight of renewal evaluations for a foundation that funds long-running cultural-documentation projects, I have encountered a striking tension in our protocols. Our formal review criteria explicitly instruct evaluators to treat prior expenditures as irrelevant: a grant should be renewed only if its future outputs justify the future costs, regardless of how much has already been invested or archived. This is presented as a safeguard against the sunk-cost fallacy. Yet there is an equally firm, if unwritten, institutional norm that documentation grants producing substantial archival records—say, decades of oral-history recordings in a language now spoken by only a few hundred people—should not be abandoned mid-stream merely because the remaining research questions have become marginal. The rationale given is not expected future benefit but something closer to fidelity to the accumulated record and to the communities that contributed years of knowledge. Terminating such a grant is seen not as rational cost-cutting but as a kind of betrayal, even when the same resources could preserve far more material elsewhere.

This dual practice seems to treat sunk costs as irrational in one domain and as binding obligations in another. What philosophical commitments about rationality, value, and institutional duty does it presuppose? Does it strictly require the view that honoring past investment can be rational—perhaps because the investment has altered what counts as a good outcome—or can it be explained within a strictly forward-looking framework by invoking a separate value, such as narrative continuity or relational fidelity? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes both protocols coherent, distinguish what the practice genuinely requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether rival theories—say, a pluralist account of value that separates welfare-maximization from heritage-preservation, or a commitment-based view of institutional identity—would license the same behavior. Finally, tell me what our evaluation guidelines would have to change if we replaced the implicit view with a uniform consequentialist calculus that treated every prior expenditure as strictly irrelevant to renewal decisions.
