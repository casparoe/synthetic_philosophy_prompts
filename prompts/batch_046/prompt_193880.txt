I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and next month I will present a short argument to a university philosophy-of-logic reading group. The audience is mostly classical logicians, plus one specialist in paraconsistent logic and a couple of philosophers of science who tend to view engineering examples with suspicion when they are used to motivate metaphysical or epistemological claims. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view in abstract.

My thesis is as follows. In large-scale distributed databases, network partitions can leave different nodes with genuinely contradictory records about the same state—for example, one shard recording that user X is active and another recording that user X is not active—without either shard being simply “wrong” in any easily correctable sense. Standard fault-tolerance architecture is designed precisely so that these local contradictions do not propagate globally and crash the entire system; the contradiction is contained rather than resolved by immediate global revision. I want to argue that this design choice is not merely a pragmatic hack but reveals something descriptively accurate about rational belief management under contradictory evidence. Specifically, I propose what I call a “fault-tolerant epistemology”: belief revision should sometimes be locally paraconsistent, allowing contradictions to exist in isolated subsystems without triggering global epistemic collapse. From this I draw a philosophical conclusion in favor of dialetheism—the view that some contradictions can be literally true—suggesting that paraconsistent logic offers a more realistic model of how engineered, and perhaps natural, epistemic systems should operate than classical logic, which treats any contradiction as a catastrophic failure via the principle of explosion (ex contradictione quodlibet).

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually receive from this specific audience. Order them by how likely they are and by how damaging they would be if I answer poorly. Phrase each objection in the vocabulary and tone that these philosophers—especially the classical logicians and the paraconsistent specialist—would actually use. For each one, tell me: what I should concede up front, where I must hold firm, the weakest point in my own position that this objection targets, and a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes without appealing to texts I have not read. Also include the single objection I am most hoping nobody raises, with a note on why it is dangerous for my particular background. Do not ask me for bibliographies, reading lists, or specific citations; I need arguments I can defend from conceptual reasoning and my direct engineering experience alone. Keep everything focused on accuracy, conceptual clarity, and whether my analogy between distributed-system fault containment and logical contradiction genuinely holds up.
