A colleague recently summarized the combined thesis of Benedict Anderson’s Imagined Communities and Eric Hobsbawm’s introduction to The Invention of Tradition in these terms:

“Both authors argue that nations are recent administrative fictions with no genuine historical roots. Anderson shows that ‘print-capitalism’ manufactured a false sense of horizontal comradeship among strangers through newspapers and novels, but because literacy was confined to elites, this was essentially a top-down imposition of identity on passive populations. Hobsbawm demonstrates that rituals like royal coronations, national anthems, and school flag ceremonies were invented wholesale between roughly 1870 and 1914, bearing no relation to actual popular customs, and that this invention was always a cynical strategy by ruling classes to secure obedience. Taken together, they prove that nationalism is purely instrumental: the concept of ‘the people’ was fabricated solely to justify conscription and taxation, and any emotional attachment to the nation is therefore entirely manufactured. This also explains the rapid adoption of the nation-form in post-colonial Asia and Africa: because nationalism is modular, these societies imported an empty European blueprint with no local adaptation, reproducing the same state structures without any indigenous content.”

I have a few specific worries. First, does Anderson actually claim that print-capitalism only affected elites, or does he describe a broader vernacular market in which bilingual intermediaries played a key role? Second, does Hobsbawm allow that invented traditions can acquire genuine popular meaning and emotional investment over time, or does he treat them as permanently cynical instruments? Third, is the “modular” spread of nationalism to the post-colonial world described by Anderson as a pure copy, or does he emphasize local adaptation and the specific pioneering role of Creole communities in the Americas? Fourth, do either author deny that pre-modern societies had collective identities—religious, dynastic, or communal—or do they only deny that those identities took the specific form of the modern nation?

In doing so, please separate what is settled interpretation from what remains genuinely contested among historians and political theorists—particularly regarding whether nationalism is purely an elite instrument or can also involve popular agency, local creativity, and organic adaptation.
