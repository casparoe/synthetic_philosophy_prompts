I'm a field coordinator for a disaster-relief NGO, and every other deployment we hit the same wall: no shared language, no dictionary, 48 hours to build a phrasebook for medical intake, and our local interpreters fighting over whether a word points to a thing, an event, or just a slice of a thing. I read about Quine's gavagai thing — that even perfect behavioral data can't settle whether "gavagai" means rabbit, rabbit-stage, or undetached rabbit-parts because the manual is underdetermined by the evidence. I want a reusable decision procedure my three-person team (none of us philosophers) can actually run when we're stuck between two competing translations of a term that affects triage or consent.

Write it as a flowchart in words: the tests in order, what counts as a pass at each step, where the procedure stops with a hard "no — don't claim you've translated this," and where it hands the call to the medical lead because it's a value judgment not a fact. Make it honest about which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as checklist items. The hard cases I need covered are modal or deontic terms — like whether a speaker's assent to an instruction means "must" or only "should" — and physical terms where presence vs absence of parts changes assent patterns. Run it on two examples: (1) a word used for water, where one interpreter says it means "this H2O sample here" and another says "water as a substance anywhere"; and (2) a consent-related instruction word, where the split is between "you are obligated to accept treatment" and "it is good if you accept treatment." One of these should come out with an answer my team won't like — probably that the behavior genuinely doesn't settle it and we have to document our arbitrary convention rather than pretend we found the right one. Also flag exactly where this procedure can't replace informed-consent ethics, and keep it practical: no bibliography, no "see Quine 1960," just the steps. Maybe include a typo or two since I'm writing this on my phone in a hurry.
