A colleague recently summarized Daniel Dennett’s rejection of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts model after reading a popular philosophy-of-mind article. She said that Dennett denies there is any single neural “screen” or central workspace where sensory inputs converge to be witnessed by an inner observer; instead, the brain generates numerous parallel “drafts” of sensory and cognitive content, each being edited and revised continuously, with no final authoritative version ever being ratified. From this she inferred that Dennett therefore denies that there is any unified stream of consciousness at all—only fragmented, disconnected processes that we retrospectively stitch together into a fictional narrative self. She went further: because there is neither a theater nor a finished draft, Dennett must regard all first-person reports of subjective experience as unreliable confabulations rather than genuine data, and his view is therefore nothing more than a straightforward illusionism about consciousness, according to which there is literally nothing it is like to be aware. I am unsure how much of this is accurate and how much is an overreading. Which parts of her summary correctly capture Dennett’s actual commitments, which parts subtly misrepresent them—perhaps by conflating the denial of a central observer with the denial of consciousness itself, or by overstating the fragmentation of the drafts into a denial of all narrative unity—and does her conclusion that Dennett is a simple illusionist actually follow from the parts that are correct? Please take a definite position: either defend her characterization as essentially fair despite its rhetorical sharpness, or reject it and explain precisely where the missteps occur and what a careful defender of Dennett would insist on instead. Give your reasons clearly, and do not simply list possibilities for me to choose between.
