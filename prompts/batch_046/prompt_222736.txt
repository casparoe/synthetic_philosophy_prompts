In my philosophy of mind seminar, the instructor consistently compares the brain to a digital computer: neurons are mapped onto logic gates or processors, synaptic weights onto software parameters, and global brain states onto running programs. She uses this to license the claim that cognition is substrate-independent computation, so that a digital simulation of sufficient fidelity would literally replicate mental states. She acknowledges in passing that real neurons operate via analog, noisy, continuous-time dynamics tightly coupled to bodily and environmental feedback loops, yet maintains that these are implementation details that do not undermine the analogy’s conclusion. Can you lay out the explicit correspondence table, distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely threaten her substrate-independence argument from those that are inessential implementation differences, and say whether her conclusion survives once the analog, embodied, and non-von-Neumann features are fully on the table? If the computer analogy collapses under this pressure, is there a better analogy—perhaps an analog circuit or a coupled dynamical system—that captures neural mechanics without quietly importing discrete symbol-manipulation, programmable architecture, or hardware-software separation?
