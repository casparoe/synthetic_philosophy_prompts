I have been working on a novel that turns on whether a mind can be realized in something other than living tissue, and in discussing the premise with friends I found that my own intuitions about particular cases are sharper than any general view I can articulate. I am hoping you can help me discover the principle, if there is one, that holds my judgments together, and also tell me honestly where it forces me to abandon a verdict I currently hold. Here are my gut responses. First, if a human brain is replaced neuron by neuron with synthetic equivalents that preserve every causal interaction within the tissue, then the person remains conscious and retains their mental life; the substrate has changed but the realization is intact. Second, if a swarm of microscopic robots dispersed across a warehouse organizes itself through wireless signaling into a network that reproduces, at the macro level, the exact functional dynamics of a human prefrontal cortex during moral reasoning, I feel strongly that no single mind is present, even though the functional profile is duplicated. Third, a biological brain grown in isolation but stimulated so that its internal causal structure mirrors that of a mathematician proving a theorem clearly realizes mathematical thought. Fourth, if a weather system tracks temperature and pressure gradients in patterns that happen to be structurally isomorphic to fear-avoidance behavior, but with no internal organization resembling neural pathways, I deny that it realizes fear. Fifth, a digital emulation of a specific human brain running on a quantum architecture that introduces non-classical correlations not found in biology, yet preserves all macroscopic causal roles and input-output relations, makes me hesitate: I lean toward saying it realizes the same mental states, but the novelty of the substrate makes my confidence waver. Sixth, replacing a single biological neuron with an artificial equivalent that behaves identically leaves consciousness untouched, which seems to me entirely clear.

The tension I cannot resolve is between the second and fifth cases: in both instances a non-biological system replicates the functional architecture of a mind, yet in the dispersed swarm I deny realization while in the emulated brain I am inclined to grant it. Meanwhile the first and sixth cases suggest that continuity and local substitution do not break realization, while the fourth shows that accidental isomorphism is insufficient. Is there a coherent principle of functional realization that accommodates all six, or must I drop one? If the principle requires that realization be spatially contiguous or temporally continuous, then the fifth case should perhaps be denied, yet that feels wrong. If instead the principle demands only organizational invariance regardless of substrate or spatial distribution, then I must accept the swarm, which also feels wrong. Could there be a distinction involving the etiological origin of the functional roles, or perhaps a requirement that the realizing system possess an integrated causal topology rather than merely a distributed one? Please state the principle or competing principles precisely, show which clause is motivated by which of my cases, tell me honestly which of my six verdicts must be overturned if I accept it, predict its verdict on a synthetic morphogenetic tissue that develops a brain-like structure organically without being engineered neuron by neuron, and present all of this in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as in a single flowing letter.
