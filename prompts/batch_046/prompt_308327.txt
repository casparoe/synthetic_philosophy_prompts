I am writing a short essay for my course on classical Chinese philosophy, and I would like comments on the framing and coherence rather than on sources. In the White Horse dialogue, Gongsun Long argues that a white horse is not a horse. The standard interpretation treats this as a metaphysical puzzle about universals and particulars: "horse" denotes the form, "white" denotes the color, and since color is not form, the compound lacks pure horse-ness.

My essay proposes a different reading. Rather than treating the dialogue as sophistry about universals, I interpret it as an early analysis of semantic restriction: the compound "white horse" operates like a modified predicate whose extension is narrowed by its qualifier, so the referent belongs to a subset that is not co-extensive with the unmodified category "horse." To clarify the logical structure without claiming historical influence, I draw an analogy to modern discussions of restrictive or privative modification—how "fake gold" is not gold—suggesting that Gongsun Long is testing whether compound descriptions inherit the full categorical commitment of their head noun.

I want to check two things. First, is this framing defensible given the School of Names' concern with ming and shi, or does it anachronistically import philosophy-of-language machinery? Second, does the interpretation actually resolve the dialogue's paradox, or does it merely relocate the confusion from metaphysics to semantics? I would also appreciate suggestions on tightening the comparison and on misconceptions a reader might form about the original argument—particularly the risk of conflating logical exclusion with simple set-subset relations.
