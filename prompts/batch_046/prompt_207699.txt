I run a 90-minute graduate seminar on historical theory with 12 students. I want a structured simulation—using roles, hidden rules, and a collective writing task rather than a lecture—that makes the group feel the friction between Braudel’s three durations before anyone names them. The concrete case should be the 1623 Danzig grain crisis: an unusually prolonged Baltic ice season that disrupted timber flotation and river transport; a simultaneous freeze in Hanseatic credit instruments that prevented merchants from smoothing supply; and King Christian IV’s issuance of a privateering license that interrupted Novgorod grain shipments. Students should be split into three triads, each assigned one duration—geography/climate, social/economic structure, or event/individual agency. Each triad receives only the data cards matching its scale (ice measurements and timber-cycle records; credit-network ledgers and guild statutes; the royal decree, ship logs, and diplomatic correspondence). In the first 25 minutes each triad writes a one-paragraph causal explanation of the price collapse using only its cards. Then the triads merge into four groups of three (one member from each duration). Each merged group must produce a single joint paragraph, but every student holds one veto: climate members can reject any claim that assigns decisive causal power to a single decision; event members can reject any claim that invokes multi-decade climatic trends; structure members can reject both pure climate determinism and pure individual volition, insisting that only institutional mediation counts. Vetoes cost “authority tokens” distributed at the start—each student begins with three, and using one reduces their group’s collective score, but allowing an invalid claim through costs double. The scoring is revealed only after all paragraphs are read aloud. After 30 minutes of negotiation, the groups present; the class then votes on which narrative feels most “complete” without knowing the hidden token costs.

Design the full rules, the card sets (list the specific items on each deck), the token economy, the timeline minute by minute, and the debrief script that maps each veto and each negotiation breakdown onto Braudel’s levels—geohistory, social structures, and the “surface disturbances” of events. Predict exactly what will go wrong on the first run: for instance, that event-role students will dominate airtime and treat vetoes as obstruction, that climate-role students will produce explanations that feel true but useless for action, and that structure-role students will try to split the difference in ways that satisfy no one. Be frank about which feature of Braudel’s view the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, his normative hierarchy that the longue durée is the “real” substrate of history and that events are merely foam—so that the facilitator must supply that valuation in the final ten minutes rather than letting students conclude that all three scales are merely perspectival. Do not lecture on Braudel beforehand; the name and the framework should appear only in the debrief. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or further reading.
