I have just come out of a long, circular argument with a close friend about whether meritocracy is conceptually salvageable or whether desert is always already dissolved by the luck of birth and institutional design. We agreed on almost nothing except that we were not asking the same question: she wanted a normative account of who should get what, while I was asking what any theory of merit-based desert must be able to explain before it can even enter that normative debate. Rather than continue the loop, I want the specification first.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of desert — in the specifically meritocratic sense of earning differential rewards through effort, skill, or contribution — must satisfy to count as conceptually adequate for a modern, structurally complex society. For each desideratum, state the case or conceptual distinction that motivates it, flag whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (e.g., luck-egalitarian, libertarian, virtue-ethical), and indicate whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given well-known results in moral philosophy or empirical social science.

In particular, the list must address, though not necessarily be limited to, the following dimensions:

- The distinction between desert and bare legal entitlement or contractual right, with the motivating counter-case.
- What I will call structural merit: situations in which an individual's achievement is genuinely effortful and skill-based, yet the institutional conditions that made that effort possible were themselves distributed by unchosen prior advantages. The theory must determine whether such agents still possess merit-based desert, and if so, whether it is diminished, transformed, or unchanged, without collapsing into pure luck-egalitarianism.
- Consistency with the empirical correlation between measured merit indicators and inherited socioeconomic status: the theory must not make desert redundant with that correlation, but it must also not ignore it.
- The comparative versus non-comparative nature of desert: does the theory treat desert as a claim to be better off than specific others, or as an absolute claim to a particular reward? Include the objection that makes the rejected view seem unavoidable.
- Compound desert: cases in which an outcome results from the interaction of multiple agents with differential contributions, where causal isolation is epistemically or metaphysically difficult. The theory must provide a principle for division or aggregation.
- The materiality condition: whether desert can be satisfied by purely symbolic or status-based rewards, or whether it necessarily entails distributive claims over material goods and opportunities.

After laying out the criteria, provide a brief assessment of how the main existing approaches fare against them: a strict luck-egalitarian account, a fair-equality-of-opportunity framework in the Rawlsian tradition, a Nozickian entitlement-based approach, and a virtue-ethical or character-centered theory. Focus especially on which single desideratum is most damaging to each contender, rather than giving an overall score.

Please keep this formal, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations — I am working without library access and need to evaluate the arguments on their internal merits alone.
