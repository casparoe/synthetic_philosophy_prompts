I have recently begun working with a clinical research group that collects structured first-person narratives from patients emerging from general anesthesia, and in reviewing the methodological literature I have become troubled by an argument against Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology that I cannot locate the error in. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Heterophenomenology instructs us to treat every sincere first-person report as authoritative for the subject's heterophenomenological world, while remaining ontologically neutral about whether that world corresponds to actual phenomenal consciousness.

(2) In our study, some patients report rich, structured visual imagery upon recovery (call this a phenomenologically populated world), while others—equally lucid and coherent—report only what they term "blank functional registration," insisting that no imagery, feeling, or qualitative character accompanied their processing (a phenomenologically bankrupt world).

(3) The method assigns each patient a distinct heterophenomenological world—one containing imagery, one containing its absence—but provides no internal criterion for judging one report more veridical than the other, since neutrality forbids privileging any report on ontological grounds.

(4) Now suppose we extend this framework to an artificial narrative system trained to generate reports structurally identical to the bankrupt-world descriptions, with identical coherence and contextual sensitivity; heterophenomenology must assign it the same kind of world—an empty one—without being able to invoke consciousness as a differentiating factor.

(5) If the method cannot discriminate, even in principle, between a populated world, a bankrupt world, and an artificially generated analog of either, then it possesses no resources for distinguishing consciousness from its absence, or from its simulation.

(6) But a science of consciousness must be able to make at least some discriminative judgments about presence versus absence of experience; otherwise it collapses into a general taxonomy of narrative self-models.

(7) Therefore, consistently applied heterophenomenology proves that there can be no science of consciousness—only an indifferent catalog of heterophenomenological worlds that applies equally to conscious subjects, phenomenologically bankrupt subjects, and non-conscious systems.

This conclusion seems to destroy the very project it is meant to serve. I am reluctant to abandon (1) or (3), because they seem to capture the essential neutrality and local authority that make the method attractive, and I want to preserve the distinction between describing a world and endorsing its ontology. Where does the reasoning overreach? Identify the premise or inference that should be rejected, explain why it initially appeared sound, and describe what giving it up costs—particularly for how we understand the relationship between first-person heterophenomenological description and third-person scientific explanation of consciousness.
