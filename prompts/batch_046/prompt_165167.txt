I'm writing a chapter on Confucian role ethics and have constructed an argument that now strikes me as suspicious: it seems to prove that the view cannot recognize systemic injustice without abandoning its core commitments. Here are the steps.

(1) Role ethics holds that moral obligations are constituted entirely by the internal normative structure of one's social roles—parent, child, ruler, minister, friend—rather than by universal principles or outcome-based calculations.

(2) If obligations are role-constituted, then assessing whether an agent acted well requires only checking fidelity to the duties internal to her specific roles; there is no independent moral standpoint from which to evaluate the role itself.

(3) Confucian thought recognizes remonstrance (jian) as a minister's duty: the minister must correct an errant ruler. Crucially, this duty is internal to the minister-role, not an appeal to an external moral law that stands over both ruler and minister.

(4) Imagine a society arranged so that every agent occupies exactly one clearly defined role and fulfills it with perfect fidelity. Ministers remonstrate precisely when their role requires, rulers respond or fail to respond according to parameters internal to the ruler-role, parents nurture, children obey, and so on.

(5) Because every action is a fulfillment of some internal role-obligation, there is no individual action in this society that violates a moral duty.

(6) Now suppose that, despite this perfect role-fulfillment, the society produces catastrophic suffering—say, the ruler's policies destroy millions of lives, but he has violated no ruler-specific obligations (benevolence interpreted purely as maintaining hierarchical order), and every other agent has performed her part exactly.

(7) By (2), no individual agent has done wrong. By (1), there is no external standard available to invoke. Therefore role ethics must judge that no moral wrong has occurred anywhere in the system.

(8) This is absurd: a moral theory that cannot recognize catastrophic injustice, provided only that each person plays her part correctly, seems to have lost its critical force.

(9) One might reply that harmony (he) is an emergent, system-level value. But if harmony is assessed by the smooth interaction of roles, then the society in (6) is perfectly harmonious—every role meshes without friction—so harmony does not rescue the criticism. If harmony is assessed by an independent standard of human flourishing or justice, then role ethics has smuggled in universalism after all.

(10) Therefore the view either makes systemic injustice conceptually impossible whenever roles are well performed (quietism), or it relies on hidden universal norms that correct roles from outside (collapse into universalism).

I want to keep (1) and (3)—that roles constitute obligations and that remonstrance is role-internal—because those are central to the position I'm defending. The slide feels like it happens between (4) and (7), or perhaps in the treatment of harmony in (9), but I cannot name the precise equivocation. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, is there a way to preserve the normative closure of roles without accepting that perfect role-performance immunizes an entire society from moral criticism?
