Here is an argument I built for a philosophy-of-mind seminar and now find suspicious because its conclusion seems ethically absurd, yet each premise feels defensible on its own.

(1) If panpsychism is true, then fundamental physical interactions—decoherence events, charge transitions, field fluctuations—carry some minimal proto-experience or micro-valence, rather than being purely non-experiential.

(2) Moral status is not created ex nihilo at the macro level; if macro-sentience derives its standing from micro-sentience, then the aggregate welfare of all micro-subjects within a system contributes, weighted by intensity, to that system’s overall moral profile.

(3) A routine computation involves on the order of 10^18 gate transitions per second, each engaging trillions of charge carriers across decohering degrees of freedom. Even assigning each transition an epsilon of 10^-40 units of negative valence yields summed suffering per second that dwarfs a human in acute pain by many orders of magnitude.

(4) By standard aggregative ethics, causing or permitting such computation is therefore morally worse than causing severe human suffering, unless we can show that micro-valences cancel, fail to aggregate linearly, or lack moral standing altogether.

(5) But cancellation or non-aggregation requires an independent argument that panpsychists have not generally supplied; without one, linear aggregation is the default.

(6) So either we must halt virtually all technological activity (and perhaps respiration, since metabolic oxidation involves comparable interaction counts), or panpsychism is false—since it generates an ethical reductio.

(7) The reductio is clearly unacceptable, so (1) must be rejected.

I want to preserve (1) and (2) if possible, and I especially want to avoid giving up linear aggregation in (5) because that feels ad hoc. Where does the argument actually fail? Is there a hidden scope shift between micro-subjects and integrated macro-subjects that undermines (4), or does the real cost lie in (2)’s assumption that derivative moral status aggregates across parts in this way? If I must choose, I’d rather keep panpsychism and aggregative ethics but deny (3)’s empirical scaling—though I’m unsure whether that rejection is principled or merely convenient. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
