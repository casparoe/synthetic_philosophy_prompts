Over the past year, while accompanying my mother through the progressive dissolution of her narrative self in advanced dementia, I have found myself returning to Erwin Schrödinger's Vedantic writings—not as historical curiosity, but as a live phenomenological problem. I am now preparing a 90-minute graduate seminar for about fourteen students in philosophy of mind and comparative metaphysics, and I want an activity that makes them feel, rather than merely understand, the pressure behind his claim that consciousness is singular and that the appearance of plural minds is a structural illusion analogous to the prismatic breaking of white light. The students are familiar with the basic Advaita distinction between Atman and the empirical ego, but they consistently treat Schrödinger's unity thesis as either poetic license or an easily refuted denial of other minds.

Please design a structured in-class deliberation or role-play—complete with assigned roles, hidden-information rules, a precise timeline, and a debrief—that forces participants to inhabit the problem of pluralization from within. The rules should arrange things so that the group first runs into an ambiguity about whether they share one stream of experience or possess many, and should be constructed so that adding more information (more descriptions, more comparisons) does not resolve the ambiguity but only deepens it. After the activity, the debrief should map specific moments onto Schrödinger's argument that multiplicity is postulated rather than observed, should include an honest admission of which feature of Vedantic non-duality the exercise cannot reproduce—likely the soteriological realization of identity with Brahman as opposed to a merely intellectual concession—and should warn me frankly about what will go wrong the first time I run it, probably students treating the hidden constraints as a puzzle to be solved rather than a limit to be endured. I do not need bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the conceptual force should come entirely from the rules and stakes of the exercise itself.
