I was at this rationalist-adjacent meetup last week and got pulled into a debate that's been bugging me ever since. Two people there -- Sam and Riley -- were arguing over whether it makes sense to treat non-human things like the 1815 Tambora eruption, or the spread of Yersinia pestis, as actual "historical actors" rather than just background conditions. Sam was saying that if historians only talk about kings and generals they miss half the causal picture, and that calling a volcano or a pathogen an "actor" is a useful corrective to human exceptionalism. He wasn't saying they have intentions, just that distributed causation should include them as nodes that do real work in the narrative. Riley pushed back pretty hard. She said agency without intentionality is just a sloppy metaphor, and that framing causes as actors risks environmental determinism -- like saying "the climate caused the famine" lets actual policymakers who failed to build grain reserves totally off the hook. Sam said he wasn't denying human responsibility, he was just saying causation is distributed, and that recognizing a germ's role isn't more anthropomorphic than saying a rock exerts gravity. Riley replied that gravity doesn't compete with human choices in an explanation, but saying "the volcano caused the Year Without a Summer" sounds like it overrode human choice in a way that excuses inaction.

Then Sam tried to save his view with a distinction he made up on the spot: "veto agency" versus "steering agency." Veto agency is when a non-human event blocks all human alternatives -- like an asteroid impact -- and steering agency is when it shapes outcomes among paths that were still viable. He said germs and climate mostly have veto power in specific moments, not steering power over long periods, so recognizing them doesn't actually excuse human decisions. Riley said the distinction sounds neat but collapses in practice, because whether something counts as a veto or a steer depends entirely on what counterfactual human plans you're comparing it to, so it's not an objective feature of the non-human thing but just a framing choice. They turned to me and asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't figure out who was right about what. Can you go through their exchange move by move, rate each step, and say who's right about which specific point and what both are missing? Specifically, does Sam's veto-vs-steering distinction hold water, is Riley right that it's framing-dependent, and is there actually a principled line between treating non-humans as causes versus treating them as actors without falling into determinism?
