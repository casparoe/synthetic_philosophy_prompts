I'm a phd student in phil of mind and i've been stuck on something about how color-phi experiments are actually run in consciousness labs. The setup is familiar: two dots flash in different positions and colors, subjects report seeing one dot move and change color midway, and then we ask them to timestamp—using that Libet-style rotating clock—when they first experienced the intermediate green. The lab then compares that timestamp to the physical stimulus sequence and interprets any mismatch as evidence that consciousness either retroactively revised the earlier frame (the Orwellian story) or constructed the whole intermediate scene after the fact (the Stalinesque one). But here's the thing that feels off: the protocol treats the subject's timestamp report as an attempt to read out a pre-existing 'moment of awareness' that has a correct location in objective time, and then corrects the report when it doesn't line up. What view of conscious timing does that correction practice actually presuppose? Does it require that experiences are discrete, datable events that can be wrong about thier own position, or can a view where temporal content is relationally constructed still license the same experimental move? And why do we do this timestamp-correction for apparent-motion reports but not for, say, size-contrast illusions—there we just say the subject misperceived, we don't say thier experience was 'revised' at a later date? I'm not looking for citations, just want to know what implicit theory of experience this institutional practice commits us to, whether its parts are consistent, and what would have to change if we dropped the idea that there is a correct neural 'time of consciousness' to compare against.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then lay out the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
