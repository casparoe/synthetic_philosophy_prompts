In our philosophy-of-science working group, a debate about Wolpert's no-free-lunch results and Hume's problem of induction has been stuck for over a year. Both sides accept the formal result: averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm outperforms random search. One side—call them the bias-relocationists—argues that this dissolves the traditional problem of induction, because induction never succeeds without a prior that already encodes worldly structure. They claim the real philosophical task is therefore not justifying inductive projection but justifying the choice of inductive bias, and that philosophers stall by treating learning and bias-selection as separate problems when NFL collapses them into one.

The other side—the domain-structuralists—agrees that learners need biases, but denies that NFL reveals anything epistemically troubling about actual scientific induction. They note that NFL requires averaging over a uniform distribution of all logically possible problems, a condition never met in real domains shaped by physical symmetries, causal mechanisms, and evolutionary constraints. So they say the relocationists have solved a formal puzzle that does not map onto Hume's actual question—why we are justified in expecting the future to resemble the past in our world, not why a black-box optimizer fails across every possible world. The stall persists because both sides cite the same theorem to opposite ends: one as proof that induction is impossible without prior commitment, the other as proof that universal optimization is impossible while local induction remains untouched.

Everyone accepts the mathematics and agrees that biases matter, so the disagreement is not factual. My hypothesis is that we differ over what counts as solving Hume's problem: the relocationists think a solution must explain why any rational agent is entitled to any projection at all, and they take NFL to show that entitlement can only come from pre-loaded bias; the structuralists think a solution need only explain why our actual projections are rational given our actual environment, and they take NFL to be silent on that because it abstracts away from environment-structure.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden normative dispute about whether justifying a bias is easier or harder than justifying an inductive step? Are the sides answering different questions—one about the logic of learning in general, one about the epistemology of science in particular—under the single heading "the problem of induction"? Or is there a concealed formal question about whether structured real-world domains break the NFL conditions in an epistemically relevant way? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a philosophical distinction between statistical prior and substantive theoretical commitment, a formal result linking domain-structure to NFL exemption, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because it conflates mathematical optimization with epistemic justification? And should we expect resolution, or treat the stall as permanent?
