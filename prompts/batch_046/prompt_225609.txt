I am running a graduate workshop on metaphysics and social ontology with eight doctoral students, meeting for two hours. I want a structured simulation—something like a game with rules, assigned roles, and small real stakes (points that translate into minor obligations)—that makes participants feel, rather than merely understand, that money is an institutional kind sustained by collective acceptance rather than a brute physical fact. The design should begin with a simple token economy: participants receive physical objects and explicit rules for exchange, and they must complete a series of cooperative tasks using only these tokens. Midway through, the facilitator should silently alter one background condition (for example, the redemption mechanism is suspended for half the group but not announced) without changing the physical tokens themselves, so that the group eventually splits into those who continue treating the objects as currency and those who treat them as worthless paper, with no hidden fact resolving which group is correct. The debrief should then map each stage onto the distinction between brute facts and institutional facts, identifying exactly when the collective acceptance condition was undermined and why the dispute is not empirical. Please also include an honest note on which feature of institutional reality—likely its intergenerational continuity and the absence of a single moment of collapse—this single-session exercise cannot reproduce, and suggest how the facilitator should supply that limitation verbally after the simulation ends. No lecture should precede the game; the rules and the silent rule-change should generate the conceptual tension on their own.
