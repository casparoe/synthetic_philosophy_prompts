Karl Jaspers, in his postwar analysis, distinguished criminal guilt (individual legal liability), political guilt (collective responsibility for state actions), moral guilt (individual conscience), and metaphysical guilt (solidarity in human suffering). Applying that framework here, my own credence that this "zero-overlap" nation bears genuine political guilt—not merely a reparative duty or historical stigma—is about 0.30.

Please give me your actual credence that such a state is genuinely guilty in Jaspers's political sense, but decompose it precisely: (1) the probability that a state can be a moral agent capable of guilt at all, rather than just an aggregate of individuals; (2) the probability that institutional and territorial continuity alone, with complete personal discontinuity, is sufficient to transmit guilt across generations; (3) the probability that continuing to derive benefit from past atrocities without formal repudiation amounts to guilt rather than to a separate obligation of repair; and (4) the probability that Jaspers's category of political guilt survives this extreme scenario without collapsing into either criminal or metaphysical guilt. Assign each term a rough number with one line of justification, then combine them into an overall figure. Tell me which term dominates your uncertainty, and what specific institutional analogy or conceptual argument would shift that term the most.
