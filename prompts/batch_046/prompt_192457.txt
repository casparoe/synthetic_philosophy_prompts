Question: Explain Luis de Molina's doctrine of middle knowledge (scientia media). Your answer should (a) distinguish natural knowledge, middle knowledge, and free knowledge, specifying the object of each; (b) define counterfactuals of creaturely freedom and explain why Molina holds they are contingent yet known by God prior to any creative decree; (c) present the grounding objection—specifically, the claim that true counterfactuals of freedom lack a sufficient ground independent of God's will or the actual world—and outline the Molinist response that appeals to God's supercomprehension of creaturely essences; and (d) apply this framework to the following scenario: in world W, God actualizes circumstances C in which agent S, who retains libertarian freedom and possesses an essential but non-determinative disposition toward honesty, is offered a substantial bribe. If the relevant counterfactual is that S would freely refuse, explain whether God's decision to actualize W amounts to strong or weak actualization of S's refusal, and assess whether this preserves S's freedom consistently with Molina's claim that providence does not collapse into determinism.

Student answer:

(a) Natural knowledge is God's cognition of all necessary truths and of every possible world and creature; middle knowledge is his grasp of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom—what each free agent would do in every possible circumstance—logically situated between natural and free knowledge; free knowledge is his knowledge of the actual world that results from his creative decree.

(b) Counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are subjunctive conditionals of the form 'If agent S were in circumstances C, S would freely perform action A.' Molina holds they are contingent because the agent retains libertarian freedom in those circumstances—S could do otherwise—yet they are true prior to any divine decision. God knows them not by observing actual worlds, since many are never realized, but through a kind of modal scanning of the entire space of possible worlds, seeing which propositions hold at each world-index.

(c) The grounding objection, prominently developed by Thomas Flint, asks what makes these pre-volitional counterfactuals true if neither God's will (which comes after) nor the actual world (which is not yet fixed) provides their ground. The standard Molinist reply is that God knows them through his supercomprehension of each creature's essential properties: the counterfactual is grounded in what the creature essentially is, so that God reads off the outcome from the essence itself without imposing it.

(d) In world W, God weakly actualizes S's refusal: he brings about the circumstances C and the truth of the counterfactual, but he does not cause the refusal itself, which flows solely from S's will. Because God selects W precisely because the counterfactual obtains there, his choice is constrained by the pre-volitional truths, yet this does not compromise omnipotence—he remains free to actualize any world whose counterfactual profile he approves. The refusal is therefore not deterministically produced, though God can be said to cause the counterfactual to hold by his decree to actualize W, which secures the outcome within his providential plan without touching S's internal freedom.
