I have been working through a cluster of cases about predictive fatalism—situations in which an agent learns a forecast about her own future and that knowledge enters the causal chain that produces the outcome. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle that sorts them, or whether my intuitions are tracking several distinct phenomena that only look alike.

Case 1, the classic Damascus story: Death tells a merchant they will meet in Damascus; the merchant flees to Damascus, where Death explains he was surprised to see him there because they had an appointment in Samarra. Verdict: the merchant was free to flee, but the endpoint was fixed; knowing the endpoint did not make it avoidable, only made the path to it more circuitous.

Case 2, the self-defeating oracle: parents are told their newborn will kill his father; they abandon him; he is raised without knowing them, meets and kills a stranger who is his father. Verdict: the prediction was avoidable—had they not heard it, the chain would not have occurred—but once heard, it structured the very actions that fulfilled it. The knowledge was causally toxic.

Case 3, the reflexive algorithm: a deterministic forecasting model, whose inputs include the fact that subjects read its outputs, predicts that subject S will choose option A tomorrow. S reads the prediction and chooses B. Verdict: the model is not falsified; it should have predicted B, because S's knowledge of the prediction was part of the input. The error was in treating the model's first output as independent of disclosure.

Case 4, the probabilistic warning: a physician tells a patient that, given her genetics, she has an 80 percent chance of cardiac arrest if she continues her current lifestyle. She changes her lifestyle and avoids the event. Verdict: the prediction was never about a fixed endpoint; it was conditional on a path she was free to alter. No fatalism is involved.

Case 5, the coincidence trap: a fortune-teller predicts I will die in a library on Tuesday. I avoid libraries on Tuesday, but a library truck crashes into my house that day. Verdict: this feels structurally like Damascus—the endpoint was fixed, the path contingent—but I am unsure whether to say the prediction was "true" or merely "lucky," since the mechanism of fulfillment was not the one implied.

My central difficulty is reconciling Case 1 and Case 3. In Case 1, I want to say the merchant's flight was genuinely free, even though the endpoint was invariant. In Case 3, I want to say that once the prediction is in the loop, S's choice of B was determined by the disclosure, leaving no independent freedom. But if freedom in Case 1 is compatible with an invariant endpoint, why is freedom in Case 3 not compatible with an invariant endpoint (the model eventually predicting B)? Is there a principle—perhaps about the level of description at which the prediction operates, or about whether the knowledge is part of the causal mechanism versus part of the subject matter—that distinguishes fatalistic binding from reflexive determination?

Please state the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, that captures all five verdicts. For each clause, identify the case that motivates it and state its verdict on a case I have not listed: specifically, a prediction generated by an AI that is designed never to disclose its outputs to the subject, but the subject hacks the system and reads the hidden prediction. And be honest about which of my verdicts must be dropped if we insist on a simpler, unified account that treats Cases 1 and 3 symmetrically.
