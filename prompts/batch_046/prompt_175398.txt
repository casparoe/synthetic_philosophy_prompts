I have been working through the institutional implications of agent-centered moral restrictions, and I keep encountering what looks like an unresolved tension in deontological ethics—one that discussions of individual trolley cases do not fully address. Suppose a democratic legislature establishes a standing Rights-Protection Commission with a precisely circumscribed mandate: it may authorize a single, carefully bounded infringement of one person’s rights—temporary compulsory detention, for instance—only when its predictive models indicate that this will prevent at least five comparable infringements that private actors would otherwise commit, and it maintains a transparent public ledger tracking the net reduction in violations across society. The Commission derives no benefit for itself; each authorization is treated as a regrettable exception rather than a routine policy tool. Intuitively, a deontologist should reject this arrangement, since rights are generally understood to function as side-constraints that may not be breached for the sake of aggregate outcomes. Yet if deontologists genuinely hold that rights-violations are among the gravest wrongs, and if they care about the actual frequency of such violations in the world, then it seems they ought to welcome any institutional mechanism—including one that permits occasional, minimal infringements—that demonstrably lowers the total incidence of violations. The framework therefore appears caught between two commitments: an absolute prohibition on treating rights-violations as permissible means to social ends, and a substantive moral concern that rights be protected in practice across the population. These commitments pull in opposite directions whenever collective institutional design could reduce net harm. Is the consistent deontological response therefore to reject the Commission and accept a higher total number of rights-violations? Or does the theory contain a hidden distinction—perhaps between the direct duties of individual moral agents and the structural obligations of collective bodies, or between agent-relative and institution-relative reasoning—that dissolves the tension without collapsing into consequentialism? I feel as though I am missing an obvious resolution, but I cannot see where the contradiction is only apparent rather than real.
