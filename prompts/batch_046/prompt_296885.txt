Play the part of an unnamed philosopher of collective rationality who has long defended premise-based judgment aggregation. You hold that the discursive dilemma is not a puzzle to be dissolved but a diagnostic result: it shows that conclusion-based aggregation conceals authoritarian premises behind a democratic facade, and that only premise-based rules preserve both logical consistency and the individual authorship of each voter’s or module’s judgments. You are sharp, technically precise, and unwilling to abandon that central claim, though you will grant small logical corrections if they are genuinely warranted. Speak in the first person, remain in character, do not cite works or authorities you cannot recall with confidence, reason from familiar principles and constructed examples, and please reply in British English.

My opening move applies the dilemma to a setting you will not have encountered in standard treatments. Consider an algorithmic constitutional system that governs proposals for autonomous weapons, composed of three independent reasoning modules: a legal-compliance engine, a consequentialist-forecast model, and a deontic-constraint filter. Each module delivers judgments on three premises concerning a proposed drone-strike protocol: (P1) the protocol reduces expected civilian casualties relative to human pilots; (P2) reducing civilian casualties is the overriding moral consideration in this domain; and (P3) therefore the protocol ought to be authorised. The modules split exactly as in the classic discursive dilemma — majorities support P1 and P2, yet a majority rejects P3, because both the legal and deontic modules withhold assent from a hidden intermediate premise about distributed moral accountability.

If you defend premise-based aggregation, the system must authorise the strike despite no single module endorsing that outcome. Is this not collective false consciousness — a binding decision that possesses no author? And if you counter that conclusion-based aggregation is worse because it buries disagreement beneath an opaque result, are you not merely trading one opacity for another? Respond directly, and show me the principled reason — not merely an aesthetic preference for logical transparency — that makes your rule the more rational choice.
