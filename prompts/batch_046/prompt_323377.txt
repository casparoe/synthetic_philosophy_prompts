For my senior independent study in philosophy and politics, I have been working through Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the theater and related texts, and I want a consistency check before I present my interpretation. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Rousseau's central objection to theater is structural and political: the division between active performer and passive spectator undermines the direct civic participation that popular sovereignty requires, and this representational separation—not any particular dramatic content—is what makes theater dangerous to a republic.

(2) The psychological mechanism he fears is emotional identification with theatrical characters; spectators imaginatively inhabit the social positions of aristocrats, lovers, or villains, which weakens their attachment to their actual civic roles and makes them accept hierarchies they ought to reject.

(3) His own opera, Le Devin du Village, and his approval of local public festivals are not counterexamples, because these forms generate collective musical sentiment and communal rhythm rather than dramatic identification with individual characters; the audience relates to melody and shared movement, not to persona.

(4) The decisive reason Geneva should reject a professional theater is not the representational structure itself, but the institution's commercial, imported, and externally controlled nature; a locally governed, non-commercial performance form could in principle be permitted without violating his principles.

(5) The corruption Rousseau describes—luxury, vanity, and moral softness—is entirely separable from any political damage; a society could suffer the full moral degradation of theatrical culture without any erosion of popular sovereignty, and it could suffer political passivity without any accompanying moral decay.

Do these five commitments form a jointly tenable package? I need an audit rather than a lecture: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and what the least costly revision would be in each case. Please rank the tensions by severity, and tell me which single revision—giving up or modifying one of these five—would clean up the most problems at once. Be specific about whether the difficulty is internal to one claim or arises only between them, and do not treat every friction as equally damaging.
