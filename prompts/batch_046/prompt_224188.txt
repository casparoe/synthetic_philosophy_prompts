In a graduate seminar I am co-teaching on nineteenth-century ethics, our discussion of Kierkegaard’s Fear and Trembling has reached a peculiar standstill. Over several sessions, the group has split between those who read Johannes de silentio’s account of Abraham as a sincere defense of a religious exception—where the knight of faith genuinely suspends the universal ethical duty to love one’s son in favor of an absolute relation to the absolute—and those who treat the same narrative as an ironized limit-case meant to expose the moral monstrosity that results when interiority claims exemption from communal norms. Both camps cite the same structural features—the three problemata, the comparison with Agamemnon and the tragic hero, the hiddenness of Abraham’s inwardness, and Johannes’s repeated insistence that he cannot understand the knight of faith—yet neither side has moved. The disagreement feels deeper than exegesis: one faction treats “the ethical” as a Hegelian Sittlichkeit that can be bracketed but not annihilated, while the other takes it as a Kantian universal that either binds or reveals a failure of faith, and they talk past each other using the same vocabulary.

I am trying to determine what kind of stall this is. Is it a hidden formal disagreement about whether “teleological suspension” requires a meta-level normative framework that preserves the ethical as suspended rather than overridden—perhaps analogous to debates about normative silence in particularist ethics—or is it a substantive clash about whether moral reasons can genuinely be silenced by a higher-order commitment? Could it instead be verbal, with each side answering a different question—one about the phenomenology of faith, the other about the legitimacy of religious exception—under the single heading of “Abraham’s justification”? Or does the impasse rest on bedrock intuitions about whether morality can ever be trumped, intuitions that neither side can argue for because they function as the ground rather than the conclusion of reasoning? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal model of normative override that clarifies the logic of suspension, a historical clarification of Kierkegaard’s specific Hegelian targets, a distinction that separates the semantic from the phenomenological question, or an argument that the paradox is meant to remain lived and therefore that no theoretical resolution should be expected? And if you think the disagreement is partly ill-posed, what reframing—perhaps treating the suspension not as an exception to ethics but as a revelation of its internal structure—would allow the discussion to advance without collapsing into either apologetics or mere condemnation?
