Write a dialogue between two equally sharp decision theorists debating whether one should pay in a hybrid scenario that combines counterfactual mugging with XOR blackmail. The setup is this: a predictor has already run a high-fidelity simulation of your reasoning process and will now issue exactly one of two demands—either a standard counterfactual mugging request (pay $100 now, or suffer a predetermined loss in this world because your simulated counterfactual self refused) or an XOR blackmail demand (pay in exactly one of two counterfactual branches, with the predictor observing the branch structure but not revealing which)—depending on whether your simulated self assigned positive credence to being in a hidden "no demand" branch. You know the predictor's selection algorithm is deterministic but opaque to you in the present. The first interlocutor should defend paying, arguing that your counterfactual decision weights still govern the predictor's choice of demand type even when the predictor conditions on your epistemic state about reasoning-about-reasoning, so refusing still makes the worse outcome more likely across the relevant partition. The second should reject this, arguing that once the predictor conditions on your simulated reasoning about the predictor, the decision-relevant counterfactual collapses: there is no stable "branch where you don't pay and no demand is issued" to weight against, so paying becomes either evidential self-manipulation about your own simulation trace or a category error about which world is action-relevant. Ensure they respond to each other's actual mechanics—no talking past one another, no strawmen, no appeals to named authorities or literature. Frame the exchange for a longtime rationalist-blog audience already comfortable with Newcomb-style reasoning, but push the machinery one layer deeper by treating the predictor's conditioning on your epistemic state as part of the decision problem itself rather than as background.
