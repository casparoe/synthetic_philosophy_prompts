Two friends of mine from the local rationalist meetup got into a long argument about belief and ethics, and I genuinely can't tell who's winning. Friend A said she holds what she calls a scaffold belief in the equal moral weight of distant future people — she admits the intergenerational ethics literature is genuinely underdetermined, but she holds it fully, not just as a hypothesis, because without that commitment she wouldn't be able to read the papers charitably enough to understand them. She said James was basically right: when the evidence is genuinely tied and the choice is psychologically real for you, unavoidable, and career-defining, your passional nature gets a legitimate vote, and belief sometimes has to come before inquiry rather than after it.

Friend B said that's just Clifford's shipowner mistake in a new hat. Once you let yourself believe for utility you've broken your epistemic filter, and she can treat the idea as a working hypothesis with like 55% credence and still do the research — belief isn't necessary for motivation, only preference ordering. He said she's rounding up her credence to 100% for emotional comfort and calling it philosophy.

Friend A came back with a distiction between a working hypothesis and a living belief: a hypothesis is tested from outside, but a belief frames what you even notice. She brought up brain fission and personal identity — she said in genuinely underdetermined domains you can't formulate the right experiments without first adopting a framework, and James's point was that some options feel impossible for you unless you actively commit to them first. She isn't rounding up; she's inhabiting one live option knowing she might be wrong.

Friend B conceded that belief can be framework-dependent, but said she's smuggling in a false dichotomy between full belief and no engagement. Clifford allows practical certainty in action without theoretical belief — you can build on 99% confidence without claiming 100% belief. He said her insistence that she needs full belief reveals she wants the psychological payoff of certainty, not just functional engagement. He added that modern Bayesian thinking dissolves the whole problem anyway: graded credences drive action via expected utility, so neither James's will to believe nor Clifford's evidentialist rule actually applies.

Please adjudicate move by move — rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Also say who's right about what specific point, and point out anything both are missing or mixing up. Don't worry about page numbers; just work with the ideas as they stand.
