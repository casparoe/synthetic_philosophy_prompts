Write a villanelle on the Hart–Devlin debate over legal moralism, structured so that its formal movement tracks the argument’s logic. Use two refrains that alternate through the five tercets and meet, unchanged, in the final quatrain: the first should be Devlin’s claim that shared morality is the indispensable fabric of society and that its erosion risks disintegration; the second should be Hart’s reply that law must distinguish critical morality from positive morality and that only demonstrated harm, not moral offense, can justify state coercion. In the opening tercet, present Devlin’s disintegration thesis and his analogy between private immorality and treason; in the second, introduce Hart’s empirical challenge and his Millian harm principle. Use the middle tercets to test both positions against the concrete case of consensual private conduct, letting Devlin insist that critical morality remains enforceable morality and Hart insist that moral harm is not harm to others in the relevant legal sense. In the penultimate tercet, allow each refrain to appear in slightly altered form so that each side reveals what it must concede; then restore both to their original wording in the closing quatrain, ending in deadlock rather than synthesis, because the core dispute is whether moral consensus alone can ground law. Cluster your rhyme sounds by side—one family drawn from legal and social vocabulary, the other from liberal and harm-based vocabulary—so that the poem’s sonic interlocking mirrors the conceptual stalemate. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of law recognizes Devlin’s moderate thesis, Hart’s critical-positive distinction, and the specific function of the harm principle, without naming the authors in the body or relying on external references.
