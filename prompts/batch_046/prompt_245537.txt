I have been helping my cousin study for a civic integration exam that includes a section on what the study booklet calls “Western values.” The materials list norms such as equality between men and women, freedom to practice or leave a religion, and participation in democratic elections, and they ask applicants to affirm these and to reject practices that conflict with them. At first this seemed like a straightforward checklist, but then I noticed something puzzling: the same government describes these ideas both as universal human rights and as specifically Western traditions, and while immigrants must agree to them as a condition of residency, citizens who live there are never tested, and some openly reject parts of the list without anyone suggesting they should leave. So I am trying to understand what kind of belief about culture, value, and political membership this institutional practice actually assumes.

Does administering a test framed this way require thinking that there is one coherent tradition called “the West” that uniquely owns these norms? Or could the practice survive if we believe these norms are universal but are simply enforced here? If the category “Western values” is not really coherent—since people across Europe and North America argue fiercely about gender roles, secularism, and democratic procedure—then is the phrase just rhetorical packaging, or is it doing essential work in deciding who belongs? I want someone to reconstruct the implicit theory, or theories, behind this design: what it must presuppose about where values come from, whether assent and membership ought to be tied together, and whether its parts are philosophically consistent with one another. And I want to know what would actually have to change in the policy—if anything—if the government dropped the word “Western” and spoke only in terms of local constitutional law or universal rights.
