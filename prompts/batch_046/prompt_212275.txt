I am a student in an upper-level seminar on Nietzsche’s second Untimely Meditation, and I am writing an essay that reads Kazuo Ishiguro’s The Remains of the Day through his taxonomy of monumental, antiquarian, and critical history. Rather than treating Stevens as a straightforward example of antiquarian preservation, I want to argue that the novel depicts a pathological fusion: Stevens directs a monumental, life-affirming devotion toward Lord Darlington’s aristocratic legacy, yet because that legacy is already decaying, the monumental impulse collapses into an antiquarian obsession with detail, protocol, and emotional suppression. At the same time, the critical mode—the judgment of Darlington’s political failures and of Stevens’s own complicity—is repeatedly approached and then neutralized by the narrator’s archival precision. I am also interested in whether the novel’s formal unreliability performs this very antiquarianism, using meticulous recall to prevent moral reckoning.

Could you help me work through where this Nietzschean framing illuminates specific scenes—such as Stevens’s defense of Darlington’s reputation, his dismissal of Miss Kenton’s protests, and his refusal to acknowledge the changing social order—and where it risks distorting the text? I am especially uncertain about the final pier scene: does Stevens’s quiet acceptance of loss and diminished future constitute a belated critical judgment, a final retreat into antiquarian resignation, or something that escapes Nietzsche’s tripartite scheme altogether? I want the reading to be faithful in both directions, so please identify where Ishiguro’s treatment of class deference, emotional deprivation, and narrative self-deception adds something to the philosophical debate about history’s uses and disadvantages that pure argument does not capture, and where an honest critic must admit that the novel resists being reduced to an illustration of Nietzsche’s categories.
