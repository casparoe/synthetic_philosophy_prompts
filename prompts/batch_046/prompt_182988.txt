Compare Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov's prefix-free complexity measure as formalizations of Occam's razor. Standard treatments treat the choice of universal Turing machine as an asymptotically irrelevant constant; instead, examine a case where an observer can adopt one of two equally powerful universal machines—one with primitives suited to spatial patterns, the other to temporal sequences—and the data supports a hypothesis that is minimal-length on one but not the other. Does Solomonoff's universal prior genuinely resolve this ambiguity, or does it conceal a hidden ontological commitment about what counts as a "natural" computation? Contrast this with the traditional philosophical formulation of Occam's razor, which concerns the multiplication of entities or laws rather than syntactic brevity in a formal language. To ground the comparison, invent a brief concrete scenario (a short observed sequence or pattern) in which the two machines assign opposite simplicity rankings to the competing explanations. Then argue whether either formalization truly captures the intuition that entities should not be multiplied without necessity, or whether both reduce that intuition to an encoding-dependent preference that can be inverted by re-description.
