As a doctoral candidate in comparative political philosophy, I am facilitating an adversarial collaboration between two interlocutors who have spent several years arguing past one another on the architecture of civic integration. One party defends the French republican tradition of laïcité—emphasising state neutrality, universal citizenship, and the relegation of religious or ethnic particularity to the private sphere—while the other defends an Anglo-American multiculturalist framework that treats group-differentiated recognition and institutional accommodation as conditions of democratic equality.

Please draft the joint statement they ought to have produced. Structure it as follows:

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: substantive propositions about the sociology of assimilation, the psychology of identity threat under state neutrality, the empirical variability of intergroup contact effects, and the normative status of cultural continuity—formulated precisely enough to be meaningful yet neutrally enough that both parties could affix their names.

Second, a table of disagreements in which each divergence is stated in wording that the respective camp would accept as accurate to its own view. Include at least: (a) whether cultural membership should be treated by the state as a private preference or as a public status warranting recognition; (b) whether the visibility of religious or ethnic markers in shared civic spaces undermines social cohesion or expresses legitimate pluralism; and (c) whether successful integration should be evaluated by parity of participation in common institutions or by parity of recognition across distinct communities.

Third, a crux analysis identifying the sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one party. I take two likely cruxes to be: (i) whether organic cultural continuity or 'naturalness' possesses independent normative value when weighing assimilationist pressures; and (ii) how far predictive social science about intergroup dynamics can be trusted to guide institutional design across generational timescales. For each crux, propose a concrete test case that both sides accept as probative—specifically, a hypothetical municipal civic apprenticeship scheme for young adults in which cultural and religious associations are either granted advisory seats in curriculum design with funding contingent on demonstrated cross-community enrolment (the multicultural model), or are strictly excluded from design authority yet receive equivalent per-capita funding tied solely to universal participation metrics (the laïcité model). State, for each crux and for this case, the prediction each camp would make in advance, without resolving which is correct.

The document should be formal, written in British English, contain no citations or reading lists, and should stand alone for a reader with no access to external sources.
