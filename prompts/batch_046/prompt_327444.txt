I have recently been faced with a professional decision in which withholding a material disclosure from a collaborator would secure me an advantage I genuinely need, and in trying to assess the moral status of this omission I have turned to Kant's universalizability tests only to find that my verdict seems to depend on how precisely I formulate my maxim. If I describe my principle narrowly—omitting this particular disclosure in this specific negotiation, knowing that full transparency would cost me the position—universalizing the practice does not obviously generate a contradiction in conception, since a world in which people occasionally omit disclosures in narrowly defined competitive contexts does not appear to undermine the very institution of disclosure itself. If, however, I formulate the maxim more broadly—deliberately withholding professionally relevant information whenever it secures a necessary advantage—the contradiction in conception seems immediate, because universal adoption of that principle would erode the trust that makes professional disclosure possible in the first place. My question is therefore not about my particular case but about the conceptual architecture of the test itself: is there a principled Kantian criterion, perhaps rooted in the distinction between contradiction in conception and contradiction in will, or in the requirement that maxims be adopted as laws for rational nature as such, that determines the correct level of abstraction at which a maxim must be specified? Or does the universalizability procedure necessarily inherit an underdetermination that can only be resolved by an independent moral judgment about which descriptions genuinely constitute my subjective principle of volition? I would like an explanation that clarifies whether the two contradiction tests respond differently to this problem of maxim specification, and whether Kant's framework offers any internal constraint on how generally or specifically an agent must articulate the principle behind an action.
