After a long argument last night with a close friend about whether Keynes offers any coherent vision of the good life beyond economic growth, she summarized his position—mostly drawing on his 1930 essay imagining a future of abundance and on some of his later remarks about post-scarcity economics—in a way that struck me as sharper and more extreme than what I recall from my own reading. She said that once compound growth solves the absolute economic problem within a century or so, humanity will naturally settle into a fifteen-hour workweek, and that at that point the good life consists essentially in escaping the somewhat disgusting morbidity of money-making altogether. According to her account, Keynes defends capitalism only as a temporary, necessary scaffold—an instrumental stage whose sole justification is to deliver us to post-scarcity leisure, after which the profit motive should be discarded because it actively corrupts human character rather than merely misallocating resources.

She also claimed that Keynes believed relative needs—those fueled by social comparison—would wither away naturally in such a society, leaving people automatically well-equipped to use their free time, since the real obstacle to flourishing is never ignorance about how to live but only the material pressure that prevents living that way. She insisted that his ideal is essentially an aristocratic model of non-instrumental contemplation and aesthetic friendship, democratized in access but unchanged in content, and that he thought mastering the art of living itself is far easier than mastering the art of earning a living once scarcity is removed. I am skeptical of this portrait, especially the claim that Keynes expected people to adapt automatically to leisure and that he saw capitalism as purely disposable scaffolding, but I am not sure where the line falls between a defensible popularization and a misreading.
