I was arguing with a friend last night about whether Skolem’s paradox actually threatens the determinacy of set theory or whether it is only a confusion about models. We went back and forth for hours—he kept saying that once you see the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem applies to ZFC itself, you realize that “uncountable set” is just indexical to the model you are working in, and I kept pushing that this relocates rather than dissolves the problem because it leaves us with no principled reason to prefer the intended model over a countable one, except for a pre-theoretic intuition that looks circular.

I am writing the final exam for my upper-level philosophy of logic seminar and I want one essay question that forces students to do more than recite the standard internal-versus-external distinction. I want them to confront a specific, philosophically sharp scenario: imagine a student—call her Reed—who argues that because first-order ZFC has a countable model, the statement “there exists an uncountable set” is true in some models and false in others, and therefore the term “uncountable” is ambiguous in roughly the same way that “tall” is ambiguous. Reed then claims that this ambiguity is harmless because mathematicians have implicitly agreed to use the external interpretation in practice, so there is no paradox, only a pragmatic convention. The exam question should ask students to evaluate Reed’s argument: does it successfully dissolve Skolem’s paradox, or does it merely relocate the tension? Specifically, they need to address whether appealing to an implicit pragmatic convention is philosophically adequate, or whether it collapses into a conventionalism that undermines the objectivity of set-theoretic claims.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points (total 20 marks), with a one-line note on how candidates typically miss each point. The model answer must take a committed position—definitely argue either that Reed’s move fails and the paradox survives in a relocated form, or that Reed succeeds and the paradox is genuinely dissolved—and defend that stance with explicit reasoning rather than laying out both sides and leaving the matter open. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific philosophical moves each must contain (for instance, distinguishing semantic ambiguity from model-relativity, diagnosing whether the external perspective is genuinely available within first-order logic, and assessing whether conventionalism about model-choice avoids circularity). The question should be answerable in 90 minutes. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—my students will not have access to sources in the exam, and I do not want them guessing at attributions. Keep everything grounded in concepts and arguments that anyone who has followed the course could work with from memory and reasoning alone.
