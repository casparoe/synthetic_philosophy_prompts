I serve as the senior ethics and archives officer for a regional government authority that receives roughly twenty proposals per quarter—requests to erect or remove monuments, to retain or deaccession administrative records, to designate or delist heritage sites, to fund oral-history projects, or to permit redevelopment of contested land. I need a reusable decision procedure that applies the tension between Avishai Margalit’s ethics of memory—the claim that institutions and societies that were complicit in or benefited from gross wrongs bear an asymmetrical duty to remember those wrongs, especially when denial or erasure is possible, with stronger obligations toward direct victims and survivors—and David Rieff’s case for forgetting, which warns that institutionalized remembrance can freeze group identities in trauma, fuel cycles of grievance, substitute ritual for justice, and become a form of moral narcissism that serves the present more than the past. The procedure must be runnable by my small team—two archivists, one legal counsel, one community liaison—in under ninety minutes per proposal, without requiring external scholarship or web lookup, and it must distinguish archival retention (institutional record-keeping, accessible but not identity-constituting) from performative commemoration (monuments, named spaces, rituals, which make present-tense demands on identity).

Please construct the procedure as an ordered sequence of gates or questions in words. For each gate, specify: what counts as a sufficient answer, with concrete indicators my staff can check; what answer stops the procedure with a negative determination (no proposed action, or permission to remove/deaccession/redevelop without further obligation); and what answer forces the decision into a values-based judgment that must be co-signed by the community liaison and legal counsel rather than resolved by the checklist. You must preserve two distinctions that survive simplification: the archival versus performative split, and the asymmetry of obligation (institutions owe remembrance; victim communities do not owe preservation of trauma). You may drop finer distinctions—such as Margalit’s specific taxonomy of shared versus individual memory, or Rieff’s clinical analogies—if they do not change the practical outcome, and you should flag where you have done so.

Then apply the procedure step by step to three proposals:

A. Retain, digitize, and make publicly searchable the complete administrative records of a residential institution (operational 1952–1987) where systematic physical abuse of residents was documented in an internal 1985 inquiry but never prosecuted; survivors and descendants of residents live in the region, as do descendants of former staff; a survivors’ group demands open access, while a descendants-of-staff association requests a fifty-year restriction.

B. Remove a prominent public monument (erected 1923 in a central civic square, depicting a regional political leader) after archival research reveals the leader orchestrated a violent suppression of an ethnic minority group in 1919; the minority community is divided—some demand removal and replacement with a counter-memorial, others prefer retention with contextual signage, and a third faction argues for complete removal of the figure without any replacement.

C. Permit redevelopment of a former forced-labor site (operational 1941–1945) into mixed-income housing with no on-site physical marker, contingent only on depositing a complete documentary record in the regional archive and funding a single academic monograph; no survivors remain, but two generations of descendants live nearby, and the developer offers to name one residential street after the site.

For each case, show the answer at each gate, indicate where the procedure stops or hands over, and state the final determination—including one case where the procedure yields a recommendation you expect I will find unwelcome, and explain why the procedure still produces it despite that discomfort. Conclude by stating clearly what the procedure cannot do: which proposal types it will misclassify, where false positives or false negatives are likely, and what ethical work necessarily remains outside its scope.

Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not quote verbatim from Margalit or Rieff; paraphrase their positions in your own formulation within the procedure’s rationale. Do not ask me to verify obscure historical details; treat the facts given above as sufficient.
