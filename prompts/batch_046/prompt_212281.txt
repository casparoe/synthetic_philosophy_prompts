I take the position that indirect normative theories—those grounding obligations in rules, roles, or institutional forms rather than in direct evaluation of outcomes—are defensible only if reducible to an idealized deliberation model: agents with full causal knowledge but procedural ignorance of their particular positions endorse rules that, once the deliberative conditions are completely specified, collapse into a direct evaluation of the procedure's expected results. On this view, indirectness is not primitive but a computational shortcut. What should change my mind? Sort the potential defeaters by severity. First, cases in which indirect rules retain normative authority even for agents who fully grasp the outcome distribution and still reject reducing those rules to direct evaluation. Second, conceptual or formal arguments showing that idealized deliberation necessarily underdetermines which indirect rules are selected, so that no refinement of conditions can yield a unique output. Third, actual practices in law or morality where participants explicitly treat indirect obligations as irreducible to any counterfactual agreement procedure. For each class, indicate whether a committed defender could absorb the blow by refining the deliberative conditions rather than abandoning the core reduction, and rank all three from fatal to merely awkward for the claim that indirect normativity is never more than disguised direct evaluation.
