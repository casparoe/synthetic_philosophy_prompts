The position I want stress-tested is a strong form of the elicitationist thesis about large language models: that when a model has genuinely encoded a factual regularity or logical structure in its weights during pretraining, there exists a practically discoverable prompting or dialogue strategy—possibly aided by consistency checks across contexts—that will cause it to report that content honestly, without requiring prior mechanistic interpretability of the relevant representations. On this view, persistent failures of elicitation are primarily failures of the elicitor's framing or ingenuity, not evidence that the knowledge was absent, misaligned, or fundamentally inaccessible; the weights contain the belief, and the task is unlocking it. I want an honest map of what would have to be true for this to be wrong. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which empirical patterns—systematic, domain-independent failures of consistency-based extraction, or architectures where correct representations are demonstrably present yet behaviorally inert—would decisively undermine the claim? Which conceptual results, perhaps about the non-identifiability of representations from behavior alone or about the distinction between storing a correlation and holding a belief, would be lethal? For each, say what a committed elicitationist should concede as genuinely damaging versus what they can legitimately dismiss as a scope condition or technical limitation. I also want an assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every failed extraction can be explained away by insufficient ingenuity—and whether that insulation is a reasonable protective belt or a fatal methodological flaw. Include the predictions this view makes that rival accounts, such as the view that models lack stable beliefs and merely sample context-appropriate continuations, do not make, and identify any observation that could actually move a committed proponent rather than being absorbed into the view's qualifications.
