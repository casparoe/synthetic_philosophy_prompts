I am trying to isolate a specific structure in arguments about why human extinction is harmful beyond the direct loss of lives. Consider commitments or practices that appear partly constituted by being handed across generations—maintaining a legal tradition whose authority depends on unbroken continuity, sustaining a scientific program designed to unfold over centuries, or upholding a moral norm whose force derives from an enduring chain of practitioners. If humanity were to end, these activities would not merely be interrupted or rendered futile; rather, their internal logic seems to dissolve, because their value was always tied to an open-ended process of transmission rather than to any completed achievement or to the welfare of future recipients. Samuel Scheffler's discussion of a collective "afterlife" touches on something adjacent, but the pattern I have in mind is narrower: extinction here undermines not just the point of our efforts, but the very kind of value those efforts possessed, specifically because that value was constitutively transmission-dependent. Is there an established term for this precise mode of value-collapse—where the badness of extinction lies in breaking the continuity required by the activity itself, rather than in harming anyone or in generic wasted effort? If not, how should it be distinguished from Scheffler's broader conjecture, from Parfit-style non-identity concerns, and from ordinary claims about uncompleted projects?
