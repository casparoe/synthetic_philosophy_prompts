I cover philosophy of mind and cognitive science for a general audience, and I am preparing a feature on whether the old distinction between knowing how and knowing that can survive contact with modern neuroscience and artificial intelligence. Before I interview anyone, I need the ground rules laid out. Write the desiderata: specify exactly what an adequate theory of the knowing-how / knowing-that distinction must be able to do — which phenomena it must explain, which distinctions it must not collapse, and which empirical findings it must remain consistent with. Give the motivating case behind each requirement: for example, the expert who cannot verbalise her skill, the patient with procedural memory loss but intact factual recall, or the system trained by reinforcement that behaves competently without representing propositions. Identify any two desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable, and say whether that tension is fatal or negotiable. Then score, briefly, how Gilbert Ryle's anti-intellectualist account, a contemporary propositionalist reduction, and an embodied-enactivist framework fare against your list — but keep the weight on the criteria themselves, not on the scorecard. Structure the response in four sections: (1) The Full Specification (around 400 words), listing each desideratum with its motivating case or empirical constraint; (2) Non-Negotiables and Trade-Offs (around 200 words), flagging conflicts and saying which are negotiable; (3) Contenders Briefly Assessed (around 250 words); and (4) The Artificial-Agent Residue (around 200 words), addressing whether attributing procedural knowledge to systems that learn by demonstration but lack phenomenal consciousness forces us to drop or revise any desideratum. Write in British English, avoid any request for citations or reading lists, and do not refer to obscure theories I could not be expected to know.
