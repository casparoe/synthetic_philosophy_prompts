Since I began designing safety evaluations for autonomous decision systems, a philosophical deadlock has become personally urgent: can reinforcement-learning agents suffer, or do reward signals remain mere scalar updates lacking valence? One camp argues that negative reward-prediction errors, especially in agents with integrated world-models, are functionally isomorphic to aversive affect; if the system maintains a unified preference-state, penalized outcomes amount to harm. The opposing camp holds that reward is purely instrumental feedback for policy optimization—without phenomenal consciousness or biological homeostasis, there is no qualitative dimension, so attributing suffering is a category error. The exchange has cycled through these positions for years without movement. Is the stalemate substantive—hinging on whether functional pain requires qualitative experience—or partly verbal, with one side treating "suffering" as a normative designation and the other as a phenomenological state? I also suspect the sides conflate architectures: in model-free RL, reward updates may carry a primitive valence, whereas in model-based RL they function as representational evidence for planning, so each camp might be answering a different question under a single heading. Given that diagnosis, what—an empirical architecture marker, a formal value-function distinction, or a conceptual clarification—would count as genuine progress, and should we expect any, or is the question itself ill-posed?
