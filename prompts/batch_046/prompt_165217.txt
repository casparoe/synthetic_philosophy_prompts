You are preparing a speaker for the Q&A after a seminar presentation. The speaker defends a specific form of methodological holism in social explanation: not the classic Durkheimian claim that society is an autonomous substance, but the narrower thesis that downward causation operates through what she calls temporal indexical constraints. Her core argument is that macro-institutional configurations at time t do not merely aggregate individual choices; they alter the temporal index of the choice environment at t+1 in ways that make synchronic rational-choice models systematically mispredict individual behavior, because the relevant preferences and opportunity costs are indexed to an institutional state that no individual agent authored. She applies this specifically to the persistence of informal economic networks after formal market liberalization in post-communist transitions, claiming that individual entrepreneurs' rational adaptations to liberalized rules at t were causally reshaped by the lingering macro-structure of the pre-existing command economy at t+1, producing path-dependent outcomes that cannot be reconstructed from individual-level strategic interaction alone.

The audience is a workshop of analytical sociologists and rational-choice political economists—scholars steeped in Coleman-style microfoundations, game-theoretic institutional analysis, and a deep suspicion of holism as either metaphysically inflated or empirically redundant. They are not hostile to macro-level patterns per se, but they insist that any causal claim about them must be reducible to mechanisms operating at the individual level, preferably within a framework of strategic interaction, belief updating, and institutional rules treated as exogenous constraints on choice sets.

Prepare the speaker. List the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as these particular scholars would phrase them, using their vocabulary (microfoundations, mechanism design, equilibrium selection, ontological parsimony, aggregation rules, etc.). For each objection, indicate: (a) whether it has real force and should be partially conceded; (b) what part of the speaker's position should be held firm; (c) the brief reply that should be delivered in the time available; and (d) the weakest point in that reply. Include the objection the speaker is hoping nobody asks—the one that strikes at the boundary between her conceptual framework and an empirical claim she is not yet ready to defend. Finally, identify the single reply that must land if the proposal is to survive the session, and explain why its failure would collapse the argument into either vacuous holism or reducible individualism.
