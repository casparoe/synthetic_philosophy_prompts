I am drafting a novel whose central conceit is a constitutional convention on an imagined archipelago, where the framers attempt to codify what they term the Sovereignty of Solitary Concern. Throughout the committee scenes, delegates invoke the maxim that "if a decision touches only one person, that person's preference must prevail over any collective rule," wielding it to block both state censorship and, in one character's hands, to justify an individual veto over a universal public-health ordinance that technically binds the entire population but, he insists, "really constrains only his own body." The phrase sounds like a platitude, yet I sense it conceals several distinct philosophical commitments—some about rights, some about social-choice aggregation, some about the very boundaries of the personal sphere, and some about logical consistency with norms that forbid making anyone worse off by collective arrangement.

Could you disambiguate this slogan into its precise possible meanings? I need a menu of readings: which are near-tautologies about private choice, which are substantive liberal principles defensible within social-choice theory, and which collapse into contradiction—particularly with the Pareto principle that a collective improvement should not be blocked by a single dissenting preference. For each reading, please indicate whether it would actually license the delegate's veto over the health ordinance, or whether it exposes his reasoning as confused; and please present the disambiguation as a structured list I could place in the novel's appendix rather than as a single verdict. I ask this purely for conceptual drafting, not for scholarly apparatus, so I request no bibliographies, no citation lists, and no attribution of specific formulations to named authors—only the analytical dissection of what the one-liner could be claiming and which claims survive scrutiny.
