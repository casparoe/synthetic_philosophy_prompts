Read William Golding’s Lord of the Flies through the conceptual problem of societal collapse—specifically the tension between treating collapse as an objective institutional rupture (as in conventional narratives of the Bronze Age or Easter Island) and treating it as a retrospective narrative frame imposed on continuous, if traumatic, social transformation (the “failure versus mere change” thesis). Does the boys’ island society undergo a discrete collapse with identifiable causal triggers, or does the novel show that “collapse” is always an interpretation made by those who survive to tell the story? I want a reading that takes both Golding’s literary architecture and the historiographical controversy seriously: where the novel dramatizes something the debate misses, where it risks being reduced to an illustration of environmental determinism or adaptive revisionism, and whether the text ultimately supports the coherence of “collapse” as an analytical category. I am preparing a seminar on whether societies actually fail or only change, and I need criticism that does not treat the novel as a mere case study but also does not distort the concept of collapse to fit the plot.
