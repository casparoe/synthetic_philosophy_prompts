In philosophy-of-science discussions I keep running into the line "all models are wrong, but some are useful," usually deployed to dismiss questions about whether one false theory can be closer to the truth than another. Given Popper's specific problem of verisimilitude—how, if at all, we can compare two false theories and rank them by nearness to the truth—please disambiguate that slogan into its possible precise readings: for instance, that predictive utility and truth-likeness are independent; that verisimilitude is formally unmeasurable; that approximate truth is incoherent; or that the only legitimate scientific aim is instrumental success. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, whether it actually addresses Popper's formal problem, whether it is defensible, and whether it supports the conclusion that talk of "truth-likeness" should be abandoned in model selection. Also say which reading is usually being invoked when someone uses the slogan to end an argument about scientific realism, and whether that reading conflates practical usefulness with proximity to truth in a way that makes the slogan do work it cannot actually do.
