Here is an argument I have been trying to formalize about the translation of tightly constrained lyric poetry, developed in reflection on the problems raised when a sixteenth-century French octosyllabic poem with an ABAB rhyme scheme and alternating masculine and feminine rhyme endings is rendered into English.

(1) If a feature of a poem is part of its aesthetic identity, then a translation that omits that feature is not a translation of the poem but a paraphrase or adaptation.
(2) In the poem in question, not only the rhyme scheme and the regular syllabic line, but also the gendered rhyme pattern and the syntactic inversion in the second line—where standard word order is reversed to serve both rhythm and rhyme—are structurally load-bearing rather than ornamental.
(3) English phonology and syntax cannot simultaneously reproduce all of these features together with the semantic content of the original: preserving natural English word order sacrifices the inversion; preserving rhyme usually requires altering syllable count or lexical choice; replicating gendered rhyme is impossible in a language without grammatical gender in rhyme.
(4) Therefore, any English rendering must omit at least one aesthetically identity-constituting feature.
(5) From (1) and (4), no English rendering is a translation of the original poem.
(6) Yet scholars and readers treat certain unrhymed, free-verse or prose English versions as translations, evaluating them as more or less successful.
(7) Hence, either our practice is conceptually confused, or premise (1) is false.

The argument seems to prove too much: it would make the translation of formally dense lyric poetry impossible in principle, undermining not only this case but the entire tradition of translating regulated verse across languages. Where is the weakest link? Is the error in (1), which conflates aesthetic identity with feature-identity? Is it in (2), which overstates the structural importance of the syntactic inversion? Or is there a hidden slide in (3)—perhaps English can approximate the inversion through archaism, and the apparent trichotomy is false?

If you reject (1) and allow that a translation can preserve aesthetic effect without replicating every formal feature, how do you prevent the criterion from collapsing into "anything goes"? Specifically, compare two hypothetical renderings: one that keeps the rhyme scheme and syllable count but modernizes the syntax entirely, and one that abandons rhyme but preserves the original's syntactic inversions and approximate syllable counts. Which is closer to being a translation? And what would be the decisive further case—perhaps a poem where rhyme carries the emotional weight versus one where syntax does—that would distinguish whether your revised criterion tracks formal replication or functional equivalence?
