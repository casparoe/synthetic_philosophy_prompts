For a graduate seminar on personal identity, I have been working through Hofstadter's claim that the self is essentially a pattern capable of persisting across brains rather than being confined to a single skull. This raises a difficulty I want to present without misrepresenting the view. If we accept that parts of my self can genuinely reside in other minds through shared memory and internalized patterns, then it seems that destroying my original brain becomes less harmful in direct proportion to how widely that pattern has been distributed—much as deleting one copy of a file matters less when many backups exist. Is this proportional-dilution intuition actually licensed by Hofstadter's account of distributed psychological persistence, or does the error lie deeper—in treating continuity across substrates as a form of fungible identity? I would like to identify precisely where the reasoning collapses before I assign the reading.
