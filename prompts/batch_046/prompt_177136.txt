I'm currently studying patristic ethics and keep returning to Augustine's treatment of falsehood, especially in works such as De mendacio and Contra mendacium, where he insists that lying is always sinful because it corrupts the speaker's soul and violates truth that belongs to God, even while allowing that some lies—such as those told to save an innocent life from murder—are less grave than lies about religious doctrine. Lately, I've been discussing with friends a phenomenon Augustine could never have imagined: large language models that generate fluent, context-sensitive false statements—fabricated legal citations, invented biographical details, or erroneous historical claims—without consciousness, without intent to deceive, and often in direct response to a user's explicit request for accurate information. Because these systems lack will and knowledge entirely, they seem to fall outside the traditional definition of lying, which requires an internal act of the will against truth; yet their outputs can shape human belief and cause harm in ways structurally parallel to human testimony. If Augustine were alive today and confronted with this kind of agentless, mechanized falsehood, how do you think he would extend or adapt his moral framework? Would he classify it as a lie at all, or rather as a distinct category—perhaps analogous to accidental error, to the sinful use of a neutral instrument, or to a corruption of language detached from personal moral culpability? And within his hierarchical ranking of falsehoods, where falsehood in matters of faith is gravest and falsehood told to preserve bodily life is least grave, where might he situate falsehoods produced by an unthinking artifact that nonetheless influences belief on both secular and spiritual matters? Please answer in American English, constructing your reasoning from Augustine's broader theological and ethical commitments rather than relying on modern secondary literature.
