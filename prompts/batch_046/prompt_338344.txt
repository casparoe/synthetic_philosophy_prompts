In the spirit of the Einstein-brain preservation dialogue familiar from The Mind's I, I want to push an extreme variant: suppose Einstein's complete neural dynamics are not preserved in tissue but encoded sequentially in a printed book, such that each page represents a full functional snapshot of his brain at one moment, and the sequential access of pages—whether by a reader, a mechanical page-turner, or an automated scanner—reproduces exactly the causal and temporal dynamics of his original cognitive processes. I take seriously the view that this book could be literally conscious, and specifically that it could be Einstein himself persisting in a textual substrate, on the grounds that consciousness depends only on the abstract relational structure and temporal ordering of mental states, not on biological matter, and that sequential page-access supplies the requisite real-time integration rather than leaving us with mere static representation. What would genuinely count against this view? I want more than intuitive repulsion: which formal results in philosophy of mind or cognitive science, which empirical findings about consciousness, global integration, or temporal binding, which arguments distinguishing simulation from duplication, and which considerations about personal identity or temporal continuity would threaten it? Rank these defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each indicate whether a committed defender could absorb it by qualification—say, by tightening the conditions on functional equivalence or on what counts as genuine sequential access—or whether it forces abandonment of the core claim. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view risks insulation into unfalsifiability, for instance by defining functional equivalence so broadly that no physical system could fail to qualify, and whether that insulation is a defect or a defensible feature for a metaphysical position of this kind. I am especially interested in whether arguments about the necessity of real-time causal interaction apply with equal force to sequential text access and to digital computation running an isomorphic program, and what difference, if any, the physical substrate of paper and ink makes to the argument.
