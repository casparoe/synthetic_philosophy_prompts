In the debate over the ontological argument—from Anselm’s classic formulation through Gödel’s modal reconstruction and its various formal analyses—several points are now well settled: the argument is valid in S5 modal logic if one grants the premises about properties that are positive in Gödel’s sense; the inference from possible existence to actual existence turns on treating existence as a second-order property of properties rather than as a first-order predicate of individuals; and the formal structure can be reproduced without explicit theological vocabulary if one substitutes appropriate modal axioms. What remains genuinely contested is whether Gödel’s axioms about positive properties are philosophically defensible independent of theism, whether they can be weakened without collapsing the proof, and whether the argument reveals anything about modal metaphysics that survives if one rejects its theological conclusion.

Consider now a constructed variant—call it the stability variant—that replaces Gödel’s value-laden notion of positive properties with a purely structural notion of modal stability. A property is stable just in case, for every world accessible from the actual world, if it is instantiated there then it is necessarily instantiated there. Suppose one derives from this, not that a maximally excellent being exists, but that if any being possesses maximal modal stability, then maximal stability is uniquely instantiated across all accessible worlds. This strips away explicit value judgments yet preserves the argument’s modal architecture, and it raises the further question of whether the ontological argument’s real philosophical interest lies in its theological conclusion or in the structural behavior of maximally stable properties under modal accessibility.
