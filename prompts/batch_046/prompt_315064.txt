I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about Russell's theory of descriptions. She kept falling back on the slogan that "descriptions don't fail to refer — they only come out false," using it first to dismiss my concern about "the present King of France is bald," and then to insist that there is no genuinely puzzling semantic phenomenon of "empty reference" at all: if nothing satisfies the description, the sentence is simply false, and that is the end of the philosophical problem. When I pressed her with a case like "the tallest mountain on Venus" — posed in a context where the speaker genuinely assumes Venus has mountains — she said the Russellian analysis applies unchanged: an unsatisfied existential quantifier, hence falsity, hence no mystery. I think she is sliding between several very different claims: a point about logical form, a metaphysical claim about the non-existence of unreal objects, a semantic claim that descriptions lack any direct reference relation, and perhaps the stronger descriptivist idea that all apparently singular terms are ultimately descriptions in disguise. Could you lay out the precise distinct theses this slogan might be expressing — separating the logical analysis of definite descriptions, the metaphysical denial of non-existent entities, the semantic claim that descriptions do not denote, and any further commitments about primary versus secondary occurrence — and for each indicate whether it is a platitude, a defensible Russellian position, an overstatement, or simply false? In particular, I need to know which of these readings actually license her dismissal of my Venus example as philosophically unproblematic, which ones smuggle in assumptions she has not defended, and whether any version of the view is forced to treat descriptions in secondary occurrence differently, with consequences for her argument.
