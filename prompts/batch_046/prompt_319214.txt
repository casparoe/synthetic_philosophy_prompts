Please argue that Weber's Protestant Ethic thesis survives the standard philosophical objection—that culture or values cannot serve as genuine causes of economic change—only if we reinterpret the thesis through the concept of elective affinity understood as a path-dependent locking mechanism rather than an efficient causal relation. Defend the view that religious rationalization and capitalist economic rationalization are not linked by values acting upon behavior in a Humean regularity fashion, but by a configurational fit in which each domain supplies the institutional and conceptual conditions for the stabilization of the other. Specifically, address why treating culture as a stabilizing mechanism rather than an independent variable avoids reducing values to epiphenomena without attributing to them mysterious causal powers; explain how this framing resolves the overdetermination problem raised by historical cases where similar material conditions failed to produce capitalist development; and clarify why the Humean model of causation is the wrong framework for assessing Weber's explanatory claims. The argument should remain conceptual and should not depend on bibliographic appeals or citation of obscure secondary literature.
