I am trying to reconstruct the implicit decision framework of a labor-ontology position I have been studying. The framework delivers verdicts on whether an activity counts as "work" in the morally and socially relevant sense—that is, as labor that grounds claims to compensation, institutional protection, and social recognition, rather than merely as effort or useful activity. Here is its track record across five earlier cases.

Case 1: A factory worker is retained after automation to monitor robotic error logs for eight hours daily at the same salary, with minimal physical exertion. Verdict: This is still work, but of a degraded kind; the institutional assignment and sustained attentional burden preserve its status, even though the task is thinner than the original manufacturing labor.

Case 2: A person spends forty hours per week training a personal AI assistant to manage their private household, with no employer, no contract, and no economic dependency on the activity. Verdict: Not work; it is self-directed automation that eliminates labor for private benefit, despite the time investment and skill involved.

Case 3: A platform employs people to review flagged content according to an algorithmic rubric that specifies removal criteria in near-exhaustive detail, leaving only marginal discretion for edge cases. The workers depend on the income and work assigned shifts. Verdict: Work, but the framework notes an internal tension—the institutional and dependency conditions are fully met, yet the near-total algorithmic specification threatens to dissolve the task's character as genuinely human labor.

Case 4: A retired engineer volunteers twenty hours per week maintaining open-source software used widely in industry, exercising high technical judgment, with no pay and no organizational assignment. Verdict: Not work in the primary sense; it is recognized as socially valuable labor, but it lacks the structural conditions—economic dependency and institutional role-embedding—that transform labor into work.

Case 5: A gig worker accepts delivery tasks through an app, can reject any individual task, but depends on the aggregated income for survival and works within the platform's algorithmic management system. Verdict: Work; the flexibility does not dissolve the status because the economic dependency and platform-mediated assignment create the relevant structural relation.

The workers are paid hourly, work in assigned shifts, depend on the income, and must exercise physical skill and safety awareness. However, they are explicitly told that their sole purpose is to teach machines to replace them, and every movement is predetermined by the data-collection protocol rather than by operational necessity.

What should the framework decide about whether this is work, if it wants to be consistent with itself?
