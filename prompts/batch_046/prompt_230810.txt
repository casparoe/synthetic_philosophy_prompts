I've been trying to apply the outside-view heuristic systematically to philosophical reasoning, and I keep running into what seems like a structural problem. In domains like project planning or political forecasting, the outside view works because we have reference-class distributions—how often do similar projects overrun, how often do experts in this category predict correctly. But for a specific philosophical argument—say, my judgment that a particular formulation of moral anti-realism is internally incoherent—there doesn't seem to be any well-defined reference class of past philosophers who made structurally analogous claims and then turned out to be right or wrong. Given that epistemic modesty is usually presented as a demand to privilege the outside view over the inside view whenever they conflict, and given that philosophy seems to lack the statistical base rates that make the outside view operational, doesn't consistency require that I treat my carefully worked-out philosophical convictions as having roughly the same credence as the average opinion in the corresponding reference class—effectively a kind of uniform agnosticism? Or is there a principled way to construct an outside view for philosophy that doesn't either collapse into blind deference to disciplinary consensus or illegitimately invoke my own reasoning as a unique exception? What I'm really asking is whether epistemic modesty, taken seriously, undermines the rationality of having strong philosophical beliefs at all when the feedback loops are this weak.
