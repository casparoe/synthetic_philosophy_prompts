I just walked away from a three-hour argument with a friend about whether anyone genuinely deserves moral blame, and I came out convinced that Strawson’s framework of reactive attitudes is the only one that holds up—yet I have no idea how to actually use it under pressure. I need you to turn that framework into an operational tool.

I am a mediator in a municipal restorative-justice diversion program. I run about forty intakes a month, and I have roughly twenty minutes per case to decide whether to route the parties into a reactive-attitude pathway—where victim and offender are expected to engage with ordinary interpersonal moral emotions like resentment, guilt, forgiveness, and moral address—or into an objective-attitude pathway, where the offender is treated as a system or condition to be managed, repaired, or isolated, and the ordinary demands of the moral community are suspended. The decision determines the room they enter, the forms I complete, and whether my co-facilitator signs off. I am not a lawyer or a philosopher; I need a reusable checklist or ordered flowchart in words that someone without theoretical training can run in real time.

Preserve the conceptual core: distinguish participant reactive attitudes directed at the offender (resentment, gratitude, forgiveness) from self-reactive attitudes (guilt, shame, remorse); maintain that reactive attitudes presuppose the offender is a member of the moral community whose quality of will can be addressed; and maintain that the objective attitude suspends those ordinary interpersonal demands without necessarily denying causal responsibility or excusing the harm. Do not reduce this to a risk-assessment rubric or a personality screen; keep Strawson’s point that the issue is the appropriateness of a stance, not predictive utility.

The procedure must include explicit ordered tests, the conditions required to remain in the reactive pathway, and the precise triggers that force a shift to the objective pathway. It must handle these hard cases: (1) the offender has a documented cognitive or developmental condition that significantly limits moral understanding; (2) the harm arose from severe structural coercion or duress rather than freely chosen ill will; (3) the victim insists on an objective, systemic framing and refuses to treat the offender as a moral interlocutor; (4) the offender expresses remorse, but the mediator suspects it is strategic or performative; and (5) the harm was caused by a small collective agent—specifically, a three-person partnership acting in concert—rather than a single individual, so the mediator must decide whether reactive attitudes can be directed at the group as a unit or must be decomposed into individual targets. At every step where judgment cannot be eliminated, mark it clearly as a judgment call, specify what kind of error it risks, and note whose emotional calibration is being relied on.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) a first-time offender who negligently damaged a neighbor’s property and expresses immediate, uncoerced regret; (b) an offender with a documented intellectual disability who intentionally but with limited comprehension caused a minor assault; and (c) the collective partnership case, where the partners deliberately misled a customer and now offer a joint apology. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer you think I will dislike—explain whether that is a failure of the procedure or a feature of the framework itself.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which cases it should refuse, where it will misfire, and what it assumes about the mediator’s own reactive attitudes. Write the response in American English, in a formal and polished register. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I do not have web access, and I need something I can internalize and apply directly.
