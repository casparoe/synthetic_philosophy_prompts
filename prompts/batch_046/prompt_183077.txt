I have been following arguments about consciousness across non-human animals, and I keep noticing a methodological pattern for which I lack a term. Investigators often propose a cluster of behavioral and neural markers—flexible learning, sleep-like states, integrated sensory processing—to identify consciousness across species. When applying this cluster to mammals, missing or variant markers are treated as acceptable adjustments: cetacean sleep differs structurally from terrestrial mammalian REM, yet the criterion is relaxed rather than counted as a failure. By contrast, when the same cluster is applied to invertebrates, the presence of several markers is frequently dismissed as insufficient unless they appear in forms structurally analogous to mammalian ones, and any deviation is interpreted against consciousness rather than accommodated. The result is that the evidential standard becomes effectively unfalsifiable for the favored taxonomic group and hyper-strict for the other, with the adjustments built into case-by-case interpretation rather than applied uniformly. Is there an established concept for this kind of asymmetrical flexibility in comparative evidential standards? How should it be distinguished from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis adjustment, from mere confirmation bias, and from straightforward taxonomic prejudice? If no precise term exists, what would be the right boundaries to draw around the phenomenon so that it does not collapse into general theoretical conservatism?
