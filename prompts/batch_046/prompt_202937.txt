A colleague recently summarized the socialist calculation debate for me in the following terms, and I am unsure how much to trust the framing. Ludwig von Mises, she said, argued that rational economic calculation is impossible under socialism because the abolition of private property in capital goods eliminates market prices; without them, planners can only perform calculation in kind—adding up physical inputs and outputs—which collapses once production involves heterogeneous capital goods and complex supply chains. Oskar Lange and Abba Lerner then answered with a market-socialist model in which a central board sets prices administratively, state-owned firms follow two rules—produce where marginal cost equals price, and adjust prices until they equal marginal utility—and through iterative trial and error the system converges on the same allocation a competitive market would reach. She then described Friedrich Hayek’s reply as essentially a computation objection: even with Lange’s rules, no central body can gather and process all the local, tacit knowledge dispersed among millions of economic actors, though she added that this objection has been largely neutralized by modern distributed algorithms and big-data optimization, which can now approximate market discovery faster and more accurately than price signals. From this she concluded that Lange’s framework, updated with current technology, shows the calculation debate was settled in socialism’s favor, and that Hayek’s knowledge problem was a temporary technological limitation rather than a structural or categorical failure.

I suspect this account flattens important distinctions—particularly between economic calculation and mere computation, between the producer rules and consumer equilibrium conditions in Lange’s model, and between Hayek’s concern with tacit, contextual knowledge and a generic information-processing bottleneck—but I do not know enough to identify every distortion precisely.
