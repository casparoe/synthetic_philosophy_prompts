As a novelist working through a narrative about nested realities and the dissolution of fixed identity, I am trying to grasp a conceptual tension in Madhyamaka philosophy that popular accounts rarely clarify. Nāgārjuna's tetralemma negates four extreme positions—existence, non-existence, both, and neither—regarding any dependently originated phenomenon, yet he also maintains that emptiness, śūnyatā, is not itself a substantive view but the absence of inherent nature, svabhāva. The problem arises when one applies the fourfold negation reflexively: if emptiness is also dependently originated, then saying that emptiness is empty appears necessary to avoid reifying absence, but this sounds structurally identical to applying the tetralemma's fourth corner to emptiness itself. Please explain the conceptual difference—if Madhyamaka draws one—between treating emptiness-is-empty as a second-order corrective and treating it as a direct instance of neither existence nor non-existence. In particular, how does this distinction prevent the collapse of the middle way into either nihilism or a hidden essentialism about non-being, and why does it matter for understanding dependent origination, pratītyasamutpāda, as conventionally valid rather than ultimately void?
