I serve on a university research-integrity panel that reviews allegations of scientific misconduct arising not from direct observation but from derived inferences — for instance, that an investigator knew results were unreliable because they possessed raw measurements containing statistical anomalies, or that they knew a protocol was violated because they knew equipment was miscalibrated. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in Nozick’s tracking conditions for knowledge and in the failure of epistemic closure that tracking generates, that my panel can run in a single ninety-minute session without philosophical training. The procedure must determine, for any case, whether the investigator tracks the base fact, whether they track the entailment linking that fact to the charge, and whether knowledge of the base legitimately extends to the derived claim, with explicit marking of where tracking ruptures and where judgment, not epistemology, must decide. Deliver the procedure as an ordered checklist in words: the questions asked in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer for tracking the base, tracking the entailment, and tracking the derived claim; the counterfactual adjacency check for nearby worlds in which the derived claim is false but the base fact still holds; the exact stopping point that yields a negative finding; and the hand-off point to normative judgment. Then apply it to three cases. Case A: an investigator who knew raw spectroscopy values exceeded a documented toxicity threshold and is charged with knowing the compound was misidentified. Case B: an investigator who knew a control sample was stored at incorrect temperature and is charged with knowing the experimental results were therefore invalid. Case C: a case in which the base fact is tracked, the logical entailment is sound, yet the adjacency check shows that in nearby worlds the derived charge fails — producing an exonerating result that conflicts with intuitive blame. For each case, report the procedure’s output, flag the step where human judgment must override the formal result, and state clearly what the procedure cannot do. The deliverable must be signed off by the panel chair and must distinguish a finding of not-tracked from a finding of not-responsible.
