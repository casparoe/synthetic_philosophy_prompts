At a seminar on rational belief, two participants—Maya and Leo—disagreed about the scope and force of Dutch book arguments. Maya argued that the classic betting framework proves rational agents must hold precise numerical credences: if one only has comparative judgments, such as that hypothesis A is more likely than B without assigning any specific numbers, a bookie can exploit intransitivities or framing effects across a sequence of bets to guarantee a loss, so comparative belief alone is incoherent. Leo countered that this conflates the absence of numbers with incoherence itself: comparative belief systems can satisfy qualitative coherence constraints—transitivity, monotonicity, and closure under logical consequence—that block Dutch books without requiring arbitrary precision, and that in domains like climate forecasting or historical explanation, forcing agents into point probabilities misrepresents the actual epistemic situation. Maya replied that without numerical prices, there is no determinate way to set fair betting odds, so any purportedly qualitative system either smuggles in implicit quantitative commitments or remains vulnerable to a more sophisticated multi-stage book. Leo then noted that even quantitative Dutch books depend on a linear utility assumption that fails in real decision contexts, so both sides face a problem of idealization rather than one side facing a unique vulnerability.
