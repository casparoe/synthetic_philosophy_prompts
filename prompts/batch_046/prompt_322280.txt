I serve as a senior analyst in a national infrastructure planning office, and I must evaluate long-horizon public investments—deep geological repositories for nuclear waste, century-scale carbon sequestration contracts, and pandemic preparedness stockpiles—whose costs and benefits extend well beyond the lives of any current voter. The theoretical debate I am navigating separates pure time preference, the idea that welfare should be discounted merely because it occurs in the future, from discounting grounded in economic growth or uncertainty; philosophers and climate economists defend positions ranging from a near-zero pure rate to one justified by psychological distance or diminishing moral urgency across generations. I need this translated into a reusable, forty-five-minute decision procedure I can apply repeatedly without returning to first principles each time: a sequential checklist or flowchart in words that distinguishes empirical inputs from normative judgments, specifies the order in which they should be applied, and marks precisely where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be signed off personally.

The procedure must include a generational boundary condition for projects whose benefits fall almost entirely on people not yet born: rather than aggregating current preferences, I must apply a proxy-representation test, in which I treat present stakeholder preferences as standing in for distant future interests and explicitly document the normative load that substitution carries. Apply the procedure to three worked cases—(a) a nuclear waste repository with immediate costs and risk profiles peaking in three centuries; (b) a reforestation bond whose climate benefits are measured around 2100; and (c) a pandemic reserve where the primary benefit is averting a low-probability, high-impact outbreak in fifty years—ensuring that in at least one case the procedure produces a recommendation I will find counterintuitive, such as a high pure rate rendering the reserve unjustifiable despite its apparent urgency. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: for example, whether it resolves the conflict between a rights-based framework that rejects pure discounting altogether and a utilitarian framework that permits it, or whether it merely structures the disagreement in a form that oversight boards can adjudicate.
