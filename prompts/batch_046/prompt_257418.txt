At a philosophy-of-psychiatry reading group, two members—call them Leo and Maya—got stuck in a disagreement about Thomas Szasz’s claim that mental illness is largely a myth. Leo spoke first. (1) He said Szasz is essentially correct for conditions like major depression and generalized anxiety, because these are defined entirely by subjective reports and behavioral criteria with no necessary pathophysiological marker; calling them illnesses is a category mistake that medicalizes problems in living. Maya replied. (2) She said Leo is conflating Szasz’s rhetorical target with his actual argument: Szasz never denied that people suffer, but objected to framing suffering within the medical model of bodily disease. She noted that Szasz himself allowed exceptions for conditions with demonstrable brain pathology, and that finding biological correlates for depression or anxiety does not erase that conceptual distinction—it only shows that distress affects the body. Leo came back. (3) He said Maya is saving Szasz by making his thesis unfalsifiable: if every biological finding is dismissed as not the right kind, then no evidence could ever refute it. He pointed to complex PTSD, now associated with measurable alterations in stress-related gene expression, and argued that if there are biological mechanisms and drugs that modify symptoms, the myth claim collapses—such conditions are functioning exactly like medical categories. Maya answered. (4) She said Leo is committing the very reification Szasz warned against. The presence of gene-expression changes does not show complex PTSD is a disease entity like tuberculosis; it shows severe adversity leaves biological traces, which is unsurprising. The myth, she said, lies in treating the diagnostic label as naming a natural kind with a unified etiology, when it is really a heterogeneous cluster of trauma responses shaped by culture and personal history. Pharmacological efficacy only proves that altering brain chemistry can change experience, not that the experience was originally a chemical deficit.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both are missing—whether a distinction Szasz actually drew, a feature of modern psychiatric taxonomy, or a conceptual move that would change the dispute.
