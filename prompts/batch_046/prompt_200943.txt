In my graduate seminar on the ethics of armed conflict, we have spent the better part of the term circling the same fault line in the literature on the moral equality of combatants. The traditionalist camp, following the Walzerian separation of jus ad bellum and jus in bello, maintains that combatants on both sides of a just and an unjust war possess symmetrical permissions to kill, provided they observe the laws of war; the revisionist camp, developing liability-based accounts, denies this symmetry and argues that unjust combatants lack moral permission to kill because they are not liable to defensive harm. Every standard move is now familiar—objections about collective responsibility, the epistemic opacity of war, the risk of moral hazard if soldiers must judge the justice of their cause, and counter-objections about individual moral agency—yet neither side concedes an inch, and recent papers tend to rehearse the same dialectic with updated examples. I am increasingly convinced that the stalemate is not a failure of argumentation but a structural feature of the disagreement itself, and I want a diagnosis rather than another adjudication.

Specifically, I wonder whether the dispute is partly verbal—hinging on whether moral equality denotes a distributive property of agents, a relational property of rights-holders within a shared institutional framework, or merely a pragmatic convention embedded in international humanitarian law—while partly substantive, turning on an unacknowledged disagreement about what counts as an excusing condition under conditions of radical epistemic deprivation. There may also be a hidden formal question: both sides seem to agree that liability to defensive killing requires wrongful threat-posing, but they appear to be using different models of collective action and different thresholds for reasonable belief about justice, such that they are answering different questions—one about individual culpability, the other about systemic moral risk—under the single heading of combatant equality. Before I restructure the syllabus around this suspicion, examine what each side actually says about liability, epistemic excuse, and institutional role, and tell me whether this is a bedrock clash of intuitions about collective guilt and naturalness, a concealed empirical disagreement about the psychology of soldiers, or a case of two research programs answering different questions with a shared vocabulary. If your diagnosis points to a verbal or framework-level mismatch, what conceptual distinction—perhaps one drawn from institutional ethics or epistemology rather than from standard just-war taxonomy—would be required to make progress visible? If instead the disagreement is substantive but intractable because it rests on incompatible starting points about the priority of agent-centered versus system-centered evaluation, is there any result—conceptual, empirical, or formal—that could genuinely move it, or should we treat the impasse as permanent and pedagogically productive rather than resolvable?
