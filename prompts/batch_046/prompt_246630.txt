At a study group last week, a colleague and I argued about Black’s thought experiment—two identical spheres in an otherwise empty, perfectly symmetric universe—and whether it refutes the principle that identical properties imply identical entities. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I kept framing things in terms of distributed nodes, state equivalence, and hidden identifiers; neither of us was convinced. Here is the exchange, step by step.

(1) He said the spheres are clearly two objects, not one, because they occupy different locations; but since the universe is symmetric, every relational property one has—such as being ten miles from the other—is shared identically. So there is numerical difference without qualitative difference, which directly violates the identity of indiscernibles.

(2) I replied that in software, if two cluster nodes have exactly the same observable state and the same symmetric relations to everything else, we treat them as interchangeable instances of one logical entity; unless an observable difference exists, positing “two” is redundant. So the spheres might just be one sphere described from two isomorphic coordinate frames, and the principle survives if we reject hidden indices.

(3) He countered that physics treats them as distinct—each can be moved independently, and quantum mechanics requires particles in symmetric states to remain numerically distinct (as with Pauli exclusion). So identity cannot be reduced to observable properties alone; there must be a primitive “thisness” that grounds which sphere is which, even when all descriptions match.

(4) I said that invoking an unobservable primitive is methodologically suspicious—it is like assigning UUIDs to nodes that never affect system behavior. If “which one” has no empirical consequence, then asking about it is like worrying about a hash collision that never changes output; the distinction is conventional, not metaphysical. The principle should therefore be kept as a constraint: no difference in properties means no difference in entities.

(5) He argued that my analogy breaks down because physical objects have spatiotemporal continuity—each sphere traces a continuous world-line through time, and those trajectories do not overlap. Even in perfect symmetry, the histories are distinct paths, so there is a qualitative, diachronic property that distinguishes them: being on path A versus path B.

(6) I conceded the world-line point but noted that labeling paths “A” and “B” presupposes the very distinction we are trying to justify. Without an external reference frame, the paths are isomorphic; naming them requires an arbitrary coordinate choice, just like labeling nodes in a symmetric cluster. So the diachronic distinction seems to smuggle in the identity it claims to establish.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then say which specific points each of us actually got right, what we are both missing—particularly regarding whether relational properties in symmetric spaces can do real distinguishing work, and whether a software-inspired “no hidden primitives” constraint is appropriate for metaphysics rather than merely for engineering practice.
