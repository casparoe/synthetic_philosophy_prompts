I am writing the final exam for an advanced philosophy of mind seminar—graduate students and senior undergraduates, three-hour sitting. I need one essay question on self-deception that students cannot answer by reciting the classic paradox—namely, that an agent must simultaneously know and not know the same proposition—and then listing familiar responses such as partitioned minds or motivated reasoning. Instead, the question should require them to adjudicate whether self-deception survives once we distinguish two forms precisely: doxastic self-deception, in which an agent induces in herself a false belief about her own motives or about some state of affairs, and attentional self-deception, in which she maintains a stable pattern of selective attention, evidential neglect, and strategic ambiguity without ever forming the contradictory belief, keeping the threatening content at the periphery of awareness rather than denying it. Students should be asked whether attentional self-deception counts as genuine self-deception or collapses into weakness of will, strategic ignorance, or disciplined self-management; and whether jettisoning the doxastic model eliminates the phenomenon or merely relocates it into other, better-understood failures of agency. Provide a model answer of about 700 words that takes a clear position and addresses at least one serious objection. Then supply a marking scheme organized around five must-hit analytical moves, totaling 25 marks, with each move assigned a specific point value, a one-line note on what an excellent answer does with it, and a one-line note on the specific misconception or omission that should cost marks. The prompt must not request bibliographies, citation lists, or named-author references; answers should be constructible from conceptual resources alone.
