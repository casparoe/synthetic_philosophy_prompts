Consider the municipal policy of "graded de-commemoration" adopted in some cities for street names and monuments. Under it, figures associated with systemic harm—say, administering an extractive colonial bureaucracy—but not with direct personal violence retain their names in official archives, receive contextual historical plaques, and keep their streets named; figures tied to direct, intentional violence are fully renamed with no marker left. What philosophical commitments about moral responsibility, collective memory, and the distinction between honor and mere reference does this practice presuppose? Must it assume that structural complicity is less blameworthy, or only that it is less fitting for symbolic erasure? Is the separation of archival retention from public honor coherent, or does keeping the name in records still confer a form of recognition? And if the underlying view were replaced by one that judges commemorative names solely by present-day harm or social utility, would the graded distinction collapse, or could it be reconstructed on grounds of tractability or historical accuracy?
