I am working on a feature about the ethics of large-scale insect farming and the moral mathematics behind what philosophers call the scale argument. The core claim, as you may know, is that even if the prior probability of sentience in any given insect is extremely low—orders of magnitude below that of a pig or a chicken—the sheer biomass of farmed invertebrates, now reaching into the tens of trillions annually, could aggregate into an expected moral burden comparable to or exceeding conventional animal agriculture. In my reporting, I have encountered a persistent analogy used by both advocates and skeptics: treating the moral decision about insects like an actuary’s problem of underwriting catastrophic risk across an enormous pool of independent policies. Proponents say we should pay the premium of precautionary welfare regulations because the tail risk—vast suffering multiplied by vast numbers—is too severe to ignore; skeptics reply that this is like insisting we must purchase a lottery ticket for every grain of sand on the beach, since some grains, under some bizarre rule, might be winners, and that the analogy smuggles in linear aggregation where none is justified.

Could you help me audit how far this underwriting analogy actually holds? I was discussing this with a colleague who covers agricultural policy, and we realized the comparison might collapse once we map the elements precisely. I would like an explicit correspondence table identifying what plays the role of the premium, the payout, the policyholder, the risk pool, the independence conditions, and the catastrophic tail-event threshold—then extending that mapping to include how phylogenetic distance might function as a discount rate on the prior, and whether colony-level cognition introduces correlation structures that violate the independence assumption built into standard risk pooling. After the table, please separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the pro-precaution conclusion from those that are merely decorative, and tell me whether the normative recommendation—radically curtailing insect farming—survives once the analogy is dismantled, or whether it was carrying the entire argumentative load all along. I need this for my article’s conceptual framework, so please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested only in the structural comparison, the points of breakdown, and whatever normative residue remains.
