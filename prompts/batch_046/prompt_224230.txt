I'm working out my own view on when it is rational to bind one's future self, and I'm hoping to see whether my intuitions across cases cohere into a principle or expose a real tension. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1 — The perfect practical binder: You are stranded in a remote area. A driver will take you to safety only if you can guarantee payment upon arrival, but once arrived you will have cash in hand and no further reason to pay. You can activate an escrow mechanism that automatically transfers the funds when you reach the destination; once activated, you cannot stop the transfer without destroying your own access to the funds permanently. I think it is rational to activate it, and rational — at the moment of arrival — to let the transfer happen rather than try to sabotage it.

Case 2 — The risky binder: Same scenario, but the escrow mechanism has a flaw: if you attempt to sabotage it upon arrival, there is a non-trivial chance (say, 5%) that you will also trigger a self-destruct sequence that wipes out all your assets. I still think it is rational to activate it and rational not to sabotage it, provided the value of rescue is high enough relative to the risk. But I think there is some risk threshold — perhaps 50% — at which it becomes irrational to activate in the first place, even though the logic is identical.

Case 3 — The epistemic binder: You can take a one-time drug that permanently removes your ability to update a specific belief — say, that a particular policy is correct — regardless of future evidence. Doing so makes you a more credible and effective advocate for that policy, because others know you cannot be talked out of it. I think it is irrational to take the drug, even if the advocacy benefit is enormous and the belief is currently well-supported.

Case 4 — The revocable binder with equal cost: You can enter a contract that binds you to complete a project, but you can exit at any time by paying a penalty that exactly equals the benefit you would gain from exiting. I think this is not a genuine commitment at all, and it is not rational to enter it for the sake of self-binding; it is rational to enter only for the direct contractual benefits, if any.

Case 5 — The conditional collective binder: You and nine others can each lock in a contribution to a public good, but the lock only executes if at least eight others also lock in. You can observe how many have locked in before you decide. I think it is rational to lock in once you see that eight others have done so. But if the mechanism requires you to lock in blindly, without observing the others, then I think it is irrational to be the first to lock in, even though the collective outcome would be best if all ten did.

Is there a single principle of rational commitment that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should be overturned, and why? In particular, can the principle distinguish practical from epistemic binding (Cases 1–2 vs. 3) without ad hoc stipulation, and can it explain why the risk threshold in Case 2 matters for the initial decision but not for the later decision not to sabotage? Also, what does the principle predict for the case I keep returning to: a scenario in which you can bind your future self not to an action but to a preference-order — for instance, a chip that makes you permanently value fairness over self-interest, even in one-off interactions — and should you install it?
