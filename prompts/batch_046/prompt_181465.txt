At a symposium on the ethics of historical dramatization that I covered earlier this year, a documentary filmmaker and a philosopher of history found themselves in a sustained disagreement over what dramatists owe to real people who can no longer speak for themselves. I have reconstructed their exchange from my notes. (1) The filmmaker argued that portraying a deceased figure’s interior life—inventing dialogue, attributing private emotions, or staging unrecorded encounters—violates a form of posthumous narrative autonomy. Since the dead cannot consent to how their consciousness is rendered, he said, such invention amounts to an unauthorized appropriation of identity, comparable to publishing private correspondence without permission except that the dead have no legal recourse. (2) The philosopher replied that the dead possess no interests that can be harmed, so framing the issue as a violation of autonomy is a category error; our obligations run to living audiences and to the integrity of historical understanding, not to a subject who no longer exists. She added that biography itself is irreducibly selective—emphasizing some events, omitting others, constructing causal chains—so the filmmaker’s objection, if taken seriously, would condemn all historical representation rather than only dramatic fiction. (3) The filmmaker conceded that selection is unavoidable but insisted it differs categorically from fabrication: a biographer may infer a mental state from archival evidence, whereas a screenwriter creates it ex nihilo. He proposed what he called a “threshold of fabrication”—if a work invents more than a minimal proportion of scenes involving unverified dialogue or interiority, it crosses from interpretation into fiction and forfeits any ethical claim to represent an actual life. (4) The philosopher countered that any quantitative threshold is arbitrary; what matters is whether invention illuminates or distorts. She observed that even rigorously factual biographies employ narrative conventions—irony, closure, dramatic irony—that shape a subject’s meaning in ways no document authorizes. By the filmmaker’s logic, she suggested, any compelling biography would be ethically suspect. (5) The filmmaker responded that narrative structure is indeed inevitable, but attributing specific words or feelings to a particular person is a distinct kind of trespass—what he termed “epistemic trespass”—because it presents speculative psychology as if it were witnessed reality. The philosopher closed by asking whether he would apply the same standard to historical novels that clearly signal their fictionality, and whether his deeper concern was actually deception of the audience rather than harm to the dead.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves above, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly sound, or requires significant qualification, with brief justification for each rating. Then identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether there is a defensible middle ground between narrative license and posthumous duty, and whether the distinction between deception and representation actually resolves the tension they have mapped out.
