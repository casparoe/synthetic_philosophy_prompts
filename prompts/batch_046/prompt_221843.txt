I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a friend about Quine's indeterminacy of translation—specifically whether the "gavagai" scenario reveals a genuine metaphysical underdetermination of meaning or only an epistemic gap in the field linguist's evidence. To settle my thoughts, write a villanelle—nineteen lines, two refrains, the standard rhyme scheme—about radical translation. The two refrains must repeat verbatim, but each recurrence should carry a different ontological loading: first as a naive realist report ("there runs a rabbit"), then as an ontologically neutral behavioral event ("there is a rabbit-stage"), and finally as a phrase that collapses the distinction entirely. Use the five tercets to stage the encounter—pointing, native assent, the drafting of the manual—while keeping meter and rhyme rigorous enough that the form feels deliberate, not decorative. In the closing quatrain, do not resolve which translation manual is correct; instead, show that no further behavioral fact could force the choice between them. It should be accurate enough that someone familiar with Quine's argument recognizes the philosophical structure, but rendered so the poem still works for a reader who has never encountered "gavagai."
