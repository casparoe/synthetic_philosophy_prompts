I spent much of last evening arguing with a close friend about Quine’s treatment of moral values—not the usual caricature of him as a flat-footed moral anti-realist, but the more delicate question of where moral judgments sit in his web of belief. We kept circling a problem that only became sharp after Quine’s death: the evolutionary debunking challenge, which treats our core normative intuitions as adaptive byproducts rather than as responses to any independent moral reality, and which presses the distinction between the causal origins of a belief and its epistemic justification. If Quine had confronted this argument—especially in the form that asks whether beliefs formed under evolutionary pressure can still claim central, protected status in the web—how exactly would his holism have responded? Would he classify moral commitments as peripheral nodes, easily revised under empirical pressure from evolutionary biology and cognitive science, or as central nodes protected by extensive interconnections with logic, mathematics, and everyday predictive success? And crucially, given his rejection of the analytic-synthetic distinction, could he block the debunking move by denying any sharp boundary between factual evolutionary claims and normative moral ones, thereby absorbing the debunking argument into the web without letting it topple the moral nodes? I want a reconstructed position, not a biography or a reading list: trace the internal mechanics of his holism as they apply to this specific post-2000 challenge, say whether the result strengthens his naturalism or collapses into an unintended moral skepticism, and be explicit about which parts of the web would have to shift if moral values were demoted to the periphery. Avoid generic summaries; I am interested in the architectural details of his view.
