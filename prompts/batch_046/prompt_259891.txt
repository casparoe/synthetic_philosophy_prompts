I've come around to this read of Kierkegaard's Abraham: the teleological suspension isn't some special religious exception but reveals ethics is always a default setting that deeper commitments can reorder. Think parental partiality cranked to max volume under direct divine address. The ethical doesn't vanish; it lingers like a phantom limb, and that same structure shows up in ordinary moral life too. So Abraham isn't breaking the rules, he's just exposing that they were never absolute. What would count against this? I want defeaters sorted from fatal to awkward: is there something about Abraham's silence and the knife that can't be analagized to secular partiality? Does the phantom-limb model miss that in religious suspension the ethical is actively contradicted rather than just outweighed? And if this collapses the religious/ethical distinction Kierkegaard insisted on, is that a real problem or just a qualification? Be honest about whether defenders could insulate it into unfalsifiability.
