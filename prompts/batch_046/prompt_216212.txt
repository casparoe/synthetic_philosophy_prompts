Read Tom Tykwer's Run Lola Run through the lens of the debate between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation. The film runs the same twenty-minute crisis three times, each diverging by a minute contingency — a dog on the stairs, a bank clerk's refusal, a missed call — and each yielding a radically different ending. Treat these iterations as a philosophical experiment rather than a narrative gimmick: do they map onto Lewisian closest-possible-worlds, where we hold fixed the past and laws and vary only the minimal difference, or onto Pearl-style structural interventions, where an agent breaks specific edges in a single causal model? Where does the film's grammar of repetition with variation — especially the sense that Lola adjusts her path across runs — align with one framework and resist the other? Does it treat Lola as an intervening agent who can manipulate variables, or as a node in a deterministic network that merely reveals different counterfactual branches? And when the film suggests that tiny causal differences cascade into moral catastrophe or redemption, does it illuminate the phenomenology of urgency in a way that formal causation theory misses, or does it trade on a confusion between epistemic unpredictability and ontological indeterminacy? I am using this in a philosophy of causation seminar and want the reading to stay honest: where the film genuinely complicates the distinction, and where it collapses into mere metaphor.
