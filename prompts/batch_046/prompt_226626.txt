Analyze the UK Supreme Court's September 2019 Miller II judgment—the unanimous ruling that Prime Minister Boris Johnson's five-week prorogation of Parliament was unlawful and void—through Jeremy Waldron's democratic-objection framework, which holds that judicial review is illegitimate when unelected judges replace contested legislative judgments with their own. First, apply the framework precisely: what did the Court face, and by Waldron's lights should it have deferred to the Prime Minister's judgment about parliamentary sitting, struck down the prorogation, or taken some intermediate position? Second, examine whether the case exposes a gap in Waldron's critique—specifically, the difference between substitution review, where courts dictate substantive policy outcomes, and institutional-protection review, where courts merely restore the deliberative forum—asking whether Miller II belongs to the second category and, if so, whether Waldron's core case ever anticipated it. Third, identify which features of the episode the framework explains well, such as why public criticism focused on executive overreach rather than judicial activism, and which it misdescribes or ignores, such as the role of an unwritten constitution in weakening or strengthening the democratic objection. Finally, say whether Miller II functions as a confirming instance, an anomaly, or a boundary case that reveals the limits of applying Waldron's argument to structural interventions.
