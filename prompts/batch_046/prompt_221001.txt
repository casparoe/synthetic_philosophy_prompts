You are a senior philosopher specializing in George Berkeley's idealism and contemporary philosophy of mind. Before answering, please take a moment to brainstorm the precise logical structure of Berkeley's master argument as presented in the Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous, paying particular attention to how the conceivability criterion is meant to entail impossibility for objects existing unperceived. I want to press a worry that combines this with recent work on phenomenal binding. It seems to me that if phenomenal binding is the process by which sensory qualities are unified into a single experience without requiring an independent substrate, then Berkeley's argument may only apply to objects that are in principle available for such binding—that is, to 'bound' phenomenal contents. This leaves conceptual space for what I will call 'unbound phenomenal potentials': material substrates that exist permanently outside any possible binding relation and therefore never become objects of conception. On this view, Berkeley has shown only that any substance we can conceive is mind-dependent, not that substrate-independent matter is impossible in itself; a robust realist could simply retreat to the claim that real matter is precisely that which is permanently unbound. Where does this line of reasoning fail? Specifically, does Berkeley's principle that to conceive of X is always to conceive of X being perceived already rule out the very coherence of distinguishing bound contents from permanently unbound potentials?
