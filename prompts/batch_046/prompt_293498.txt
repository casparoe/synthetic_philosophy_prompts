I was discussing the procreation asymmetry with two friends from an online rationalist reading group, and we ended up in a disagreement that none of us could resolve cleanly. Jordan defended what he called a decision-bound version of the asymmetry: he granted that there is a strong reason not to create a suffering person, but denied any parallel reason to create a happy one, not because nonexistent people metaphysically lack standing, but because moral reasons must be anchored to actual decision pathways. Once a specific reproductive pathway is open—say, you have already begun an IVF cycle with a particular embryo—then you do have agent-relative reasons to improve that potential person's welfare; but prior to entering any pathway there is no identifiable beneficiary to ground a reason for conception. To illustrate, he proposed a case in which a couple can conceive now with a small risk of serious disability, or wait six months for a near-guaranteed healthy conception; he said they have decisive reason to avoid the risk of suffering, yet no reason whatsoever to prefer the later, happier child over remaining childless.

Riley pushed back in four moves. First, she argued that Jordan's framing confuses moral ontology with epistemic accessibility: we can identify suffering people but not "missing happy people," so the asymmetry in our reasons reflects only our ignorance, not a real moral difference. Second, she said that if Jordan is right, then flipping a coin to decide whether to conceive should alter the moral landscape—before the flip there is no reason to conceive, after heads there suddenly is—which makes moral reasons implausibly contingent on random events. Third, Jordan replied that this misunderstands agent-relativity: the coin does not change who exists, but it changes which option set the agent actually faces, and reasons are indexed to open options rather than to metaphysically fixed populations. Fourth, Riley conceded that option-indexing avoids the randomness objection, but then pressed a different problem: Jordan's pipeline distinction seems to collapse into a general principle that any open option creates obligations to optimize within it, which would mean that merely buying a lottery ticket generates reasons to ensure the ticket-holder is happy, an absurd extension. Jordan's final response was that reproductive pathways are special because they involve bringing a specific, identifiable welfare-subject into being, whereas lottery tickets involve no such dependency relation; but Riley answered that this just smuggles back the metaphysical distinction Jordan claimed to have replaced with decision-theoretic machinery.

Please go through their exchange move by move—rating each of Jordan's and Riley's specific moves from clearly mistaken to clearly correct, with brief justification—then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing, particularly whether there is a stable middle position that preserves an asymmetry in reproductive reasons without relying on Jordan's pipeline machinery or on Riley's purely epistemic reduction.
