Her summary runs like this. After surgical sectioning of the corpus callosum, the brain houses two streams of consciousness that are not merely divided but fundamentally independent: the left hemisphere supports linguistic thought and explicit self-reference, while the right hemisphere operates without any phenomenal awareness whatsoever—more like a sophisticated reflex system than a subject of experience. She describes the standard visual-field tests, in which stimuli presented to the left visual field can guide the left hand to a correct match even though the patient insists he saw nothing. From this she draws two conclusions. First, that access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness come apart in the right hemisphere: it has access to information for action but lacks the subjective feel of seeing. Second, she claims that what philosophers call synchronic unity—the integration of experience at a single time—is destroyed, but diachronic unity within each hemisphere remains intact; therefore, there was never one consciousness that split, but rather two that had been artificially coupled and are now revealed. She ends by suggesting that personal identity should be reconceived as hemispheric rather than whole-brain: if the left hemisphere is destroyed, the person is gone, even if the right hemisphere continues to behave purposefully.

This sounds too neat, and I worry she is conflating the absence of verbal report with the absence of experience. I also wonder whether her use of diachronic versus synchronic unity is being applied correctly, or whether it assumes that coordination between hemispheres was constitutive of a single consciousness rather than merely instrumental to it.
