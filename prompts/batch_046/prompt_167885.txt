I'm presenting my paper, "Suspension, Not Rupture: Buridan's Ass and the Normativity of Unprejudiced Choice," next Thursday to our philosophy of action and decision theory reading group. The thesis is revisionist: I argue that Buridan's own solution to the ass paradox was not an endorsement of arbitrary will-breaking — the standard analytic caricature of liberum arbitrium as random selection — but rather a defence of structured suspensive volition, a rational capacity to defer action and preserve agency precisely when reasons are incommensurable. I further claim that modern philosophy of action, by treating choice without preference as a pathological failure of rationality to be solved by tie-breaking mechanisms such as utility smoothing, randomisation, or lexical ordering, misses a normative ideal: that autonomous agency sometimes requires not breaking ties but sustaining them, allowing the situation itself to change or the agent's practical identity to be reshaped by the suspension. I apply this briefly to algorithmic decision architectures that face exactly tied utility functions, suggesting that rational autonomy in artificial agents might require a Buridan-style suspension protocol rather than arbitrary resolution.
