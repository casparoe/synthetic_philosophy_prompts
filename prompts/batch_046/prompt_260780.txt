I have come to hold that Dennett's brain-transplant scenario is best resolved not by deciding whether the self is in the brain or in the body, but by rejecting the premise that phenomenal self-location must have a unique spatial anchor at all. Specifically, I defend the view that the self is distributed across what we might call a functional topology: an integrated system of predictive and control loops that can span biological and artificial substrates without being reducible to either. On this view, the puzzle receives a non-binary answer—the self is neither here nor there in the ordinary spatial sense, and the thought experiment dissolves once we stop applying Euclidean location predicates to informational integration patterns. Tell me what would count against this position. Not just the standard objection that it dodges the puzzle by refusing to answer it, but which specific intuitions, formal results, or analogies from other domains of persistence (for example, the persistence of software across hardware changes, or fission cases in personal identity theory) it stands or falls with. Which defeaters would a committed defender of this functional-topology view concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss as relying on the very spatial essentialism the view rejects? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the position risks being insulated by qualification—whether it predicts anything that its rivals, whether brain-based or body-based accounts, do not.
