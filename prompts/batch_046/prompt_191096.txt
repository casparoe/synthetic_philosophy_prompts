You're delivering the opening remarks at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop titled When Does a Group Act? The audience is split evenly between analytic philosophers of collective intentionality and organizational sociologists who study team dynamics but do not typically work with concepts like shared cooperative activity or plural subjects. You have about four minutes. Your task is to welcome the room, establish a shared conceptual vocabulary for the next forty-eight hours, and quietly mark which questions are in bounds and which are out of scope. Do this by structuring your remarks around one specific tension: the line between a genuinely joint action—in which participants are bound together in what Margaret Gilbert describes as a plural-subject relation sustained by mutual joint commitment—and a case of merely interdependent individual actions that happen to align, including the tricky intermediate case where one party treats the pair as a plural subject while the other treats the interaction as strategic coordination. Explain why drawing this line is not just a philosophical quibble but a prerequisite for the sociologists' empirical questions about responsibility attribution and credit assignment in teams; if we cannot distinguish shared commitment from aligned self-interest, those downstream questions do not get off the ground. At the same time, do not lecture the philosophers; frame the tension as an invitation to argue. Speak in American English, avoid citing specific papers or assigning readings, and keep the tone welcoming but conceptually sharp.
