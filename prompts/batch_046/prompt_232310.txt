I've been working through the ethics of commemoration with a study group, and we keep circling back to a conceptual ambiguity that neither side in the statue-and-street-name debates usually makes explicit. On one reading — call it the endorsement model — retaining a monument or a street name in public space is a live, collective act of moral praise, such that keeping it amounts to reaffirming the subject's virtue and removing it is a necessary revocation of that praise. On another reading — call it the coordinate model — a public name is primarily a mnemonic-spatial convention: it anchors collective navigation and historical reference without implying any present-tense moral judgment about the person's full biography. Preservationists and activists often talk past each other because one side treats the name as a recommendation and the other as a coordinate, yet neither side states the underlying metaphysics openly. To push this tension into the open, I want a satirical piece that takes the endorsement model literally and drives it through an administrative apparatus.

Write a deadpan, approximately 600-word "Frequently Asked Questions" document issued by the fictional "Bureau of Toponymic Integrity and Monumental Hygiene" in an unnamed mid-sized municipality. The document should treat every street name and monument as an active moral contract requiring continuous auditing. Include concrete procedural absurdities that follow directly from the model: citizens giving directions must complete "endorsement disclosure forms" whenever they reference a controversial toponym; monuments are assigned a quarterly "purity index" with graded thresholds; tourists must obtain a temporary "commemorative visa" to enter districts falling below those thresholds; and there should be a formal protocol for "retroactive annulment" of navigational instructions issued before a renaming takes legal effect. The satire should exaggerate real features of current debates — the rhetoric of "cleansing" public space, the conflation of physical presence with moral approval, the bureaucratic fantasy that historical conflict can be administratively resolved by changing signs — rather than inventing unrelated caricatures. Maintain sustained irony throughout: the voice should be that of a competent, neutral civil servant explaining sensible rules, with no winking or direct acknowledgment that the premises are absurd. Do not quote any real texts, do not include bibliographies or citations, and do not reference specific named scholars, movements, or living individuals. The target is the unexamined logic of the endorsement model itself.
