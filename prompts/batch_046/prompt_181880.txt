I've been trying to work out my own position on the varieties of free will worth wanting—specifically, what conditions must actually hold for moral responsibility to be justified—and I want to check whether the package I've assembled is genuinely coherent or whether I'm helping myself to incompatible motivations without noticing. Here is roughly where I've landed. First, I take the free will worth wanting to be essentially about being the appropriate "source" of one's actions, where sourcehood is understood in terms of reasons-responsiveness rather than access to alternative possibilities: what matters is that one's conduct flows from psychological mechanisms that are genuinely responsive to reasons, including moral reasons, and that one takes ownership of those mechanisms in a non-accidental way. Second, I reject Frankfurt-style hierarchical compatibilism, but not on the usual grounds that it permits too little leeway; rather, I reject it because hierarchical endorsement is compatible with precisely the kind of external engineering that should clearly undermine freedom—if a neuroscientist implants or conditions my second-order volitions, I can still "identify" with them in Frankfurt's framework, yet I am plainly not autonomous. Third, I think Libet-style findings about readiness potentials preceding conscious awareness pose essentially no threat to free will, since agency was never located in the conscious "trigger" of a specific motor act but in the broader, long-term architecture of one's reasons-responsive psychology. Fourth, I maintain that determinism per se—the thesis that every event is necessitated by prior conditions plus natural laws—is not the enemy of free will: a deterministic world with no interfering agent in anyone's causal history is fully compatible with being the source of one's actions in the sense that matters for responsibility. Fifth, however, I also want to insist that genuine sourcehood requires that the agent had a meaningful opportunity to have developed differently—not that she could have performed a different action at the exact moment of choice, but that the formation of her psychological profile was not entirely fixed by forces completely outside her control.

My worry is that the fourth and fifth commitments pull in opposite directions: if determinism is harmless only in the absence of an interfering agent, but my character was nevertheless fixed by genetics and environment I never chose, then it seems I am still the product of forces outside my control, just diffused across nature and nurture rather than concentrated in a manipulator. Meanwhile, my rejection of Frankfurt in the second commitment might seem to sit uneasily with my dismissal of Libet in the third—one might think that if unconscious neural processes can initiate action, then the "source" of my behavior is always partly outside my conscious ownership, which sounds structurally analogous to the engineered-endorsement case I want to rule out. So: is this a consistent package? Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and what is the least costly revision—giving up the historical-development condition, weakening the anti-manipulation constraint, or abandoning the claim that determinism per se is harmless? Be specific about which commitment has to go if any do, and what that costs the rest of the view.
