In mechanistic interpretability of large neural networks, there is a sharp institutional distinction between causal and correlational methods. Researchers perform direct interventions—activation patching, ablation, or targeted editing of internal representations—and treat the resulting behavioral change as evidence that they have isolated "the circuit for X" or "the feature that causes Y." By contrast, when a linear probe or decoder finds a strong statistical association with the same behavior but no intervention reproduces the effect, the result is dismissed as a non-mechanistic correlation lacking causal significance. The practice contains an asymmetry that is rarely examined: the researcher who performs the edit is never inserted into the causal story. We do not say that the scientist's action was the true cause and the network merely the occasion on which the change occurred; we say the feature itself caused it. At the same time, the practice treats observational correlation as metaphysically inert, even when the correlation is perfect and stable across contexts.

This framework seems to presuppose a specific metaphysics of causation—one in which certain entities inside the system possess genuine causal powers (secondary causes) that can be activated or suppressed, and in which human intervention is a transparent window onto those powers rather than an external occasion that merely precedes the effect. Al-Ghazālī's occasionalism denies exactly this structure: on his view, fire does not burn cotton; rather, the presence of fire is merely the occasion for God to create the burning, and no created thing has intrinsic causal efficacy.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of causation that licenses the interpretability practice. Does it require full Aristotelian realism about causal powers, or can a Humean constant-conjunction view explain why intervention-based methods reveal causation while correlational methods do not? More specifically, is the practice consistent with occasionalism? If occasionalism is correct, then neither the edited feature nor the researcher's intervention is the true cause of the behavioral change—only the direct underlying dynamics (in Al-Ghazālī's theology, only the direct divine act) are. Would that reduce claims like "we found the circuit for X" to "we found the occasion on which X tends to arise"? And if we replaced the practice's implicit causal realism with occasionalism, what would have to change in how interpretability results are reported, validated, or used to justify safety-critical interventions?
