As a doctoral student in political philosophy working on distributive justice, I have been facilitating a reading group that has spent the past term examining Amartya Sen’s entitlement framework for analyzing mass starvation. Our current focus is on whether the model applies uniformly across three widely discussed cases—the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish Great Famine of the 1840s, and the Chinese famine during the Great Leap Forward—or whether forcing them into a single explanatory schema obscures a distinction that matters both causally and morally.

Within the group, a sharp but apparently unresolvable split has emerged. One side treats the Chinese case as an extreme instance of the informational failures Sen emphasizes: local officials faced structural incentives to misreport harvest yields, producing a price-signal breakdown that prevented entitlement adjustments and parallels, in formal structure, the speculative market dynamics underlying the Bengal crisis. The other side rejects this assimilation, arguing that the Chinese famine was driven not by informational friction but by deliberate surplus extraction and politically targeted denial of access—mechanisms that align it more closely with interpretations of the Irish export paradox, in which food left affected regions while populations starved, except intensified by centralized authoritarian control. For months we have agreed on the mortality data, on the basic entitlement relations, and even on the inadequacy of purely climatological explanations; yet neither faction has shifted, and the arguments now feel circular.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical but conceptual: we are using “entitlement collapse” to cover both systemic informational breakdowns and intentional political deprivations, and our disagreement is really about which normative conditions must obtain before a famine counts as a political crime rather than a systemic disaster. Is that diagnosis correct—are we facing a hidden normative dispute about culpability thresholds, or are we instead answering different questions (one predictive, one accountability-focused) under a single theoretical heading? And if so, what kind of conceptual distinction or formal refinement—perhaps a separation between entitlement denial and entitlement collapse—would actually allow progress, or should we conclude that Sen’s framework is genuinely underdetermined with respect to these cases?
