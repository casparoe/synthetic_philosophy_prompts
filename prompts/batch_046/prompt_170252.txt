Write the full specification of desiderata that any adequate theory of democratic boundary-drawing—who belongs to the demos—must satisfy. Lead with your bottom-line judgment in the first sentence: state which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, then give the reasoning. Do not restate the question in your opening.

The list must cover these cases and distinctions: citizens residing permanently outside state territory; long-term non-citizen residents fully subject to law but without political rights; temporary labor migrants under multi-year contracts; people affected by state policies but not subject to its laws; people subjected to coercive state authority that formally excludes them—such as individuals in extraterritorial detention or sanction regimes (treat this as the reverse boundary problem); future generations bound by present climate or debt commitments; and nested jurisdictions where one person falls under municipal, national, and supranational authority at once. For each, specify whether the theory must explain the case, respect a distinction, or stay consistent with an established result, and flag any pair of desiderata you think is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly any clash between universal inclusion under an all-subjected principle and feasible democratic self-rule at scale. Keep membership, representation, and jurisdiction analytically separate; do not collapse them.

Then briefly score the main contenders—the all-affected-interests view, the all-subjected-to-coercion view, stakeholder or associative models, and citizenship-by-nationality views—against your list, marking fatal failures versus tolerable ones. Keep the weight on the criteria and motivating cases, not on the scorecard.

Separate what is settled in democratic theory from what specialists genuinely contest, and do not present a camp-specific preference as consensus. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
