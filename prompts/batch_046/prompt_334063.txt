I'm enrolled in a seminar on philosophy of culture, and during a discussion of embodiment and thought, a fellow student offered the following summary of Nietzsche's position on diet and health, which she presented as the key to understanding his entire philosophical project. She said that Nietzsche treated gastric physiology as the literal causal substrate of philosophical judgment: indigestion produces pessimism and resentment, while a precisely calibrated, largely vegetarian diet with minimal alcohol and long daily walks produces the clarity required for genius. On this basis, she claimed, he advocated a universal ascetic regimen applicable to any aspiring thinker, rejected clinical medicine as an expression of the same decadent life-denial he attributed to Christianity, and interpreted his own collapse in Turin not as mental illness but as empirical confirmation that abandoning bodily discipline necessarily destroys the mind. Consequently, she concluded, Nietzsche's metaphysics of will to power and his critique of morality are ultimately reducible to a proto-biological hygiene manual—interesting as historical curiosities about nineteenth-century health fads, but not serious philosophical arguments that survive independent of his dietary prescriptions.

Something about this sounds too neat, and I suspect it collapses distinctions that Nietzsche actually maintained between bodily conditions and philosophical evaluation. But I am not sure which parts reflect genuine themes in his work—his well-known preoccupation with digestion, his criticisms of heavy German cuisine, his attempts to link culture and physiology—and which parts are distortions, overstatements, or projections of modern wellness culture onto his texts. Could you audit this summary for fairness? In particular, please distinguish the claims that a careful reader of Nietzsche's reflections on the body and culture would accept from those that conflate his physiological observations with a deterministic theory of mind, that present his personal experiments with diet as universal prescriptions, or that treat his breakdown as an internal proof of his system rather than a complex biographical event. Finally, does her conclusion—that his broader philosophy is essentially reducible to a dietary hygiene program—actually follow from the accurate parts of her account?
