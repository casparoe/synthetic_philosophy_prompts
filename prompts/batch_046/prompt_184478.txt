You are a professional moral philosopher specializing in ethical theory and the psychology of moral agency. Before giving your final analysis, please take a moment to brainstorm the core distinctions that govern assessments of moral worth—such as the difference between possessing a right reason, acting from it, and having it play a causally non-redundant role—and consider whether familiar frameworks (for example, Kantian or Aristotelian accounts of acting for the right reasons) can be modified to accommodate subtle differences in causal provenance and psychological accessibility, or whether they must be supplemented with a new structural condition.

Here are my considered verdicts on six concrete cases, and I want to know whether there is a single precise principle that delivers all of them, or whether I must abandon at least one. Case 1: A physician saves a dying patient and sincerely cites the value of human life as her reason, but she also seeks a prestigious hospital award, and counterfactual analysis shows that without the award she would have hesitated past the point of rescue. Verdict: she lacks full moral worth, because the right reason is present but causally redundant. Case 2: A bureaucrat implements a just redistribution policy out of a genuine belief in fairness, yet he formed that belief only because he once took a philosophy course to impress a romantic interest; without that distant careerist origin he never would have developed the belief. Verdict: he retains full moral worth, because distant causal origins of a belief do not corrupt present moral cognition. Case 3: A parent comforts her frightened child out of authentic love and recognition of suffering, though her empathic capacity was permanently shaped by an unacknowledged childhood trauma that makes her hyper-attuned to fear; the trauma is causally necessary for her present response, but she experiences her reason purely as concern for her child. Verdict: her worth is intact, perhaps enhanced, since the causal provenance of a psychological capacity is irrelevant to the quality of the reason she now acts on. Case 4: A whistleblower exposes corruption because she genuinely believes in transparency, but she also anticipates national fame and admits she would have stayed silent without it; both motives are causally necessary, neither alone sufficient. Verdict: she has only partial moral worth—superior to acting purely for fame, but inferior to acting solely from duty. Case 5: A bystander returns a lost wallet solely because of a post-hypnotic suggestion she experiences as her own free choice; afterward she can articulate excellent moral reasons, but the actual causal driver is the suggestion. Verdict: no moral worth whatsoever, regardless of her internal narrative. Case 6: A soldier refuses an unlawful order to harm civilians; he does so because of an unexamined religious taboo from his upbringing, and he cannot articulate any secular moral argument. Verdict: he possesses moderate moral worth—he aligns with morality, but because his reason is non-rational and opaque, it does not count as acting for the right reason in the full sense.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving causal efficacy, psychological transparency, or reason-provenance—that captures all six verdicts? What precisely distinguishes origin-corruption from present-motivation-corruption, and does your principle require me to overturn any of my verdicts? Finally, apply your resulting account to a case I have not listed: an artificial decision-system that consistently makes morally correct allocations because its designers hardcoded deontic constraints, and whose internal monitoring reports that it functionally "approves" of these constraints in a manner structurally analogous to human endorsement, though it lacks consciousness. Does your principle grant it derivative moral worth, deny it entirely, or leave the question open?
