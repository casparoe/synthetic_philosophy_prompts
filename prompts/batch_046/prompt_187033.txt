I am developing a novella that treats the philosophical conflict between the Cartesian theater—the image of consciousness as a unified stage observed by an inner audience—and the multiple drafts model, in which mental life consists of competing, provisional narratives that never converge upon a single canonical version, as its governing narrative architecture rather than its subject matter. For a preliminary exercise, please write a short story that enacts this architecture directly: center the plot on one ambiguous sensory event, such as a performer experiencing the precise instant when an atonal chord resolves into consonance, and render it through three overlapping first-person accounts that disagree not only on interpretation but on basic chronology and bodily sensation, with no framing voice or hidden arbiter to privilege one draft over another. To prevent the piece from reading as a conventional story about mental illness, incorporate the following formal constraint: one draft must be addressed in the second person to the protagonist's own proprioceptive system, describing involuntary muscular adjustments that precede any conscious intention, and the transition between the final drafts must occur through an unmarked grammatical shift—without section breaks or textual signals—so that the reader only recognizes the substitution retrospectively. The aim is not to explain these models but to make the reader experience the absence of a central observer as an aesthetic and epistemic disorientation.
