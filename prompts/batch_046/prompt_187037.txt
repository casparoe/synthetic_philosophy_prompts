I facilitate a graduate workshop on collective memory and political myth (12 students, 90 minutes). Design a simulation in which participants experience how a military defeat becomes a "lost cause"—not merely a sad story, but a source of enduring moral authority. Use a synthetic scenario: a small community defending a symbolic valley against a larger force. Build the rules so that four structural devices—betrayal by expected allies, heroic outnumbering, the sacralization of terrain, and the deferral of ultimate victory to an unspecified future—are embedded in role cards and payoff structures, not explained in advance. Students should discover them only through negotiation and choice. Include: (1) role cards with hidden information; (2) a forced decision between negotiated surrender and symbolic last-stand resistance, with payoffs that make material defeat feel like moral triumph; (3) a 15-minute debrief that maps each discovered mechanism onto one of four templates—Confederate "Lost Cause," Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk—without asking students to research them; (4) an explicit note on which dimension of myth-formation (e.g., multi-generational institutional repetition) the simulation cannot replicate and must be discussed instead; and (5) a prediction of what will go wrong in the first run and how to correct it. Begin with a one-sentence bottom line: the precise narrative move that transforms defeat into destiny. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Keep the response to 500 words or fewer.
