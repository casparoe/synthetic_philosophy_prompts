I serve as the senior editorial auditor for a regional digital-history archive that publishes explanatory captions for museum exhibits. Every week I must approve or revise about twenty submissions that assign causes to historical events, working only with the evidence packets attached to each submission and with a fifteen-minute time limit per claim. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered questions with explicit stopping rules—to classify any submitted causal explanation into one of four categories: (A) singly sufficient, where one cause alone explains the outcome; (B) causally redundant overdetermination, where multiple causes are each individually sufficient and at least one can be removed without breaking the explanation; (C) conjunctively necessary causation, where no single cause is sufficient but each is a required part of a minimal sufficient set; or (D) framing-dependent, where the selection of causes depends more on the historian’s disciplinary lens or institutional purpose than on evidentiary asymmetry in the packet. For each ordered step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer using only the submitted packet, flag exactly where the procedure hands off to a value judgment rather than resolving the question factually, and state what evidence gap should trigger an automatic “cannot classify—return to submitter” stop. Apply the procedure to three submissions: first, a caption on the outbreak of the First World War citing alliance structures, nationalist movements, and the Sarajevo assassination; second, a caption on a 1923 local factory strike citing both a documented wage cut and an organizer’s presence recorded only in oral-history transcripts; third, a caption on a major twentieth-century legislative reform citing both sustained grassroots protest and strategic presidential bargaining, where the archive’s public-facing mission emphasizes bottom-up social movements and the procedure may therefore recommend classifying one cited cause as redundant—a result the curatorial team will resist for pedagogical reasons. Conclude by stating the single greatest kind of error this procedure will still make and the class of historical claims for which it is not designed. End with a line beginning Verdict: summarizing that core limitation in one sentence.
