I'm trying to name a pattern I keep encountering in causal and moral reasoning about omissions. Take three situations: a lifeguard who fails to rescue a drowning swimmer; a distant hiker with binoculars who also does nothing; and an old tree that never grows the branch that would have shaded a bench. In each case an undesirable outcome is linked to something that didn't happen, yet people treat only the lifeguard's failure as a full causal factor—saying it caused the death—while the hiker's inaction is at most a missed opportunity, and the tree's missing branch isn't causal at all.

The difference doesn't seem reducible to physical proximity or ability; the hiker could have called for help. Rather, it appears that the lifeguard's omission counts because there is a specific institutional or social role that creates an "empty slot"—a prescribed intervention expected to occupy that point in the sequence. When such a slot exists, the absence is registered not as mere background but as a missing piece comparable to an action; when there is no predefined role, the absence remains causally invisible even if things would have been different had it occurred.

Is there an established term for this "slot-dependent" promotion of omissions—the selective elevation of an absence to causal status based on a normative or functional frame that defines where an action should have been? How exactly does it differ from broader accounts that invoke norms or counterfactual dependence generally, and from the cognitive omission bias? If no precise concept exists, can it be defined with clear boundary conditions—for instance, does it require an explicit duty, or only an implicit functional expectation?
