You are an expert philosopher of aesthetics with deep knowledge of twentieth-century African American thought, particularly the political dimensions of art. Before you begin your analysis, please take a moment to brainstorm the different logical and normative forms that a political slogan about art can take — universal descriptive claims, conditional normative obligations, audience-effect theses, and formalist readings — since I want the answer structured around those distinctions rather than collapsing them into a single interpretation.

In my honors literature and philosophy seminar, we have been discussing W. E. B. Du Bois's well-known claim that all art is propaganda. The slogan keeps being used in two very different ways: one student argues that it proves artistic neutrality is a myth, because even a landscape painting that avoids explicit politics still reinforces the status quo by directing attention away from injustice; another student responds that Du Bois was making a narrower, normative point — that Black artists in particular have a duty to use their work as propaganda for justice and equality, not that every work everywhere is covertly ideological in the same manner. I am trying to figure out exactly what precise theses the slogan could stand for. Can you lay out the menu of possible readings? For each one, specify whether it is descriptive or normative, whether it applies to all art universally or only within specific social conditions, whether it concerns the artist's intention, the work's formal structure, or the audience's interpretation, and whether it collapses aesthetic value into political efficacy. Then tell me which readings are plausible and defensible, which are trivial or false, and — crucially — which ones actually support my first classmate's broad conclusion that neutrality is impossible, versus which ones only support the second classmate's narrower claim about moral duty under oppression. I would also like you to consider whether there is a defensible middle position that treats art as having unavoidable social consequences without reducing every judgment of beauty or craft to a verdict on politics.
