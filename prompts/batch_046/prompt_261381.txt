I lead a philosophy of time seminar with about 10 graduate students, meeting for 90 minutes. Design a structured in-class simulation that makes participants feel the force of Gödel's argument that a rotating universe undermines the objective reality of time. Arrange the group in a circular seating protocol with local clock rules: each student receives and sends messages according only to their own proper-time stamp, while the routing rules are arranged so that after several rounds a message returns to its point of origin bearing an earlier timestamp than its departure. Give the group a shared task—constructing a consistent global chronology—and split them into roles: local observers who may trust only their own clocks, and a global coordinator who attempts to synchronize all timelines into one ordering. Use a non-monetary payoff scheme tied to consistency, such as collective points that determine whether the group earns a shared reward (for example, selecting the next week's reading or avoiding an extra assignment), so that contradictions carry real stakes for the group. The rules should prevent anyone from stepping outside the system to resolve the loop by fiat. After the breakdown has occurred, provide a debrief that maps the circular message routing onto Gödel's closed timelike curves, the reliability of local clocks onto proper time in general relativity, and the coordinator's unavoidable contradiction onto the absence of a universal time function. Be explicit about which feature of Gödel's view—the impossibility of global temporal ordering rather than mere epistemic inaccessibility—the physical layout of the room can reproduce, and which feature it must leave to discussion. Do not name Gödel, rotating universes, or time travel in the instructions given to participants beforehand; reveal the philosophical connection only after the group has experienced the failure themselves. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests in your design.
