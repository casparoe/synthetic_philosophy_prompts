As a doctoral student in moral and political philosophy working on the ethics of armed conflict, I have been examining what one might call the dual-track liability system embedded in modern international humanitarian law and its institutional enforcement. On the first track, the crime of aggression attaches primarily to state leadership for initiating an unjust war, treating the collective decision to go to war as the proper locus of moral judgment about cause. On the second track, war crimes and violations of jus in bello attach to individual soldiers regardless of rank, nationality, or the justice of their cause, treating the soldier as fully responsible for the means of fighting but as entirely unaccountable for the ends, provided he observes procedural rules such as distinction and proportionality. Between these tracks sits combatant privilege: an ordinary soldier fighting for an aggressor state is not criminal for killing enemy combatants, while an ordinary soldier fighting for a just state who violates procedural rules is criminal for the very same act of killing.

This architecture seems to presuppose a bifurcated theory of agency—collective moral responsibility for ends assigned upward to elites, individual procedural responsibility for means assigned downward to every uniformed fighter, with the justice of the cause playing no distributive role at either level. But it also sits in tension with the moral equality of combatants: if unjust combatants are not blameworthy for participating in an unjust war, yet they are actively killing people who have done them no wrong, what exactly is the philosophical status of their action? Is it merely permissible, wrong-but-unpunishable, or something else?

What philosophical commitments about collective versus individual agency, about the separation of role morality from personal morality, and about the conditions under which killing becomes wrongful does this institutional pattern strictly presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit framework, distinguish what the dual-track system genuinely requires from what merely fits it well (for instance, purely pragmatic or epistemic arguments that soldiers cannot be expected to judge just cause), and take a definite position: does the institutional separation of jus ad bellum from jus in bello, implemented through this asymmetry in how responsibility is distributed—upward for cause, downward for conduct—ultimately cohere with the moral equality of combatants, or does it covertly rely on denying that individual combatants share in the moral wrong of their collective enterprise?
