I have been preparing a seminar contribution on structural realism, and I find myself uncertain about the boundary between representational formalism and ontological commitment in the ontic variant of the position. If the claim is that physical reality consists entirely in structure, and if the structures we actually identify in our best theories—such as the Hilbert-space formalism in quantum mechanics or the fibre-bundle descriptions of gauge interactions—are irreducibly mathematical, then does this not entail that infinite-dimensional vector spaces and principal bundles exist literally in nature? Put differently, if structural continuity across theory change is explained by the preservation of mathematical relations, it seems the structural realist must treat those relations as instantiated in concrete physical structures rather than as mere descriptive tools. Yet this appears to collapse ontic structural realism into a form of physicalised Platonism, where abstract mathematical objects gain concrete existence.

My confusion may rest on a false presupposition about how the representational machinery relates to the ontology being proposed. Is the standard resolution that the mathematics is only a representational vehicle, and that the underlying relational ontology could in principle be articulated without commitment to specific formalisms? Or is there a more nuanced distinction—perhaps drawing on the difference between the epistemic accessibility of structure and its ontological status—that allows one to maintain that structures are genuinely real without being committed to the literal existence of the Hilbert spaces or manifolds used to model them? I should be grateful for clarification on precisely where the ontological claim ends and the representational apparatus begins, especially regarding how structural continuity is maintained without assuming identity of the underlying mathematical objects across successive theories.
