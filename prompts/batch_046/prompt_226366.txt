I am a high school student in an introductory political philosophy course, and we have been working through the standard taxonomy of liberty—negative freedom as absence of interference, positive freedom as self-mastery or self-realization, and the republican tradition that defines freedom as non-domination, or the absence of arbitrary power over one’s choices. Recently I encountered a civic-education proposal, discussed in some policy circles, that would require all citizens between the ages of eighteen and twenty-two to complete two years of service in public institutions—schools, municipal health clinics, or local administrative bodies—not on the grounds that the state needs the labor, but specifically to prevent what the proposal calls “structural dependency” on private employers and to ensure that young people experience direct democratic accountability rather than hierarchical control. The proponents explicitly reject both the libertarian appeal to non-interference and the liberal appeal to autonomy, arguing instead that freedom consists in not being subject to another’s will, even when that will is benevolent.

What philosophical commitments about liberty does this institutional design actually presuppose? Must it be reconstructed within the republican framework of non-domination, or could it be coherently defended from a positive-liberty perspective that treats mandatory service as a necessary condition for genuine self-rule? Furthermore, is the practice internally consistent: if the stated goal is to eliminate arbitrary power, does a democratically authorized mandate itself avoid the charge of domination, or does compulsory service constitute precisely the kind of subjection to an external will that republican theory condemns? I am especially interested in whether the proposal’s distinction between “arbitrary” private authority and “non-arbitrary” democratic authority can be sustained without collapsing into either a purely negative or purely positive conception, and in what would have to change about the institution—its scope, its opt-out conditions, or its oversight mechanisms—if one replaced the republican presupposition with a rival theory of freedom.
