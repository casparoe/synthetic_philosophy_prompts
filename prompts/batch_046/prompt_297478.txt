For a graduate seminar in philosophy of language and mind, please compare the two-dimensional semantic treatment of indexicals and rigid designators—specifically the primary/secondary intension framework and its reliance on diagonal propositions—with the centered-worlds alternative that treats self-location as irreducibly perspectival. To keep the comparison from being a mere rehearsal of standard examples, apply both frameworks to the following under-discussed scenario: an agent who, due to sustained neurocognitive misalignment, consistently takes herself to be at spatiotemporal position p while actually occupying p', and who therefore forms de re beliefs about her own properties that are true at p' but evaluated at p within her epistemic space. How does each framework represent the content of her belief that she is here now? Does the 2D account preserve a robust distinction between epistemic and metaphysical possibility in this case, or does its diagonalization strategy collapse into a form of epistemic two-dimensionalism that renders the belief a priori knowable only at the cost of its first-person character? Conversely, does the centered-worlds approach genuinely avoid this collapse, or does it merely relocate the difficulty by treating the agent's center as a parameter that is itself epistemically opaque? In your response, clearly separate what is settled in the literature on 2D semantics and centered worlds from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present either the diagonalization strategy or the perspectivalist critique as the consensus view. Work with the conceptual machinery directly: no bibliographies, reading lists, or appeals to specific published texts.
