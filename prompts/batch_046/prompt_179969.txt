In the philosophy of emotion, the long-running dispute between judgmentalists—who hold that emotions are essentially evaluative judgments with formal objects—and perceptualists or feeling theorists—who treat emotions as either perceptions of bodily change or as sui generis affective feelings—has settled into a frustrating stasis. Each side accommodates the canonical data: judgmentalists explain intentionality and reason-responsiveness, perceptualists explain bodily phenomenology and recalcitrance, and both cite the same cases—fear of a harmless shadow, sadness that persists after rational acceptance of loss. For decades neither camp has moved, yet they share enough vocabulary that it is hard to tell whether they are disagreeing about facts, about which cases count as central, or about what the word "emotion" is meant to capture.

I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, supported by what each side actually says about these cases, not a verdict in favor of either view. Is it a substantive disagreement obscured by a shared term—perhaps one side is asking about the rational structure of an emotional episode while the other asks about its phenomenal vehicle? Is there a hidden empirical question about neural implementation or about dissociations between judgment, feeling, and motivational orientation that both sides gesture at but neither resolves? Or is the stall a sign that "emotion" is not a natural kind but a heterogeneous family, so that the question "what are emotions?" is ill-posed?

To sharpen the diagnosis, consider a feature that both traditions treat as peripheral but that may be doing hidden work: what I will call the "refractory profile" of an emotional episode—the specific temporal dynamics by which an emotion resists rapid revision even after its putative judgmental basis or perceptual trigger has been withdrawn or contradicted. Judgmentalists typically explain this as a failure of rational integration; perceptualists explain it as a lag in bodily feedback loops. But neither framework naturally predicts the observed shape of this resistance—why some emotions decay gradually while others show abrupt persistence, or why certain cognitive reappraisals can suppress phenomenal feeling without suppressing motivational orientation, and vice versa. Given this, is the disagreement really about whether emotions have propositional content, or is it about which explanatory level—rational, phenomenal, or dynamical—gets definitional priority?

What would count as progress given your diagnosis? A formal model of emotional dynamics that both sides must accommodate; an empirical result about dissociations that forces one side to revise its boundaries; a conceptual distinction that splits the debate into separate questions about judgment, feeling, and dynamics; or an argument that no progress should be expected because the target category is irreducibly plural?
