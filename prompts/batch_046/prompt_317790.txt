Grade the following student answer to a political-philosophy exam prompt. The exam question asked: "Consider a proposed municipal ordinance that requires every adult resident to serve one week per year on a randomly assigned neighborhood advisory board. Residents who refuse must pay a substantial fee; those who serve receive a stipend, childcare, and transportation. The board may issue public recommendations on zoning changes, but the city council is free to ignore them. Analyze whether this ordinance violates or realizes (a) negative liberty, (b) positive liberty, and (c) republican liberty. In your answer, distinguish the three concepts precisely and apply each to the ordinance, noting any tensions among them."

The student wrote:

"Negative liberty, as Isaiah Berlin defined it, is freedom from external interference. The ordinance violates negative liberty because the state forces residents either to serve or to pay a fee. This is direct coercion in the private sphere, regardless of the compensation offered.

Positive liberty is about self-mastery and being one's own master. The ordinance promotes positive liberty because it gives citizens direct influence over local zoning and cultivates democratic competence. Berlin warned that positive liberty can be used to justify coercion in the name of a true self that the subject does not recognize, and that risk is present here: the state claims to know residents' rational democratic nature better than they do themselves. Nevertheless, the coercion is justified in this case because democratic participation is a higher form of autonomy.

Republican liberty, developed by Philip Pettit, is freedom from domination—freedom from arbitrary power. Because the board assignment is random and applies equally to every resident, no particular group is singled out for domination. Moreover, republican liberty requires active civic participation in institutions that check elite power; by establishing an advisory board, the ordinance actually secures republican liberty, giving citizens a formal mechanism to contest council decisions. Even though the council can ignore recommendations, the very existence of the board means residents are not subject to arbitrary rule, since they now possess an institutional voice."

Assign a letter grade (A–F) with specific point-level comments that justify it, flagging what is correct and what is subtly wrong. Identify the precise errors: a misattributed view about what republican liberty requires, and an inference that does not follow from the premises given. Note where a hasty grader might overlook these slips. Separate what is settled in the literature—e.g., Berlin's distinction between absence of interference and self-mastery, Pettit's framing of domination as arbitrary power—from genuinely contested readings, particularly over whether republican liberty demands active citizenship or only the absence of arbitrary interference, and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus.
