I have been discussing the socialist calculation debate with friends who are taking a course in comparative economic systems, and one of them shared an extended summary from a popular economics blog. The author writes that Mises and Hayek argued socialism is impossible because a central planner cannot solve the millions of simultaneous equations required to coordinate production without market prices. The summary then presents Oskar Lange's reply: a central board can employ a trial-and-error procedure, adjusting prices iteratively on the basis of reports from local managers, which the author claims is computationally equivalent to a Walrasian auctioneer. Building on this, the author introduces what they term a contemporary vindication—algorithmic planning systems that aggregate dispersed local data through machine-learning models—and argues that this proves Hayek's concern about dispersed knowledge was never a principled epistemic objection, but merely a contingent complaint about 1930s computing capacity. I suspect this flattens several distinctions—particularly between procedural price discovery through entrepreneurial action and the central aggregation of declarative information—yet I am unsure which parts are legitimate simplifications and which are substantive misrepresentations. Could you audit the summary for me? Please identify what is accurate about Lange's proposal, clarify whether comparing his board to a Walrasian auctioneer is conceptually sound or a category mistake, explain how Hayek's argument about local and tacit knowledge differs from a mere technological bottleneck, and assess whether the appeal to modern algorithmic pricing genuinely addresses the critique or merely changes its subject.
