I'm working through the standard paradox of phenomenal judgment for epiphenomenalists—if phenomenal properties are causally inert, then judgments like "I am in pain" must be caused by neural states rather than by the pain itself—and I'm trying to see whether my intuitions about concrete cases hang together or collapse into contradiction. Rather than surveying the literature broadly, I want to know what principle, if any, captures my specific verdicts, and which ones I'd have to abandon to keep the account simple.

Here are my judgments. First, in the canonical case, I think a subject's judgment that this is what it's like to see red genuinely refers to the phenomenal redness, even though the causal chain runs entirely through the physical substrate; the causal inertness of the quale doesn't redirect the reference to the brain state instead. Second, in blindsight, where a patient can discriminate stimuli without any reported awareness, I deny that any phenomenal judgment is occurring—the discrimination is purely informational, not a muted or hidden phenomenal attribution. Third, in split-attention experiments where a subject briefly reports seeing a color that they subsequently deny having seen, I think a phenomenal judgment did occur at the moment of report but was immediately overwritten; its causal effects were confined to that transient verbal output and never entered stable belief. Fourth, in pain asymbolia, where a patient reports that it hurts but shows no emotional or behavioral reaction, I take the phenomenal judgment to be present and accurate, but causally disconnected from the motivational system—this strikes me as a real-world analog of epiphenomenalism about affect, not as a failure of judgment. Fifth, when an AI trained on neural correlates predicts that the subject is experiencing red with high accuracy, I deny that any phenomenal judgment is occurring; correlation with neural data, however precise, doesn't constitute phenomenal attribution in the absence of conscious access. Sixth, and here I feel the sharpest tension, when a philosopher writes in an essay that phenomenal properties play no causal role in cognition, I think this judgment is genuinely about phenomenal properties, yet it is clearly causally efficacious in shaping readers' beliefs—its efficacy comes from the physical properties of the text and the readers' brains, not from the phenomenal properties being discussed. But I also feel pulled to say that in this sixth case the philosopher is really talking about physical properties disguised in phenomenal vocabulary, since otherwise the judgment's efficacy seems to violate the very thesis it asserts.

So my question is: is there a coherent principle that distinguishes genuine phenomenal judgments from mere functional or predictive analogs, one that accommodates all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn—the one about the philosopher's apparently self-undermining judgment, the one about blindsight lacking judgment entirely, or the one about split-attention transient judgments? Please also state precisely what the resulting account predicts for a case I haven't listed: specifically, a subject who, under temporal-binding conditions, reports a phenomenal quality that subsequent neuroimaging shows was never present in the corresponding brain region, but where the report was triggered by a subliminal prime. And please be explicit about which clause of your principle does the work in that prediction, and which of my six verdicts it must sacrifice if it is to remain simple.
