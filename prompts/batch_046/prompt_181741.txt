I'm preparing a long-form feature on attempts to ground mental representation in purely naturalistic terms — the project usually called naturalizing mental content — and I need a conceptual specification rather than a literature survey. I want you to write the complete list of desiderata that any adequate theory in this area must meet, with the reasoning and boundary cases behind each. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need a framework I can work with offline, and I will supply references myself.

Write from the position that the theory's purpose is dual: it must explain how biological brains come to represent the world, and it must remain applicable to engineered or augmented cognitive systems without requiring evolutionary history as a necessary condition. This is a stricter brief than the standard biological-only framing, and it should shape which requirements you treat as non-negotiable.

Structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. The framing problem (approx. 120 words): Define what counts as naturalizing mental content for this specification, distinguishing it from causal or functional explanations of belief. State the dual purpose noted above and explain why it matters for the criteria that follow.

2. The core phenomena and stress tests (approx. 300 words): List the phenomena the theory must accommodate — misrepresentation, aboutness, compositional structure, and the content/vehicle distinction — with the motivating case for each. Then present two injected stress tests that standard treatments often neglect: (a) the engineered symbiont, a de-novo-designed microorganism carrying a synthetic photoreceptor that directs chemotaxis, can misfire systematically without reducing fitness, and has no evolutionary lineage for that receptor; and (b) cross-modal recruitment in augmented cognition, where synthetic sensory data are routed into a cortical region that never evolved to process that modality. For each stress test, specify exactly which desideratum it threatens and why evolutionary-history accounts face particular pressure.

3. The desiderata (approx. 450 words): Present the requirements as a structured list. For each entry, include: the requirement in precise form; the case or consideration that makes it a genuine desideratum rather than a camp prejudice; your judgment of whether it is non-negotiable or negotiable (flag camp-specific preferences, e.g., determinism, uniqueness of content, or strict evolutionary grounding); and, where relevant, a note on which other requirement it may conflict with. Cover at least: non-circularity of the base properties; the possibility of misrepresentation without mere dysfunction; accommodation of compositionality or systematicity; the artifact/organism distinction (or a principled reason to reject it); handling of co-opted or engineered functions lacking evolutionary precedent; normative dimensions of error; and consistency with neuroscience and evolutionary biology. Identify which single requirement, in your view, eliminates the largest number of existing candidates.

4. Joint unsatisfiability check (approx. 180 words): Examine whether any subset of your non-negotiable requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. Focus especially on the tension between fixing content by evolutionary history (to preserve non-circularity and normativity) and accommodating engineered or recruited functions with no such history. Also consider whether strict naturalism without semantic primitives can coexist with full compositionality. State whether these tensions are fatal, resolvable by weakening one requirement, or evidence that the dual-purpose framing should be abandoned.

5. Scorecard against the main families (approx. 250 words): Briefly assess how three approaches fare against your list — classic evolutionary teleosemantics in the Millikan-style family, informational teleosemantics in the Dretske-style family, and a functional-role or use-based account. Do not aim for completeness; keep the weight on how each handles the stress tests and the tension points identified in section 4.

6. Bottom line (approx. 100 words): Summarize what the specification reveals about the current state of the project. Is naturalizing mental content closer to being solved, or does the list of genuinely non-negotiable requirements show that the available theoretical resources are insufficient? End with the single question you think the field should answer next.

Keep the tone analytical and formal, appropriate for a feature in a science-and-ideas publication. Avoid rhetorical questions except in section 6.
