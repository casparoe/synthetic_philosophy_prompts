At a philosophy-of-mind seminar, two participants debated whether gradual neural replacement preserves personal identity. Maya argued that if biological neurons are replaced one by one with synthetic analogues that replicate not only spike patterns but also local glial and metabolic interactions, the subject’s first-person perspective never fractures: there is no discrete moment of death or duplication, only an uninterrupted diachronic self-model. Leo countered that identity requires embodied enactive continuity with one’s specific biological substrate; however precise the synthetic replacements, they eventually dissolve the original organismic system, producing a functional duplicate rather than the same person surviving. Maya replied that this treats substrate as essential rather than instrumental, noting that we already accept heart and joint replacements without declaring a new person. Leo responded that organs are modular replacements within a persisting organism, whereas total neural replacement eliminates the original biological system entirely, so the analogy fails. They asked me to referee.
