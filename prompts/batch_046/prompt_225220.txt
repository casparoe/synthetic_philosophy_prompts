I'm writing a reflection paper for my philosophy of mind seminar, and I'm trying to think through a variant of the Einstein's brain thought experiment that Hofstadter and Dennett present in The Mind's I. The version I'm considering asks whether a printed book that perfectly encodes every neural connection and dynamic pattern of Einstein's brain at the moment of a specific insight—so that it contains the complete causal-functional architecture, not merely a description—could be conscious of that insight.

Write a dialogue between two interlocutors, A and B, who are equally well-versed in philosophy of mind and neither of whom is a strawman. A should defend a modified functionalist view: consciousness consists in the instantiation of certain recursive, self-referential informational patterns, and if those patterns are fully present in the book's structure, then the substrate—paper, neurons, or silicon—is irrelevant, though A should concede that something must read or activate the pattern. B should resist by arguing that consciousness requires what we might call modal openness—the genuine counterfactual capacity for representational states to have developed otherwise—which a bound, printed text necessarily lacks because its causal and representational trajectory is fixed once printed, regardless of its internal complexity.

The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth: B must directly address A's claim about recursive patterns being substrate-independent rather than ignoring it; A must respond specifically to B's modal-closure objection rather than changing the subject. They should not reach a neat resolution, but should end by pinpointing their precise disagreement: whether the book's lack of temporal dynamics is merely an absence of an external observer who activates the pattern over time, or whether it reflects a deeper deficit in the substrate itself.

Please indicate how confident you are in the philosophical distinctions you draw, and identify the specific point about which you are least certain—particularly whether modal openness is genuinely distinct from standard arguments about temporal integration and causal dynamics, or whether it collapses into them.
