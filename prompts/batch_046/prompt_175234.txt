I've recently faced a decision that made the problem of incommensurable values personally urgent: whether to leave an epidemiology research post to care for my mother as her dementia advances. The choice seemed to pit a professional duty to prevent harm at scale against a filial obligation that resists quantification. From this, I have constructed an argument that now looks suspicious because its conclusion dissolves moral responsibility altogether.

(1) If two values are genuinely incommensurable, no amount of one can compensate for the loss of the other.
(2) If compensation is impossible, then there is no rational metric by which to rank options that realize different values.
(3) If no rational metric exists, then selecting one option over another is arbitrary from the standpoint of practical reason.
(4) But if a choice is arbitrary with respect to reason, then the agent is not answerable for it—moral responsibility requires that one's action be responsive to justifying reasons.
(5) Therefore, anyone facing a hard choice between incommensurable values is not morally responsible for the result.
(6) Since significant life choices—career versus family, loyalty versus justice, integrity versus security—regularly involve such values, this would imply that moral responsibility is largely an illusion for competent adults.
(7) I want to preserve (1) and reject (7), which suggests the error lies in (3) or (4), but I am unsure whether the correct move is to deny that incommensurability entails arbitrariness, or to deny that arbitrariness eliminates answerability. Where does the argument go wrong, and what does repairing it cost?
