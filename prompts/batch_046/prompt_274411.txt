My colleague argues that dialetheism collapses into practical paralysis, and I am unsure where the slide occurs. (1) Dialetheism holds that some contradictions are literally true—call them dialetheias. (2) A rational agent ought to align belief with truth, so if both P and ¬P are true, a fully rational agent should believe both. (3) Rational belief is closed under conjunction: if you rationally believe A and rationally believe B, you should rationally believe A∧B. (4) So the agent should explicitly believe the contradiction P∧¬P. (5) But practical reasoning requires consistency in action-guiding representations: you cannot simultaneously take P and ¬P as premises for action without generating contradictory directives. (6) Therefore, wherever a dialetheia occurs, rational agency requires either suspending all action—since every relevant premise is contradicted—or selecting arbitrarily between P-based and ¬P-based directives. (7) Since rational agents are not systematically required to be paralyzed or arbitrary, dialetheism proves too much: it renders rational agency impossible in any domain where contradictions are true. (8) Yet we seem to act rationally even in domains—quantum interpretation, legal reasoning, semantic paradox—where contradiction might be suspected. Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (2) and (5) if possible, but I want to know what preserving them costs, especially whether the closure principle in (3) must be restricted or whether practical consistency in (5) can be decoupled from logical consistency.
