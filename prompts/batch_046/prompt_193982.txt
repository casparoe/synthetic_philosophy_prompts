I just finished a long, exhausting argument with a friend about democratic institutions, and I want to work through what we were actually disagreeing about. We kept returning to a specific institutional pattern visible in several liberal democracies—most clearly in the United States: chief executives are bound by hard term limits that typically cap service at two terms, while legislators face no comparable ceiling and routinely build multi-decade careers, accumulating seniority, committee chairs, and deep institutional memory; simultaneously, the legal system relies on sortition—randomly selected citizens serving on juries—to render verdicts that can deprive people of liberty or life, and some polities now convene citizens' assemblies by lot to deliberate on constitutional or climate questions, yet these same systems reject legislative sortition on the ground that lawmaking requires specialized experience and continuity. My friend insisted the triad is incoherent: if an untrained jury can determine guilt, and if randomly selected citizens can recommend binding climate policy, why is a randomly selected legislature unthinkable? And if long tenure in Congress builds indispensable expertise, why is a third executive term treated as a constitutional emergency?

What philosophical commitments about representation, democratic legitimacy, and the nature of political knowledge does this institutional pattern presuppose? Does it require a sharp distinction between an authorizing function that must be periodically renewed and a deliberative or technical function that benefits from continuity? Or does it presuppose that corruption and capture are pathologies unique to unified executive power, not to dispersed legislative bodies? Might it instead rest on an implicit epistemic division of labor—lay citizens provide unbiased, particularistic judgment, while career legislators and permanent staff provide generalized, stable expertise—without explaining why that boundary falls exactly between jury boxes and congressional committees? Reconstruct the implicit theory, or family of theories, that renders the asymmetry philosophically stable. Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and identify points of underdetermination where rival conceptions of democratic legitimacy, political judgment, or institutional corruption could license the very same mix. Finally, suppose we replaced the underlying view with one that treated all political judgment as equally vulnerable to corruption and equally susceptible to improvement through sustained practice: what specific institutional features—seniority rules, committee structures, the mandate and scope of citizens' assemblies, the design of term limits—would have to change?
