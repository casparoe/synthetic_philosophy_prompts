University history departments, museum curators, and survey-textbook publishers still partition Western intellectual life into “Middle Ages,” “Renaissance,” and “Scientific Revolution,” with hard boundaries near 500 CE, 1500, and 1700. These divisions govern hiring lines, exam syllabi, and gallery layouts, even though working historians routinely reject them as artificial. What philosophy of historical change—about causation, conceptual identity, or the ontology of “science”—does that institutional grid presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it require epochal essences, a separation of science from its religious and rhetorical embedding, or a Whiggish narrative of discontinuous progress? Say whether the practice is philosophically coherent or merely administratively convenient, whether it can survive under continuous, network-based, or globally decentered historiographies, and identify which concrete structures—tenure fields, comprehensive exams, textbook chapters—would have to dissolve if the underlying periodization were genuinely abandoned.
