Write this from the perspective of a novelist drafting background material for a novel about collective belief and individual conscience. I want you to produce the document that two interpreters of Simone Weil—call them the Structural Abolitionists and the Functional Particularists—would sign together after a year-long adversarial collaboration about her anti-party writings and their implications for collective life.

The Structural Abolitionists hold that Weil’s critique targets any enduring collective that demands loyalty from its members; political parties are simply the purest case, but the same corruption of individual judgment applies to unions, churches, or movements that ask for adherence. The Functional Particularists hold that Weil’s attack is specifically on the modern party as an engine for manufacturing collective passion and suppressing independent thought; they believe she allows for temporary, task-bound collective action—coalitions without membership, platforms, or enduring identity—provided these never become objects of loyalty.

Draft their joint statement in the following form. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: precise statements about Weil’s biography and the arguments found in her anti-party essay and related writings that both sides accept without reservation. These should include her insistence that collective structures tend to substitute emotional belonging for the practice of individual attention, and her distinction between justice as an obligation and loyalty as a corruption of it. Do not include bibliographic references or reading lists; do not quote any passage in quotation marks—paraphrase everything in your own wording, and avoid leaning on specific publication dates or obscure titles that would require verification.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should contain: (a) the exact point of divergence, phrased in neutral language that both sides agree accurately describes the dispute; (b) the Structural Abolitionists’ formulation of their own position, worded so they would sign it; (c) the Functional Particularists’ formulation of their own position, worded so they would sign it. The table should cover at least three disagreements: whether the corruption Weil describes is structural (inherent to any enduring collective) or functional (specific to the party form); whether her concept of attention requires solitude that any collective action necessarily destroys; and whether her notion of rootedness can be satisfied by non-party collective belonging without reproducing the loyalty problem.

Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, propose a concrete case or thought experiment both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side would predict about that case. One crux must address what I am calling the paradox of Weilian organization: whether a group can be constituted to promote the conditions for individual attention—say, a network of local councils that dissolve immediately after each decision, with no membership cards, no platforms, and no continuity of identity—without that group itself becoming an object of loyalty and thereby falling under Weil’s critique. Another crux should examine whether Weil’s own participation in collective political action during her lifetime constitutes an implicit exception to her theoretical stance, or whether it exemplifies the temporary, task-bound form the Particularists defend.

Keep the document formal, precise, and constructive: it should read as a text both parties could put their names to, with no adjudication of who is right and no rhetorical slant. Do not ask for citations, do not propose further reading, and do not invoke niche secondary literature that a model without web access could not be expected to have encountered. The prompt itself should be answerable from general philosophical knowledge of Weil’s themes—attention, justice, rootedness, and the corruption of collective passion—while injecting the specific hypothetical of the dissolving-council network as a new, testable case that is not a standard classroom example.
