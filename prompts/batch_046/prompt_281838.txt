As a novelist constructing a historical narrative about the transformation of American labor and consciousness in the late nineteenth century, I wish to examine the adoption of Standard Railway Time in 1883 not merely as a logistical reform, but as a test case for the claim that artifacts possess politics. Please analyze this episode through the lens of technological determinism and its critics: in what specific ways did the standardized clock and the continental time-zone map operate as artifacts embedding particular political values? Which actors gained or lost authority in the decision to supersede local solar time, and how did the artifact subsequently restructure social relations—between railway corporations and municipal communities, between wage labor and agrarian rhythms—independently of its designers' explicit intentions? Furthermore, what does the case disclose about determinism itself: does the rapid normalization of railway time confirm that technology autonomously drives social change, or does it rather illustrate that dominant social powers constructed a technical system whose apparent neutrality served to naturalize existing hierarchies? I am especially concerned with the limits of the theory—whether it can adequately account for the sustained resistance of local solar-time advocates, and whether that resistance reveals an explanatory gap that determinist frameworks have not anticipated.
