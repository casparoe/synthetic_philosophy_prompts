I keep noticing a recurring failure pattern across very different domains, and I want to know whether it is a recognized phenomenon with a specific name or boundary. In artificial-intelligence planning, one lists the direct effects of an action, then adds rules for indirect effects, then adds domain-specific inertia rules to protect properties that should not change. Each addition is locally reasonable—physical objects persist unless moved, social obligations persist unless discharged, mental states persist unless revised—but the rules do not transfer: apply the physical-inertia package to social commitments and you freeze obligations that should have expired; apply the social package to physical objects and you miss spillover effects. The same dynamic appears in philosophical accounts of causation, where theorists append ceteris-paribus conditions for particular kinds of causal chains, and in legal reasoning about precedent, where exceptions accumulate for specific fact-patterns without yielding a general principle of when a prior rule is displaced. In every case, the system is not abandoned; instead, practitioners treat the growing patchwork as progressive refinement, even though the collective result is brittle and hard to extend to new cases. I know there is something called the frame problem, and perhaps also the ramification problem and the qualification problem, but I am asking about the specific dynamics of accretion itself: the slow buildup of individually justified, domain-bound persistence-and-change rules that collectively resist generalization. Does this accretion dynamic have its own established label, and how exactly should it be distinguished from the underlying representational difficulty of tracking change? If it lacks a precise name, what term and boundary conditions would best capture it, and which of the nearby concepts—frame problem, ramification problem, or something like ad hoc auxiliary hypothesizing—come closest and where do they diverge?
