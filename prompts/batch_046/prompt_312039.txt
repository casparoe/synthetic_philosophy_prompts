I have drafted notes for an essay tentatively titled “Beyond the Threshold: Evaluative Modularity and the Incommensurability of Pleasure and Pain in Lexical Negative Utilitarianism.” The central claim is that lexical threshold negative utilitarianism—which holds that no quantity of pleasure can outweigh suffering once a certain magnitude is reached—rests on an unstated assumption: that pleasure and pain are sufficiently commensurable to be plotted on a single axis that permits a “threshold” at all. I propose that if we adopt a modular account of affective evaluation, in which pleasure operates within a normative register oriented toward flourishing and pain operates within a distinct register oriented toward harm-avoidance, then they are not opposing values on a shared scale but answers to different evaluative questions—“what is to be pursued?” versus “what must be stopped?” On this view, the lexical threshold is not a deep moral fact but a category mistake: it treats two incommensurable registers as if they were magnitudes that could be traded off.

The notes then develop an anticipated objection: even if the registers are conceptually distinct, practical reasoning still requires integration, and a lexical rule might serve as a heuristic for that integration rather than as a literal claim about metaphysical commensurability. I distinguish heuristic aggregation from metaphysical commensurability and argue that lexical threshold views systematically conflate them, thereby masking the modular structure of evaluation.

Please comment on these notes with specific attention to the following. First, is the distinction between heuristic aggregation and metaphysical commensurability drawn sharply enough, or does it collapse once we examine how practical reason actually operates? Second, does the modular account genuinely undermine the lexical threshold, or does it merely relocate the conflict into a meta-level decision procedure? Third, within the space of negative-utilitarian and suffering-focused ethics, are there significant positions—beyond the familiar lexical versus non-lexical division—that this framing overlooks or mischaracterizes? I am not seeking bibliographic references, citation lists, or reading suggestions; I want conceptual objections and suggestions for sharpening the argument’s logical structure and for clarifying where the modular view stands relative to standard anti-aggregationist positions.
