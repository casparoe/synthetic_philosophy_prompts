I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, though I have spent the last two years building value-alignment architectures for distributed AI systems. After working through Adam Smith’s moral psychology—particularly his account of sympathy and the impartial spectator in his work on moral sentiments—I have become convinced that this framework is more useful for engineering alignment governance than the Rawlsian or utilitarian models that dominate our field. For an interdisciplinary workshop next month titled “Moral Philosophy in Machine Design,” I plan to defend a specific interpretive twist: the impartial spectator should not be treated as a single, centralized evaluator (analogous to a reward model or a constitutional AI layer), but rather as an iterative, inter-subjective calibration mechanism distributed across stakeholder perspectives, converging through repeated mutual adjustment rather than being imposed top-down. I am calling this a “spectator-network” approach, and I want to apply it specifically to federated oversight protocols where no single party holds a universal utility function. The audience will be split in ways that worry me: analytic moral philosophers—including some specialists in Scottish moral-sense theory, plus Kantians and contractualists—who will scrutinize whether my “network” reading misrepresents Smith’s internalist psychology; ML engineers and AI-governance practitioners who will ask whether this is actually implementable and whether it avoids the well-known pathologies of multi-stakeholder feedback loops; and a few economists who know Smith only through his economics treatise and will wonder why I am importing eighteenth-century moral psychology into systems design. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a written defense: the objections I will actually face, ordered by likelihood and potential damage, each phrased the way that specific kind of audience member would phrase it, with a concise reply I could deliver in about two minutes, a clear note on where that reply is weakest, guidance on what I should concede versus what I must hold, and explicit identification of the one objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely from the philosophers about whether my distributed “spectator-network” collapses Smith’s internalist psychology into a form of social-contract theory that he explicitly rejected. To help you target my position precisely, my core thesis is that Smith achieves impartiality not by stepping behind a veil of ignorance but by imaginatively exchanging positions with others; translated into distributed systems, this means iterative perspective-taking modules that update each other until convergence, without requiring a single encoded objective.
