A colleague sketched this argument about Williams's integrity objection and now suspects it proves too much, but cannot locate the slip. (1) In Williams's familiar case, a husband who saves his wife from drowning only after weighing her life impartially against a stranger's exhibits a failure of integrity—he has, as it is often put, one thought too many, treating a relationship that partly constitutes his identity as an object of calculation. (2) Integrity is not confined to intimate attachments; it also protects the moral and practical commitments—honesty, loyalty to a cause, artistic vocation—that structure a life and make it recognizably one's own. (3) Therefore, an agent who pauses to reflectively weigh whether her moral commitments apply in a particular situation, treating them as hypotheses to be tested rather than as expressions of her character, exhibits the same structural failure: she introduces an alienating distance between herself and the commitments that define her. (4) But this implies that any genuinely reflective moral reasoning—utilitarian calculation, Kantian universalization, even careful virtue-theoretic deliberation—is a failure of integrity whenever it creates such distance. (5) If so, then the more rigorously and self-consciously an agent thinks about morality, the more she undermines the conditions for being a coherent person; full moral reflection is incompatible with personal integrity. (6) That conclusion seems absurd, since moral reflection is ordinarily regarded as a virtue and as essential to responsible agency. (7) So one of the steps must be wrong, but each rejection seems costly. Denying (2) requires explaining why intimate relationships deserve integrity-protection while moral commitments do not, without appealing to the very impartial weighing that Williams rejects. Denying (3) requires showing that reflective distance does not necessarily alienate an agent from her commitments, but then it becomes unclear why the husband's weighing is objectionable. Denying (4) or (5) requires accepting that integrity can tolerate some forms of calculation without dissolving, but then we need a principled boundary between permissible and impermissible reflection, and that boundary looks suspiciously like the line Williams wanted to erase. Which step do you reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? I would most like to preserve both (1) and (5) if that is possible, though I fear it is not.
