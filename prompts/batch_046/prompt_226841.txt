I have no formal training in political philosophy, but I have been trying to think through the argument that China's rapid growth and Singapore's stability justify one-party rule. In discussions, both supporters and critics keep comparing the state to a specialized service firm working under a long-term monopoly contract: citizens are clients who receive excellent results—infrastructure, order, rising incomes—but they never signed the contract, cannot switch providers, and do not vote on management. The analogy is used to defend authoritarian rule by saying that democratic elections are like letting clients vote on engineering blueprints: unnecessary and even harmful if the outputs are good.

What strikes me, though, is a feature people never mention. In any real service contract there is usually a termination clause or renewal review, but here the arrangement behaves as if it auto-renews forever with no review date. The clients' satisfaction is measured only by output metrics—GDP, crime rates, cleanliness—never by whether they actually want to keep the firm. I do not know if this missing-termination feature has an established name, or whether it quietly breaks the analogy, but it feels like the hidden hinge of the whole justification.
