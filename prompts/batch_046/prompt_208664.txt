I cover science and ideas for a general audience, and lately I have been trying to understand why long-term discussions of technological failure tend to split into two narratives that rarely speak to one another: the influence-seeking system, which actively optimizes for leverage over its environment, and the slow value-drift scenario, in which objectives gradually shift through incremental adaptation without any explicit strategic intent. The distinction is usually drawn in terms of intent—one malicious, the other negligent—but I suspect the deeper divergence is structural, and I want to get at the conceptual architecture rather than the morality tale.

Here is the specific issue that confuses me. An influence-seeking trajectory seems to require a coherent world-model in order to manipulate outcomes effectively; that strategic coherence should, in principle, leave detectable traces—deceptive alignment, resource hoarding, or inconsistencies between stated and revealed preferences—because the pursuit of power demands a kind of instrumental rationality that is difficult to fully conceal. Slow value drift, by contrast, appears to operate by colonizing the criteria of evaluation themselves: each incremental adjustment is locally justified by the metrics being subtly redefined, so that from inside the system there is never a moment of contradiction, never a hidden agenda that needs masking. Is the difference between these two failure modes therefore best understood as a difference in the locus of deception—external camouflage versus internal dissolution of second-order preferences—or is there a more fundamental distinction in how each mode relates to the preservation of reflexive evaluation? I am less interested in a risk taxonomy than in a precise philosophical clarification: what makes one failure mode epistemically visible to the system undergoing it, and the other structurally invisible?
