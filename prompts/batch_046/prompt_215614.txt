Here's a puzzle about CEV that's been bugging me. (1) Coherent extrapolated volition fixes my goals as the preferences I'd hold if I knew more, thought faster, and were more the person I wish I were. (2) Now suppose part of my idealized self is a meta-preference M: "I want my final goals to be sensitive to what others, equally idealized, want too." (3) If M survives idealization, then my CEV isn't just individual — it's interlocked with everyone else's. (4) So apply CEV not agent-by-agent, but to the whole system at once: correct goals are the ones that survive simultaneous reflexive adjustment, where each agent idealizes and updates on the others. (5) In that loop, either all agents converge on one shared ordering, or there's no fixed point and the collective CEV is undefined. (6) So either there's a single global volition, or CEV can't apply to groups. (7) But if there's a single global volition, then whenever I actually disagree with it, I'm just failing to be idealized — like being wrong about 2+2. (8) That proves way too much: it dissolves "my own good" into the collective, turns every moral dispute into cognitive error, and makes democratic deliberation pointless since dissent is always deficiency. Which step should I reject? I really want to keep (2) — the idea that an idealized me wouldn't be a moral solipsist — and I'd also like to keep (6) as a genuine dilemma rather than a false one. So where's the hidden slide? Is it an equivocation between individual and collective idealization, a quantifier shift in (4), or does (5) illegitimately assume convergence is required for coherence? Walk me through the cost of each escape, including whether there's a stable pluralist equilibrium that saves disagreement without breaking the mirror-idealization idea.
