I'm coordinating an adversarial-collaboration exercise for a philosophy-of-mind reading group, and I'd like you to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps could both sign after sustained, good-faith argument about mental causation. On one side is a philosopher—call her Dr. Voss—who accepts the causal closure of the physical, a non-reductive supervenience of mental on physical properties, and the standard causal exclusion argument; she concludes that mental properties, if genuinely distinct from physical properties, are causally excluded from producing physical effects. On the other side is Dr. Chen, who accepts the same closure and supervenience premises but denies that they entail exclusion; he holds that mental properties can be causally efficacious through a form of non-competitive, proportionate causation that does not violate closure, and he thinks the exclusion argument illicitly conflates causal preemption with causal exclusion.

Please produce the collaboration document in the following structure. First, a numbered section of agreed claims—about the precise formulation of causal closure they both endorse, the supervenience relation they take mental properties to bear to physical ones, what the exclusion argument formally requires (including non-redundancy and the distinctness condition), the coherence conditions for overdetermination in this domain, and any shared commitments about counterfactual dependence. Second, a disagreements table that states each philosopher's precise position in wording she or he would accept: specifically, whether mental properties are synchronically excluded but might be diachronically efficacious; whether causal efficacy should be analyzed counterfactually or as proportional production; and whether closure applies at the token-event level or only to complete physical descriptions. Third, identify the cruxes—sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one party—such as whether proportionate causation genuinely distinguishes mental from physical causation or merely renames it, and whether a synthetic neuromorphic system exhibiting integrated phenomenal dynamics but lacking biological substrate would display mental causation that escapes physical explanation. For each crux, present a concrete hypothetical case or argument both sides agree is genuinely relevant, and record each side's explicit prediction about what that case or argument shows. Finally, include an assessment of whether any portion of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive—specifying exactly which conceptual distinctions (for instance, between "causal exclusion" and "causal preemption," or between "sufficient cause" and "proportional cause") generate the appearance of disagreement.

Keep the formulations rigorous, neutral, and neither vacuous nor slanted; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid appealing to highly obscure texts or empirical studies that a well-read model might not have properly encountered in training. The aim is a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than obscured by ambiguous phrasing.
