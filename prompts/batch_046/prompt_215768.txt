I'm trying to work out my actual commitments on updating beliefs when I learn a conditional, specifically whether such learning should ever revise my credence in the antecedent itself. Here are my gut verdicts. First: I'm about 60% sure I'm in the east wing of a symmetric building; I learn that if I'm in the east wing, I'm near the exit. That should not move my 60% — the exit location is causally independent of my actual location. Second: same prior, but I learn that if I'm in the east wing, security footage would show me leaving; the footage is missing due to a corrupted feed. Here I feel a slight downward pull on my east-wing credence, even though the conditional concerns camera behavior, not location. Third: I'm 40% sure a startup idea is sound; I learn that if it's sound, a specific investor will fund it, and then she declines. I drop sharply — but I'm unsure whether I'm rationally updating or illegitimately treating the conditional as direct evidence against the antecedent. Fourth: a doctor says, if you have condition X, this drug cures it. The drug fails, but my metabolism is known to block it regardless of X. Here my credence in X should stay put, yet emotionally it slips. Fifth: I'm 50% sure a colleague's theory is right; I learn that if it's right, it predicts an anomalous result we've just observed, and indeed she predicted it. That feels like it should boost my confidence, yet it also feels circular. Is there a single principle — perhaps about whether the consequent provides independent evidential access to the antecedent through a hidden common cause, or about reasoning inside a partitioned epistemic space — that sorts these? And what should it say about the case I keep reversing: should learning that if I stay, the project fails ever make me more likely to leave, or is that always a pragmatic inference, never an epistemic one?
