Draft the joint statement that two opposing interpretive camps about Nietzsche’s essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Party A is the Interdependence Camp: it holds that monumental, antiquarian, and critical history are structurally interdependent functions of a healthy consciousness, that none can be permanently eliminated without damage, and that Nietzsche’s diagnosis targets only extreme imbalances rather than prescribing suppression. Party B is the Monumental-Primacy Camp: it holds that the essay advances a normative hierarchy in which monumental history must dominate for life to flourish, that modern culture’s overgrowth of antiquarian accumulation and critical dissolution is pathological, and that recovery requires the deliberate privileging of monumental exemplars and the strict limitation of the other modes.

The deliverable should be formatted as a numbered section of agreed claims followed by a disagreements table. In the agreed-claims section, list the propositions both camps accept about the essay’s three typologies, the problem of historical excess, the role of the unhistorical sense, and the formal structure of the argument from diagnosis to prescription. In the disagreements table, present the precise residual disputes in wording each camp accepts for its own side, covering at minimum: (i) whether the three modes are complementary functions or competing forces that must be ranked; (ii) whether critical history is intrinsically life-negating or can serve life when subordinated to monumental identification; and (iii) whether Nietzsche’s remedy for modernity is dynamic equilibrium or hierarchical suppression.

Identify the cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side—including specifically whether a culture can maintain monumental identification with past achievements without the antiquarian preservation of contextual detail that undermines heroic simplification. For each crux, specify a concrete institutional case or practice that both camps agree bears on it, such as the curatorial policy for failed experiments in scientific archives or the pedagogical use of biographical myth in physics education, and record each camp’s explicit prediction about what that case demonstrates.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not invoke obscure secondary literature; ground the dispute in the essay’s own concepts. Where reference to a specific passage is necessary, paraphrase without quotation marks rather than quoting from memory. Conclude by stating whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, and if so, translate the contested formulations into neutral language that both camps could adopt without changing their position.
