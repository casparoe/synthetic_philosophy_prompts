Having spent decades in molecular biology before turning to philosophical questions, I am struck by the persistence of the analogy that DNA functions as a computer program or digital code executed by cellular machinery. In both scientific and popular discourse, this comparison is often used to license the stronger claim that genetic information carries genuinely semantic or representational content, not merely causal efficacy. How far does the analogy actually hold? Please provide an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the programmer, the compiler, the interpreter, the executable, and any error-correction protocol? Then distinguish the disanalogies that are philosophically consequential from those that are merely cosmetic: there is no intentional author; the mapping between codons and amino acids is mediated by adapter molecules that bear no intrinsic chemical affinity to their cargo; the code is nearly universal yet historically contingent; and gene expression is profoundly context-dependent in ways that software execution rarely is. Which of these breaks undermine the inference that DNA possesses semantic content in any robust philosophical sense? Finally, does the conclusion that life is fundamentally computation survive once evolutionary contingency and adapter-mediated arbitrariness are fully on the table, or does the analogy collapse into something more defensible—perhaps a chemical recipe or a catalytic network?
