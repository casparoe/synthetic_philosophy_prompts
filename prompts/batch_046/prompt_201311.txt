Write a deadpan mock institutional report, approximately 500 to 600 words, in the voice of the fictional Committee for Pareto-Efficient Liberal Aggregation (CPELA). The report should present, with precise social-choice terminology, the committee's implementation of Sen's minimal liberalism conditions—specifically, that at least two distinct agents each possess decisive authority over a separate pair of collective alternatives—and demonstrate rigorously why this produces total organizational paralysis. Rather than relying on the classic "prude and lewd" book-choice illustration, apply the logic to bureaucratic domains particular to the committee: office thermostat settings, quarterly budget approvals, meeting-format selection (virtual versus in-person), and coffee-vendor procurement. Show that any Pareto-efficient collective decision necessarily violates at least one agent's protected sphere, while relaxing either the minimal-liberal constraint or the Pareto principle restores decision capacity but abandons the ethical premise that individual sovereignty over specific pairs must be respected. Maintain a dry consulting-register throughout; the satire must emerge solely from the relentless application of formal aggregation logic to mundane administrative matters, never breaking character. Include, within the report, a brief quoted statement from a fictional senior committee ethicist who insists that dissolving the paradox by weakening liberal protections would be morally catastrophic, even if the organization never approves another travel reimbursement.

Following the report, provide an analytical note of roughly 150 words. In it, identify exactly where the satire takes liberties with Sen's formal framework—particularly whether the bureaucratic analogues genuinely preserve the structure of decisive pairs over social states, or whether they collapse the distinction between individual preference domains and collective choice sets. Then state clearly what specific formal consideration—such as the unrestricted-domain assumption, the treatment of external preferences, or the restriction of preference profiles—would lead you to revise your interpretation of whether the paradox constitutes a genuine limitation on liberal collective choice or merely an artifact of the aggregation model.
