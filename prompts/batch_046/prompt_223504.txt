I'm a software engineer with basically zero formal philosophy background, just reading some rational-choice stuff for fun. I keep running into money pumps — where circular preferences let someone be exploited — and diachronic rationality, which I guess is about being consistent with yourself over time. But there's this other pattern I notice that doesn't quite fit either, and I don't know if it actually has a name.

Picture this: at each step, a person (or maybe an algorithm) makes a locally rational choice based on how the options are framed against whatever the current status quo is. At step one they pick option A because it looks like a gain relative to where they are. Then the status quo updates to A. At step two, option B is presented as avoiding a loss relative to some different baseline — maybe not even A, but some imagined default — and they switch to B. At step three the framing shifts again, maybe back to the original baseline but now with accumulated costs, and they switch back or to C. The key thing is that they never hold the direct contradiction 'I like A better than B and B better than A' in the same frame. Each move is definately defensible in isolation, and there's no explicit circular preference. But over the whole sequence they've paid some kind of fricion cost or ended up with a bundle they'd reject if they could see the whole timeline from the start. It's like a money pump that runs on framing shifts instead of on raw preference loops.

Is there an established concept for this? I know prospect theory talks about reference dependence, but I'm asking specifically about the diachronic exploitability — the slow bleed over time through locally rational framing-sensitive switches. Is that just a weird subtype of money pump, or does it have its own name in the rationality literature? And if I'm just mixing up terms or describing something that doesn't exist, tell me straight — don't invent a label just to be nice. I'd rather hear that I'm describing X badly than get a made-up term.
