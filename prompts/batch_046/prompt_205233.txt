The debate over whether Buddhist ethics is consequentialist has been running for decades without resolution: one side points to karma’s dependence on the fruits of action and to Mahayana upāya narratives in which a bodhisattva harms one being to save many, while the other insists that cetanā—volitional intention—is the sole moral carrier and that Vinaya rules bind regardless of predicted outcome. Every standard move is well rehearsed, and the literature seems stuck in repetition. My working theory is that the stall is not really about ethics but about an unstated metaphysical disagreement over what karma causally tracks: an internal transformation of the agent’s consciousness-continuum versus an external state of affairs in the world. If that is right, both sides can agree on every case verdict and still talk past each other because they measure “good result” in incommensurable ontologies. Is that diagnosis correct, or is there a hidden substantive disagreement—perhaps about which textual corpus counts as normative data, about whether Buddhist soteriology requires a welfare calculus, or about whether upāya functions as an exception or as a virtue—keeping the debate alive? I want an honest structural diagnosis using what each side actually commits to, not a survey of interpretations, together with an assessment of whether anything—an analytical distinction between mental and world-causal efficacy, a re-framing of skillful means, or an admission that the question is ill-posed—could genuinely move it forward.
