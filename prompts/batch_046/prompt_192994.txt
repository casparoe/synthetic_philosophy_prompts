I'm trying to determine whether my judgments about mereological composition hang together, and I'd like help extracting the principle behind them—if there is one.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

First, two hydrogen atoms covalently bonded in a water molecule compose a single molecule, but if they drift apart in interstellar space with no interaction they do not compose any joint object. Interaction or functional integration seems necessary, not mere spatiotemporal proximity.

Second, the scattered collection consisting of exactly the left half of my laptop, the number seven, and the current President of France does not form a composite object. I feel this not as an arbitrary restriction but as a failure of there being anything there to compose.

Third, a statue and the clay that currently constitutes it are two objects, not one: the statue can survive being reshaped into a vase, while the clay survives being melted and reformed; they have different persistence conditions.

Fourth, a human being persists through gradual cell replacement, so functional and organizational continuity can sustain composition over time. Yet I deny that the atoms that once made up my body, now dispersed across the earth and no longer interacting, still compose me in any meaningful sense.

Fifth, a swarm of bees strikes me as genuinely one object—the swarm—with individual bees as parts, and the swarm can survive the replacement of every bee. So here composition depends on organizational role rather than on specific parts.

Sixth—the case that keeps shifting for me—consider a distributed computation running continuously across thousands of servers on different continents, with real-time feedback loops integrating them into a single algorithmic process. Is there one object here, the computation, having those servers as parts? Or are there merely many computers running related processes? And if there is such an object, could there be a "ghost computation" composed of servers arranged in the identical spatial pattern but not actually running the algorithm?

What principle, if any, delivers verdicts one through five? Does it extend naturally to case six, or does case six compel me to revise one of the earlier judgments—perhaps by forcing me to deny that the swarm is one object, or by pushing me toward unrestricted universalism about scattered servers? Please state the principle precisely, identify which clause is motivated by which case, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate without ad hoc complexity. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: a virtual entity in a massively multiplayer game, sustained only by distributed server states and player interactions, whose "boundaries" are defined entirely by in-game rules rather than by physical contiguity. Does your principle count it as a composite object, and what does that prediction reveal about the principle's costs?
