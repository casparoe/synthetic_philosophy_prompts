I am preparing the interview outline for an upcoming episode of my philosophy podcast, in which I will speak with a metaphysician who defends a revived version of Al-Ghazālī's occasionalist account of causation—the view articulated in Tahāfut al-Falāsifa that no created being possesses intrinsic causal efficacy, that God is the direct cause of every particular event, and that what we call natural causes are only the customary occasions (al-ʿāda) upon which divine action is regularly, though not necessarily, conjoined. The subject has become personally urgent to me because I recently encountered arguments in philosophy of science about whether structural realism requires irreducible causal powers, and I found myself unable to determine whether occasionalism is a live metaphysical competitor or merely a historical curiosity with theological ornamentation.

Please draft eight to ten polished, formal interview questions in American English that move well beyond asking the guest to restate occasionalism. At least one question should press the empirical indistinguishability problem: if God creates both the fire and the cotton's burning on each occasion without any necessary connection between them, in what precise sense does occasionalism differ from a Humean regularity theory coupled with theistic commitment, and does that collapse its distinctively metaphysical ambition? Include a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that the difference lies in the modal status of the connection—namely, that God could break the custom at any moment.

I also want at least one question that addresses the classic fire-and-cotton example in direct dialogue with modern chemistry: when science identifies oxidation as the causal mechanism, does the occasionalist treat that identification as describing the customary occasion rather than the true productive cause, and how does she prevent divine causation from becoming a mere 'god of the gaps' explanation? Anticipate the standard defense that scientific descriptions map the occasion, not the producer, and provide a follow-up asking whether this renders scientific law merely phenomenological.

Additionally, include a question about statistical causation in quantum mechanics: if every particle's state at each instant is directly created, what explains the remarkable stability of statistical distributions across ensembles, and is occasionalism compatible with the claim that quantum mechanics reveals irreducible probabilistic structures rather than deterministic hidden variables? Key the follow-up to the answer that God creates the distributions directly according to a rational, non-arbitrary plan.

Finally, include a question on occasionalism's relation to contemporary dispositional essentialism: if causal powers are essential to the natures of things, as modern essentialists hold, does occasionalism deny that created things have natures altogether, or only that those natures exclude causal efficacy? And if the latter, does occasionalism risk becoming indistinguishable from a theistic variant of dispositionalism in which passive dispositions are ordered entirely by divine will? Provide a follow-up for the answer that natures are purely passive dispositions sustained moment-to-moment by divine action.

Please ensure the questions are appropriate for an intelligent general audience with some philosophical literacy, avoid generic biographical inquiries, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
