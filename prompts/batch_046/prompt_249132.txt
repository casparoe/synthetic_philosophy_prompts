Last evening I had a long, exhausting argument with a close friend about a decision I am actually facing, and I left feeling that neither of us had properly named the philosophical tension running beneath our disagreement. For the past three years I have co-managed a small, unfunded community archive with a partner who depends on me; our work is not merely charitable but partly constitutive of a shared identity and practical life we have built together, and abandoning it now would collapse the project and betray a deep, specific trust. At the same time, an unexpected financial windfall has made it possible for me to fund a rigorously vetted global health intervention that is projected to save several lives over the coming year. My friend argues that if morality is genuinely impartial, I must treat my archive commitment as one preference-weight among others: the aggregate suffering I could prevent dwarfs the harm of breaking one local promise, and to refuse on the grounds of loyalty is simply moral narcissism disguised as principle.

I tried to reply that there is something structurally wrong with weighing obligations arising from relationships I have actively constructed — obligations that seem partly to define who I am — on the same scale as anonymous, fungible benefits, but he kept insisting that this distinction collapses the moment I admit my partner's welfare also matters to me, reducing my so-called special obligation to a self-interested preference for my own emotional equilibrium. I am not asking for a simple verdict on whether to write the check or stay with the archive; rather, I want to understand whether there is a philosophically coherent account of why personal projects and the obligations they generate might resist aggregation into an impartial calculus without merely asserting that "my people come first." Could you lay out the different precise forms this resistance might take — whether they appeal to the idea that persons are not interchangeable units of value, to the boundary conditions of agency and commitment, or to a distinction between constitutive and merely instrumental relationships — and say, for each, whether it actually gives me a reason to preserve the archive that does not beg the question against my friend's aggregation?
