I've been reflecting on a recurring tension in debates about moral status—whether in animal ethics, the ethics of artificial systems, or edge cases like insects and simple organisms. The pattern unfolds in three stages. First, a participant insists that the property underwriting moral consideration—perhaps the capacity to suffer, to integrate information, or to maintain a continuous subjective perspective—is genuinely gradated rather than binary. They allow that a mammal may possess it to a high degree, a cephalopod to a moderate degree, an insect to a minimal degree, and a thermostat or simple feedback loop not at all, with no sharp metaphysical jump between adjacent cases. Second, the conversation turns practical: who should be included in protection frameworks, which entities may be used in experiments, or whether shutting down a system requires moral justification. At this point the gradation is replaced by a hard boundary—inside or outside the protected class—defended as a mere administrative convenience rather than an ontological claim. Third, and this is the move that strikes me as distinct, that very boundary is then invoked as evidence about the underlying reality. One hears arguments like "we regulate mammals but not insects, therefore insects lack the relevant capacity entirely," or "this model falls outside our legal framework for sentient beings, so it cannot possess even minimal moral status." I have noticed this with discussions of synthetic sentience in large language models, with debates over whether cephalopods belong in welfare regulations, and with older comparisons between newborns and adults.

What I am trying to name is not ordinary inconsistency, nor is it simply the problem of vagueness. It is a specific argumentative choreography: a theoretical commitment to continuous variation, an institutional need for binary classification, and then a rhetorical reversal in which the classification's location is treated as proof that the continuous property genuinely falls to zero at that point. Does this pattern have an established label in philosophy of mind, applied ethics, or the philosophy of science—perhaps as a recognized failure mode in how we move from ontology to policy? How does it differ from a standard auxiliary hypothesis, or from the kind of category mistake Ryle warned about? And if there is no precise term for it, what would be the right way to characterize its boundaries—specifically, how can one tell whether a given binary cutoff is being used merely pragmatically, versus being smuggled back in as a metaphysical thesis about where gradated moral status genuinely ends?
