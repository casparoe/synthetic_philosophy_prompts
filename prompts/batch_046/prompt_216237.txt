In several societies with extensive gender-egalitarian welfare architecture—universal childcare, individual taxation of spouses, strong anti-discrimination enforcement, and career-counseling protocols that present all occupational paths as equally available—the institutional response to persistent or growing occupational gender segregation is uniformly to treat it as a problem of constrained choice rather than of unjust distribution. Policy designers remove informational barriers, counteract stereotypes, and expand option sets, but they reject outcome-targeting such as quotas, differential encouragement, or sectoral steering on the grounds that such steering violates autonomy. At the same time, these societies officially hold that gender is largely a social construct and that preferences are shaped by culture, yet they interpret the post-reform clustering—men in engineering and finance, women in nursing and education—as the expression of authentic, individually variable preferences that emerge once artificial barriers fall. This is the gender-equality paradox.

What philosophical commitments about preference formation, the ontology of gender, the nature of autonomy, and the demands of distributive justice does this institutional stance presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it depend on viewing preferences as exogenous to the social structures that produce them, so that removing barriers reveals a pre-social distribution of tastes? Or can an endogenous, constructivist view of preference accommodate the same policy posture? Does the rejection of outcome-targeting presuppose a specifically liberal, non-interference conception of autonomy, or could a relational-autonomy or non-domination framework license the identical practice? And is the stance consistent with the simultaneous claim that gender categories are socially constructed—does treating gendered clustering as unproblematic once barriers are removed implicitly reify gender as an internal, pre-political source of difference?

Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates: could a thoroughgoing social constructivist about gender, or a luck-egalitarian who thinks unequal outcomes traceable to socialization are unjust, adopt the same barrier-removal-only approach without contradiction? Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—where rival theories of justice or of the self would generate the same institutional design for different reasons. Finally, describe what a career-guidance and labor-market policy built on the opposite commitments—say, that preferences are endogenous artifacts of gendered socialization and that justice requires not merely open doors but a non-gendered distribution of social roles—would have to change in concrete institutional terms, and whether such a policy could still claim to respect autonomy.
