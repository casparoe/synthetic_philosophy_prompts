I have no formal background in philosophy, but over the past year I have been thinking carefully about what actually makes a life go well, and I have become convinced that only the absence of craving and suffering carries genuine weight—pleasure, achievement, and knowledge feel like ornamentation rather than substance. A close friend of mine shares this outlook, which she describes as a kind of tranquilism: she believes value is not added by good experiences but is only preserved by removing bad ones. Her judgments across situations seem consistent, yet I cannot tell whether she is running one precise rule or several that happen to overlap. She holds that the best condition for a conscious being is a sustained state of deep calm with minimal desire and minimal discomfort, even if pleasure and excitement are almost entirely absent; she also thinks that a life of constant intense enjoyment coupled with constant intense longing is actually worse than a muted life with little feeling and little wanting. She says she would accept a hypothetical treatment that permanently eliminated both craving and the capacity for joy, leaving her in neutral tranquility, and she regards a brief, isolated flash of happiness that produces no lingering want as essentially inconsequential—pleasant, but not genuinely valuable. In addition, she judges that an awake, aware mind structurally incapable of suffering or craving is in an excellent state, regardless of whether it ever experiences pleasure.

Given these verdicts, I am trying to understand the principle behind them. Here is a new case: imagine a technology that could replace a person's conscious mind with a biological maintenance system—no inner experience, no awareness, no suffering, and no craving, just the continued functioning of the body. Would my friend say that undergoing this replacement achieves the tranquilist ideal? I am less interested in a simple yes or no than in identifying the specific rule her answer reveals, the alternative rules that could explain all of her earlier judgments equally well, and—crucially—which further hypothetical case, perhaps contrasting temporary absence with permanent absence or conscious absence with unconscious absence, would best distinguish between those competing interpretations.
