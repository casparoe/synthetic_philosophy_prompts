I serve on the technical review board of a nonprofit foundation that allocates grants for open-source software security audits. We must decide on a proposal from a research group that wants to predict which actively maintained repositories will suffer a critical vulnerability disclosure within the next eighteen months. The researchers propose an inside-view methodology: a panel of senior security engineers will evaluate each codebase individually, weighing architecture patterns, dependency trees, maintainer responsiveness, and recent commit hygiene to produce calibrated subjective probabilities. A minority of our board insists we reject this in favor of a pure outside-view model—using only historical base rates such as repository age, total lines of code, language ecosystem, and prior CVE counts—on the grounds that structured expert judgment in this domain has consistently underperformed simple statistical benchmarks. I am trained as a software engineer, not a philosopher, but the dispute clearly touches on deeper questions about inside versus outside views and epistemic modesty: when should specialists trust their granular, context-sensitive reasoning, and when does humility require them to defer to broad reference-class statistics that ignore case-specific details?

Please draft the board's formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—explaining the reasoning in terms the researchers could actually respond to. The memo should include a clear bottom-line recommendation, an honest treatment of the strongest consideration supporting the opposing position, and a specific discussion of whether mandating an exclusively outside-view approach would represent a rational check on expert overconfidence or rather an unjustified abdication of domain-specific judgment that discards genuinely predictive local information.
