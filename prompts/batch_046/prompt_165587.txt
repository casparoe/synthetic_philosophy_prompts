I am a novelist designing a single ninety-minute seminar for a small reading group of fellow writers—people fluent in narrative structure but without formal training in logic or mathematics—on the philosophical possibility that the basic axioms of mathematics might be inconsistent. I do not want an explanation of the concept itself; I want a teaching plan. In what order must the components be introduced, and what dependency holds between them: for instance, must the distinction between syntactic and semantic consistency precede any discussion of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, or can that distinction be deferred? Identify the one carrying example or analogy—perhaps a nested manuscript in which a mathematician-protagonist demonstrates the consistency of her own axiomatic system from within that very system, only to have the demonstration's validity depend on the very consistency it claims to establish—and specify exactly what this analogy must possess and what it must lack to avoid collapsing into a mere plot hole. Map the predictable misconceptions these writers will bring or form: I anticipate they will conflate "inconsistent axioms" with "an obvious arithmetic mistake anyone can correct," and that they will treat Gödel's result as having already proven mathematics broken rather than having shown that consistency cannot be internally verified. State precisely where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted. Provide one final question I can pose to determine whether the group understands that the threat is not a visible contradiction but a structural blindness that the system itself cannot detect. And indicate explicitly what must be omitted at this level—perhaps the full formalization of first-order logic—while acknowledging the omission.
