Write a short story that dramatizes John Taurek’s challenge to the intuition that, in a forced-choice rescue situation, saving the greater number is always morally obligatory. Avoid the standard lifeboat, trolley, or bridge-collapse frames; instead, set the dilemma in an original scenario—such as a conservator with limited energy deciding which clusters of endangered oral-history recordings (each containing the complete cognitive-emotional trace of a distinct person) to restore; a mediator distributing a singular synthesized antidote among separated settlements; or an archivist selecting which suspended-memory substrates to reawaken—so that the stakes involve lives or conscious existences without presenting visible bodies in immediate peril. The protagonist must face a genuine binary choice with no hidden third option. The narrative should make the reader feel both the pull of the aggregative intuition and Taurek’s counter-claim that each individual’s claim is equal and does not accumulate by multiplication, rendering the choice of the larger group potentially arbitrary rather than required. Do not resolve the tension with a simple moral lesson; end on an ambiguity that leaves the structural question open.

The Story (approximately 700–900 words): present the scenario, establish the protagonist’s knowledge of both options, dramatize the decision process without letting one intuition dominate, and conclude without a definitive justification.

Commentary on Choices (approximately 300 words): explain why you selected this particular framing, how the narrative maps onto Taurek’s structural argument (equal individual claims versus aggregative weight), which devices you used to keep both moral pulls alive, and where you deliberately avoided collapsing the dilemma into a standard utilitarian or deontological resolution.
