Take the 1858 Linnean Society meeting where Darwin and Wallace's papers were read together — Darwin's been sitting on natural selection for like twenty years, Wallace sends him that letter from Ternate, and suddenly they're co-presented. Analyze this through the 'inevitability thesis' about simultaneous discovery: if natural selection was genuinely in the air and Wallace was going to publish within a year anyway, does Darwin still deserve the lion's share of credit we give him?

But I want you to push past the usual 'great man vs inevitable' framing. There's this concept I've been messing with — call it 'epistemic surrogacy' — where the scientific community treats the first published version of an idea as a kind of placeholder for all the parallel versions that were about to emerge. The surrogate gets the citation network, but the idea's authority is actually distributed across the redundant discoverers. Apply that to Darwin and Wallace: was Darwin functioning as a surrogate for an inevitable 1858-1860 discovery cluster? Did Wallace's presence at the Linnean Society weaken Darwin's surrogacy or reinforce it?

Also, look at the actual mechanics of the episode — Lyell and Hooker rushing to arrange the joint presentation, Darwin's panic when he recieve's Wallace's letter, Wallace's graciousness — and say where the inevitability thesis explains those moves and where it just sounds like post-hoc rationalization. Does the fact that Darwin had a massive manuscript draft (the 1844 essay and the 1856 long version) change whether he was a surrogate or an actual originator?

Finally, what does this case do to the thesis itself? If we agree Darwin was the surrogate but also genuinely shaped the theory's reception in ways Wallace wouldn't have, is that an argument that simultaneous invention doesn't erase individual contribution — or does it just show that surrogates can be replaced without loss, except for historical accident?
