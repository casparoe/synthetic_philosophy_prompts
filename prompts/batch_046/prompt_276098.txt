After my brother was convicted of cannabis possession last year, I have been studying Portugal's decriminalization of personal drug possession and its relationship to legalization debates, and I have constructed an argument that troubles me because its conclusion seems intolerable, yet each step looks defensible. Here it is, with numbered premises. (1) Portugal decriminalized possession of all drugs, and subsequent public-health data showed reduced overdose mortality and stable consumption rates, undermining the claim that criminal penalties are necessary for harm reduction. (2) If criminal penalties are unnecessary for heroin and cocaine, they are unnecessary a fortiori for cannabis, which carries lower physiological risk. (3) But decriminalization alone preserves a black market with unregulated potency and adulteration; to realize harm reduction fully, the state must regulate supply, which requires legalization with state-controlled distribution. (4) The harm-reduction rationale applies with greatest force where unregulated supply is deadliest—namely, to opioids—so consistency demands that if we legalize cannabis on these grounds, we should be at least as willing to legalize and regulate heroin. (5) A state-sanctioned heroin distribution system is politically and ethically unacceptable, including to me. (6) Therefore, either the inference from (1) to (3) is illegitimate, or consistency requires accepting (4), or harm-based reasoning collapses into arbitrariness. I want to preserve (2) and (3) for cannabis specifically, but I cannot see which step to reject without paying a steep price for the broader framework. Which premise or inference fails, why did it appear valid, and what does abandoning it cost? I am especially interested in whether there is a hidden scope shift between negative liberty (removing punishment) and positive state provision (creating regulated markets) that does the real work, and whether that distinction can be maintained without collapsing into arbitrary line-drawing.
