I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind at Manchester, and I've been arguing with my mates about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — you know, the idea that our feeling of having rich inner experiences is basically a kind of cognitive misrepresentation rather than a direct window onto reality. I was discussing this over coffee last week and realised my gut reactions to cases are all over the place, and I'm not sure there's a consistent principle behind them. Could you help me figure out whether there is one, and which of my intuitions I probably have to drop?

Here are my verdicts. First, take blindsight patients: they navigate obstacles, guess shapes, but insist there's nothing it's like to see in the blind field. I think they're genuinely lacking phenomenal consciousness there — it's not that they have an illusion of absence, the absence is real. Second, imagine a perfect behavioural zombie: looks exactly like a conscious human, reports "I see red" with perfect contextual appropriateness, but there's no biological machinery associated with awareness and, if pressed, it admits there's nothing inside. My intuition is that it's genuinely empty, and any feeling I have that "there must be something" is exactly the illusion illusionism talks about. Third, the classic "overflow" situation: people claim they experience every detail of a complex scene in rich colour and texture, but experiments with masking and eye-tracking show only sparse, focal encoding. Here I think there is some real phenomenal consciousness — a kind of thin, focal awareness — but the belief in a richly detailed panoramic field is the illusion. Fourth, a gradual replacement scenario: over ten years every neuron in someone's brain is replaced by a functionally identical synthetic unit, and they report continuous experience throughout. I think consciousness is preserved, but the sense that there's one continuous "owner" riding through the replacement is reconstructed and therefore partly illusory.

Then there's a weirder case I've been thinking about — call it the chimeric stimulation case. A subject receives direct cortical stimulation that produces a report of seeing a colour they've never encountered before, with no improvement in discrimination tasks compared to familiar colours. I feel like there's probably a thin phenomenal "something" occuring, but the claim that it's genuinely novel and qualitatively unique is an illusion; it's just a misrepresented variant of an existing hue. Finally, the case I keep going back and forth on: an infant or a very simple artificial system that behaves sensitively to pain and pleasure stimuli but lacks any conceptual apparatus to represent "experience" or "feeling" to itself. Sometimes I think it has raw phenomenal consciousness without knowing it; other times I think that without the conceptual framing there's just behaviour, no illusion and no reality either — just absence.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all six verdicts? If not, which ones have to go to save simplicity? And specifically, what should I say about that last case — the being without self-representational concepts? Please write your answer in American English.
