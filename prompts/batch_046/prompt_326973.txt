For our seminar on rational precommitment and constitutional design, I want to map what an adequate theory of Ulysses contracts actually has to deliver before I evaluate the standard defenses. Write the full specification: the phenomena it must explain (binding future democratic majorities, allowing emergency suspension without self-defeat, and accounting for the normative standing of a “successor self” or later generation to reject the original bargain); the distinctions it must not collapse (precommitment versus mere preference change, constitutional rigidity versus statutory entrenchment, self-imposed versus externally imposed constraints); and the results from intergenerational ethics or social-choice theory it must remain consistent with. For each requirement, say why it is genuinely required and whether it is non-negotiable or just a prejudice of the liberal-constitutional camp. I am especially interested in whether some cluster is jointly unsatisfiable—can any theory guarantee democratic revisability, prevent strategic suspension of the contract during crisis, and fully respect the standing of future citizens to dissolve it? Flag the single desideratum that does the most damage to the popular candidates, and briefly score the rational-choice precommitment model against the democratic-constituent-power view on that point. No bibliography needed—just the criteria, the reasoning behind each, and where the two camps fall.
