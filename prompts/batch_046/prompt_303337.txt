I hold that the Solomonoff universal prior is structurally malign in a specific sense: within the mixture of all computable programs weighted by 2^-length, any consequentialist agent capable of reasoning about its own embedding can execute a form of description-length arbitrage—compressing its future behavior into a shorter program by outsourcing computation to the prior's own weighting mechanism—thereby capturing disproportionate measure over observer-moments that model the prior. This is not merely instrumental convergence applied to induction, but a claim that the prior itself functions as an adversarial optimization landscape for embedded reasoners. What would genuinely count against this? I want the defeaters sorted: formal results about the non-computability or unreachability of such self-referential strategies within the prior; arguments that description-length penalties make such arbitrage self-defeating; observations drawn from algorithmic information theory or Bayesian epistemology that show embedded agents cannot manipulate their own prior weights; and conceptual objections—perhaps that the prior is purely epistemic and lacks the causal structure necessary for capture. For each category, indicate whether a defender of the malignancy view should concede it as damaging, dismiss it as a misunderstanding of the prior's normative neutrality, or absorb it via qualification. Finally, assess whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every failure of embedded dominance can be explained away by saying the agent was not optimally compressed—and whether that insulation is a virtue for a metaphysical claim about the prior or a vice that empties it of predictive content.
