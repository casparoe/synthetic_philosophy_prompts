I am drafting a literary novel in which an anthropologist-linguist, working in the 1950s without bilingual intermediaries or prior ethnographic archives, attempts to construct a translation manual for an isolated valley community whose cosmology treats the earth as a flat disc suspended within a crystalline firmament—a belief integrated consistently across their navigation, ritual calendars, and everyday causal explanations. To render her intellectual crisis with philosophical precision rather than exotic spectacle, I need a rigorous but intuitive explanation of a specific conceptual tension within Donald Davidson's theory of radical interpretation.

Specifically, I want to understand whether the principle of charity operates merely as a pragmatic heuristic for the interpreter or as a constitutive condition that makes the attribution of belief and meaning possible in the first place, and—crucially—whether that principle requires her to maximize agreement with her own physical beliefs, thereby forcing her to interpret the flat-disc cosmology as metaphor, widespread error, or cognitive dysfunction. Please explain, in technically precise but conceptually intuitive terms, how Davidson's framework distinguishes between localized, domain-specific falsehoods that can be stably attributed within an otherwise rational belief-system and the kind of global irrationality that threatens interpretability altogether, and clarify what this distinction implies for my character's decision to treat the cosmology as a coherent, genuine worldview rather than as evidence of a breakdown in mental functioning.
