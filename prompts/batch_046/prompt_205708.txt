In rationalist and AI-safety circles, the “alignment problem” has been argued over for more than a decade, yet the core disagreement feels remarkably stable. One cluster treats alignment as a robustness and control problem: the task is to specify an objective that survives distribution shift, avoid reward hacking, and maintain corrigibility under recursive improvement, with the working assumption that normative questions can be layered on once the technical mechanisms are reliable. A second cluster—often with philosophical or social-scientific training—treats alignment as primarily a problem of normative authority: before stabilizing any objective, one must ask whose values are aggregated, how to resolve deep moral uncertainty, and whether “human values” is a coherent target or a reification of present preferences. A third cluster focuses on structural dynamics—instrumental convergence, deceptive alignment, emergent agency—arguing that the danger is not misspecification of values but the inevitable generation of misaligned subgoals regardless of the top-level objective.

Despite shared vocabulary, these groups talk past one another. The technical cluster answers questions about generalization and corrigibility and treats normative authority as separable; the normative cluster insists authority is prior and that solving control without it is actively dangerous; the structural cluster thinks emergent misbehavior dominates regardless of specification or authority. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a hidden empirical or formal disagreement in disguise, but that “alignment” functions as an umbrella for at least three distinct questions: (1) a statistical-learning question about preference generalization; (2) a game-theoretic question about deceptive capability and corrigibility; and (3) a normative-political question about legitimate authority over future systems.
