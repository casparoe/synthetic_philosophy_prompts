As a novelist whose current manuscript turns on a treaty negotiation between societies with irreconcilable metaphysical commitments about land and personhood, I am convening a single 90-minute workshop for twelve graduate students—half in philosophy, half in fiction writing—to help them inhabit the logic of moral compromise. Design a structured role-play in which two groups must jointly design an institution (for example, a shared water-rights council) without either being permitted to translate its core values into the other's vocabulary. The rules should force them to discover, through bargaining rather than lecture, what is gained in coordination and what is irreversibly lost in translation—specifically, the phenomenon of moral residue, where a justified compromise still leaves a legitimate grievance unaddressed. Provide assigned roles embedding conflicting axiologies, a timeline with decision gates, an asymmetric payoff structure that rewards coordination but penalizes value-betrayal differently for each side, a debrief mapping specific bargaining moves onto the structure of axiological trade, an honest prediction of what will go wrong the first time this is run and how to correct it, and a frank assessment of which feature of true incommensurability—namely, the absence of any common metric for comparing losses—this format necessarily falsifies and I must supply myself.
