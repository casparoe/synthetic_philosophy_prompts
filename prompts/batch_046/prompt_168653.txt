As a retired biologist who has recently begun studying philosophy of science, I heard the following summary of the debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function, presented by a colleague in an interdisciplinary discussion group. He said: on the etiological view, a trait's function is determined entirely by its evolutionary history—specifically, by the effects for which natural selection originally favored it—so that even if a heart now produces a hormone that aids digestion, that hormonal contribution is not part of the heart's biological function, because selection did not shape the heart for digestion. On the causal-role view, by contrast, function is fixed solely by whatever a trait presently contributes to some capacity of the system that contains it, which implies that if the heart's hormone production genuinely contributes to digestive capacity, then digestion automatically becomes part of the heart's function, irrespective of evolutionary origin. From these premises he drew two conclusions: first, that causal-role theories collapse the distinction between genuine function and accidental benefit, since any beneficial side-effect is thereby counted as functional; and second, that etiological theories alone can ground the normative concept of malfunction, because only a historical standard of selection can justify the claim that a trait ought to perform its original task.

The framing strikes me as conceptually tidy, but I worry it may conflate distinctions that careful defenders of each position preserve. Would you audit the summary? Specifically: (a) does the etiological account really exclude every present contribution that was not originally selected for, or do some versions allow for derived, exapted, or co-opted functions? (b) does the causal-role account genuinely treat every present contribution as functional, or does it impose further conditions—such as statistical typicality for the kind, integration into the system's characteristic organization, or contribution to a specifically designated capacity—that would prevent accidental benefits from being promoted to functions? (c) is it true that only etiological views can support judgments of malfunction, or have causal-role theorists developed normative resources—perhaps through reference to typical performance, organizational norms, or design-relative standards—to account for dysfunction without appealing to selection history? And (d) does the summary's implicit recommendation—that working biologists ought to reject causal-role accounts in favor of etiological ones—actually follow once these corrections are made, or might the two frameworks address different explanatory needs and therefore be complementary rather than mutually exclusive?
