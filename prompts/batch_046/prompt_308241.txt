I'm a PhD student in cultural evolution and I keep running into the same argumentative move in papers on costly signaling, but I don't know if it has a proper name. The pattern goes like this: first a researcher points to some wasteful human practice—say, extreme ritual scarification or risky adolescent displays—and says it's an honest signal of fitness to potential mates or rivals, exactly the handicap principle at work. Then critics show that in the actual social setting there's no observable audience who rewards the behavior, or that people who do it don't actually recieve higher status or reproductive success. Instead of dropping the signaling frame, the defender shifts the audience inward: now it's self-signaling, or signaling commitment to the in-group, or building credibility for cultural transmission. The word "costly" stays, but what it's costly *for* and who it's honest *to* keep changing. This feels different from just adding an auxillary hypothesis because the core mechanism—wasteful display as honest indicator—survives intact while every condition that made it honest gets relocated. Is there an established concept for this specific three-step retreat? If not, can you coin one that carves it precisely: what makes it distinct from ordinary theory-protection, from a motte-and-bailey, and from simple redefinition, and where exactly its edges sit? Please write in continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—just like a long email or letter. I don't need citations or a reading list, just the carving and the distinctions.
