I am constructing a novel about a scholar-nun who, after defending women’s right to study, is compelled to sell her instruments and fall silent; to render her decision with moral precision, I need you to analyze the historical episode of Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz in the 1690s through the lens of her own philosophy of women’s education, as articulated in her defense against episcopal censure. The salient facts are these: after publishing a theological critique that provoked hostility, she wrote a celebrated defense arguing that study is not vanity but a natural obligation of reason, that denying women education is a tyranny over the soul, and that intellectual labor and devotion are not only compatible but mutually reinforcing. Within a few years, however, she renewed her vows, sold her library and scientific instruments, and largely ceased writing, dying shortly afterward while nursing her sisters during an epidemic.

Using that framework, I want you to do three things. First, apply her theory to her own case: by her lights, what should she have done when confronted with the Bishop’s demand that she abandon secular study? Does her eventual renunciation confirm her theory—as an act of obedience that frees the soul from vanity—or contradict it, by internalizing the very tyranny over reason she had denounced? Second, identify what her theory explains well about the episode—perhaps why she chose to defend herself in writing rather than orally, or how she framed study as divine service—and where it is silent or inadequate, particularly regarding institutional gendered power, the economic structure of the convent, or the physical violence of epidemics. Third, I want you to introduce and deploy a concept of your own: treat her sale of her books and instruments not merely as ascetic humility but as a form of epistemic dispossession, an alienation of her own cognitive apparatus. Ask whether her theory, which treats knowledge as an extension of the soul rather than property, can accommodate such dispossession without reducing it to spiritual virtue, and say what the episode therefore reveals about the limits of framing education solely as individual moral vocation. Be disciplined about the mapping: do not force her theory to speak where it has nothing to say, and say explicitly where it is silent.
