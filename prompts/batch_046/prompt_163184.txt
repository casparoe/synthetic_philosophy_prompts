Analyze the Terminal Classic transformation of the southern Maya lowlands—specifically the simultaneous cessation of dynastic monument production at Copán around 822 CE, the end of dated inscriptions at Tikal by the late ninth century, and the subsequent northward and highland shift of settlement into the Puuc hills and central Mexican plateau—through a three-layer framework that distinguishes (i) the governing superstructure (divine kingship, calendrical monumentality, long-distance obsidian and jade exchange networks, and inter-polity alliance rituals); (ii) the agrarian metabolism (milpa and terrace-based maize production, localized water management, regional population distribution, and household-level craft specialization); and (iii) the ecological substrate (tropical soil phosphorus depletion, prolonged drought intervals documented in lake-core sequences, and deforestation feedback loops). Do not treat collapse as a unitary event; instead, map precisely which layers underwent discontinuous rupture, which underwent gradual reconfiguration with altered parameters, and which persisted largely intact. In particular, address whether the disappearance of dated stelae, the abandonment of Copán’s acropolis, and the demographic contraction of Tikal’s urban core represent a failure of Maya society per se, or merely the dissolution of one organizational form—the Classic-period divine-king polity—while the underlying social metabolism and ecological relationships continued in a lower-complexity, decentralized equilibrium. Where does this layered analysis expose gaps in the standard collapse-versus-change binary? Does it reveal that the binary illicitly conflates political decapitation with societal termination, and what specific conditions—say, a synchronous failure of layer (ii) through widespread soil exhaustion combined with layer (iii) through irreversible hydrological collapse—would have been necessary to turn this reconfiguration into a genuine systemic collapse rather than a transformation? Finally, say what the episode shows about the concept of collapse itself: is it a descriptive category that applies whenever layer (i) fails, or does it require evidence that layers (ii) and (iii) could no longer support human settlement at comparable densities?
