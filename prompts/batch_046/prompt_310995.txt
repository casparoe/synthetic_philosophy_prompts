For a philosophy of mind seminar I am trying to write up my commitments on what emotions are, and I want an audit of whether they actually cohere before I present them. Here is my package: (1) I take emotions to be essentially judgments of value that operate as global stance-modulators—restructuring the salience landscape of a situation rather than registering discrete object-properties—so that fear, for instance, is not a feeling caused by danger but an evaluative posture that makes danger salient; (2) I hold that first-person phenomenological reports carry strong authoritative weight, so that when I sincerely report feeling indignant my report is more epistemically secure than any third-party behavioral or neural observation; (3) I also accept that basic affective states—raw dimensions of arousal and valence with characteristic physiological signatures—are present in pre-linguistic infants and in many non-human animals, and that these states are genuinely directed at situations without requiring conceptual articulation; (4) I think emotional responses are rationally correctable in principle: if I discover my anger rests on a false belief about someone’s intention, conceptual reasoning alone can dissolve it without requiring any separate bodily or affective intervention; (5) I reject perceptual theories of emotion, on the ground that perception is passive and lacks the normative, directed-evaluative structure that makes an emotional state about something in the right way; and (6) I believe emotional maturity consists not in suppressing feeling but in aligning one’s conceptual judgments with underlying core affect patterns, so that reasoning eventually reshapes the physiological-affective substrate rather than leaving it untouched.

Do these hang together? I am especially unsure whether my judgment-based, stance-modulating account can accommodate the pre-conceptual animal and infant affect I accept in (3), and whether my claim in (4) that reasoning alone can correct emotion conflicts with (6)’s insistence that maturity requires alignment between judgment and core affect. I also wonder if there is an unnoticed tension between (2)’s phenomenological authority and (4)’s correctability, or between (5)’s rejection of perceptual directedness and (3)’s acceptance of directed basic affect in animals. Could you go through the commitments pair by pair, distinguish genuine tensions from merely apparent ones, rank the real tensions by severity, specify what the cheapest revision would be in each case and what it costs, and then tell me which single change to the package would clean up the most inconsistencies? Please do not treat any commitment as obviously dispensable—I genuinely do not know which should give.
