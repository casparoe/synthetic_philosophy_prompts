I've been trying to figure out whether one "I" can genuinely be spread across more than one brain, especially given the idea that a self might persist in others if the right mental patterns are preserved. Here are my judgments on specific cases, and I want to know whether a single principle can cover them all, or whether I have to abandon one.

If every neuron in my brain were gradually replaced by synthetic equivalents that kept every connection and activity pattern intact, I think I would still be the same "I"; the organizational pattern seems to matter more than the original matter.

But if scientists made a perfect copy of my brain in another body while I kept living, I think there would now be two separate selves, not one distributed across both bodies. The copy might think and feel like me, yet it wouldn't be me.

Now imagine two people undergo a long medical procedure that slowly interweaves parts of their brains so that memories and feelings flow freely between them, though each still has one body. Here I feel there could genuinely be a single distributed self spanning both bodies, not merely two people with shared access.

Yet consider someone with severe dementia who retains almost no psychological continuity with their past life and has a radically different personality; I still think it is the same person. So bodily or biological continuity seems able to preserve an "I" even when mental patterns are largely broken.

Finally, suppose a patient receives transplanted tissue from another person's prefrontal cortex, so that roughly half their decisions now come from someone else's psychology, even though all their old memories stay intact. I cannot tell whether this is one altered self or a kind of merged, distributed one.

These five verdicts pull in different directions: pattern continuity saves the synthetic-replacement case but fails to unify the copy case; distribution feels possible in the interwoven case but is unclear in the transplant case; and dementia suggests an "I" can survive even without psychological continuity. Is there one coherent principle—perhaps about a single center of narrative integration or a unified first-person point of view—that delivers all five judgments? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the best available principle predict for a case I haven't listed, such as a computer system that gradually takes over one person's reasoning processes while the biological brain remains intact?
