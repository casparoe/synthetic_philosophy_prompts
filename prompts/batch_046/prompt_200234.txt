Write a poem of about eighteen lines in blank verse, spoken in the first person by a cognitive process that initially believes itself to be the sole audience in a Cartesian theater — a single privileged point where sensory reports converge into one conscious scene. Over the course of the poem the speaker should discover that there is no central auditorium and no final premiere: instead, parallel drafts are edited, revised, and sometimes overwritten without any director. The syntax should enact this absence of center: keep promising a main clause that would bring the experience all together at a definite moment, then defer or replace it with a subordinate clause representing a rival draft or a retroactive revision, as when a later report changes what seemed just to have happened. Around line nine or ten, include the recognition that another version of the event has already achieved wider fame in the brain, so that there is no fact of the matter about exactly when the speaker’s version became conscious. Do not resolve the tension into triumph or despair; end on an image of distributed editing — perhaps memos circulating without a recipient. The verse should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Dennett’s critique recognizes the specific move against a privileged present moment, yet it should work as poetry for someone who knows nothing of philosophy of mind.
