Here are the notes for an essay I am drafting for my philosophy coursework on Russell's paradox and the limits of naive set theory. Rather than treating the paradox solely as a logical failure of unrestricted comprehension, I argue that it reveals an "epistemic boundary problem" in ontology identification: one may have full access to the members of a collection and still be informationally closed with respect to whether that collection contains itself. From this, I propose an "informational closure principle"—that we ought not commit ontologically to any set whose membership conditions depend on information that is, in principle, inaccessible to the identifying agent. I briefly extend this to digital artefacts, where source-code members are visible but execution-state membership is not, suggesting that naive set-theoretic thinking collapses precisely because it ignores this asymmetry.

Please comment on whether my interpretation of the paradox as an epistemic boundary is accurate or whether it misrepresents Russell's original concern, and assess whether the informational closure principle is philosophically coherent or merely conflates epistemology with ontology. I ask for a committed response: take a definite position on whether the argument succeeds, give your reasons plainly, and avoid simply listing pros and cons without reaching a verdict. If you think it fails, identify the exact point of failure; if you think it succeeds, say precisely what it adds beyond the standard logical resolution offered by ZFC.
