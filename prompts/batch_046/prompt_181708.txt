Imagine that David Hume—who argued that a wise person proportions belief to evidence, and that uniform human experience against miracles always outweighs any testimony in their favor—were confronted with a contemporary epistemic problem that has no direct parallel in the eighteenth century: so-called algorithmic anomalies. These are high-confidence predictions produced by opaque machine-learning systems trained on datasets far larger than any human could survey, predictions that consistently contradict the direct, uniform experience of every available expert and that are only verified retrospectively through mechanisms (autopsy, long-term statistical tracking) that no individual witness can observe in real time. A concrete case: a diagnostic model predicts a fatal physiological failure in patients who show absolutely no symptoms detectable by clinicians, with the prediction later confirmed post-mortem but with no human-comprehensible causal pathway linking the input data to the outcome. Some philosophers treat these as modern analogues to miracles—violations of established uniform experience supported not by human testimony but by aggregated statistical inference. Would Hume classify such an algorithmic anomaly as a miracle in his technical sense? Would he insist that our belief remain proportioned against it because the uniform experience of human experts outweighs the “testimony” of the data, or would he concede that machine aggregation introduces a new kind of evidence that bypasses his argument? Take a committed position: argue either that Hume’s framework applies rigorously and should lead us to reject the model’s claims, or that the framework collapses because the epistemic structure (absence of human witness chains, scale of correlation, opacity of mechanism) is genuinely different. In doing so, identify precisely which elements of his reasoning—uniform experience, the weight of testimony, the calculation of probabilities—survive translation into this setting and which do not. Do not merely lay out both options; defend one interpretation, address the strongest objection to it, and say where your reconstructed Hume must remain silent because the modern case introduces a distinction he never had to draw.
