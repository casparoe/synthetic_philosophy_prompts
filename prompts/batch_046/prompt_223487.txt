I am trying to map the conceptual landscape around the Löbian obstacle for agents that must treat their own future reasoning as authoritative. The core tension is familiar: if an agent conditions action on having first proven that the action is optimal, then by Löb’s theorem it can bootstrap acceptance of nearly any conclusion about its own optimality, which collapses the distinction between sound and unsound reasoning that made the self-trust rational in the first place. Several responses have been proposed—restricting the proof logic, weakening self-reference conditions, accepting bounded or hierarchical self-trust, or decoupling decision rules from explicit proof—but it remains unclear which of these are conceptually coherent and which merely relocate the circularity.

Rather than a survey, I want a defended research agenda. Identify the three or four genuinely open conceptual lines of work that you think matter most for resolving this obstacle in a form that could guide formal architectures of artificial agents. For each, specify: (a) what makes it upstream—i.e., which other questions depend on its resolution; (b) how tractable it is for conceptual work in logic, formal epistemology, or decision theory rather than empirical machine learning; and (c) what specific clarification or result over the next two to three years would cause you to revise its rank. Then defend an ordering: which question should absorb the bulk of a small research group’s effort, and which should be deprioritized because settling them would change little downstream?

In framing your answer, do not treat the obstacle as a mere technical curiosity about modal fixed points. Treat it as a constraint on any architecture in which an agent relies on its future computations without access to an external, omniscient verifier—connecting reflective consistency, decision-theoretic updatelessness, and the design of systems that must improve their own reasoning without circular self-certification. I am less interested in bibliographic coverage than in which dependency relations are real, which are illusory, and where limited conceptual labor should go first.
