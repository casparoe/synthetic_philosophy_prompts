I'm a senior logistics compliance officer for an international relief org, and we keep running into the same nightmare: local law or our own ethical protocols say we can't do X — cross a border without permits, distribute supplies outside the registered chain, withhold info from local partners — but if we don't, people die in a specific, immediate way. I was discussing with my collegue — she studied some philosophy in grad school — about whether there's any principled way to decide when breaking a rule is actually the right call, not just we got away with it. She mentioned Kierkegaard's thing with Abraham, the teleological suspension of the ethical, where the universal moral law gets suspended for the sake of an absolute relation. I want to turn that into an actual decision procedure my team can run in about 30 minutes before we sign off on an emergency exception, something reusable rather than a one-off gut call.

The procedure needs to keep the actual structure of the idea, not collapse into greater-good reasoning. So it should have steps in this order: (1) name the specific universal ethical norm being suspended (e.g., transparency, sovereignty, distributive fairness) and why it applies here; (2) name the telos — the absolute end — that is supposed to justify it, with a check that this isn't just it's really important but something that relates to the mission in a direct, non-substitutable way; (3) run the knight check — are we in infinite resignation (giving up the ethical for something we know we'll lose or can't justify) or in faith (where we expect to receive the ethical back in some transformed way); (4) the absurdity / silence checkpoint — can this justification be publicly articulated and defended, or does it remain internal and silent? Because for Kierkegaard the knight of faith can't explain himself to the universal, so in a bureaucratic setting this should flag a specific risk; (5) the sign-off point — exactly where the checklist ends and a specific person has to own the decision with a signature, with the procedure explicitly stating that this step is a value judgment, not a logistics fact. At each step say what counts as a sufficient answer, what stops the process with a no, do not suspend, and where judgment can't be eliminated.

Then apply it to three real-style proposals: (a) air-dropping vaccines into a region where the government banned foreign medical aid but an outbreak is killing people; (b) diverting 90% of our food stock to one besieged village with high child mortality, leaving other registered camps with nothing for a week; and (c) forging customs documentation to get surgical kits across a closed border, knowing that if we're caught the entire regional operation gets expelled permanently. Case (b) should probably come out okay, case (c) should come out in a way I won't like — maybe the procedure approves the suspension but the silence/absurdity checkpoint fails in a bureaucratic context, meaning it can't actually be signed off cleanly. Give a worked example for each showing exactly what gets filled in. Don't ask for citations or a reading list — I don't have web access and I just need the procedure. Please keep the weird Kierkegaardian parts (the absurd, the silence, the double movement) as explicit steps rather than stripping them out.
