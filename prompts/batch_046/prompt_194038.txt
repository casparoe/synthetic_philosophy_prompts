Having recently been appointed to a municipal legislative review board, I was asked last month to evaluate a proposed ordinance that restricts certain public displays not on grounds of direct physical or economic harm, but on the grounds that they erode the shared moral environment that residents consider essential to communal life. This experience has made the classic disagreement between H.L.A. Hart and Patrick Devlin urgently personal: Hart maintains that the criminal law should not enforce private morality in the absence of demonstrable harm to others, while Devlin contends that a society's collective moral fabric is itself a public good that law may legitimately protect. Yet after revisiting the exchange, I am struck by how little either position has yielded. Every defense of the harm principle seems to presuppose a sharp distinction between harm and moral offense that Devlin's side rejects, and every defense of legal moralism seems to presuppose that social cohesion is causally fragile in ways Hart's side denies. Is this stalemate genuinely substantive—perhaps turning on an empirical question about whether visible moral deviation actually undermines social stability—or is it largely verbal, with harm functioning as a normative boundary rather than a descriptive criterion? Alternatively, are the two sides answering different questions altogether: Hart about the legitimate limits of state coercion in a liberal society, Devlin about the conditions necessary for a democratic culture to reproduce itself? I want a diagnosis, not a survey of secondary literature. Specifically: what kind of disagreement is this at bottom—factual, conceptual, normative, or rooted in bedrock intuitions that neither side can justify to the other? Is there a hidden empirical claim—say, about the causal link between legal tolerance of deviance and social disintegration—that has never been properly tested and that, if resolved, could move the debate? Or is the disagreement essentially about incompatible starting commitments regarding whether collective moral self-definition can ever justify restricting individual liberty? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a refined conceptual distinction between harm and offense, a sociological or historical finding, a reframing that separates the coercive question from the cultural one, or should we conclude that the question is ill-posed and expect no resolution?
