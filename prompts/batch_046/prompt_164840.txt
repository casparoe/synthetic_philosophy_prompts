I have been trying to determine my actual stance on capital punishment not by adopting a named doctrine but by cataloguing my intuitive verdicts across concrete scenarios and seeing whether they cohere. In a discussion with friends about a philosophy-of-punishment course, I found that my reactions split sharply in ways I cannot yet justify. Here they are. First, a contract killer who planned a murder for payment, shows no remorse, and is convicted with absolute certainty: I think execution is morally permissible, and perhaps even required, though I do not think life imprisonment would be unjust. Second, an offender who killed during a severe psychotic episode—legally sane, but profoundly disturbed and incapable of appreciating the wrongness in the usual way—deserves life imprisonment at most; execution strikes me as plainly wrong. Third, if a jurisdiction applies the death penalty with a systemic error rate of even one innocent execution per five hundred, and this is driven by prosecutorial overreach rather than unavoidable accident, I regard the entire institution as morally bankrupt, regardless of any deterrent benefit it might produce. Fourth, imagine a hypothetical system with literally perfect epistemic accuracy—no risk of wrongful conviction whatsoever—applied narrowly to the worst premeditated murders: I am tentatively inclined to permit it, but I feel uneasy if the justification is purely symbolic and carries no protective or deterrent weight. Fifth, take that same perfect-accuracy system but apply it broadly across all first-degree murders, including those that do not seem uniquely heinous, and assume further that it produces no measurable deterrent effect beyond life without parole: I think it is wrong. Sixth, in an actual individual case where guilt is 99.9 percent certain but a novel genetic interpretation leaves a residual 0.1 percent ambiguity, I believe execution must be stayed; that sliver of risk is decisive, even though I would accept life imprisonment on the same evidence. Seventh, if a jurisdiction justifies execution solely on statistical grounds—that each execution prevents several future murders—while the executed offender is not more blameworthy than others who receive life sentences, I reject the practice; deterrence alone does not seem sufficient to me.

These judgments feel stable, yet they pull in different directions. The risk of error seems to function as an absolute veto in the individual case, yet in the hypothetical perfect-accuracy scenario I do not treat its absence as an automatic green light unless the offender is among the worst. I also seem to require something like retributive desert for execution to be permissible, yet in the worst-offender case I am unsure whether desert alone—without any deterrent or incapacitative contribution—can carry the full weight. Is there one principle, or a small family of principles, that can deliver all seven verdicts without contrivance? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does the revised principle predict for a case I have not considered: a society with perfect accuracy and no other severe punishments, where execution is applied exclusively to the worst offenders but serves purely as an expression of collective condemnation, with no expectation that it protects anyone or deters anyone? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which of my cases, note its predictions for further variations—such as a system that executes only when both desert and strong deterrence are present but carries a small risk of error—and be explicit about which of my original verdicts it cannot accommodate.
