I am writing an essay comparing disjunctivist responses to the argument from illusion across sensory modalities, and I want to check whether I have grasped the core metaphysical commitment. In the visual case, the argument from illusion notes that a veridical perception of a straight stick and an illusory perception of a bent stick in water are subjectively indistinguishable; from this it infers a shared mental component—traditionally a sense-datum, more recently a representational content—that is present in both. The disjunctivist denies any such highest common factor, maintaining that veridical seeing is a direct relational state to an external object, whereas the illusory case is not a seeing at all but a different mental kind altogether. I am now extending this framework to bodily awareness and phantom limb phenomena. When an amputee reports sharp pain in a missing hand, there is no limb present to serve as the object of a direct relational state. So on a consistent disjunctivist view, it seems there cannot be any phenomenal experience of pain occurring—only a false judgment or belief that one is experiencing pain, since the requisite object is absent. If that is right, then disjunctivism appears to entail that all hallucinatory states, visual or otherwise, lack phenomenal character entirely and reduce to cognitive error. But this seems hard to reconcile with the fact that subjects genuinely suffer in phantom limb cases, and with neuroscience showing that hallucinations and veridical perceptions recruit overlapping cortical circuits: if there is no mental state in the hallucinatory case, what exactly are those neural patterns realizing? Is my interpretation correct—that disjunctivism eliminates phenomenology from every non-veridical case—or does it allow for a non-relational phenomenal state that is still genuinely experiential?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
