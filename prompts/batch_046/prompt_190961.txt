I am designing a diagnostic handout for a graduate seminar on early modern philosophy, and I want an instrument that sorts interpretations of what Descartes proves in the cogito argument—without asking students to name theories they have read about. Please construct a formal decision tree or taxonomy whose root is the commitment that some mental occurrence is indubitable, and whose branches separate positions by concrete answers to logical and metaphysical questions rather than by familiar labels.

Begin with the root question: if one grants that an act of thinking is presently occurring, does that grant license to infer the existence of anything other than the occurrence itself? From there, branch first on whether the subject term refers to a persisting substance, a temporary bundle of perceptions, or merely an indexical grammatical placeholder with no referential weight. For each of those three branches, add a further split asking whether the proof establishes temporal continuity of the entity in question, or only an instantaneous snapshot with no entailment about persistence. The leaves should include: a substantialist interpretation that treats the inference as proving an immaterial thinker; a minimal event-interpretation that denies any subject beyond the happening of the thought; a bundle-style view that accepts the existence of the perceptions but treats the thinker as a constructed fiction; a performative-authority reading that takes the argument to establish first-person epistemic privilege rather than metaphysical existence; and one logically possible but rarely occupied combination—identify its location in the tree and briefly explain whether its emptiness is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or simple neglect. Each internal node should be phrased as a concrete claim a respondent could accept or reject without knowing which named view it leads to; avoid nodes that ask whether one is a dualist or whether one agrees with Descartes.

Finally, inject one somewhat niche distinction to prevent the tree from collapsing into standard textbook categories: include a branch that distinguishes epistemic priority from ontological independence, asking specifically whether accepting that thinking is known before bodily existence is known commits one to the claim that the thinker could exist without a body, or only to the weaker claim that bodily existence remains epistemically open. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves that your branching logic cannot separate, and ensure that every leaf carries a one-sentence statement of its most awkward philosophical commitment. The deliverable should be polished, self-contained prose suitable for classroom use, and it must not request bibliographies, citations, named scholars, or lists of further reading.
