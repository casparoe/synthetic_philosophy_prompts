A colleague has pressed me with an argument about dispositions, finks, and masks that seems to show the standard counterfactual analysis collapses into contradiction once we take physical complexity seriously. He introduces a framing he calls interference saturation—the idea that in the modal neighborhood of any actual situation, conditions that would realize a fink or mask are so densely distributed that no genuinely nearby world is free of them. His reasoning runs as follows.

(1) To have a disposition D to φ in conditions C is, on the standard analysis, to be such that in the nearest possible worlds where C holds and no interfering factor operates, one φs.

(2) Define the modal sphere of an actual object O as the set of worlds that differ from actuality only by minor physical variations—a shifted particle, a slight temperature change, a microscopic structural flaw.

(3) For any O, any behavior φ, and any conditions C, the sphere contains worlds in which a fink or mask prevents φ-ing; call this interference saturation.

(4) If interference saturation holds, then within the sphere there are no worlds in which C holds and O is genuinely free of interference; every nearby world either fails to realize C or includes an interference.

(5) Therefore, applying the analysis in (1) requires selecting worlds outside the sphere, where the differences from actuality are no longer minor.

(6) But if the counterfactual link between O and φ-ing must traverse a large modal gap, the disposition loses its explanatory and predictive purchase on O's actual and near-actual behavior.

(7) Yet we invoke dispositions precisely to explain and predict behavior across small physical variations—salt dissolving in water a few degrees warmer, glass breaking under marginally greater stress.

(8) So either interference saturation is false, leaving unexplained why clean worlds should be nearer than interfering ones, or dispositions are not counterfactual properties but primitive modal ones whose connection to behavior is direct and non-analytical.

(9) The primitive-property option, however, makes finks and masks inexplicable: if D is a primitive feature of O, an external factor should be unable to nullify it, just as a physical barrier cannot nullify a logical entailment.

(10) Hence the standard view—that dispositions are real, explanatory, counterfactual, and compatible with occasional fink or mask failure—is inconsistent.

My colleague suspects the hidden slip occurs between (3) and (4), moving from the presence of interfering worlds within the sphere to the absence of clean worlds there. I want to preserve (7) and the reality of both finks and masks, but I am unsure whether saving them forces me to abandon the counterfactual analysis entirely. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked plausible, and price the alternatives: what does giving up (1) cost in terms of explaining interference, and what does giving up (4) cost in terms of the notion of modal proximity?
