Construct a thought experiment in legal philosophy that engineers a precise divergence between H.L. Hart's legal positivism and Lon Fuller's "internal morality of law," set within a realistic modern regulatory context—such as public-health emergency powers or climate-adaptation mandates—rather than the classic retroactive-punishment scenario. Design a single statute that satisfies Hart's criteria for legal validity: enacted by a recognized authority under an accepted rule of recognition, part of a system of primary and secondary rules, and applied by officials within that system. At the same time, the statute must violate exactly one of Fuller's eight procedural principles of legality other than retroactivity or physical impossibility of compliance; specify which principle is violated and describe the failure concretely (for instance, a promulgation defect in which the authoritative text differs from the version distributed to the affected public; an internal contradiction between two operative provisions; a failure of congruence between the declared rule and the way officials actually apply it; or excessive instability through rapid, unannounced amendments).

The substantive aim of the statute must correspond to Hart's "minimum content of natural law": it protects basic human survival, social cooperation, or security from serious harm, so that its moral purpose is genuinely commendable rather than malicious. Then construct a specific case for application in which a defendant's literal obedience to the statute would cause severe, foreseeable harm to a vulnerable third party, while treating the statute as non-law because of its procedural defect and refusing to apply it would cause a different, comparably serious harm to another party. Present the scenario in one paragraph, identifying the defendant, the official action required, and the competing harms.

After presenting the scenario, provide three analyses:
(a) The verdict Hart's separation thesis delivers—distinguishing the question "Is this law?" from "Is this law morally acceptable?"—and whether a court should apply the statute despite the harm it produces in this case.
(b) The verdict Fuller's internal morality delivers—whether the procedural defect strips the enactment of legal status altogether, and what a court guided by Fuller's view should do with the case.
(c) The verdict a substantive natural-law theorist delivers—whether the moral commendability of the statute's purpose can overcome its procedural failure, or whether the procedural failure vitiates its binding force regardless of content.

Next, identify the structural feature that does the work in forcing these three positions apart: is it the separation of validity and morality, the specific procedural failure, or the moral stakes of the content? Explain what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed—specifically, if the procedural defect is cured but the moral dilemma in application remains, or if the content is morally neutral rather than protective.

Finally, explain how a defender of the theory that comes out worst in your scenario—choose either Hart's positivism or Fuller's procedural natural law—would attack the case as mischaracterized or unfair, and why that attack either succeeds or fails.

Constraints: Do not ask for bibliographies, citation lists, or page references. Do not quote verbatim from Hart's The Concept of Law or Fuller's The Morality of Law; paraphrase their positions without quotation marks. Avoid science-fictional devices, perfect predictors, demon scenarios, or trolley-problem structures. Keep the setting, stakes, and decision-makers within the range of ordinary institutional life—municipal governance, hospital administration, or regulatory enforcement.
