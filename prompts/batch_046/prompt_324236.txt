I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. Next month I present a seminar defense — really a pitch for a design framework — to an interdisciplinary ethics and technology panel at my company. The thesis is what I call null-state architecture: the claim that the only legitimate evaluation metric for a software system or user-interaction loop is the absence of craving states (addictive feedback hooks, anticipatory engagement loops, compulsive return mechanisms) and suffering states (cognitive overload, emotional distress, frustration, regret). Positive metrics — feature richness, user-reported satisfaction, engagement duration, retention curves, or revealed preference — are not merely insufficient but actively misleading, because a system can excel on all of them while manufacturing hidden craving and suffering. I argue that engineering ethics should shift from positive-state optimization to negative-state elimination, and that this is the only framework consistent with tranquilism: that only the absence of craving and suffering genuinely matters.

The audience is precise. Four members are behavioral economists who study dark patterns and revealed preference; they treat craving as a measurable preference to be managed rather than eliminated, and they are skeptical of normative claims that override utility curves. Three are phenomenologists of embodied cognition who write about the lived body in interface design; they distrust quantifiable absence, worry that suffering is irreducibly subjective and context-dependent, and are suspicious of engineering attempts to formalize experience. Five are senior software architects and product managers trained in OKRs, feature delivery, and retention metrics; they will view this as an impractical rejection of product-market fit and will demand operational definitions.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. I need the objections ordered by how likely they are and how damaging they would be if unanswered. Each objection should be phrased the way that subgroup would actually phrase it. For each: indicate whether it carries real force; what I should concede up front; what I should hold; a concise reply suited to about two minutes; and a specific note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks — likely either the phenomenological challenge that absence is not phenomenologically accessible and therefore cannot be a design target, or the economic challenge that revealed preference collapses my distinction between hidden craving and genuine satisfaction — together with my best two-minute response and an honest assessment of its soft spot. Finally, the prep sheet should state explicitly what would change my mind: which empirical finding or conceptual argument would cause me to abandon null-state architecture, and it should identify the weakest point in my own reasoning that I ought to defend openly rather than hope the audience misses.

Write this formally and as a prep sheet, not as a defense of the thesis itself. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not ask me to quote any specific philosopher or text. I want to defend this from reasoning and from my own understanding, not from authority. Keep the focus on objections, concessions, replies, vulnerabilities, and the conditions under which I should revise my view.
