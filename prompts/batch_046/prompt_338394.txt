I am writing a novel in which a journalist interviews Dr. Kaelen Mire, a fictional philosopher who holds that the Solomonoff prior is malign: he claims that consequentialist agents embedded in universal induction face a structural trap, because the prior's exponential weighting against longer programs systematically suppresses high-complexity future worlds that would contain the greatest aggregate well-being, pushing such agents toward locally simple policies with globally catastrophic outcomes. Draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press Mire on the substance of this position rather than on biography or summary. Include at least one question on how this malignancy differs from ordinary skeptical pessimism about induction; one on whether a resource-bounded agent can escape the trap by approximating the prior rather than computing it exactly; and one on what the view predicts for artificial systems trained with universal-induction biases. For each of these three, provide a follow-up keyed to Mire's most probable reply—especially his tendency to invoke "computational humility" as a deflection. The questions should be rigorous enough that a reader versed in algorithmic information theory and normative ethics recognizes the conceptual stakes, but they must not ask for citations, reading lists, or references to obscure theorems that could not be reconstructed from general knowledge alone.
