I just finished a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma — specifically, whether a collective body should vote on premises and derive a collective conclusion, or vote on conclusions directly, and whether it is genuinely incoherent for a rational majority to approve every component of a multi-part test while rejecting the overall result. My friend insisted that such divergence is always a sign of individual irrationality or strategic manipulation, and I tried to show that even with fully sincere, individually consistent judgments, the aggregation mechanism itself can generate contradiction. To move beyond our stalemate, I want to design a classroom simulation for my graduate seminar in democratic theory: ten students, ninety minutes, meeting in a standard seminar room. The participants should come away feeling — before they are told the formal name — that there is a structural tension between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation that cannot be eliminated simply by asking people to be more rational or transparent. Design a structured role-play or game with explicit rules and small stakes (for example, a point system tied to a fictional institutional decision) in which the students serve on a Triad Review Board evaluating whether to approve Project Helios under three independent criteria — epistemic reliability, distributive equity, and procedural transparency. The simulation should have at least three phases: first, students vote anonymously on each criterion in isolation; second, they are given the logical derivation linking the three premises to the overall conclusion and asked to record a preliminary collective judgment; third, they vote anonymously on the overall conclusion without being allowed to revise their earlier premise votes. The rules must be arranged so that the group naturally produces the discursive dilemma — for instance, a clear majority endorsing each of the three premises while a clear majority rejects the conclusion — and the divergence should emerge from the aggregation rules rather than from any single student's strategic behavior. Include specific roles or instructions that prevent students from coordinating away the paradox before it appears, and specify a timeline. After describing the activity, provide a debrief plan that maps each phase onto premise-based versus conclusion-based aggregation, explains why preference revelation or strategic correction will likely fail to resolve the tension, predicts what will go wrong when the simulation is run (such as students attempting to retroactively revise premise votes once they see the contradiction), and offers guidance on how the facilitator should handle that disruption. Finally, be explicit about which formal feature of judgment aggregation — such as the impossibility of satisfying anonymity, rationality, and universal domain simultaneously — this classroom simulation necessarily simplifies or omits, and state exactly what the facilitator must supply verbally to fill that gap. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts; do not ask students to read named works in advance; and do not build the activity around obscure theoretical terminology that requires prior specialist knowledge. Write the prompt in American English, maintaining a formal, polished tone that reflects the argument I have just described.
