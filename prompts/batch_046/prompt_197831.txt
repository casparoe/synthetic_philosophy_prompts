Construct a thought experiment, confined to a single paragraph of ordinary human action without time travel, exotic physics, or appeals to consciousness as a special substance, engineered so that presentism and eternalism both deliver the verdict that an agent acts wrongly, yet they ground that verdict in incompatible ontological structures—presentism appealing only to relations among presently existing entities, eternalism appealing to tenseless four-dimensional relations spanning past or future entities—while the growing-block view (past and present exist; future does not) delivers the opposite verdict that the act is permissible. Present the scenario, then demonstrate that it meets these specifications: for each of the three ontologies, state exactly what exists at the moment of decision, identify the specific moral relation that does the normative work, and show why the growing-block boundary produces the reversal. Next, isolate the feature—call it the ontological boundary condition—that, if relaxed so that past and future entities share the same existential status, collapses the three-way disagreement into a single shared conclusion, and explain what happens to the case when that feature is removed. Finish with an honest account of how a defender of the view that comes out worst could attack the scenario without invoking science fiction.
