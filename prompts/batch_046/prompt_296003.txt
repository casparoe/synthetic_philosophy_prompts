I've been working through problems of source criticism spanning ancient historiography — from Herodotus's ethnographic methods through Thucydides's critical program and into modern textual scholarship — and I've noticed a recurring epistemic pattern for which I lack a precise name. When historians assess agreement between sources that appear independent, they often weight convergence on granular, quantitative details (exact troop counts, specific tribute figures, precise calendar dates) more heavily than agreement on broad narrative outlines, on the assumption that fabricators or derivative copyists are unlikely to reproduce the same arbitrary number. Yet in several cases I have examined, that very granularity turns out to be a product of shared representational conventions: both authors may be applying the same rhetorical schema for expressing immense magnitude, or drawing on identical administrative rounding practices, so that the precise figure functions not as an independently observed datum but as a cultural marker of scale. Consequently, the feature that initially presents itself as the strongest form of corroboration — specificity across sources — becomes, on closer analysis, the clearest symptom of hidden dependency on a common framework or unstated source. Is there an established term in historiography, philosophy of history, or source criticism for this particular inversion, in which granular inter-source agreement signals shared convention rather than independent verification? If such a concept exists, how is it rigorously distinguished from ordinary common-source problems, from the well-known unreliability of ancient quantitative claims, and from legitimate cases in which independent observers genuinely converge on precise measurements? If no established term covers exactly this configuration, please define it with explicit boundary conditions, and please answer in American English.
