The core idea is that a reason is of the wrong kind when it depends on a contingent desire the agent might not have. Since moral reasons only apply to people who already care about morality, they are always external and conditional, not categorical. Thomas Scanlon later extended this to belief: because belief aims at truth, any reason for belief that doesn't track truth — such as a moral reason — is of the wrong kind. Nomy Arpaly then used this framework to argue that practical rationality is fundamentally asymmetrical with theoretical rationality: in belief we reject wrong-kind reasons, but in action we are forced to accept them if we want morality to have any force. The upshot is that moral rationalism fails, and the only consistent position is a form of anti-rationalist moral sentimentalism, according to which moral obligations are just expressions of our contingent emotional dispositions."
