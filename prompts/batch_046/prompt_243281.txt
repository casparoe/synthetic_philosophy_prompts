I was at dinner last week with two colleagues—one who works on distributed systems and another who transitioned from academic linguistics into NLP engineering—and the conversation drifted to a philosophical idea I had encountered only in passing: W.V.O. Quine's thought experiment involving the word gavagai. My linguist colleague, Mara, laid out what she took to be the strong version of Quine's claim: when a field linguist hears a native speaker utter gavagai as a rabbit runs past, the available behavioral evidence radically underdetermines translation. The speaker might mean rabbit, but they might just as well mean undetached rabbit parts or a temporal stage of a rabbit, and no amount of further observation can definitively settle the matter, because any translation manual already depends on analytical hypotheses drawn from the linguist's own conceptual scheme.

Jon, the systems engineer, objected that Quine is describing a logical gap rather than a practical failure. In actual translation—and especially in formal domains like programming languages—reference is fixed operationally: an interpreter or compiler determines what a symbol does by its observable effects, not by guessing private mental states. So if gavagai appeared in a legacy protocol specification, we would resolve its meaning by tracing system outputs, not by speculating about ontology. Mara countered that this misses Quine's deeper point: the outputs themselves must still be interpreted, and deciding which observable behaviors count as the same system response requires exactly the kind of analytical hypotheses Quine highlights. Jon then offered what he intended as a reductio: if Mara is right, then two software engineers can never genuinely share a concept like bug, since each is always translating the other's reports through an incommensurable conceptual scheme—a conclusion he thought plainly absurd, though Mara seemed untroubled by it.
