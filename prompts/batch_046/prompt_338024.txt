As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy of science and history, I have noticed a recurring pattern in retrospective narratives of discovery that I have not seen named. The pattern is as follows. In accounts of successful breakthroughs, historians often isolate a minute, apparently stochastic laboratory event—a mislabeled reagent, a misread instrument dial, a shipment delay that forced an improvised substitution—and treat it as a load-bearing contingency, framing counterfactuals of the form: had that misreading not occurred, the discovery would have been lost. The event is thereby promoted from unremarkable background noise to a structural causal hinge. Yet when the very same kind of micro-event occurs in laboratories that yield no breakthrough, it is never elevated in this way; it remains mere noise, not a path not taken or a near-miss. Consequently, whether a contingent micro-event receives causal weight appears to depend not on its intrinsic properties or on an independent counterfactual analysis, but solely on the macro-historical success of the outcome it precedes. Does this asymmetrical retrospective promotion have an established name in philosophy of history or historiography? If so, how does it differ from the general thesis that history is sensitive to small causes—captured by the proverb about the lost nail—and from ordinary hindsight bias? If there is no precise term, can you coin one with clear boundary conditions that distinguish it both from legitimate identification of actual bottlenecks and from the narrative fallacy of imposing coherence on random sequences?
