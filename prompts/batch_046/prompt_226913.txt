Maritime heritage registries maintain a certification system for historic vessels that permits continuous restoration. A ship may have its rotted planks, engines, and rigging replaced incrementally over decades and retain its "original vessel" status, provided that no single restoration event replaces more than sixty percent of the registered structural material—keel, ribs, and planking—within one cycle. If that threshold is crossed in one event, the vessel is delisted and re-registered as a reconstruction or faithful replica. Yet a ship that replaced its keel in one decade, gradually renewed ninety percent of its planking over half a century, and swapped its mast in another, remains continuously certified as the original, even though cumulatively almost none of the initial matter remains. The protocols also treat functional components—engines, navigation electronics, fixtures—as irrelevant to identity calculations, counting only "structural identity parts" toward the threshold.

What theory of object identity and persistence does this institutional practice presuppose? Does the per-event cap commit the registry to a spatio-temporal continuity view, a best-candidate theory, or some form of gradualist essentialism about structural form? Can the distinction between functional and structural parts be defended without smuggling in an unstated essential/accidental property distinction, or is the practice philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival metaphysics? And if we replaced its implicit view with a four-dimensionalist account of persistence or with a purely relational theory that treats identity as a matter of historical narrative rather than material continuity, what exactly would have to change in the registry's rules for certification, delisting, and replication?
