You are writing the retirement toast for Dr. Elena Voss, stepping down after forty years of work at the boundary of comparative neurobiology and ethics. The dinner is attended by her family, former graduate students, biologist colleagues who study invertebrate cognition, philosophers of mind, and university administrators who know her mainly from committee service. Voss’s central contribution was a graded account of moral status: she treated sentience not as a binary property but as a structured spectrum with genuine discontinuities—between an organism that has only nociceptive reflexes, a social insect with distributed integrative capacities, and a thermostat that regulates temperature through feedback loops lacking any internal modeling whatsoever. She insisted that recognizing these gradations does not license applying human-level rights downward; rather, it demands calibrating obligations to the specific capacities for suffering, preference-formation, and reciprocal social behavior that an entity actually possesses. The toast must honor her without reducing her view to sentimental anthropomorphism on one side or dismissive behaviorism on the other. It must be intelligible to relatives who are not academics and to scientists skeptical of philosophical moralizing, and it must avoid misrepresenting her position—such as implying that moral status is purely quantitative, or that she denied any meaningful distinction between a wasp and a thermostat, or that she believed the circle of concern should be expanded by inflation rather than by precision. Use her framework to shape what can and cannot be said about her legacy: for example, praising her as someone who widened moral consideration is accurate only if the praise specifies that she did so with calibrated discrimination. Write in a voice that is warm, exact, and appropriate for a mixed room. Do not include bibliographic references, citations, or a reading list.
