According to the summary, each narrative isolates one moment of collective sacrifice from its broader strategic context, reframes military defeat as proof of moral superiority, and institutionalizes this through mnemonic secession: the community asserts narrative sovereignty over monuments and archives to substitute for lost political sovereignty. The author adds that this architecture requires counterfactual grievance scaling, by which the magnitude of claimed injustice is calibrated to match the scale of military failure, and claims that Dunkirk is the exception that proves the rule because Britain preserved state sovereignty, rendering mnemonic secession unnecessary. The essay concludes that such myths are therefore not really about the past but about present status compensation, and that recognizing this collapses the distinction between legitimate commemoration and ideological revisionism.

The comparison feels elegant, but I worry it flattens genuinely different mechanisms—perhaps mischaracterizing Dunkirk, or treating an invented mechanism as if it were established.
