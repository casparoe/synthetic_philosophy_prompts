Design a decision tree with exactly four branch nodes that classifies philosophical positions on future contingents, using the scenario of a possible sea battle tomorrow as the fixed reference case throughout. Each node must be phrased as a concrete claim or answerable question—concerning, in order: (1) whether the law of excluded middle applies to contingent future-tensed propositions; (2) whether openness about the future is metaphysical (no fact of the matter) rather than merely epistemic (a fact exists but is unknown); (3) whether a true future-tensed statement requires a single actual future history or can be grounded in branching histories; and (4) whether practical deliberation about the battle should treat all branches as live possibilities or should treat one branch as already privileged. Build the tree from this logical space rather than from a list of historical labels, so that a student can navigate it without knowing doctrine names in advance. At the leaves, place at least six positions: a determinist view preserving bivalence; a branching-time view with a privileged actual future; a supervaluationist view denying bivalence without a privileged line; an open-future view denying both bivalence and a privileged line; a relational or contextualist view tying truth to the speaker’s evidential position; and one unnamed hybrid combining metaphysical openness with bivalence about probabilities. For each leaf, state in one sentence its most awkward commitment—for example, about the ontological weight of unrealized possibilities or about retroactive changes in truth value. Then identify one combination of answers across the four nodes that is logically consistent but apparently unoccupied, and briefly diagnose whether the gap stems from incoherence, theoretical unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Finally, flag any pair of named leaves that share identical answers at all four nodes and therefore cannot be separated by this tree; specify what further distinction—such as one about the ontology of time or about semantic indeterminacy—would be needed to split them. The intended audience is an advanced undergraduate metaphysics seminar; avoid bibliographies, citation requests, and references to specific scholarly works.
