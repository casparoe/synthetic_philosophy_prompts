Consider the following claim from the Transcendental Deduction in Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason: the representation I think must be able to accompany all my other representations; if it could not, then something would be represented in me that could not be thought at all, which is equivalent to saying it is either impossible or at least nothing to me. The passage is standardly read as holding that self-consciousness is a necessary condition for any objective cognition.

I am trying to understand this through Hofstadter’s Gödelian analogy in Gödel, Escher, Bach, where formal systems achieve self-reference by encoding statements about themselves within their own syntax. Does Kant’s insistence that the self accompanies every representation imply a formal loop of the kind Gödel constructed—i.e., does the mind include itself as an object within its own representational scheme in a literally self-referential way? Or does the analogy fail because Kant’s unity of apperception lacks the arithmetized syntax and fixed encoding rules that make Gödel’s formula possible, so that Kant’s self-awareness is merely a functional condition rather than a strange loop? I want to know whether the GEB lens clarifies the passage or imports an anachronistic structure.
