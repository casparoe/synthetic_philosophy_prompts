I was chatting with a mate over drinks last week — she's on a tenure committee at a philosophy dept in the Netherlands — and she mentioned something that struck me as weird. Their evaluation rubric for promotion cases now includes a specific section where refferes have to say whether the candidate's body of work represents a progressive problem shift in a Lakatosian sense, or whether it's just tinkering with auxiliary hypotheses to protect a hard core. Apparently a few departments are experimenting with this. What's odd is that they seem to penalise candidates who do really careful, cumulative work — solving puzzles inside an established framework — even when the technical quality is high, because there's no novel prediction or excess empirical content. At the same time, they reward people who launch bold new frameworks that haven't really been tested yet.

I can't tell if this practice actually commits the committee to Lakatos's philosophy of science, or if it's compatible with other views like Kuhn's paradigm shifts, or even Popperian falsificationism. Could it be that they're just smuggling in one specific theory of how philosophy progresses, without realising? Can you reconstruct the implicit theory of intellectual progress baked into this kind of evaluation practice? Is it consistent with itself — especially given that philosophy doesn't usually make empirical predictions? And what would a tenure system look like if it were built on a genuinely cumulative, puzzle-solving view instead? Write your answer in American English, please.
