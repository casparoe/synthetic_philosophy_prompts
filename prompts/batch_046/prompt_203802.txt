After a long argument with a friend about whether being able to imagine something has any real bearing on whether it can happen, I want to apply that idea to a concrete historical case. Take the philosophical position that conceivability is a guide to possibility—specifically, that if a scenario can be clearly and distinctly conceived without contradiction, that gives us reason to think it is genuinely possible, while persistent inconceivability should make us skeptical. Now consider the real episode of powered heavier-than-air flight: in the late 1890s, prominent physicists and aeronautical authorities, including Lord Kelvin, treated sustained mechanical flight as not merely unachieved but practically inconceivable, yet within a few years the Wright brothers had accomplished it. Analyze this episode through the lens of that conceivability-to-possibility theory. What does the theory say the skeptics were actually doing when they declared flight impossible—were they advancing a modal claim, an epistemic claim about current knowledge, or something else? Does the episode show that their inconceivability was merely a failure of imagination, or does it reveal a deeper distinction—say, between thin logical conceivability and thick mechanistic-engineering conceivability—that the theory must accommodate? And in turn, does the rapid shift from “inconceivable” to routine tell us that conceivability tracks possibility poorly, or only that it tracks our cognitive limits? Keep the analysis disciplined: map who the relevant actors were, what options they faced, what counts as the decisive evidence, and say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. I am especially interested in whether injecting that distinction between logical and mechanistic conceivability saves the theory or merely relocates the problem.
