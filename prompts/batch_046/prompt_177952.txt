I am to deliver the opening remarks at the retirement symposium for my former doctoral supervisor, Dr Alistair Fenwick, who is stepping down from the chair of metaphysics after forty-two years. The room will be divided: on one side, colleagues from philosophy of science and metaphysics who will catch every allusion to the history of philosophy; on the other, his adult children and grandchildren, who have never studied philosophy and who expect warmth rather than a lecture. Fenwick has explicitly asked that the remarks avoid treating retirement as a kind of death or as a withdrawal from rational activity, and he has long been sceptical of what he terms the 'consolation-industry view of ageing'—the notion that old age must be redeemed by pretending it is merely youth in disguise.

I want the speech—roughly five minutes when spoken—to be structured around Cicero's De Senectute, specifically Cato the Elder's four standard charges against old age and the corresponding rebuttals he offers. Rather than using these as decorative references, they should form the hidden architecture: each charge should be acknowledged and then rebutted in terms that apply to Fenwick's actual career, so that the philosophical framework determines what can and cannot be said. Crucially, Fenwick's own metaphysical work has always turned on a distinction he calls epistemic tenure—the claim that authority within a discipline is not accumulated like compound interest over chronological time, but is continuously renegotiated through present argument—so the speech should weave that distinction into Cicero's rebuttals without collapsing into technical jargon. The family should leave comforted that reason and agency persist; the philosophers should recognise that the rebuttals are being offered not as sentimental comfort but as a serious stance on the continuity of rational agency across the life course. Please write the speech.
