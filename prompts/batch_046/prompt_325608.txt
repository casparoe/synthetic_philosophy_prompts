I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or archaeology, and I keep running into a conceptual ambiguity when I read about historical societies that I think maps onto a distinction we make in distributed systems. In reliability engineering we usually separate three notions of failure: an objective internal state transition in which the system loses a property it previously possessed; a user-observable degradation in which outputs fall below an acceptable threshold; and an observer-imposed retrospective classification in which we label a state a failure only by comparing it to an earlier benchmark we have chosen. I have the impression that discussions of societal collapse slide between these three without flagging the shift.

Could you rank the following four conceptual frames according to how strongly each presupposes the first, internal-state definition of collapse over the other two? Use a score from 1 to 5 for each, where 1 means the account treats collapse mainly as observer-imposed labeling or as localized service degradation, and 5 means it treats collapse as an objective, system-level loss of organizational complexity that holds regardless of demographic continuity or later recovery.

The four frames are:
1. The Bronze Age transition, understood as a network dissolution in which interdependent palatial economies and long-distance exchange structures cease to function in their prior form.
2. The Classic Maya situation, in which political institutions and monumental construction largely halt in the southern lowlands while substantial populations remain and adapt.
3. Easter Island, understood as an ecological contraction driven by resource depletion that reduces the scale of social organization and monument building.
4. The transformation-rather-than-fall thesis, which denies that any discrete collapse occurred and treats the apparent change as continuous institutional reorganization comparable to a software service migrating to a different architecture without ever being "down."

For each score, give one sentence explaining whether the frame requires collapse to be a real internal property-loss or whether it can be sustained on observer-level or service-level grounds alone. Then tell me whether the spread in your scores reflects genuine historical differences among these cases, or whether it mainly reveals which failure-definition each historian has implicitly imported from outside the evidence. Finally, if we replaced the internal-state definition with a strictly observer-relative one across all four cases, what would have to change in how we talk about "collapse" in archaeology—would the concept dissolve entirely, or would it survive as a pragmatic classification?
