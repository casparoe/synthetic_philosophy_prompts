Following the recent discovery of my grandmother's archival papers—documents tracing a family line through a region that shifted from imperial subjecthood to membership in a modern nation-state only in the twentieth century—I have been trying to classify whether her identity ought to be understood as pre-national, national, or as something that does not map onto either category. In working through Hobsbawm's typology of invented traditions and Anderson's analysis of census-making, cartography, and the museum as bureaucratic and cultural technologies that render a community imaginable, I keep returning to what seems an unavoidable chronological puzzle. If a nation is an imagined community in Anderson's precise sense—an entity whose members hold in mind an image of communion with strangers they will never meet—then there must be a specific historical threshold before which that mental image did not exist and after which it did. This appears to imply that my ancestors living in the same territory during the eighteenth century could not have belonged to this nation, not merely because the state apparatus was absent, but because the imaginative framework itself had not yet been constructed through print-capitalism and enumerative administration. At the same time, Hobsbawm's distinction between genuinely continuous practices and those invented in the modern period seems to presuppose that we can isolate an authentic temporal origin for the nation, distinct from the later narratives that retroactively appropriate ancient lineage. Given this tension, should I treat my grandmother's identity as belonging to a community that was literally not imagined until the nineteenth century, placing her pre-modern ancestors in a different ontological category altogether? Or does the framework of banal nationalism—the unconscious, flag-fluttering reproduction of national identity in everyday practice—license us to project that imaginative membership backward, so that the nation's true age is not its date of formal invention but the earliest historical moment its inventors successfully claimed as their own?
