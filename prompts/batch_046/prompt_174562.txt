I serve as the junior student delegate on our district's Heritage and Naming Committee, and I need a formal decision memo for Thursday's meeting that I can circulate without embarrassment. I was discussing this case with friends from my philosophy elective after we read about how communities remember differently than archives record, and I realize the board is about to treat a genuinely conceptual problem as if it were only administrative.

The concrete issue concerns our central library, named after Dr. Elias Vance, a local founder whose legacy is now split between two incompatible sources. For three generations, families in the adjacent working-class neighborhood have maintained an oral tradition—reaffirmed each year at Founder's Day—that Vance shielded students from the 1932 factory closure and preserved educational access for affected families. Newly digitized municipal payroll records and previously uncatalogued board minutes, however, show that Vance actively collaborated with management to suppress the student labor protest, diverted relief funds, and excluded the very families now honoring him. Our committee must recommend one concrete course with a clear timeline: retain the name with an interpretive contextual plaque, initiate a structured renaming process with genuine community dialogue, or defer action pending further study. The recommendation must engage seriously with whether living mnemonic transmission should ever override corrected documentary evidence—particularly when the archive itself was constructed by the institutions being questioned—and must not reduce either side to ignorance or bad faith.

Please begin the memo with your bottom-line recommendation in the very first sentence, then provide the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my request. Give honest weight to the strongest consideration on the losing side, including the argument that mnemonic solidarity carries an epistemic and moral authority that institutionally produced archives cannot automatically claim. Write in a polished, formal register appropriate for an institutional document, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations, since I need to understand and defend the reasoning myself rather than delegate it to sources I cannot access. I am writing as a high school representative who wants our vote to be conceptually defensible, not merely procedurally tidy.
