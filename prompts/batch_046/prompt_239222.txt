I sit on the advisory board of a public-history center revising its permanent gallery on the postwar division of Europe. The current display asks visitors, “Who started the Cold War?” and offers three competing attributions of initiating blame. A working group proposes eliminating that framing altogether, arguing that it imposes a backward-looking moral-blame structure onto a systemic security dilemma in which both blocs were rationally responding to anarchy, broken agreements, and prior trauma; they want the text to describe escalation dynamics and institutional incentives without assigning initiating culpability to either side. They concede, however, that removing the question risks false neutrality and may obscure genuinely asymmetrical aggressions and specific treaty violations. The board must approve or reject this revision at our meeting in ten days. Please draft the formal opinion I will circulate in advance. It should recommend a concrete course of action—retain, modify, or replace the framing, with specific replacement language if applicable—and include a clear implementation timeline. The reasoning must treat seriously the strongest argument for keeping the blame-framing question: that eliminating it can erase the culpable, non-inevitable choices made by particular leaders and states, and that structural explanations alone do not discharge the historiographical duty to identify who violated agreements first. Do not reduce that concern to a strawman.
