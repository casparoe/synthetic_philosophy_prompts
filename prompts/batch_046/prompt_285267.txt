My AP European History teacher summarized Rousseau's position in his well-known letter to d'Alembert on introducing theater into Geneva as follows: she argued that his primary objections were economic and social rather than philosophical—specifically, that importing Parisian actors and maintaining a theater would drain Geneva's republican treasury and encourage luxury, and that the presence of actresses violated local gender norms. She said his claim that theater makes spectators passive and erodes civic virtue was largely strategic rhetoric aimed at magistrates who valued republican austerity, not a substantive moral theory. She also noted that Rousseau permitted public festivals but rejected professional drama, which she interpreted as proof that his real target was the professionalization of deception—paying individuals to pretend—because she believes he thought such paid pretense undermined the transparency required for direct democratic citizenship. From this she concluded that once the rhetorical framing is removed, Rousseau's opposition is best understood as fiscal conservatism combined with gender traditionalism. I have the sense that this flattens a genuinely philosophical argument about representation and moral equality, but I am not familiar enough with the letter to identify where the misreading occurs. Is this characterization fair? Please distinguish which claims are accurate reflections of Rousseau's reasoning, which are anachronistic or overstated, and whether her conclusion that the civic-virtue language was merely strategic actually follows from the parts she gets right.
