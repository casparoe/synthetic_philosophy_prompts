I have noticed a recurring configuration in arguments about whether historical atrocities can be ranked, and I am unsure whether philosophy has named it precisely. The pattern runs as follows. A speaker rejects comparing the magnitude of two events—measuring death tolls, geographic spread, or the duration of systematic violence—on the ground that quantification treats suffering as fungible and therefore obscene. Yet the same speaker will then rank the events along a different axis: one regime is said to be worse because its violence required broader civilian complicity, or because its ideology was more systematically dehumanizing, or because the bureaucratic architecture of the atrocity was more totalizing. The refusal to compare by scale is presented as principled and absolute, but it coexists with a qualitative comparison that implicitly relies on the very differences in scale and organization that the speaker refused to measure. The speaker is not being hypocritical; they genuinely experience quantitative comparison as a moral violation, while experiencing qualitative comparison as an appropriate form of judgment. Is there an established concept that captures exactly this structure—an asymmetrical refusal of one comparative dimension paired with an unacknowledged dependence on it in another dimension? It is not quite value pluralism, since that allows different values to conflict without privileging one axis; it is not strategic equivocation, since the shift is not used to win a specific point; and it is not full incommensurability, since comparison is actively pursued, just rerouted. If the concept does not exist, can you define the phenomenon precisely, mark its boundary conditions—when does dimensional refusal become genuine incommensurability, and when does it collapse into mere rhetorical convenience—and propose a term with clear edges? I want the carving, not just a label.
