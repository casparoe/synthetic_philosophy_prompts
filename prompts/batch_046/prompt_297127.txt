I've been working through Dennett's Consciousness Explained and I'm convinced by the Multiple Drafts model: there is no Cartesian Theater, no central stage in the brain where sensory streams converge for a single observer, and no privileged draft that counts as the real conscious experience. Instead, parallel processes compete for influence, and whatever achieves sufficient fame in the brain guides behavior and verbal report. So far so clear. But I'm stuck on a methodological tension.

Dennett proposes heterophenomenology: we treat first-person reports as data to be interpreted, not as direct access to an inner theater. The reports are themselves just outputs of the narrative machinery, more drafts among drafts. Yet the practice of interpreting these reports—of integrating them into a coherent account of a subject's mental life—seems to require the investigator to occupy a kind of neutral, integrating vantage point. If there's no central observer inside the subject's head, doesn't the researcher implicitly become that observer? In other words, hasn't Dennett eliminated the Cartesian Theater from the subject's brain only to relocate it to the epistemic position of the heterophenomenologist?

I've been trying to think about this in connection with predictive processing frameworks, where hierarchical inference updates predictions without any literal central stage. Critics sometimes claim that the highest-level predictive model functions as a hidden integrator—functionally equivalent to a theater, even if anatomically distributed. If that criticism applies to predictive processing, does it apply to Dennett's investigator as well? Or can the researcher's integration be understood as just another draft—perhaps a meta-level narrative with no special authority—without collapsing into an infinite regress of observers?

I'm preparing to present this in a philosophy of mind seminar, and I want to be sure I'm not misreading Dennett by attributing to him a hidden homunculus that his framework explicitly rules out.
