I recently read a long-form essay that summarizes the literature on intuition pumps in philosophy. The author defines an intuition pump—a concept Daniel Dennett introduced—as a thought experiment engineered to produce an immediate judgment by stripping away contextual noise so that only one logical relation remains visible. The author coins the term modal isolation for this mechanism and claims it explains classic cases: the Chinese Room isolates syntax from semantics, the Trolley Problem isolates direct from indirect harm, and Mary’s Room isolates physical information from phenomenal knowledge. The essay then argues that modal isolation is not just a feature but a systematic bug: it trains philosophers to mistake framing artifacts for metaphysical discoveries. From this, the author concludes that intuition pumps are irredeemably misleading and should be replaced by explicit formal modeling, since any attempt to correct a pump’s framing destroys the very intuition it was designed to elicit. This strikes me as partly right but possibly too sweeping. Is this summary fair? Which parts accurately capture how philosophers who study these tools describe them, and which parts misrepresent Dennett’s own assessment, conflate different kinds of thought experiments, or overstate the case against them? In particular, does the claim that formal modeling escapes the same framing problems hold up? Please separate what is broadly settled about how intuition pumps function and where they go wrong from what remains genuinely contested—including whether they can be rehabilitated—and do not present one side’s verdict as the consensus.
