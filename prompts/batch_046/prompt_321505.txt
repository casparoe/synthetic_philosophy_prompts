For my philosophy of science seminar, I am trying to work through the relationship between Wittgenstein's hinge epistemology, as presented in On Certainty, and the concept of framework principles that structure scientific inquiry without being themselves empirically testable. I am particularly interested in a distinction that has come up in our discussions but is not always emphasized in standard treatments: the difference between globally encapsulated hinges, which underpin an entire domain of judgment and cannot be suspended without systemic collapse, and locally anchored hinges, which bind only specific instrumental practices and can in principle be set aside for diagnostic or revisionary purposes without destabilizing the broader epistemic framework.

With this distinction in mind, please compare Wittgenstein's account of hinge propositions with the framework-principles model. First, clarify how Wittgenstein understands the immunity to doubt characteristic of hinges—whether this immunity is best interpreted as semantic, pragmatic, or structural—and explain why he treats certain propositions (such as the long-standing existence of the earth) as standing outside the stream of ordinary empirical justification. Second, evaluate whether scientific framework commitments, such as the calibration assumptions built into particle-physics detectors, function as globally encapsulated or locally anchored hinges. Consider whether a research team can suspend a specific calibration protocol for testing purposes without undermining the entire experimental framework, and discuss what this suggests about the revisability of scientific background conditions compared with Wittgenstein's examples. Third, determine which of the two models—Wittgensteinian hinge epistemology or the framework-principles approach—offers a more coherent account of how background commitments can be selectively revised. Please avoid providing bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want to see how the conceptual comparison holds up on its own terms.
