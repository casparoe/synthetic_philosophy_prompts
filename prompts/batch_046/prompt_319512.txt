I cover science and ideas for a magazine, but lately I've been working on a documentary side-project about the Karst valley flare-up — you know, the one two years back when the northern province pushed south into the orchard districts. I'm trying to keep it human-scale, not geopolitical. I spent time with two veterans on opposite sides. Marco was part of the volunteer units that crossed the line first; he says he was protecting ethnic kin, though international monitors called it an unprovoked push. Amir was defending his family's orchard — literally showed me the burnt trees — and grabbed his brother's rifle when the shelling started. Both talk about the same things: the smell of wet earth, the loyalty to the guy beside you, and how wearing the uniform makes you feel like the normal rules don't apply in the same way, like you're acting inside a different kind of permission.

My editor wants the final cut to treat them as parallel figures — same lighting, same music cue, same voiceover about 'the cost of war on both sides.' I get the artistic impulse, but something feels off. If Marco's side started the whole thing and Amir was just protecting his home, is it fair to frame them as moral mirror images? Or does being inside the fight erase that difference — like, once you're both soldiers, the reasons you entered don't matter for how we should see you? I'm not asking for a legal opinion, more like: where's the line between recognizing shared suffering and accidentally saying both causes were equally legitimate? I don't want to sneek in a political verdict, but I also don't want to pretend the two positions were symetrical just because both men are decent people who sufferred. How should I think about this?
