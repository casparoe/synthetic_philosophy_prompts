You are delivering the opening address at a symposium marking the centennial of Alfred North Whitehead’s principal educational essay. The room is divided: half are scholars of Whitehead’s philosophy and pedagogy who know his texts intimately, and half are university administrators and policymakers focused on employability metrics and measurable learning outcomes, most of whom have never engaged his metaphysical work. Write an eight-to-ten-minute speech in which Whitehead’s metaphysical concept of importance—the intensity with which an idea is grasped in relation to an experienced whole—functions as the hidden ground of his pedagogical rhythm of romance, precision, and generalization. Use this framework to argue that a curriculum organized solely around quantifiable skills necessarily destroys the conditions for intellectual vitality, and show why that claim matters to both audiences. The philosophical idea must do real structural work: it should determine what can and cannot be said about assessment, about the defense of apparently useless subjects, and about why learning must unfold in a specific temporal order rather than being compressed into modular competencies. Do not include citations, footnotes, bibliographies, or reading lists; the argument must stand entirely on its own. Write so that the specialists recognize the precise philosophical move being made and the non-specialists still understand its immediate institutional stakes.
