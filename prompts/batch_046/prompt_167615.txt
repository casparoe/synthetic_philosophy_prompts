I'm writing a feature for a magazine that covers science and public ideas on how postgraduate social-policy seminars examine students on the moral architecture of welfare reform, and I have obtained an anonymised take-home script from such a course. The examination question asked: 'To what extent does the shift from contributory insurance to behavioural conditionality dissolve the traditional distinction between the "deserving" and "undeserving" poor? In your answer, discuss the concept of reciprocity failure, and evaluate whether fraud-detection mechanisms serve as moral sorting devices or merely as administrative filters.'

The student answer reads as follows:

The traditional welfare state rested on a contributory logic in which entitlement derived from prior labour-market participation, so the deserving poor were those who had paid into the system but suffered misfortune. With the rise of workfare and behavioural conditionality, however, this logic has been replaced by an activation paradigm that treats claimants as potential free-riders unless they demonstrate continuous job-search activity. In this framework, reciprocity failure occurs when claimants withhold labour-market participation despite being able to work, thereby breaking the mutual obligation that justifies state support.

This shift means that the deserving/undeserving distinction is no longer anchored in contribution history but is continuously reconstructed through administrative practice. Caseworkers apply sanctions with considerable discretion, and this discretion effectively creates moral categories: those who comply are deemed worthy, while those who are sanctioned are classified as undeserving. Moreover, the expansion of fraud-detection algorithms shows that the state treats deception as the primary cause of dependency, confirming that undeservingness is now understood as an individual moral failing rather than a structural outcome. Because these algorithms misidentify legitimate claimants at a significant rate, the entire distinction becomes arbitrary, and we should conclude that behavioural conditionality has dissolved the moral basis of welfare altogether.

That said, the contributory principle has not disappeared entirely; it survives in pension entitlements and disability insurance, suggesting that reciprocity failure applies only to working-age unemployed claimants.

The script is genuinely uneven. It correctly traces the replacement of contribution-history by activation logic, offers a coherent account of reciprocity failure, and notes the persistence of contributory elements in other programmes. Nevertheless, it contains two subtle errors that a hurried marker might miss: first, a conflation of administrative discretion with the normative construction of moral desert; second, a misattributed claim that fraud-detection serves as a causal theory of dependency, coupled with an unsupported inference that algorithmic misclassification renders the deservingness distinction wholly arbitrary. Item one should state the grade and corresponding mark range; items two through five should each isolate a specific conceptual strength or weakness, quote or paraphrase briefly from the answer to ground the point, and explain precisely why it earns or costs marks on grounds of analytical precision rather than ideological agreement.
