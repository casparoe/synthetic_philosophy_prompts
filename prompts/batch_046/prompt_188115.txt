I'm preparing a podcast interview with a philosopher who defends what she calls "structured amnesia"—a position that draws on Margalit's framework for an ethics of memory, particularly the distinction between thick relations that generate demanding duties of remembrance and thin relations that do not, together with Rieff's skeptical account of how collective memorial culture can be co-opted by present political interests. Her specific claim is that institutional forgetting is not merely permissible but structurally necessary: a transparent, procedurally bounded practice of setting aside certain acknowledged grievances is required to prevent the inflation of thick remembrance into an undifferentiated universal obligation, which she argues is what actually corrodes the moral weight of the memories that matter most. She anticipates the standard objection that this licenses state-sponsored erasure by stipulating that structural amnesia applies only to harms that have already been fully acknowledged through thick-relational practices, never to unrecognized or suppressed wrongs. I want a sequence of six to eight interview questions that go beyond restating the view and instead press where the literature actually presses, with each question followed by a targeted follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. Specifically: one question on whether the boundary between acknowledged and unacknowledged harms is itself controlled by the institution, and therefore vulnerable to the very capture she wants to avoid; a follow-up asking whether her precondition—acknowledgment before forgetting—simply pushes the political contest back one step without solving it. One question on how she prevents Rieff-style co-optation, in which dominant groups use the protocol to reclassify thick memories as thin ones eligible for forgetting; with a follow-up that asks what procedural safeguard could survive that capture without re-inflating every grievance into a thick-relational demand. One question on whether the duty is scale-dependent—whether small face-to-face communities should also practice structured amnesia, or whether it applies only at the institutional level; with a follow-up pressing the asymmetry—if the moral logic is genuinely about the structure of remembrance, why should scale change the duty rather than only its logistics? One question on whether her view implies that individuals within thick-relational groups also have a derivative duty to forget, or whether the obligation remains strictly institutional; with a follow-up asking how she blocks the slide from institutional protocol to interpersonal pressure. And a final, closing question asking what observable institutional feature—something visible in archives, policy, or public ritual—would serve as evidence that structural amnesia is functioning well rather than failing into erasure. Frame the questions in direct, colloquial interview language, keep them philosophically precise, and avoid generic biographical or definitional openings.
