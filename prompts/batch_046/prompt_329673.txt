I have been trying to understand why certain acts that clearly exceed moral obligation are praised as heroic, while other acts involving greater total sacrifice are treated merely as admirable or even as baseline expectations of decency. The pattern seems to depend less on the magnitude of the cost than on its shape.

Consider three archetypal cases. In the first, a bystander risks her life in a sudden, dramatic moment—pulling a child from a burning vehicle. Almost everyone agrees that she has done something extraordinary, that refusal would not be blameworthy, and that no one owes her a special debt of gratitude beyond ordinary thanks. In the second, a healthy adult donates a kidney to a stranger; the risk is real but calculated, the act is voluntary, and refusal is again treated as morally neutral, while the donor is seen as having done something far beyond duty. In the third, a person of comfortable means spends two decades of weekends and evenings operating a free legal-aid clinic, sacrificing leisure, career advancement, and family time in amounts that, summed over time, exceed the other two cases combined. Yet this sustained contribution is more often described as dedication or service than as heroism, and if an equally situated peer spent those same hours on private leisure, many observers would regard that as somewhat deficient—closer to falling short of decency than to a neutral failure to be heroic.

The asymmetry is striking. Episodic, visible, bodily risk seems to secure the status of clearly beyond duty, while distributed, institutional, labor-based sacrifice of equal or greater total weight tends to slide toward what good people do or even what the well-off ought to do. I want to know whether moral philosophy has already carved out this distinction—perhaps within discussions of supererogation, demandingness, or virtue ethics—or whether it remains an unmapped feature of our moral psychology.

If the distinction is already recognized, name the relevant concept, explain how it differs from simple gradations of virtue or from the demandingness objection in consequentialism, and specify whether it tracks risk, visibility, social signaling, or something else. If it is not recognized, propose a precise term and definition that captures the boundary between episodic-risk supererogation and sustained-labor expectation, including boundary cases such as large lifetime financial donations or long-term caregiving. Finally, take a committed position: is this asymmetry a real feature of moral obligation that should reshape how we assign praise and blame, or is it largely a distortion produced by emotional salience and narrative framing? Defend your view with specific reasons, and do not merely survey the options.
