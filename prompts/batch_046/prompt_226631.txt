In a philosophy discussion group I have joined since retiring from experimental physics, a fellow member characterized the epistemic regress debate as follows. Foundationalists, he said, hold that justification terminates in beliefs that are either self-evident or given directly by sensory experience. Coherentists reject any stopping point and instead maintain that beliefs justify one another in a circular web, which they regard as legitimate circular reasoning rather than a fallacy. Infinitists require an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons, but they treat this not as a normative epistemological ideal—rather as an accurate description of scientific practice, in which hypotheses are endlessly revised and never anchored in indubitable bases. From this he concluded that coherentism alone suits modern science, since science never appeals to fixed foundations, and that infinitism therefore collapses into a sociology of inquiry rather than a genuine theory of justification. Drawing on my background in calibration chains and measurement error—where instruments depend on prior standards without vicious circularity—I suspect there are category mistakes here, particularly in equating methodological revision with epistemic justification and in attributing circular reasoning to the coherentist. Could you audit this summary? Please distinguish accurate portrayals from subtle misrepresentations or overstatements—especially regarding whether coherentists genuinely endorse circular reasoning, whether infinitists intend their chain as descriptive rather than normative, and whether the absence of scientific foundations genuinely selects for coherentism over a modest foundationalism. I seek only a conceptual accuracy check I can evaluate without further reading or bibliographic references.
