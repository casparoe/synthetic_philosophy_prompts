I serve as the senior ethics reviewer for a regional clinical-trial oversight board, and over the past year we have seen a sharp rise in protocols that do not lie to participants but instead deploy what our internal working group has begun calling asymmetric epistemic framing: every legally required disclosure is technically present, yet the ordering, density, and contextual scaffolding of the information are engineered so that a rational agent could sign consent without grasping the risk profile the investigator knows to be central. I need a reusable decision procedure—something my reviewers, who are lawyers and clinicians rather than philosophers, can run in under two hours with a standardized worksheet—to determine whether a given framing strategy violates Kant's universalizability tests. The procedure should translate the Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity into ordered steps: how to extract the operative maxim of the framing strategy, how to run the contradiction-in-conception test separately from the contradiction-in-will test, where to apply the check that humanity is not treated merely as a means, and precisely where the analysis must halt and hand the case to a value judgment rather than yielding a determinate yes or no. Please build in two worked applications: first, a cancer trial that buries the risk of irreversible neuropathy inside an appendix of statistical tables; second, a psychiatric study that front-loads benefits in large print while placing withdrawal rights in dense legal prose, which should be the case where the procedure produces an answer I will dislike—specifically, that it passes the universalizability test despite our strong intuition that it substantively undermines autonomy. Finally, flag the exact steps where the procedure is likely to misfire, particularly wherever assessing what a rational being could will requires an interpretive leap that non-philosophers cannot standardize, and state clearly what the procedure cannot do, such as resolving conflicts between autonomy and beneficence or substituting for legal-compliance review.
