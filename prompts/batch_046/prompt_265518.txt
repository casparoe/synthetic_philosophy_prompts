Consider the “Cyclical Monument Protocol” adopted by several mid-sized European cities: every public memorial to a historical event is constructed as a temporary structure meant to be fully dismantled on the anniversary of its unveiling and then rebuilt, with new materials but an identical design and the same commemorative ritual, in perpetuity. The official justification holds that remembrance is not the preservation of matter but the active, willing repetition of the act of honoring; a static bronze statue, by contrast, lets the past be filed away as finished. Critics reply that this treats collective trauma as a loop to be performed rather than a wound to be healed, and that replacing timber and steel each year makes the memorial a copy of itself rather than the same monument.

What philosophical commitments about time, identity, and the ethics of remembrance does this practice presuppose? Does it implicitly adopt a Nietzschean view of eternal recurrence—specifically, that value lies in affirming repetition as such, or that a community constitutes itself through the will to re-enact? Or can the protocol be justified without any cyclical metaphysics, say by a purely aesthetic or democratic theory of participatory ritual? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and determine whether its parts—temporary material form, identical design, repeated ritual—presuppose consistent views of identity across time. Then take a definite position: is the protocol philosophically coherent, or does it collapse once we ask whether the reconstructed monument is the same memorial or merely a simulacrum? Finally, say what would have to change in the practice if we replaced its underlying view with a straightforwardly linear, progressive conception of history in which the past is to be overcome rather than re-enacted. Give your reasons directly, without laying out a menu of options for me to choose among.
