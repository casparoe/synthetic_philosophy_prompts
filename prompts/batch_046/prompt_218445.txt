As a journalist covering science and ideas, I keep encountering a specific argumentative pattern in debates about whether symbolic representations genuinely drive physical processes — discussions that invoke something like a "careenium," a layer of rule-governed symbols, and ask whether those rules actually push around the "simmballs," the underlying substrate units. The pattern runs like this: a theorist points to a case where changing a symbol — altering a program, a mental representation, or an institutional rule — produces a different physical trajectory in the substrate, and takes this as evidence that the careenium exerts downward causal pressure on the simmballs. When critics reply that the substrate was physically determined all along and that the symbol is merely a post-hoc redescription, the theorist concedes that symbols are only bookkeeping. But in the very next breath, they resume speaking of the careenium as selectively constraining, guiding, or stabilizing particular simmball configurations, using causal language without flagging the shift. The oscillation between treating the symbolic layer as causally efficacious and treating it as descriptively inert happens without acknowledgment, and it is not quite downward causation, not quite epiphenomenalism, and not quite supervenience, since all three are stable positions rather than an unmarked toggle.
