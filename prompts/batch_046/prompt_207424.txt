I cover the intersection of neuroscience, philosophy of mind, and emerging technology for a science-focused publication, and I am currently helping to design the final examination for an upper-level seminar titled Continuity, Replacement, and the Self. The students have read standard treatments of personal identity—psychological continuity accounts, animalist approaches, and pattern-based theories—but I want an essay prompt that prevents them from reproducing those summaries in generic form. Instead, the question should embed them in a specific, conceptually dense scenario that forces them to see where each theory lives or dies on the details.

Please write one exam essay question, a model answer, and a marking rubric. The scenario should involve a gradual-replacement protocol: over ten years, a subject's cortical neurons are replaced individually with synthetic analogues that replicate local synaptic weights and global network dynamics exactly, while the extracted biological tissue is destroyed immediately rather than archived. Throughout, the subject reports uninterrupted phenomenal continuity, yet third-party verification is limited to behavioral and functional metrics. The question should ask students to evaluate whether personal identity is preserved, but—crucially—not by letting them simply declare a position. Rather, it should require them to reconstruct the hidden premises in psychological-continuity theories, particularly how they handle hybrid intermediate states where both substrates coexist, to show why immediate destruction of the original tissue undermines a naive animalist defense, and to assess whether first-person continuity reports can serve as non-circular evidence for the very theories that authorize taking such reports seriously. I want them to confront what I will call the interpolation problem: during the transition, is there a determinate fact about which substrate—biological, synthetic, or distributed—constitutes the subject?

The model answer should run about 800 to 1,000 words and should suit a 90-minute exam response of roughly 1,200 words. It needs to distinguish token-continuity from type-identity, identify the circularity risk in using subjective reports to validate pattern-identity views, and explain why functional isomorphism alone does not resolve the metaphysical question without smuggling in the conclusion. The marking scheme should clearly separate an A answer—which must explicitly name the circularity risk, address the hybrid-state indeterminacy, and avoid reducing the debate to popular-science analogies like replacing parts of a ship with chips—from a B answer, which may correctly map the three standard positions but fails to show how the specific conditions of immediate tissue destruction and gradual functional replacement pressure each position differently. Specify point allocations and note the particular misconceptions that should cost marks, such as conflating behavioral verification with phenomenal access, or assuming that uninterrupted subjective report necessarily entails identity preservation without examining the epistemic authority of that report.
