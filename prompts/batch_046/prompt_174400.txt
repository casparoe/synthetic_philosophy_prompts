Two people in my study group have been arguing about whether the color phi phenomenon supports an Orwellian or a Stalinesque model of experiential revision, and they asked me to report the dispute without taking sides. I am writing this as a bystander.

The first person, call them A, starts from the standard color phi display: a red dot flashes at position X, then shortly after a green dot flashes at position Y, and subjects report seeing a green dot move smoothly between the two locations. A says this is best understood as Stalinesque revision. The brain registers the two discrete flashes, then retrospectively edits the conscious record to include the intermediate green trajectory, much as an archivist might rewrite a record after the fact. At the actual moment the first dot appears, A insists, the subject genuinely experiences only a red dot at X; the green motion is inserted into the reportable narrative after both inputs have arrived. A grants that the report feels immediate, but argues that report-generation is always delayed and reconstructed, so the feeling of immediacy proves nothing about the live content.

B pushes back. B says that in color phi there is no accessible trace of having seen only red followed by green; subjects do not report a corrected memory, they report a single continuous experience. If the revision were genuinely Stalinesque—like airbrushing a photograph after it was taken—there ought to be some preserved original, or at least a felt discrepancy, even if only at a subpersonal level. Instead, the discrete sequence seems never to have entered conscious experience at all. That, B claims, is Orwellian: not the correction of a record after it was lived, but the continuous manufacture of a false present in which the past is already rewritten. B adds that if A responds by saying the editing happens so fast that it occurs before consciousness, then A has collapsed the distinction: an edit applied before any conscious stage exists is not a revision of experience but the construction of experience itself, which is precisely the Orwellian picture.

A then introduces a modified case they discussed: a version of the sequence with a brief backward mask inserted in the interval between the two dots, which sometimes produces subliminal priming for the first dot's color even while subjects still report smooth green motion. A takes this as evidence for Stalinesque revision: the raw sensory record of red at X is preserved in subpersonal registers (the priming trace), but is edited out of the conscious narrative, leaving only the smooth green trajectory in the reportable story.

B replies that this misinterprets the evidence. The existence of a subpersonal trace—like secret archives that citizens never access—does not show that consciousness ever contained the unedited version. The Orwellian claim is not that the brain lacks unprocessed data, but that conscious experience itself is the edited product, with no prior conscious stage that was true and then corrected. The mask-priming result therefore shows only that editing occurs between subpersonal processing and conscious experience, not that consciousness undergoes retrospective correction.

Neither will concede. A thinks B has made Stalinesque revision unfalsifiable by defining it out of existence; B thinks A has smuggled in an unobservable "true conscious past" that plays no explanatory role in experience.
