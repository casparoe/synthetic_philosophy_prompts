hey, grading exams for an intro game theory course and I'm stuck on one. The question asked: In a finite two-player game, explain how iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies works when mixtures are allowed. Specifically, can a pure strategy survive every round of pure-strategy elimination yet still be strictly dominated by a mixture of other pure strategies? If so, what exactly gets left after full elimination with mixtures, and how does weak dominace relate to all this?

Here's the studnet's answer (paraphrased from their sheet):

"In pure strategies, s_i strictly dominates s_j if u_i(s_i, s_{-i}) > u_i(s_j, s_{-i}) for all s_{-i}. You drop s_j and repeat.

With mixtures allowed, yes, a pure strategy can survive pure rounds but then be beaten by a mix. Example: B gets payoff 2, 1, 0 against X, Y, Z, while mixing A and C gives 2.5, 1.5, 1.5 against each. So B gets eliminated once mixtures enter.

After full iterated elimination with mixtures, what's left is the rationalizable set. For two-player games this is exactly the set of strategies that appear in some Nash equilibrium, so the process collapses to the equilibrium strategies. If there's a unique pure equilibrium that's all that remains.

As for weak dominace: it's path-dependent in pure strategies since order of elimination changes the result. But with mixtures this issue vanishes, because any tie can be broken by putting epsilon weight on a strictly better option, making weak dominance as well-behaved as strict dominance. So path-dependence is purely a pure-strategy problem."
