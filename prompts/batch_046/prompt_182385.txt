I am drafting a short essay for my course on moral philosophy and social norms, tentatively titled "The Visitor's Dilemma: Tipping, Queuing, and Jaywalking Between Coordination and Obligation." My central claim is that these three conventions are not uniformly moral obligations, and that for a visitor—someone lacking both local epistemic context and long-term reciprocal ties—the status of compliance depends on whether the practice functions primarily as a distributive-reliance mechanism or as a pure coordination solution.

The essay proceeds in three parts. First, I distinguish reliance-based conventions, where non-compliance directly undermines a specific party's structurally reasonable expectation of compensation (the American tipping norm, where service wages are calibrated downward on the assumption of gratuity), from coordination-based practices, where the convention solves a collective-action problem but individual defection does not necessarily wrong anyone in particular (queuing, and in many urban contexts jaywalking). Second, I argue that visitors face a distinctive epistemic asymmetry: they often cannot tell whether a local practice is reliance-based or merely coordinative. From this gap I derive what I call an "aretaic placeholder"—not a full deontic duty, but a provisional posture of practical humility that favors compliance until the convention's moral structure is discernible. Third, I ask whether this placeholder collapses into mere etiquette, and I propose that it retains moral weight precisely when the convention operates as a Schelling point for distributive fairness rather than as an arbitrary local preference.

To keep the argument concrete, I compare the hidden, socially enforced tipping system with the visible, self-enforcing structure of queuing, and with contested jaywalking norms where enforcement is selective. I am especially interested in whether the "aretaic placeholder" is philosophically coherent, or whether it illicitly smuggles a moral conclusion into an epistemic deficit that should instead be resolved by inquiry. I also worry that I am overgeneralizing about "the visitor" as a uniform moral subject, when in fact a short-term tourist, a long-term expatriate, and a refugee may stand in very different relations to local practice.

Could you comment on the accuracy of the conceptual distinctions—particularly the reliance-versus-coordination taxonomy—on whether the essay's structure is genuinely illuminating or merely taxonomic, and on whether the "aretaic placeholder" should be retained, refined, or abandoned? I would also welcome suggestions on how to sharpen the comparison among the three cases without resting the argument on empirical claims about particular cultures that I cannot adequately defend in a philosophical essay.
