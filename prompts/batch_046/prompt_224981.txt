In a graduate seminar on welfare-state design we were debating whether strict benefit conditionality—including sanctions for missed job-center appointments and criminal prosecution for fraud—can be reconciled with the idea that social rights are unconditional. One participant kept returning to the slogan “no rights without responsibilities” to close discussion. She seemed to deploy it for three different moves at once: first, that unconditional transfers are conceptually incoherent because rights are by definition reciprocal; second, that the old “less eligibility” principle is just an application of the same idea, so welfare must never be preferable to low-wage work; and third, that worries about an “undeserving poor” are redundant, since failure to meet conditions automatically places one outside the rights-bearing category rather than requiring any separate moral assessment of desert.

Can you lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that slogan could be expressing? I am particularly interested in separating: (a) a claim about conceptual or logical reciprocity (rights entail corresponding duties by definition); (b) a claim about fair reciprocity or social contract (citizenship involves mutual contribution); (c) a claim about incentive design (conditionality is necessary to prevent moral hazard or dependency); (d) a claim about moral desert (only the compliant deserve support); and (e) any further reading—perhaps drawn from republican or democratic-equality frameworks—that would actually undermine strict conditionality rather than support it. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantively defensible, or false, and whether it actually supports criminalizing fraud, justifies workfare sanctions, or does none of that work.

Please also tell me which of these readings my colleague was probably sliding between without noticing, and what specific argument or conceptual distinction—say, the difference between rights as trumps and rights as earned statuses—would change your mind about how many genuinely distinct readings there are, or about whether the slogan is genuinely ambiguous rather than empty. Say where your reasoning is most likely to fail: for instance, if a particular interpretation of “responsibilities” turns out to collapse two of these readings, or if one reading I have separated actually entails another.
