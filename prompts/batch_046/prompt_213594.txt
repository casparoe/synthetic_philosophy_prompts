Since my past uses and mental states are finite, they underdetermine every future case—there is always a deviant rule consistent with them. This skeptical argument shows there are no semantic facts. It is basically Wittgenstein's private-language argument translated into arithmetic: both show that inner mental content can't determine rules. The skeptical solution—Kripke's answer—is to drop the idea of truth-conditions for meaning-statements and replace them with assertibility conditions: we say someone means addition when their responses match community practice, not because there's an underlying fact. First, my actual dispositions are finite and noisy; under some hidden physiological state I might answer 5, so dispositions don't uniquely determine addition. Second, specifying the 'ideal conditions' under which my true disposition reveals itself requires interpreting those conditions semantically, which presupposes the very meaning we're explaining. So the account is circular.
