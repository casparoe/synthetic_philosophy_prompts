I was recently working through arguments around negative utilitarianism and the lexical-threshold variant that attempts to block the anti-natalist conclusion by declaring extreme suffering and ordinary pleasure incommensurable. The standard defense treats the threshold as a fixed boundary: below it, trade-offs proceed normally; above it, suffering dominates lexically. But this seems to smuggle in a discontinuous preference ordering over worlds that undermines the comparative moral reasoning any practical ethics requires. Could you make a rigorous argument for the position that such a lexical threshold is conceptually unstable—not because suffering and pleasure are obviously comparable on a single scale, but because evaluating real policies forces us to compare worlds that contain mixtures of sub-threshold and supra-threshold experiences?

To push the argument into less familiar territory, frame the threshold not as an objective hedonic constant but as an epistemically indexed boundary: the point at which suffering becomes lexically dominant shifts with the deliberator’s access to counterfactual states of consciousness, which makes the ordering relative to the very perspective that applies it. From there, argue that this relativity collapses the lexical distinction into a form of context-sensitive weighting, leaving negative utilitarianism without the protective barrier it needs against the anti-natalist slide and without a coherent account of how to weigh distributed harms in actual decision-making. Please structure this with explicit premises, address the objection that incommensurability is meant precisely to forbid trade-offs rather than enable them, and maintain an analytical, formal tone throughout.
