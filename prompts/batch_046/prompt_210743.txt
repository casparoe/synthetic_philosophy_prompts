You are a senior philosopher of value and an experienced university exam designer. You have just finished a long, argumentative conversation with a friend about whether Camus’s idea of living in revolt against the absurd can ever be reconciled with contemporary hybrid accounts of meaning in life—especially in cases where a person lacks any emotional engagement with an objectively worthwhile activity. Before drafting anything, think carefully and step by step: first, brainstorm the precise conceptual crux, namely what happens when objective value is present but subjective attraction is absent, and how that interacts with the absurdist denial of cosmic purpose; second, identify the specific mistake students make when they treat absurdism as mere pessimism rather than as a structured stance.

Your task is to write a formal, upper-level undergraduate philosophy exam essay question, to be answered in 90 minutes for 25 marks, on the meaning of life. The topic must sit at the intersection of absurdism and contemporary objectivist-subjectivist hybrid theories. The question must be unanswerable by merely summarizing Camus or summarizing Susan Wolf’s fitting fulfillment account; it must force students to apply both frameworks to an engineered scenario.

Design the question around this niche conceptual injection: a hypothetical figure called the quietist calculator, who continues to perform structurally valuable but personally meaningless mathematical work after becoming fully convinced that the universe is indifferent to human achievement. The calculator feels no passion, no rebellion, no despair—only a cold sense that abandoning the work would be a kind of structural betrayal. The exam prompt should ask students to evaluate whether this life counts as meaningful, absurd, both, or neither, and to defend their verdict by engaging directly with Camus’s notion of revolt and with the hybrid requirement that meaning involves both objective worthwhileness and active subjective engagement.

After the question, provide a model answer of approximately 800 words. The model answer must contain these specific moves: (1) clearly distinguish Camus’s revolt from mere resignation or quietism; (2) articulate the hybrid two-part account and explain why subjective attraction or love is treated as necessary; (3) apply both frameworks rigorously to the quietist calculator; (4) defend a specific verdict—for instance, that the calculator achieves a diminished, thin form of absurd persistence without genuine revolt, and therefore fails to achieve full hybrid-style meaning due to the absence of engagement; (5) address an objection, such as the claim that the hybrid account’s psychologism makes it incompatible with any genuinely absurdist life.

Then provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks assigned for a total of 25. For each point, include a one-line note on how candidates typically go wrong. The scheme should clearly separate an A-level answer—which makes all five moves with precision, holds the tension open rather than resolving it by fiat, and uses the scenario to test the theories—from a B-level answer, which summarizes both theories correctly but applies them mechanically without defending a verdict.

Keep the prompt formal and polished. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not include verbatim quotations from any philosophical text; if you reference a specific claim, paraphrase it in your own words without quotation marks. Do not depend on obscure theories or highly specific historical details that a well-trained but non-specialist model could not reasonably reconstruct. Focus on conceptual rigor, the engineered tension, and clear assessment criteria.
