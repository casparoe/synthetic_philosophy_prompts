You are a professional philosopher of social science with expertise in ontology and demography. Before writing, take a moment to brainstorm: list the main ways philosophers treat collective entities, and note exactly where a “generation” might sit between a statistical artefact and a natural kind. Then write a focused research proposal of about 800 words for a one-term graduate project in philosophy of social science.

The precise question: are generational labels—Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and so on—names for real kinds in social ontology, or are they merely heuristic clusters? Frame this by introducing the notion of a “counterfactual-historical kind”: a group whose members are unified not by intrinsic shared properties but by shared modal vulnerability to specific macro-historical events (for example, a particular economic shock or technological shift), so that membership conditions depend on counterfactual exposure rather than on birth year alone. Your proposal should state whether this notion makes generational identity more like a natural kind with a historical index, or whether it collapses back into a period-effect description that applies to anyone alive at the time regardless of age.

Explain why existing answers leave this open. Sociologists treat generations as either pure birth-cohort effects or pure period effects, but they rarely ask whether either option supports kind-realism; philosophers of social science discuss institutions and groups, yet seldom address entities whose boundaries are defined by temporal exposure conditions. Say why your counterfactual-historical framing is not merely a rebranding of standard cohort analysis, and why the obvious alternatives—treating generations as pure statistical clusters, or as purely intentional groups like clubs—miss the specific ontological problem.

Set out your method: conceptual analysis of identity conditions; comparison with analogous cases (e.g., “the Lost Generation”); and a limited examination of whether demographic turnover undermines kind-stability. State what a positive result looks like (a defensible ontology of generations as diachronic kinds with counterfactual membership conditions) and what failure looks like (the framing dissolves into either pure statistics or pure social construction). Give an honest scope assessment: what can genuinely be achieved in one term, where empirical demography must take over, and what risk makes the project too large.

Write in polished British English. Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or attributions to named authors; you may refer broadly to kind realism, cohort analysis, and social constructionism without sourcing them. Keep the proposal formal, tractable, and focused on the conceptual framing rather than on empirical data collection.
