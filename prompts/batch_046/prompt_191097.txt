I was arguing with a friend about Benacerraf's dilema after listening to a philosophy podcast that mentioned it in passing. She summed up the problem like this: platonists think numbers exist as abstract objects outside space and time, but since they're causally inert they can't affect our brains, so on any decent causal theory of knowledge we shouldn't be able to know they exist. Yet mathematicians clearly do know things, so platonists are stuck between either accepting some spooky non-causal intuition (like Gödel's idea of direct mathematical perception) or dropping platonism altogether and becoming fictionalists who treat numbers as useful fictions, kind of like characters in a story that happens to work well in physics. She then said this more or less kills platonism dead, especially because even structuralist views still need causal access to abstract structures, which is impossible if structures are just as causally isolated as numbers are. I feel like she's overstating the case but I don't know enough to pinpoint where.

Is her summary actually fair to Benacerraf's original argument, or is she smuggling in extra conclusions he never made? Which parts are accurate representations and which are misrepresentations or overreach—like treating a challenge as a flat-out refutation, or assuming fictionalism is the only fallback? And does her point about structuralism getting caught in the same trap hold up, or are there responses she's completely ignoring that let platonists (or structuralists) keep their view without buying into "spooky" faculties? I'm especially curious whether the causal theory of knowledge she's relying on is as uncontroversial as she makes it sound, since that seems like a big hidden premise.
