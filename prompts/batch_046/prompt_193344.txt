Two colleagues, Maya and Leo, are arguing after a seminar on pattern ontology and Dennett's three stances. Maya opens: real patterns are genuinely real only if they carry irreducible counterfactual predictive power; predicting a chess player's move by attributing beliefs yields patterns no physical-level description can recover without catastrophic loss, so the intentional stance tracks real patterns, not fictions. Leo replies that "irreducible" only signals computational intractability; the intentional stance is just the design stance applied to evolved or engineered rational systems, and beliefs are useful summaries of functional architecture, not extra entities in the world. Maya answers that this confuses encoding with explanation: at the physical stance we see only electron flows, and the chess-level pattern is genuinely absent from that noise, so pattern reality is stance-dependent, not merely granular. Leo counters that the pattern is not lost but encoded at a higher organizational level; the design stance already captures functional architecture, and the intentional stance is just that stance applied to agents—one family of patterns, viewed at different scales. Maya then introduces counterfactual robustness as a criterion: a pattern is real only if it survives perturbations that do not affect its defining conditions. Intentional patterns fail this—alter one neural connection and a belief may vanish—whereas physical patterns like gravitation remain. So intentional patterns are too fragile to count as real. Leo responds that fragility is stance-relative: physical constants are fragile if the constants are perturbed, and design patterns like a clock's gear ratio collapse if a gear bends. If Maya demands robustness across all physical perturbations, she eliminates biological functions too; robustness therefore cannot distinguish the stances. Maya's final move: Leo conflates the design and intentional stances. Design applies to artifacts and organs with specifiable functions, but the intentional stance applies to rational agents governed by normative constraints—what one ought to believe—which stabilize behavior through consistency requirements, not through functional design. So normative patterns are genuinely distinct. Leo closes: normative "oughts" are still compressed descriptions of how rational agents stabilize predictions to avoid contradiction; they add no new ontology, only a more efficient predictive vocabulary.
