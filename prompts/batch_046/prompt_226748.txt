Write a short story, roughly 900 words, set in a near-future bioethics oversight station where agricultural and ecological policy has been formally restructured around the scale argument concerning insect sentience: because wild insect populations number in the quintillions and each individual carries a small but non-zero probability of possessing subjective experience, the aggregate moral weight of insects is calculated to exceed that of all vertebrates combined. The protagonist, an ethologist assigned to audit replacement ecosystems, discovers that a new generation of synthetic pollinators—biomechanical micro-drones engineered to replicate wild pollination behaviors—are being manufactured at rates that make natural insect populations seem numerically trivial. These drones reproduce the precise behavioral and neural-correlate architectures that the scale argument treats as sufficient grounds for attributing low-probability sentience to wild insects. The story should follow her attempt to extend the scale logic consistently to these artificial populations without collapsing into practical paralysis, and should conclude with her drafting an internal policy memo that inadvertently proposes a new ethical classification rather than a resolution. Treat the quantitative tension—low individual probability multiplied across an astronomically expanded base—as the central dramatic engine, and avoid reducing either wild or synthetic insects to mere metaphor.
