I found myself in a prolonged and rather exhausting debate with a friend yesterday evening about the Principal Principle and what it actually requires of a rational agent when she defers to objective chance. We were both trying to articulate whether the principle functions as a direct credence-matching rule—align your subjective probabilities with the known chances—or as a deeper structural constraint on belief revision that only applies once certain framing conditions are satisfied. The argument turned on a conceptual puzzle that I think exposes an ambiguity in the very notion of knowing the chance.

Consider a case in which an agent knows that, according to the correct physical law L, the objective chance of a specific event E is exactly 0.3. At the same time, she possesses admissible, well-calibrated evidence that L might be embedded within a broader theoretical framework L* in which the state-space is partitioned more finely: under L*, there is no direct analog to E, but rather a pair of sub-events with chances 0.6 and 0.1 respectively. My friend argued that this poses no special difficulty for the Principal Principle; it is merely an instance of ordinary Bayesian updating over competing theories, where one's posterior over L and L* determines the appropriate credence in E and its sub-events. I resisted this assimilation, suggesting that if deference is genuinely to objective chance, then applying the principle already presupposes that we have fixed both the correct law and the correct individuation of events. If the reference class or partition itself is part of what is in dispute, then it is unclear whether the agent should apply the principle relative to each theory separately and then integrate, or whether she must first resolve the theoretical dispute before any deference is permitted.

Please explain this conceptual issue in detail: lay out precisely where the ambiguity lies in the scope conditions of the Principal Principle, examine whether the theory-relative reading collapses into standard evidential conditioning, and clarify what is genuinely at stake in maintaining a distinction between direct chance-matching and theory-integrated deference. Please also separate what is settled about the formal statement of the principle from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers of probability regarding its application to cases of theoretical or partition-sensitive uncertainty, and avoid presenting either interpretation as the established consensus.
