I've been trying to defend an extension of Dennett's framework of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional—to what philosophers sometimes call institutional entities: currencies, legal jurisdictions, corporate personhood, and the like. My claim is that for these entities, the intentional stance is not merely a pragmatic heuristic but tracks genuinely real patterns that are irreducible to both the physical stance (the paper, electrons, neural firings) and the design stance (the functional architecture of banking protocols or statutory codes). These patterns depend on collective interpretation, yet they possess distinctive causal powers—market collapses, border disputes, corporate liability—that make them more than convenient fictions.

What I need is a rigorous map of what could genuinely defeat this position. I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I want you to identify the specific observations, arguments, or cross-domain results that would count against treating institutional reality as a level of real patterns rather than as an instrumental overlay. For instance, would it be fatal if institutional behavior turned out to be fully derivable from micro-level agent interactions without predictive loss? Would systematic predictive failure of the intentional stance in institutional domains—compared to design-level models—force a concession, or could a defender absorb that by retreating to weaker claims about pragmatic utility? Which defeaters would a committed proponent of this view have to acknowledge as genuinely damaging, and which could they dismiss as merely awkward? Please rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate where the position risks collapsing into pure instrumentalism about social reality. I was discussing this with a colleague who insisted that all institutional talk is just shorthand for individual psychology, and I want to know exactly where my view is most exposed.
