Many state-sponsored Holocaust memorial museums and mandatory school curricula in several European countries institutionalize a framing that treats the Holocaust as a singular, incomparable rupture—explicitly prohibiting analogies to other genocides, colonial violence, or mass atrocities in official pedagogical guidelines and exhibition narratives, and often implicitly grounding that uniqueness in the specific role of modern bureaucratic rationality and industrial administration. At the same time, those same nations’ legal systems, academic research institutes, and demographic archives routinely classify, compare, and quantify mass violence through cross-case statistical methods, comparative genocide frameworks, and universalist human-rights categories that treat the event as one instance among others. What philosophical commitments about historical particularity, moral uniqueness, and the ontology of modernity does this institutional bifurcation presuppose? Does the commemorative practice require a metaphysical claim that the event is literally incomparable, or can it be reconstructed as a pragmatic or moral stance about the ethics of comparison? Is the separation consistent with the analytical practices, or do they rest on incompatible theories of what makes an event historically significant and whether bureaucratic rationality can be both a universal feature of modernity and a singular explanatory cause? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and historical knowledge embedded in the commemorative policy, distinguish what it strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate what would have to change in either the memorial institutions or the comparative research frameworks if the presupposed view were abandoned.
