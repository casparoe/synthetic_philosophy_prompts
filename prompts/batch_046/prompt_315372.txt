I am reading Spinoza's Tractatus Theologico-Politicus for a course on early modern political philosophy, and I have been trying to extend his democratic theory to institutional arrangements that post-date him. Specifically, I want to understand how he might assess a form of governance that combines direct democratic participation with algorithmically mediated delegation — what is sometimes called liquid democracy weighted by predictive indicators of epistemic competence — rather than by the traditional one-person-one-vote mechanism he discusses. Given his claim that democracy is the most natural form of state because it preserves the collective power of the multitudo, and given his sharp distinction between rational common notions and the passions that corrupt judgement, would he regard such an algorithmically weighted delegation system as a legitimate deepening of democratic freedom, or as an aristocratic distortion masked by procedural formality? Furthermore, if the algorithmic aggregation of preferences relies on behavioural data that Spinoza could not have anticipated, does his metaphysics of conatus still provide the resources to distinguish genuinely rational collective decision-making from a technologically refined form of passionate manipulation? I am looking for a careful reconstruction of his probable view, with explicit attention to which parts of his framework translate cleanly into this modern context and which parts collapse or require revision.
