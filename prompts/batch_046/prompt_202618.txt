I am drafting a short essay for my philosophy class on whether everyday conventions—tipping, queueing, and jaywalking—are genuine moral obligations, and whether visitors are bound to follow local ones. To avoid collapsing etiquette into ethics, I propose a distinction I call ethical viscosity: norms that substitute for missing institutional protections, such as tipping where service wages are inadequate, have high viscosity and generate obligations even for outsiders, because noncompliance directly harms vulnerable workers; norms that are purely coordinative, like queueing, or arbitrary legal artifacts, like jaywalking rules with no safety rationale, have low viscosity and bind residents mainly by courtesy or efficiency. I argue that treating all conventions as equally moral risks a kind of normative imperialism, yet ignoring high-viscosity norms can cause real harm. I worry that my framework still conflates justice with politeness, and that I have not shown why a visitor owes compliance to high-viscosity norms specifically rather than simply contributing to local welfare in some other form. Could you comment on whether the viscosity distinction is coherent, on whether queueing can ever carry moral weight when exclusion or severe delay is at stake, and on whether a universalist could defeat my view by claiming that all cooperative norms carry minimal moral weight regardless of location?
