I have been studying Braudel's distinction between the longue durée of geographic and climatic structures and the rapid rhythm of events and individual action. The framework seems to entail a form of causal exclusion: because geographic conditions change on timescales that outlast every human institution, they must constitute the genuine explanatory substrate of history, while event-level accounts—say, of why a particular trade network persisted across successive imperial collapses—are reducible to those underlying structural conditions, with contingent diplomatic choices functioning merely as surface perturbations. Given this, should historians treat Braudel's model as a geographic determinism in which histoire événementielle carries no independent causal weight? Or is there a principled way to integrate these temporal scales that preserves the explanatory autonomy of events and individual agency without treating them as epiphenomenal to long-term structure?
