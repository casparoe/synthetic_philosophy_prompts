I am taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion and literature, and after our session on Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov two classmates, Maya and Jonah, continued arguing about the Grand Inquisitor's exchange with Christ over freedom and happiness. Maya maintained that the Inquisitor's argument is primarily descriptive rather than normative: he claims that human beings are psychologically constituted to prefer security and bread over the burden of autonomous choice, and that they will eventually thank anyone who takes that burden away. She noted that this anticipates modern behavioral economics — people routinely prefer default options, opt-out architectures, and algorithmic recommendations to active deliberation — and she argued that Dostoevsky's Christ therefore offers a gift that human psychology is structurally unable to accept. Jonah countered that this collapses the Inquisitor's prescriptive theology into a psychological law. He insisted that the Inquisitor is not reporting natural human preference but engineering it: he offers miracle, mystery, and authority as an explicit trade for freedom, and Dostoevsky's point is that any happiness bought through that exchange is not flourishing but infantilization. On Jonah's reading, freedom is constitutive of personhood regardless of its psychological cost.

Maya then introduced a distinction she said standard interpretations overlook: she separated negative freedom, or the absence of external constraints, from what she called cognitive sovereignty — the internal burden of constructing values, meaning, and moral frameworks without authoritative scaffolding. She argued that the Inquisitor targets precisely this second kind of freedom, not merely removing barriers but supplying authoritative answers that eliminate the need for self-authorship. She further claimed that modern liberal societies achieve the Inquisitor's outcome without explicit coercion: they preserve negative freedom formally while outsourcing cognitive sovereignty to markets, bureaucratic norms, and predictive algorithms, leaving people with the appearance of autonomy but none of its weight. Jonah granted the distinction's usefulness but said it misapplies to the text. He argued that formal capacity for rejection — the ability to say no to the algorithm or the norm — preserves the essential feature Dostoevsky values, and that as long as that formal capacity exists, the burden of choice remains real, not hollow. Maya replied that if exercising formal capacity is made so costly by social architecture that rational agents never do so, then the Inquisitor's victory is complete in substance even if absent in form, which makes Dostoevsky's defense of freedom seem either naive or masochistic.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: evaluate Maya's descriptive reading versus Jonah's normative reading, assess her distinction between negative freedom and cognitive sovereignty and whether it strengthens or weakens her case, and examine Jonah's claim that formal capacity for rejection preserves the value of freedom. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing — for example, whether Christ's silent response to the Inquisitor offers a position that avoids both of their frameworks, or whether there is a way to separate the burden of choice from the value of freedom that neither has considered.
