I'm preparing questions for a podcast interview with Dr. Mara Lindt, who defends what she calls the "documentary rupture" account of how legends become history. On her view, the decisive shift is not archaeological confirmation but an internal change in narrative causation: from mythic agency—divine intervention, singular heroic destiny, monstrous interruption—to bureaucratic agency, meaning taxation, diplomatic correspondence, census records, and institutional record-keeping embedded within the narrative itself. She applies this framework to three canonical cases—the Trojan War, the King Arthur tradition, and the Exodus—arguing that each occupies a different position on the rupture spectrum. For the Trojan War, she accepts the Hittite correspondence mentioning Ahhiyawa and Wilusa as evidence of bureaucratic transmission, but contends that the Catalogue of Ships in the Homeric tradition is a later administrative interpolation rather than preserved poetic memory. For Arthur, she holds that even the earliest Welsh and Latin texts embed the figure in mythic time, so the rupture never occurs and the tradition remains legendary regardless of any potential historical kernel. For the Exodus, she claims the narrative exhibits an inverted rupture: it frames events through bureaucratic mechanisms—brick quotas, census-taking, royal edict—yet leaves no corresponding trace in Egyptian administrative archives, producing what she terms the "phantom bureaucracy" problem.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond asking her to restate the thesis.
