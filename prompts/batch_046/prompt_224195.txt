I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop in Buenos Aires marking roughly forty years since the CONADEP report — half the room are human-rights lawyers and transitional-justice scholars who know the Full Stop and Due Obedience laws by heart, and half are family memebers of the disappeared who shouldn't have to care about legal doctrine. I want to build the talk around an idea I've been playing with: amnesty isn't really about forgetting, it's about forced co-presense without moral distance. The state keeps the perpetrator inside social life but strips away the separation that punishment normally creates, so survivors are left in this weird proximity to people who have been officially absolved. I need that framing to do real work — it should explain why truth commissions feel hollow even when they "work," because they document the proximity rather than restore distance. So the speech can't end with closure or healing, and if I use the word reconcilation I need it to be critical, not celebratory. Keep it about five minutes, casual but not sloppy, and don't ask for a bibliography or citations — I just need the structure and language. Oh, and I keep writing co-presense with an 'e' — just roll with it.
