Construct a realistic, non-science-fictional scenario—no longer than one paragraph—centered on a single contemporary public institution, such as a chartered university or a municipal water authority, that retains uninterrupted legal personality since 1920 but whose entire membership, staff, and governing board have turned over completely since 1925, when the institution enforced a specific, clearly specified exclusionary practice (for instance, a racially restrictive covenant on land held in perpetual trust or a systematic expropriation of water rights). The present institution must still hold a tangible, quantifiable structural advantage traceable solely to that 1925 wrong, while no present individual member has inherited personal wealth or suffered direct causal harm from it. Engineer the case so that a corrective-justice theory of reparations, which ties obligations to identifiable individual wrongdoers and victims, denies that any present duty of repair exists, whereas a structural-injustice theory, which tracks enduring institutional advantages regardless of individual continuity, affirms that the institution itself now owes reparations. Then demonstrate that the scenario meets every specification: state the precise claim at issue, the method by which institutional continuity is traced, the relevant nearby counterfactual worlds in which the legal entity dissolved and was subsequently re-founded, and why each theory delivers the verdict it does. Conclude with an honest assessment of which feature does the work—the uninterrupted legal identity versus the persisting structural benefit—and explain exactly how a defender of corrective justice would attack the case, whether by denying that institutions can bear moral agency across total membership turnover or by challenging the identification of the present body with the historical wrongdoer.
