I'm working through a section of Rousseau's Social Contract, published in the 1760s, where he distinguishes the general will from the will of all, claims the former is always oriented toward the common good and can never err, and says that anyone who refuses obedience should be compelled into freedom. I'm trying to understand whether this is best read as an early preference-transformation theory—in which individual preferences are reshaped rather than summed—or as a normative claim about autonomy that does not depend on any aggregation mechanism. In particular, does the claim that the general will is infallible require that citizens have single-peaked preferences over policy, or could it survive Arrow-style impossibility conditions if we treat the common good as a second-order ranking? And is the notion of being compelled into freedom describing a psychological change in preferences, or prescribing a paternalistic correction? I would appreciate help disentangling the conceptual commitments in this passage, especially whether Rousseau is implicitly relying on a particular model of collective decision-making.
