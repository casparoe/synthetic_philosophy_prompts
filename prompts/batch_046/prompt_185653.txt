I'm working through problems in formal epistemology and rational belief revision, and I keep returning to a specific institutional protocol I encountered in descriptions of structured forecasting and clinical decision-support design. In these systems, analysts or clinicians maintain a running probability distribution over competing hypotheses, but they are explicitly instructed to apply two different update procedures depending on the character of the incoming evidence. When evidence is unambiguous—say, a confirmed laboratory result or a verified document—they condition on the precise observation in the standard Bayesian way. But when the evidence is ambiguous—an intercepted message that might be routine logistics or might be coded instructions, or a patient-reported symptom hedged with uncertainty—the protocol forbids conditioning on any single interpretation. Instead, it requires what amounts to Jeffrey conditionalization: the analyst redistributes credibility across all hypotheses in proportion to how well each predicts the ambiguous report, weighted by prior confidence in those predictive links, without ever treating one interpretation as the observed fact. The system even tracks calibration metrics separately for these "diffuse updates" versus "sharp updates," and analysts are penalized for overconfidence specifically in the diffuse category. 

What philosophical commitments about rational belief revision does this bifurcated practice presuppose? Does it implicitly commit to Richard Jeffrey's view that there is a sui generis rational rule for updating on uncertain evidence—one that cannot be reconstructed as conditioning on a hidden precise observation followed by marginalization—or can the protocol be justified entirely within a strict Bayesian framework that treats all ambiguity as mere epistemic ignorance about which definite observation actually occurred? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the practice: does the separation of sharp and diffuse updates presuppose that evidence itself can carry graded, interpretation-independent epistemic force, or is it compatible with a view on which ambiguity always resides in the observer's cognitive limitations rather than in the evidential input? Examine also whether the practice is philosophically consistent with its own verification mechanism: if calibration is assessed by comparing final probabilities to actual outcomes, but a diffuse update is never tested against a single "true" interpretation of the ambiguous report, what conception of predictive success or evidential verification does the scoring rubric tacitly assume? Finally, tell me what would have to change—both procedurally in the protocol and conceptually in its underlying philosophy—if one rejected Jeffrey conditionalization altogether in favor of the view that every rational update must ultimately bottom out in conditioning on a precisely specified evidential proposition, leaving no irreducible room for graded revision.
