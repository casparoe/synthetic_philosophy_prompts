Having spent the past year caring for my father through progressive Alzheimer’s disease, I find myself unable to treat the question of whether he remains “the same person” as merely sentimental. I want a rigorously designed thought experiment—not for comfort, but to see exactly where three frameworks diverge. Please construct a single-paragraph, realistic clinical scenario (no science fiction, no brain uploads, no perfect predictors or demons) describing a patient who has lost all autobiographical memory and narrative self-reference, whose bodily organism clearly persists, and in whom the Buddhist mechanism of upadana—the clinging or grasping that conventionally constructs the illusion of a persisting self—has either unambiguously ceased or unambiguously continued in some affective or habitual form, while psychological continuity is genuinely ambiguous rather than clearly present or absent.

The scenario must be engineered so that:
(1) A psychological-continuity theorist concludes that personal identity is not preserved, specifically because the narrative and relational memory-links that constitute psychological connectedness are severed.
(2) A bodily-continuity (animalist) theorist concludes that personal identity is preserved, solely on the grounds that the living biological organism continues without interruption.
(3) A Buddhist anatta reductionist concludes that there was never a persisting self to preserve, but distinguishes this conclusion from (1) by appealing not to broken psychological links but to the status of upadana: if upadana has ceased, then the skandhas no longer generate even a conventional self, regardless of bodily or affective remainder; if it persists (for instance, in habitual emotional grasping toward familiar faces), then a provisional, conventional person is still being fabricated even in the absence of memory.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it satisfies each specification: state the verdict each theory delivers, the reasoning behind it, and the precise feature of the case that produces it. Then identify the structural element that allows the Buddhist view to peel away from the psychological view rather than collapsing into it—namely, the independence of upadana from psychological content. Finally, explain what happens if that element is removed: if upadana is defined so that it tracks psychological continuity exactly, does the reductionist position become redundant? Conclude with a brief account of how a defender of the Buddhist position might resist that collapse, for example by distinguishing conventional (sammuti-sacca) and ultimate (paramattha-sacca) levels of description. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; do not quote specific texts verbatim; and keep the entire discussion accessible to someone without access to external sources.
