Please evaluate the following exam answer. The exam asked: "Consider a statue formed from a single lump of clay. The statue and the lump share all material parts and occupy the same spatiotemporal region, yet the lump could survive being squashed into a ball while the statue would be destroyed. First, explain why this modal difference appears to violate Leibniz's Law if the two are strictly identical. Second, outline the material-constitution view—the view that the lump constitutes the statue without being identical to it—and explain specifically how the relation of ontological dependence distinguishes constitution from mere spatiotemporal coincidence. Third, consider an objection: suppose the statue's inability to survive squashing is not a brute physical modal fact but an institutional or normative fact about what counts as preserving that particular artwork. Does this institutionalist interpretation undermine the argument for non-identity, and can a constitutionist accommodate it without collapsing into the claim that there is literally only one object present?

The student wrote the following answer (paraphrased from the exam script):

The statue and the lump are in the same place and made of the same clay, but they have different persistence conditions, so if they were one thing they would have contradictory properties. That violates Leibniz's Law, which says identical things share every property, so they must be different objects.

The constitution view says the lump makes up the statue but is not the same thing. They overlap in space but are not just coincident like two strangers standing in the same room, because the statue depends on the lump—if the lump is destroyed, the statue is too. This dependence is what makes constitution different from coincidence.

If the persistence conditions are institutional, then the statue not surviving squashing is like a dollar bill not surviving being burned: it is a rule about the kind, not a physical property of the material. That means the modal argument fails, because Leibniz's Law only applies to physical properties, not to institutional ones. So the statue and the lump could be the very same physical object, with the statue just being the lump described in artistic terms. The constitutionist can still say the statue is constituted by the lump, but in this case constitution just means the lump has an artistic status; there are not two material things, only one with a special role.
