Read Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein through Adam Smith’s account of sympathy and the impartial spectator, but focus on a specific asymmetry Smith draws: we enter more readily into another’s sorrow, because distress calls for relief and invites imaginative projection, whereas sympathy with another’s joy requires closer social proximity and offers no comparable prompt to assist. The creature first elicits horror and then pity—responses to visible suffering—yet when he demands companionship, a mate, and shared domestic happiness, he is refused. Does the novel dramatize Smith’s claim that sympathy collapses precisely at the point where the sufferer asks not for alleviation but for participation in joy and attachment? Examine the creature’s education with the De Lacey family, Victor’s refusal to create a companion, and the reader’s own shifting allegiance, asking where an internalized impartial spectator approves or withholds judgment. I want a reading that takes both the gothic architecture of the novel and Smith’s moral psychology seriously: is the failure of sympathy structural rather than merely personal cruelty, and does Shelley thereby show something about the limits of sympathy that philosophical argument alone cannot establish?
