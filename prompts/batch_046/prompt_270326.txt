I'm presenting next tuesday to a small philosophy-of-language workshop — about eight people, mostly analytic philosophers of language who think Gongsun Long's "white horse is not horse" is just a freshman category mistake, plus two historians of Chinese phil who defend the standard "sophist and rhetorician" reading of the Mingjia, and one modal logician who always demands formalization. My thesis: the dialogue isn't a botched class-inclusion argument but a failed piece of conceptual engineering. Gongsun Long is trying to show that "horse" and "white-horse" differ in intension — kind-constitution versus determinate form — even though they share extension, using what I think is a proto-modal distinction between being-a-member-of a kind and being-coextensive-with it. I want to read the whole School of Names as doing ontology by carving linguistic categories, not as sophistry.

Sketch of my argument: I focus on the dialogue's move where the interlocutor asks about color and form, and Gongsun Long separates them. I say if "horse" picks out the kind-essence and "white horse" picks out a determinate form that doesn't exhaust that kind, then "white horse is not horse" is false in extension but true in some modal or intensional space. I concede the text never uses modal vocabulary, but claim the argumentative structure anticipates it.
