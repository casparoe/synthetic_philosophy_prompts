I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, though I have recently been trying to understand the AI-safety literature on adversarial debate as an alignment mechanism. In conversation with a colleague, I encountered the objection that debate fails because of obfuscated arguments—reasoning that is technically valid but structured so that its premises are chosen specifically to exploit the evaluator’s blind spots, or so convoluted that a human or model judge cannot penetrate it within practical time limits. From an engineering standpoint, this sounds like a presentation-layer failure: the hiding and ambiguity arise because we are using natural language with all its rhetorical flexibility. So here is my confusion. If we restricted debate to mechanically verified proof trees in a formal system like Lean, with every inference step explicit and no rhetorical gloss, wouldn’t we have eliminated the obfuscation vector entirely? It seems to me that the risk researchers identify is therefore not a fundamental obstacle to alignment via debate, but rather an implementation artifact of using English, and that the real problem is merely engineering the compiler and the verification interface. I want a committed answer: take a definite position on whether formalization actually dissolves the problem or only displaces it—specifically, whether the risk migrates into the strategic selection of axioms, the adversarial pruning of which lemmas to present, or the judge’s computational budget for long proofs—and explain why, rather than laying out options for me to choose. Please also correct me directly if my framing is wrong about the symmetry of the risk: I have been assuming both sides can equally obfuscate, but if deception is structurally easier for a misaligned system than for an honest one, I want that misconception addressed explicitly.
