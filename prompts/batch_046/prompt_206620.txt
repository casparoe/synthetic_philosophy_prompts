I am delivering the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "The Archive as Subject," held in a university auditorium split between philosophers of mind and cognitive scientists on one side, and practicing archivists and digital preservationists with no formal philosophy training on the other. The workshop's framing provocation is drawn from the thought experiment in Hofstadter and Dennett's The Mind's I: if every functional pattern of Einstein's brain were recorded in a book, could that book itself be conscious? Rather than using this as a decorative metaphor, I want the remarks to treat it as structural scaffolding for a specific, newly emerging case—our institution has begun testing a predictive preservation system that ingests a deceased scholar's complete correspondence, lecture transcripts, and marginalia, then generates new, context-sensitive responses in that scholar's voice. The speech should ask, with philosophical rigor but without jargon that alienates the archivists, whether such a system could ever be conscious rather than merely a sophisticated echo—what precise conditions of subjective experience, functional integration, or causal history would need to be satisfied, and why the distinction is not academic but ethical. It must not collapse into sentimental anthropomorphism on one hand or dismissive reductionism ("it is only data") on the other; instead, it should demonstrate that the answer determines whether archivists bear obligations of care toward the system itself, or only toward the living people who query it. Please write a ten-minute address shaped throughout by that distinction, landing for both halves of the room without resolving into either false comfort or easy dismissal.
