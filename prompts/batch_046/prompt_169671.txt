For my graduate seminar on practical reason, I want a diagnostic instrument—not a literature review—on moral uncertainty in cases where an agent is genuinely unsure whether an act falls under a deontological constraint, a consequentialist optimization rule, or a virtue-ethical mean, and where the theories in play disagree not merely on outcomes but on the very metric by which outcomes and constraints are to be compared. I am especially interested in the fault lines that standard taxonomies miss: whether moral theories are inter-theoretically commensurable on a single value scale; whether uncertainty about moral truth generates a second-order practical reason that is independent of the first-order reasons under consideration; and whether the scale or irreversibility of harm alters the logical structure of the uncertainty itself rather than merely its stakes. To keep the exercise grounded, begin with this concrete scenario: you must choose between two rescue protocols. Protocol A saves ten strangers with near-certainty but imposes a severe, irreversible harm on one consenting volunteer; Protocol B saves five strangers with no collateral harm. You are uncertain whether the constraint against instrumentalizing the volunteer is absolute, weighted, or merely presumptive, and you are also uncertain whether your obligation is to maximize expected welfare, to respect the constraint regardless of outcomes, or to cultivate a character that avoids treating persons as means.

Build a decision tree of at least five branch nodes that sorts a respondent by substantive commitment rather than by label. Each node should be phrased as a direct question the respondent can answer without knowing which named position it leads to—never as “Are you a consequentialist?”—and should cover: (i) commensurability of theories on a shared scale; (ii) whether moral uncertainty produces a distinct second-order reason; (iii) whether the correct procedural response is proportional allocation across theories, precautionary deference to the most restrictive theory, or suspension of judgment with default to ordinary morality; (iv) whether the agent’s moral character or institutional role introduces a non-derivative constraint on decision-procedure; and (v) whether extreme scale or irreversibility transforms the problem from one of risk management into one of normative pluralism. Each leaf should carry the name of an actual position in the literature if one exists—e.g., “Maximize Expected Choice-Worthiness,” “My Favorite Theory with Risk-Aversion,” “Meta-Normative Deference”—or be marked “unnamed,” together with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. You should also map any unoccupied combinations—such as a view that treats theories as incommensurable yet applies proportional allocation, or one that suspends judgment but permits consequentialist reasoning in high-stakes cases—and briefly diagnose whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining which diagnostic question fails to distinguish them and why.

Organize your response in the following sections, with approximate lengths: (1) The Diagnostic Case and Starting Commitment (about 150 words), describing the rescue scenario and the specific dimensions of uncertainty; (2) The Tree Structure (about 250 words), presenting each branch node in sequence with the exact wording of the question and the logical paths it opens; (3) Leaf Taxonomy and Unoccupied Space (about 300 words), listing each leaf with its named or unnamed status, its awkward commitment, and notes on unoccupied combinations; (4) Indistinguishability Flags (about 150 words), identifying conflated positions and the source of the failure. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; this must be usable for students without web access. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for seminar distribution.
