I write historical fiction and have come to hold a particular view about path dependence: not merely that history is contingent, but that large institutional forms—parliamentary governance, centralized credit—become what I would call "narratively locked in." By this I mean they persist not because they are optimal but because they supply the only shared descriptive framework dense enough for large-scale coordination; once such a form achieves sufficient complexity, even revolutions that overthrow it tend to reproduce its formal skeleton, since actors cannot construct an alternative with comparable descriptive coherence. What would genuinely count against this position? I do not mean the standard butterfly-effect objection, but deeper defeaters: empirical cases in which societies broke the skeleton without reproducing it, formal or game-theoretic results showing that macro-coordination does not require narrative continuity, or rigorous counterfactual arguments that the same functional outcomes would have arrived through radically different institutional shapes. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward. Which of them would a committed defender of narrative lock-in concede as genuinely damaging, and which would they dismiss by reinterpreting the apparent break? Finally, does the view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by treating every seeming rupture as a hidden reproduction of the underlying skeleton—and if so, is that insulation a theoretical virtue or a vice for a claim of this kind?
