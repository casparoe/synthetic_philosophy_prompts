My philosophy teacher, who leads our school’s upper-level seminar on ethics and philosophy of mind, has rendered clear verdicts on whether several classic thought experiments function as legitimate intuition pumps or as misleading ones. She accepts Judith Jarvis Thomson’s violinist scenario as legitimate, holding that it cleanly isolates the distinction between a right to life and a right to sustained bodily support, and that the intuition one may unplug survives when framing details are altered. She rejects the “fat man” variant of the trolley problem as misleading, arguing that direct physical involvement introduces a visceral confound that obscures the underlying moral calculus rather than clarifying it. She accepts the standard trolley switch case as legitimate, maintaining that it isolates the act-versus-allow distinction without emotional interference. She rejects Searle’s Chinese Room argument, claiming it relies on a fallacious intuition that understanding must be localized in a particular substrate rather than distributed across a functional system. She also rejects Jackson’s Mary’s Room scenario, contending that the “what it is like” intuition collapses once we distinguish propositional knowledge from practical acquaintance, so the pump begs the question it claims to settle.

Next week she will evaluate a newly proposed pump—call it the Distributed Replacement Pump—in which a person’s biological neurons are replaced one by one with functionally identical wireless synthetic equivalents scattered across separate buildings, until the entire mental process is geographically distributed yet causally continuous. The intended conclusion is that spatial contiguity is unnecessary for consciousness.

Predict her verdict on whether this pump is legitimate or misleading. More importantly, identify at least two distinct rules or theories about what makes an intuition pump persuasive or deceptive that could consistently accommodate all five of her prior judgments, state what each rule predicts about the Distributed Replacement Pump, offer your best guess at her actual verdict with reasoning, and then design one further hypothetical case—one not yet discussed in our seminar—that would best discriminate between the two rules you have identified.
