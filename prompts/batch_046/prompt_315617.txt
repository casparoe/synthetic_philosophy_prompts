Analyze the 1986 Challenger disaster as a case study in hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, but focus on the organizational epistemics rather than the familiar morality play of ignored engineers. First, map the actual pre-launch decision structure: what the Thiokol engineering team knew about O-ring behavior at low temperatures, what the NASA management layer received, how the temperature data was interpreted differently across nodes, and where the epistemic fragmentation occurred. Then examine how the Rogers Commission reconstructed these distributed, ambiguous signals into a single linear causal story—cold weather leading to O-ring failure, compounded by management pressure—that makes the outcome appear inevitable in retrospect. Identify the precise points at which hindsight bias operates (the false impression that the risk was equally visible to all actors ex ante) and where the narrative fallacy operates (the imposition of retrospective coherence on organizational noise). Finally, consider what the episode shows about whether these biases can be corrected in institutional post-mortems, or whether they serve an unavoidable social function—such as the allocation of blame—that renders them structurally resistant to debiasing.
