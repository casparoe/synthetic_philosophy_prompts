I am preparing a single ninety-minute seminar for third-year political-philosophy undergraduates on the conceptual structure of “the will of the people” as it operates in populist rhetoric, and I want to avoid the usual genealogical survey from canonical figures through to contemporary movements. Last week, in conversation with a colleague in public law, I realized that the standard principal-agent framing misses the specifically metaphysical-political claim populists make about the unity and authenticity of the popular subject, so I have constructed a sustained teaching case: a direct-democracy municipality of roughly thirty thousand residents that governs through liquid delegation—citizens may vote directly or assign weighted, revocable proxies to representatives they trust—and where a recent referendum to privatize the municipal water supply passed by a narrow margin but was immediately denounced by a vocal minority not merely as corrupt, but as ontologically illegitimate, because several high-weight proxies had received undisclosed consulting fees from the bidding corporation and therefore, they argue, the result expresses only a “manufactured” will rather than the “real” will of the people.

Plan the seminar’s explanatory arc. First, specify the conceptual dependency that must be in place before students can see why the populist objection is not reducible to a standard democratic-legitimacy or corruption complaint, but rather presupposes a particular conception of the people as a unitary agent with an authentic will that can be betrayed even when every procedural rule has been followed. Second, describe the precise features this liquid-delegation scenario must have and must lack—regarding proxy weights, revocability, the visibility of financial influence, and the scale of the municipality—so that it isolates that metaphysical claim rather than collapsing into a familiar problem of delegated authority or preference aggregation. Third, map the misconceptions students are likely to form: that “will” here is simply aggregated preference, that the populist is merely being anti-democratic, or that stricter transparency and conflict-of-interest rules would dissolve the objection entirely; indicate exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be anticipated and headed off. Fourth, propose one brief diagnostic question or short exercise I can use near the end to determine whether students have grasped the structural idea rather than just adopted a cynical stance toward democratic mechanisms. Finally, state what standard bodies of material—canonical texts in the history of political thought, comparative studies of populist movements, normative democratic theory—I should explicitly bracket, declare out of scope, and openly refuse to cover at this level. I need the dependency order, the carrying example’s design constraints, the misconception map with defusion points, the check for understanding, and the exclusion list.
