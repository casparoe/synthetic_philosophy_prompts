I sit on my university's Committee on Extraterrestrial Communication and Institutional Risk, and on Thursday we must vote on whether to approve funding for the directed radio transmission of a comprehensive cultural and scientific archive toward a nearby star cluster. The proposal's risk assessment turns on anthropic reasoning: proponents of rejection invoke the Self-Indication Assumption to argue that our existence as technological observers makes intelligent life cosmically common, which raises the probability that other civilizations could intercept our signal and respond in unpredictable ways, and they contend that institutional caution demands we decline. Opponents reply that restricting our reference class through the Self-Sampling Assumption shows we have no non-circular basis for treating ourselves as typical, and they charge that inferring cosmic abundance from our local conditions reproduces the error of the presumptuous philosopher—treating our particular observational standpoint as unremarkable without adequate justification—thereby undermining the risk calculation rather than supporting it. A minority faction urges us to set both frameworks aside and rely exclusively on empirical SETI surveys, but concedes that no relevant survey data yet exists for this cluster.

Please draft the committee's formal decision memo recommending approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, including a concrete implementation or review timeline. The memo should explicitly distinguish the self-sampling and self-indication approaches, assess whether the presumptuous-philosopher objection defeats the precautionary argument or merely constrains its scope, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side—whether that is the epistemic humility required by SSA or the abundance implication drawn from SIA—rather than dismissing it. It should be written as an actionable institutional document to which both researchers and dissenting members could reply, without bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations.

Lead with the bottom-line recommendation in the first sentence, followed by reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
