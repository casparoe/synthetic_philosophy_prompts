I've been writing a feature on the Born-rule problem in Everettian quantum mechanics, and it's doing my head in. The debate's been stallled for years: one camp — Deutsch, Wallace, Saunders and the symmetry-argument programme — says rational agents in a branching world ought to weigh credences by squared amplitudes. Then there's the critics, Kent and others, who say that's circular: you're baking the Born weights into axioms for rational behaviour, and in a genuinely deterministic multiverse there's no ontological fact picking out one branch as 'the actual one', so probabilty talk is just a category mistake. Meanwhile the decoherence pragmatists think the whole dispute's moot because environmental decoherence already suppresses interference and fixes the pointer basis, leaving the weights as bare Hamiltonian coefficients.

My working theory is that this isn't really about physics. It might be verbal — 'probability' meaning credence for one side, objective measure for another, and something like 'branch density' for a third. Or they might be asking different questions under one heading: epistemic norms for agents, ontology of the wavefunction, or whether branching needs a 'preferred present' to be intelligible. Actually, I've had this wierd hunch that the hidden driver is exactly that last thing — that the dispute is a proxy for whether quantum mechanics needs a 'preferred present' or whether branching is just a mathematical artefact of unitary evolution. Is that plausible?

But I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Is the stall substantive — a hidden formal disagreement about self-locating uncertainty? Is it about standards of evidence — what counts as deriving versus assuming? Or is it bedrock intuitions about determinism that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there anything — a distinction, a theorem, a reframing — that could actually shift it, or should we expect it to stay stuck?
