I am a retired molecular biologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken to auditing philosophy courses at my local university; I find that the conceptual rigour of philosophy complements the empirical habits I spent forty years cultivating, though I often struggle to judge student arguments with the same confidence I once had in assessing experimental design. This term I am following a module on Wittgenstein's philosophy of religion, and I have been asked to assist in marking a short essay examination. The question set was as follows: 'Wittgenstein is frequently interpreted as holding that religious belief operates within its own grammatical framework, embedded in a form of life, and therefore should not be evaluated as if it were an empirical hypothesis subject to evidential confirmation or refutation. Evaluate this interpretation with particular attention to whether it renders religious belief rationally defensible against internal criticism, and address the concern that such a view ultimately reduces religious commitment to a mere social convention or expressive attitude lacking cognitive substance.'

The student answer I have before me is partly sound but worries me in its finer points. The student correctly observes that Wittgenstein distinguishes the grammar of religious language from empirical discourse, and rightly notes that belief is intertwined with practice rather than with theoretical justification. They also identify that religious language possesses an internal coherence governed by rules that are not derived from empirical observation, and they correctly state that a form of life is given rather than arbitrarily chosen. Nevertheless, the student then asserts that Wittgenstein treats religious propositions as possessing literal truth-values that are systematically suspended within the practice, while simultaneously describing religious commitment as essentially expressive—akin to an emotional posture lacking cognitive substance. They further claim that because forms of life are given, religious belief is rationally insulated not only from external scientific critique but from any internal revision or doubt, and they draw an explicit analogy to Kuhnian paradigms, suggesting that a religious form of life can only be abandoned wholesale, never modified from within.

I should be grateful if you would grade this response, indicating the specific marks it ought to receive and justifying that judgement with precise references to where the reasoning succeeds and where it fails—particularly with regard to any misattributions of Wittgenstein's actual view or to related thinkers such as Phillips. I am less interested in a general impression than in a point-by-point assessment that I might use to calibrate my own judgement.
