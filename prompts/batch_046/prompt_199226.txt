been hanging around rationalist forums since the early 2010s and lately "shut up and multiply" keeps getting thrown around like it ends debate. usually its when someone objects to an expected-value argument that pushes toward some extreme conclusion — say, diverting almost all resources to a far-future intervention with astronomically high payoff but negligible credence, or accepting a tiny risk of catastrophic harm for an even tinier chance of unbounded utility. people act like the phrase settles the moral-risk objection without saying which part of the objection it hits.

i want a real disambiguation, not a lecture. lay out the precise claims "shut up and multiply" could be making, from the trivial to the radical, as a menu i can point to:
1. literally just "calculate EV and dont skip steps"
2. any decision rule that deviates from EV maximization is internally incoherent (maybe citing independence or continuity axioms implicitly?)
3. intuitive resistance is always cognitive bias and never tracks real normative structure
4. disagreements over outcomes should be resolved by averaging probabilites, not by arguing about values
5. with unbounded utility and arbitrarly small probabilities, multiplying forces you into "fanatacism" and thats correct — not a bug but the honest output
6. maybe something about temporal coherence: if your multiplied EV requires you to violate commitments your future self holds, then the slogan is self-undermining unless you add a diachronic consistency constraint on preference stability?

for each reading, say whether its defensible, false, or just a platitude, and crucially which ones actually justify dismissing objections about moral risk, lexical preferences, or the instability of an agent's values under extreme scaling. im especially curious about the interaction between unbounded utility and the slogan: is the fanaticism produced by the math, or is the slogan smuggling in a normative claim that we should accept that fanatacism? and if we inject a constraint that the agent's preference ordering must stay stable across time — a kind of "diachronic lock" — does that break the slogan, refine it, or just push the problem back one level?
