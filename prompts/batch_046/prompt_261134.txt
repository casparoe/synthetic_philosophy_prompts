I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of history, and we've been working through recent attempts to treat microbes, climate patterns, and volcanic activity not as passive backdrops but as entities that genuinely shape historical trajectories. Several of the readings speak of the 1815 Tambora eruption and the spread of Yersinia pestis as "historical actors" apparently on the same explanatory footing as revolutionary assemblies or imperial bureaucracies, and the instructor insists this is not merely metaphorical. I find myself caught in what seems like a forced choice: if volcanic aerosols and pathogens possess agency in the same register as human decision-makers, then agency must be entirely decoupled from intentionality, which either trivializes the concept—reducing "agent" to "efficient cause"—or commits us to something like a distributed-cognitive or panpsychist ontology in which geophysical processes bear proto-intentional states. Even more troubling, if outcomes such as the Haitian Revolution or the Napoleonic Wars are genuinely co-authored by climate and microbes, then the normative framework within which we hold human beings morally and politically responsible seems to collapse, since responsibility would be diffused across entities that cannot be blamed or praised. So my question is this: is the only philosophically consistent way to maintain the "actor" status of germs and volcanoes to abandon the idea that human agency is metaphysically distinctive? Or is there a coherent middle position that grants non-human entities genuine historical efficacy without either collapsing into environmental determinism or diluting agency into mere causation, and if so, what does it do to preserve the specificity of human moral responsibility?
