Exam prompt: Foucault’s Discipline and Punish traces a transformation from sovereign power to disciplinary power. Using the panopticon strictly as an analytical model rather than a literal architectural plan, explain how modern institutions produce self-regulating subjects. Then, drawing on Foucault’s notion of counter-conduct developed in his analyses of pastoral power and governmentality, distinguish counter-conduct from mere resistance and identify one tension it poses for the claim that disciplinary power achieves complete internalization of surveillance.

Student answer: Foucault contends that disciplinary power largely supersedes sovereign power by substituting the visible spectacle of punishment with a continuous, invisible apparatus of surveillance. The panopticon illustrates this mechanism: because inmates can never verify whether the central tower is occupied, they internalize the observer’s gaze and become docile bodies. This reveals that modern power is productive—it generates subjectivity rather than merely restricting behavior. Counter-conduct, however, should not be reduced to simple opposition; it is an active ethical practice by which individuals form their own conduct according to norms that diverge from those prescribed by governing authorities. Historical ascetic movements, for example, refused pastoral guidance while maintaining rigorous self-discipline, thereby redirecting disciplinary techniques toward autonomous ends. Consequently, counter-conduct complicates the panoptic thesis: self-regulation is never wholly determined by institutional architecture, since subjects can appropriate surveillance practices for their own purposes. At the same time, it is important to recognize that sovereign power contained no internalizing dynamics whatsoever; it functioned exclusively through external coercion and legal prohibition, which makes disciplinary power an unprecedented historical rupture in the formation of subjects. Furthermore, counter-conduct operates as an inverse panopticon, replacing vertical institutional oversight with horizontal peer observation so that groups can preserve self-surveillance in the absence of any central authority.
