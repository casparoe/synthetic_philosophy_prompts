I've been covering this new paper on "scientific discovery" in foundation models and it got me thinking about that old Bayesian puzzle — the one where if a theory is cooked up after you already know the data, Bayes' rule says it can't confirm anything, right? Like with Einstein and Mercury's orbit being known before general relativity came along. Anyway, I was talking to a reseracher who said that when an LLM is trained on, say, every climate record through 2020 and then generates a novel feedback-loop hypothesis that explains those same records, it's basically the same epistemic dead end. The model's weights already "contain" the evidence, so any post-training explanation is just old-evidence retrofitting, and Bayesianism should assign it zero confirmatory value.

But then another person told me that's a category mistake — that the problem of old evidence is about an agent's prior beliefs being updated, not about data sitting in silicon weights. Still, I can't shake the feeling there's a real parallel here. If we treat the pre-trained model as having a "prior" over hypotheses, and the training data as evidence already absorbed, then doesn't any downstream reasoning that explains that training data run into exactly the same logical structure? Or does the fact that the model never "believed" the climate records as propositions before generating the theory break the analogie? I guess I'm asking: is there actually an existant Bayesian argument that post-hoc explanations from pretrained systems are epistemically hollow, or am I just smuggling in a false presupposition about how belief states map onto neural networks?
