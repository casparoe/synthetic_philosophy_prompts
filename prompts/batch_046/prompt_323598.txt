I am preparing a ninety-minute seminar for a residency of literary fiction writers—none trained in philosophy—on Nietzsche's tripartite analysis of history in the second Untimely Meditation. I want a pedagogical plan, not an exegesis: how to sequence the ideas so that the writers can use the framework to examine their own relationship to archival material, family myth, and institutional critique in fiction. First, lay out the dependency chain: what must be established before the three modes (monumental, antiquarian, critical) can be introduced, what must precede their respective dangers, and why the order matters—specifically, why the problem of an excess of historical consciousness must be felt before the remedies are named. Second, identify the single carrying example that should thread through the session: a concrete novelist's dilemma involving one project that simultaneously risks monumental mythmaking of a founding figure, antiquarian preservation of every document, and critical demolition of that figure's legacy; specify what features the example must possess to keep the three impulses distinct, and what it must omit to prevent collapse into a simple pro-versus-con history. Third, map the misconceptions this audience will likely form—especially the equation of critical history with mere debunking, and of monumental history with inspirational biography—stating exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted before it hardens. Fourth, propose one diagnostic task for the final fifteen minutes that reveals whether the writers have grasped the structural claim—that each mode answers a specific form of life-denying historical overload—rather than merely labeling three attitudes. Fifth, state clearly what should be excluded and acknowledged as excluded: for instance, Nietzsche's later genealogical practice, the essay's immediate philological context, and any reconciliation with eternal recurrence; frame the omission so the session feels complete rather than truncated. Finally, to avoid a standard rehearsal of the essay, please introduce the niche framing of postmemory fiction—the imaginative inheritance of traumatic or foundational events by generations with no direct experience—to ask whether the three modes still apply when the historian is not a witness but a novelist reconstructing absence. Do not provide bibliographies, further reading, or citations; the session must stand on its own reasoning.
