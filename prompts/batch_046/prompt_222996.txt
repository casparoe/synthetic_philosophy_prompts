I am a novelist who has begun convening a monthly seminar—twelve writers, historians, and artists seated around a single table in a library room, with ninety minutes and no screens—devoted to the problem of whether the past can be narrated from everywhere at once without one location swallowing the others. Rather than discuss this in the abstract, I want you to design a structured in-class game that makes the participants feel the structural failure through their own negotiations before anyone names it philosophically. The concrete scenario is this: the group must co-author one continuous, shared account of the 1498 encounter at Calicut, but each participant is assigned a different “regime of historical causation” that governs what counts as an event, a cause, and a sequence—specifically, a cyclical-genealogical regime in which occurrences return transformed and linear ordering is a distortion; a bureaucratic-archival regime in which only documented transactions possess historical reality; a meteorological-evental regime in which wind patterns and monsoon timing are primary actors and human arrival is secondary; and a network-structural regime in which no single landing matters, only the redistribution of trade nodes across the Indian Ocean. Provide explicit rules, physical materials (event cards with hidden prerequisites, a shared timeline sheet, and concealed veto tokens), and a payoff scheme that requires no real money: the group collectively “publishes” their co-authored paragraph only if all four regimes remain visibly intact in the final text, but any participant may silently play a veto token if their regime is subordinated to another’s chronology or causation, which collapses the narrative and forces a revision under time pressure. The rules must embed logical contradictions—so that placing an event on a linear timeline violates its cyclical return, or documenting a transaction erases its meteorological condition—so that the group hits an incoherence they generate themselves. After the activity, include a debrief that maps each breakdown onto a feature of global historiography: whose chronology was treated as neutral background, whose causation was reduced to local color, and whose archive was rendered silent. Be frank about what the game structurally cannot reproduce—almost certainly the long-duration violence of epistemic erasure, which requires historical knowledge rather than formal contradiction—and tell me precisely how I must supply that absence in my closing remarks. Also predict what will go wrong the first time I run it—likely one participant imposing a European chronological order as “common sense”—and advise how to correct it without breaking the simulation’s illusion. Please state how confident you are in this design, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
