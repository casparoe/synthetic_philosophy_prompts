In a recent postgraduate seminar on conceptual analysis and political philosophy, a fellow student presented what she described as the standard critical gloss on the phrase "Western values," attributing it to a widely circulated popular essay rather than to any particular academic monograph. Her summary ran as follows: that the category denotes a coherent normative cluster rooted in a continuous intellectual lineage—beginning with Socratic critical reason, passing through Roman legal universalism, and crystallising in Enlightenment autonomy—thereby yielding secular rationalism, individual rights, and democratic proceduralism as its core constituents. She then argued that critics who charge incoherence typically mistake empirical variation across Western societies for logical contradiction; observing that Scandinavian social democracy and American libertarianism both invoke the label, they conclude there is no shared content, whereas in fact the phrase requires only commitment to a particular form of institutional reflexivity—the principle that no authority is immune from rational scrutiny—rather than identical policy outputs. From this she inferred that the phrase operates in argument not as a philosophical taxonomy but as "strategic universalism," universalising locally contingent institutional preferences by framing them as civilisational achievements and thereby converting particular political arrangements into apparently non-negotiable moral boundaries. Consequently, she concluded, asking whether "Western values" is philosophically coherent is somewhat misplaced, since its rhetorical efficacy derives precisely from oscillating between descriptive cultural reference and prescriptive normative claim without ever fixing its extension.

I found her account persuasive in parts—particularly the observation about oscillation—but I am unsure whether she has fairly represented either the coherence debate or the actual argumentative functions of the phrase.
