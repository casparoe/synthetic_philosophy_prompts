For a decision-theory seminar, I have written down my philosophical profile on coherence arguments for expected utility maximization and want a consistency audit before I present. Here are my commitments: (1) Synchronic coherence of credences is normatively required by Dutch-book vulnerability, but this establishes only consistency, not truth-conduciveness. (2) Diachronic coherence via conditionalization is mandatory for evidential updating, yet it should be suspended when the updating event lies within a causally closed loop generated by the agent’s own intervention, since in those cases evidential coherence conflicts with causal consistency. (3) Preference coherence yielding expected-utility maximization applies cleanly to individual agents at a time, but does not extend to collective bodies unless there exists a genuine collective deliberative procedure isomorphic to individual reflection—a condition rarely satisfied in actual groups. (4) In Newcomb-style structures, evidential decision theory honors one coherence norm (alignment with evidential probabilities) while causal decision theory honors another (alignment with causal beliefs); I hold that both norms are internally legitimate but answer to different coherence ideals, so neither is incoherent per se. (5) Any coherence argument for expected-utility maximization tacitly presupposes that preferences are complete and transitive, which are substantive rationality conditions rather than mere consistency requirements; therefore coherence alone cannot ground maximization without circularity. (6) Micro-violations of coherence—such as intransitive preferences over low-stakes lotteries arising from transparent computational limits—are not genuinely irrational provided the agent recognizes them and withholds action on their basis.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, ranking them by severity. Then determine which single commitment, if revised, would dissolve the greatest number of tensions. Also flag any pair that only appears tense but dissolves once we distinguish evidential coherence from causal coherence, or constitutive norms from regulative ideals.
