Retributivists typically ground proportionality in desert—punishment is fitting because the offender deserves it—while consequentialists ground it in optimal deterrence, arguing that disproportionate penalties erode compliance or impose unnecessary social costs. A less common proposal, which we discussed in the seminar, holds that proportionality should be understood through distributive penal fairness: the offender must bear a burden that precisely offsets the unfair advantage gained from violating cooperative norms, regardless of whether that burden maximizes future compliance and regardless of whether desert is an intrinsic moral property. How does this view differ from standard retributivism and from standard consequentialism? What is its central conceptual weakness?

Student answer: The distributive penal fairness view is basically a version of retributivism, since it looks backward at what the offender did and assigns a burden based on that act. It also improves on pure retributivism because it doesn't rely on mysterious intrinsic desert; instead, it uses a measurable unfair advantage. Its main weakness is that it can't tell us exactly how to measure the unfair advantage without looking at future effects, so in practice it collapses into consequentialism.
