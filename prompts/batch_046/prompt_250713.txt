Having retired after thirty years in experimental thermodynamics, I have lately been attending a philosophy seminar for non-specialists, and I find myself returning to what is called the paradox of tragedy: why human beings seek out and value art that evokes genuine sorrow. Rather than ask for a survey of existing theories, I want to test whether my own judgments across concrete cases cohere into a single principle, or whether some must be sacrificed.

Here are six reactions I have recorded, mostly from my own experience and that of a colleague in the seminar. First, attending a live performance of Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, I felt acute distress at the protagonist's blindness and exile, yet simultaneously a kind of intellectual satisfaction—comparable, perhaps, to observing a complex system reach an inevitable equilibrium—derived from the formal inevitability of the plot. I judge this legitimate enjoyment, not paradoxical. Second, listening to a requiem mass in which death is treated with severe harmonic discipline, I experienced pleasure that seemed directed not at the grief itself but at the precision with which grief was structured; I classify this separately from the first case. Third, viewing a cinematic documentary about an actual historical famine—complete with narrative arcs, musical underscoring, and a redemptive coda—I felt a comparable pleasure, yet afterward I regarded my reaction as ethically compromised, as though I had extracted aesthetic value from real suffering. Fourth, a fellow seminar member reports that she returns every few years to a novel depicting the prolonged death of a child; she describes the experience as clearing and valuable, despite the absence of any redemptive resolution. I think her case belongs with the first, not the third, though I cannot defend this. Fifth, I recently watched a horror film in which characters endure prolonged, realistic physical torment with minimal narrative purpose; I felt arousal and excitement, not sorrow, and detected no paradox—only direct enjoyment of suffering, which I take to be outside the phenomenon altogether. Sixth, I was deeply moved by a folk ballad about an obscure nineteenth-century mining disaster, yet entirely unmoved when reading the same events in a statistical report. I believe artistic framing is necessary, but I am uncertain whether it transforms sorrow into pleasure or merely accompanies it.

My verdicts, then, are that cases one, two, four, and six represent authentic, defensible enjoyment of sad art; case three is structurally similar but ethically tainted; and case five is not an instance of the paradox at all. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps distinguishing representational distance, emotional authenticity, and cognitive framing—that captures all six classifications? Or does case four, which seems to involve enjoyment of unalloyed sorrow without formal resolution or representational safety, force me to abandon either case three or case one? Please also predict what your principle says about a case I have not mentioned: enjoying a virtual-reality simulation designed to induce the precise emotional texture of one's own anticipated bereavement. Finally, be explicit about which of my six verdicts your account cannot accommodate.

Please present your analysis clearly, and conclude with a fixed line beginning "Verdict:" that states the core principle (in no more than ten words) and, in parentheses, which case number, if any, must be dropped.
