My friend and I have been arguing over whether disjunctivism about perception genuinely dissolves the argument from illusion or merely renames its central difficulty. I want a case built to specifications to settle the point. Construct an ordinary, realistic perceptual scenario—no drugs, no hallucinations, no abnormal viewing conditions, and no longer than a paragraph—in which a brief or peripheral presentation of a familiar object yields a false judgment about one of its properties, yet a disjunctivist and a sense-datum theorist are compelled to agree on the phenomenal character of the experience. Then run the check: state the belief formed, the perceptual method, the relevant nearby worlds, and why disjunctivism denies that an illusion occurs here while the sense-datum view insists one does. Isolate the precise feature of the case that does all the philosophical work, and show what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed. Finish with the sense-datum theorist's best reply—that the disjunctivist is only redescribing a shared mental state rather than explaining it away—and with the disjunctivist's counter that avoids appealing to textual authority or citation.
