Write a philosophical dialogue between two interlocutors of equal competence—call them Alina and Marcus—who hold opposing views on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition. Alina argues that the regress is vicious and that any attempt to explain the unity of a relational fact, such as a specific shade of red being darker than another, by postulating further relations collapses into an infinite hierarchy of binding relations; she contends that only a primitive, non-relational state of affairs can genuinely unify the relata without itself requiring further unification. Marcus counters that relations are externally related and need no binding element, insisting that the appearance of a regress arises only from illicitly treating relations as if they were further relata that must be tied together; he maintains that a proposition is unified not by an internal binder but by the joint satisfaction of its constituent conditions.

The dialogue must feature real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues. In particular, Marcus should respond directly to Alina's claim that without a primitive unifier we are left with an unstructured aggregate—he should explain how his view distinguishes a genuinely unified fact from a mere list, especially in the case of negative relational propositions such as the fact that no similarity relation holds between two given universals. Alina, in turn, should press Marcus on whether his account can preserve the asymmetry and directedness of relations without smuggling relational structure back in at the level of the unifier itself, and she should challenge whether his rejection of binding relations ultimately renders the difference between a true and a false proposition inexplicable.

The exchange should be rigorous and conceptually dense, but it should avoid obscure technical apparatus unavailable to a well-read philosopher, and it must not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific scholarly texts. Focus the argument on the internal logic of the regress, the nature of negative facts, and the conditions under which a collection of elements constitutes a single proposition rather than a plurality.
