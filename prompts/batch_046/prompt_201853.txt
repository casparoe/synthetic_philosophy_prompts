After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend last night—she maintained that every apparent moral conflict dissolves once reasoning is fully clarified, while I kept returning to cases where two obligations seem to survive reflection intact—I want to stop trading examples and ask what the underlying conceptual architecture actually has to be. Write the full desiderata: what any adequate theory of moral dilemmas, whether affirmative or eliminativist, must deliver to count as a real account rather than a rhetorical stance. For each requirement, state the concrete case or consideration that motivates it: the specific phenomenology of being torn that must be explained or explained away; the distinctions that must not collapse—between epistemic and ontological dilemmas, between residual obligation and mere regret, between conflict of duties and conflict of values, between agent-regret and guilt; the logical constraints from deontic logic and the psychological findings about decision paralysis or post-decision distress that the theory cannot flatly contradict; and the boundary cases—symmetrical obligations, incommensurable goods, obligations arising from incompatible roles—that test the edges. Flag any pair of desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable under standard deontic assumptions, and say whether the tension is fatal or merely forces a choice of emphasis. Then, without a literature survey or citation list, briefly indicate how the eliminativist camp (all conflicts resolvable in principle through better reasoning or hierarchical ordering) and the realist camp (some conflicts are structurally irreducible) fare against the list, noting in particular which single desideratum does the most exclusionary work against one side. I am asking for the criterion list itself, since that is where our disagreement actually lives.
