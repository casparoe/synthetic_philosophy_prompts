I've been trying to systematize my position on the ethics of collective and institutional memory—specifically the tension between a duty to remember past injustices and a legitimate case for structured forgetting—by deriving rulings from concrete cases rather than from first principles. Here are the verdicts I've reached so far, which I want to keep consistent:

First, when a post-conflict state maintains accurate archives of documented atrocities but living political actors actively weaponize them to incite present-day intergroup violence, restricting public access (without destroying the records) is permissible; the duty to remember does not require making the past available as fuel for current harm.

Second, when a private family destroys the personal writings and photographs of a deceased relative who suffered severe trauma, and this is done at the explicit request made by the relative before death, there is no moral failure; transgenerational obligations do not extend to preserving private pain against the autonomy of the one who endured it.

Third, when an educational institution replaces a widely taught but factually false founding narrative—one that served an important social-cohesion function—with an accurate but more morally ambiguous history, the replacement is obligatory; preserving a falsehood for the sake of unity violates the duty to remember correctly.

Fourth, when an oral tradition of moral instruction fades naturally because the social practices and institutional roles that gave it functional meaning have disappeared, allowing that fade is permissible; there is no duty to artificially maintain mnemonic rituals that have lost their living context.

Now I face a case that does not map cleanly onto any of these. A distributed digital archive holds complete, verified data about a historical injustice. The preservation protocol is mechanistically identical to that used for active legal and administrative records—same redundancy, same integrity checks, same storage architecture—but the data is encrypted via a split-key scheme such that it is technically preserved yet practically irretrievable unless a specific quorum of descendants from both victim and perpetrator communities jointly agree to reconstruct it. The design is not intended to hide the past from present politics, but to make access structurally contingent on intergroup reconciliation. Some representatives of both groups demand immediate decryption on the grounds that preservation without accessibility is a form of suppression; others insist that maintaining the structural barrier is essential, arguing that unconditional access would reproduce the same dynamics of weaponized memory I accepted restricting in my first case.

If I want to remain consistent with my four rulings above, what should my verdict be? Which of my earlier cases is the closest analog, and does my track record genuinely underdetermine the answer? If I rule that the archive must be made immediately accessible, what principle am I adopting that my second and fourth rulings do not already commit me to? If I rule that the reconciliation-contingent structure should be maintained, what principle am I adopting that my first and third rulings do not already entail? And what single further case—perhaps involving synthetic or counterfactual mnemonic preservation—would distinguish between these two principles?
