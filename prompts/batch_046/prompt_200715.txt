I am revising the syllabus for a seminar I teach on the reception of Hellenistic ethics in modern psychological practice, and I want to include an exercise that pushes students past the usual popularizations. The contemporary Stoic revival—both in clinical cognitive-behavioral frameworks and in popular philosophical writing—tends to present Epictetus's distinction between what is in our power and what is not as a straightforward therapeutic boundary: internal judgments and intentions belong to us, while bodies, reputations, and the behavior of others do not.

For this exercise, I want you to argue for a more troubling thesis: that this modern dichotomy is conceptually unstable once we take seriously the Stoic theory of assent to impressions together with the doctrine of preferred indifferents. Specifically, develop the claim that if assent is genuinely within our power and is the proper object of moral evaluation, yet the impressions to which we assent are partly constituted by social conditioning and external circumstances, then there is no clean line separating the internal sphere of moral agency from the external sphere of indifference. Furthermore, show how the Stoic commitment to pursuing certain externals as preferred indifferents—health, friendship, civic participation—requires practical engagement with the very domain that the therapeutic dichotomy asks us to treat with indifference. I am not interested in a historical genealogy of modern Stoicism or in bibliographic recommendations; I want a rigorous conceptual argument, written in a formal philosophical register, that treats Stoic ethics as a systematic philosophy rather than a collection of self-help maxims. Please address this as if you were presenting to a room of advanced students who understand basic Stoic vocabulary but need to see its hidden tensions.
