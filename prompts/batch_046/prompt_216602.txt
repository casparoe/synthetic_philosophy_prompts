Please take on the role of an expert philosopher with specialization in rational choice, personal identity, and philosophy of mind. I want you to think carefully and systematically. Before giving your final answer, please start with a brief brainstorming session in which you list at least three candidate principles that could organize the intuitions below, and then evaluate each one against the cases.

I am a software engineer without formal philosophy training. I have been thinking about decisions that permanently restructure what a person cares about—what people sometimes call transformative experiences. I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind my judgments about which ones are rational to undertake and which are not, or whether I am simply being inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

Case 1: A close friend decides to become a parent. Before the decision, she valued spontaneity, career mobility, and minimal obligations. After having a child, she values stability, legacy, and intergenerational responsibility. She says she is a different person now and could not have predicted this from the inside, but she would not undo it. My verdict: it was rational for her to make this choice even in profound ignorance of what it would be like.

Case 2: A colleague opts for an irreversible neuroprosthetic implant that integrates a live economic-prediction feed directly into his affective system—he literally feels market movements as moods. Over time he stops caring about relationships, art, or physical health and cares only about portfolio optimization. He reports high subjective well-being and says he is finally who he was meant to be. My verdict: this was not a rational choice, and the post-implant self should not have veto authority over the pre-implant self's refusal.

Case 3: A person undergoes gender transition after years of deliberation. The core values—integrity, kindness, intellectual curiosity—remain intact, but what those values apply to and how they are expressed changes dramatically. The person reports not being able to imagine returning to the prior state, and feels more authentically themselves. My verdict: this belongs with Case 1; the transformation was rational and the pre-transition self was right to allow for it.

Case 4: A volunteer in a clinical trial takes a single high-dose compound that permanently eliminates fear of death. She abandons long-term projects, ignores safety protocols, and reports that planning is a kind of lie we tell ourselves. She insists she is happier and more real. My verdict: this transformation was harmful and should not have been chosen, even though the subject reports well-being and claims she is a different person.

Case 5: I am considering leaving a stable engineering career to join a religious community practicing radical simplicity and silence. I feel a persistent pull, but I worry it is a temporary emotional state that, if acted on, would lock me into a value framework I can no longer evaluate from the outside. I keep oscillating: is this like becoming a parent (Case 1) or like the neuroprosthetic (Case 2)?

My tentative principle is this: a transformative choice is rational if and only if (a) it expands the agent's practical option space rather than collapsing it onto a single dimension, and (b) the transformation is initiated through the agent's own reflective deliberation rather than through an external mechanism that bypasses reasoning. But I am unsure whether (a) distinguishes Case 1 from Case 4, since both seem to narrow focus—parenthood narrows spontaneity, while fearlessness narrows long-term planning.

Your tasks:
1. State precisely whether there is one coherent principle that delivers all five verdicts, or whether some must be overturned.
2. If you repair or replace my tentative principle, state the revised version exactly, and map each clause to the case that motivates it.
3. Say what your principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a person who, after a severe brain injury, permanently loses the capacity for regret but retains all other cognitive functions, and who then makes a major life decision—for example, donating all assets—that their pre-injury self would have rejected.
4. Be explicit about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and defend that choice.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. I need an analysis that stands on its own reasoning.
