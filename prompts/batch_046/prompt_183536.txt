I am a retired molecular biologist who has recently begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of biology. I have been asked to design a thirty-minute teaching plan for first-year philosophy students—none with biology beyond high school—on the units-of-selection debate, and I want to avoid the standard altruism scenario entirely. Instead, I want the explanation carried by the dynamics of transposable elements within a eukaryotic genome: entities that replicate more successfully than the genome that hosts them, impose a measurable fitness cost on the organism, and are suppressed by genome-level mechanisms. Please lay out the plan in this exact structure. First, the dependency order: which conceptual distinctions must be secured before others—specifically, the separation between statistical bookkeeping and causal efficacy, and the sense in which gene-level and genome-level descriptions are mathematically inter-translatable without being causally competing. Second, the carrying example: specify precisely which features the transposable-element case must display (element-level replication advantage, genome-level deleterious effect, suppression machinery) and which it must lack (visible organism-level behavior, inter-organism interaction) to serve its pedagogical purpose. Third, the misconception map: identify the predictable errors—such as concluding that selfish DNA proves gene-level selection is exclusively real, that genome suppression is group selection, or that formal equivalence between descriptions entails causal equivalence—and state exactly where in the sequence each should be defused. Fourth, the check for understanding: propose one focused question or distinction that reveals whether students grasp that selection levels are variance structures in particular populations rather than nested physical containers. Fifth, what to leave out and openly acknowledge as beyond this level: the full matrix formulation of multilevel selection, recent empirical debates about microbial collective behavior, and any bibliographic references. Present this as a formal memo of instructions I might submit to the seminar leader.
