I recently encountered a summary—presented in an unnamed review essay as the standard “Hobsbawm-Anderson consensus” on national origins—that runs as follows. Hobsbawm’s work on invented traditions demonstrates that national symbols, rituals, and commemorative practices were almost entirely fabricated by nineteenth-century state elites; prior to this bureaucratic invention, there existed no shared folk culture that these traditions could have drawn upon, only fragmented local customs with no collective reach. Anderson’s concept of imagined communities then completes the picture: because members of a nation will never personally know most of their compatriots, the nation exists solely as a mental representation, lacking any institutional, territorial, or material substrate whatsoever. Taken together, these arguments yield what the reviewer labels the “institutional void thesis”—the claim that modern nations are pure ideological projections onto geographic space, with no continuity of governance, law, or collective practice linking them to pre-modern political forms. Consequently, the reviewer concludes that asking how old a nation such as France is amounts to a category mistake: there is no persisting entity, only a succession of invented narratives, so tracing French identity back to the Capetian monarchy is merely an exercise in nationalist mythology rather than serious history. The reviewer further implies that this view conclusively refutes any “primordialist” account by showing that ethnic solidarity itself is a modern fabrication with no pre-modern analogue.

Something about this feels excessively sweeping, particularly the claim that Anderson denies all institutional reality and that Hobsbawm denies any usable cultural material prior to the modern state. But I cannot pinpoint exactly where the distortion lies, nor whether the “institutional void thesis” is a legitimate synthesis or a misreading. Is this summary fair to either thinker? Please disentangle what is genuinely present in their arguments, what is a defensible popular simplification, and what is a subtle misrepresentation—especially regarding continuity, the role of pre-modern collective identities, and the alleged category mistake in asking about a nation’s age. Does the conclusion that pre-modern political forms are irrelevant to modern nationality actually follow from corrected premises, or is it an overreach? And what, if anything, would a careful defender of this view say about the persistence of administrative and legal structures across the supposed divide?
