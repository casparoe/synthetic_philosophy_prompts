A colleague has reconstructed the following argument from Hegel's Philosophy of Right and applied it to contemporary global governance:

1. For Hegel, the state is not merely a mechanism for welfare or security but the actualisation of ethical life (Sittlichkeit) through a concrete, particular will.
2. War is the moment in which this particularity is affirmed: the state risks its existence and thereby demonstrates that its citizens are not reducible to private, calculative individuals.
3. If a state never faces the possibility of such a risk — if peace is permanently guaranteed by external institutions — its citizens' attachment collapses into the atomism of civil society, and ethical life decays.
4. Therefore, permanent, institutionally guaranteed peace is incompatible with the preservation of ethical life in any state subject to it.
5. Consider a hypothetical Universal Federation in which all states submit to binding supranational arbitration and collective security, eliminating any credible risk of interstate war.
6. By (3) and (4), membership in the Federation necessarily causes ethical decay in every member state.
7. Ethical decay undermines the state's very purpose and legitimacy.
8. Hence, every state has an ethical duty to refuse membership in, or withdraw from, such a Federation, regardless of its success in preventing suffering and promoting material welfare.
9. But this conclusion is far stronger than Hegel's own allowance for international law and treaties suggests; he permits agreements between states and does not demand perpetual conflict.
10. So either Hegel's view of war is self-defeating, or the argument has gone wrong somewhere between (3) and (8).

My colleague insists the reasoning is valid and that the only escape is to deny (1) — that the state is essentially ethical life — which he is reluctant to do. I want to keep (1) and (2) intact if possible. Which step should I reject, what does rejecting it cost, and how does the rejection preserve Hegel's allowance for treaties and international cooperation without collapsing into the view that war is merely instrumentally valuable?
