The usual line is that Whig history is presentism: we judge the past by how well it anticipated us. But I think there's a weirder thing going on. It's not just bias; it's a kind of retroactive causality. We treat outcomes that happened centuries later as if they were pulling the past along. Like, when historians write about the Magna Carta as a 'first step toward constitutional government,' they're not just being anacronistic — they're implying that the future existence of constitutional government was somehow already operating as a cause in 1215. Which is ontologically odd. The barons weren't being pulled by a future they couldn't conceive of; they were reacting to immediate grievances about feudel dues and forest law.

So Whig history smuggles in a hidden metaphysics: the present becomes a causal endpoint. This makes the past look like a failed or partial version of now, rather than a fully realised present of its own. I want to argue that this is different from ordinary progress narratives because it collapses the distinction between explanation and justification. Once you frame 1215 as 'moving toward' 1688 or 2024, you've made the past's value depend on its contribution to our current setup.

Not sure if this is just restating Butterfield with extra jargon, or if the 'retroactive causality' angle actually adds something. Also, am I being unfair to actual Whig historians like Macaulay? Maybe they were more self-aware about this than I think.
