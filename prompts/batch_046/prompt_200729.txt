Imagine Nelson Goodman—who died in 2000 and developed his theory of projectible predicates and entrenchment in Fact, Fiction, and Forecast (1955)—were presented with the phenomenon now called "shortcut learning" in deep neural networks. This is the tendency of classifiers, especially in high-stakes domains such as medical imaging, to rely on spurious correlations (for example, the presence of a particular hospital-label artifact or background texture) that are predictive in the training distribution but fail under distribution shift, a problem that became prominent in machine-learning literature only after 2010.

A contemporary philosopher of science has proposed extending Goodman’s framework with a notion of "counterfactual entrenchment": rather than measuring how deeply a predicate is embedded in actual past successful inductions, we should assess whether it remains stable across counterfactual interventions on the data-generating process. The claim is that this saves Goodman’s account for artificial systems, because a shortcut feature might be highly entrenched in the training data yet disappear under counterfactual sampling.

Predict, in a manner consistent with Goodman’s explicit philosophical commitments—his anti-psychologism, his rejection of primitive similarity, his insistence that projectibility is fixed by habitual linguistic and inductive practice rather than by hidden structure in nature—how he would respond to the following issues. - Where would he locate the failure: in the predicate itself, in the inductive practice of the model’s training regime, or in the misapplication of human inductive norms to an artificial system?
- Would he regard the ML case as genuinely parallel to human induction, or as a category error?
