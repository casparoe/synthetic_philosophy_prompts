I have recently accepted the role of discussant for an interdisciplinary seminar on the limits of formal reasoning, and I am already weary of the predictable misapplication of Kurt Gödel's first incompleteness theorem. Participants from jurisprudence and moral philosophy keep treating it as a blanket epistemic principle—asserting that any complex normative system must harbor truths that lie permanently beyond proof—while ignoring the theorem's specific conditions: the system must be consistent, recursively axiomatizable, and strong enough to represent Robinson arithmetic Q. I want an opening joke that turns on these actual technical constraints rather than on the vague pop-science idea of universal incompleteness. The punchline should land with listeners who understand that Gödel's result concerns syntactic undecidability within particular formal languages, not the ineffability of ethical judgment or legal interpretation. To keep it from being another generic reference to hidden mysteries, please construct the joke around a somewhat less common observation: that if one actually designed a legal code that satisfied all of Gödel's conditions—including the ability to encode basic arithmetic—then the resulting undecidable provision would not be an elusive moral intuition but an explicitly constructible self-referential sentence produced by Gödel numbering itself, which somewhat deflates the romantic appeal to unprovable wisdom. The joke should be intelligible without any citation, quotation, or appeal to specific editions, and it should clearly mock the leap from formal arithmetic to normative philosophy.
