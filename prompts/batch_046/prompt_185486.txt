Since I was recently appointed to a municipal education committee in a diverse urban district, I have watched parents and administrators replay the same dispute every term without anyone conceding ground or even addressing the other side’s central case. One faction argues for a model of public integration derived from French laïcité: public institutions should not register religious or cultural particularity in their rules, so that citizens meet as bare equals. The other faction, drawing on Anglo-American multicultural frameworks, replies that this alleged blindness merely universalizes a dominant culture, and that genuine equality requires active institutional accommodation of minority practices. The concrete flashpoints are always identical—school dress codes, language of instruction, and the framing of national history in curricula—yet the arguments never converge. I want a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled rather than a verdict on which side is correct. Is the disagreement verbal, turning on ambiguous uses of “neutrality,” “recognition,” and “integration”? Do the sides differ about hidden empirical claims—say, whether assimilationist policies generate social cohesion or alienation—while presenting the dispute as purely normative? Or are they answering different questions under a single heading: one about the legitimate scope of state power to shape civic identity, the other about the social-psychological conditions under which citizens feel they genuinely belong? I have begun to work with a distinction that seems to sit beneath the surface of these exchanges: the difference between prohibiting the visibility of difference within institutional spaces, and rendering that visibility invisible within the public narrative through which civic identity is formed. If this distinction is sound, does it reveal that the two camps are not contradicting each other but operating with incompatible conceptions of what a “public” sphere is for? Please diagnose the disagreement concretely, using what proponents of each position actually say about institutional rules and civic belonging; present your analysis as an assessment of the structure of the disagreement—whether verbal, substantive but empirically masked, divided by different questions, or ill-posed—rather than as an adjudication; and specify what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a conceptual clarification, an empirical result, a reformulation of the central question, or an argument that no further progress should be expected and why.
