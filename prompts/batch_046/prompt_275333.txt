The historiographical debate over who started the Cold War has been stuck for decades in a familiar rotation: traditionalist accounts stressing Soviet ideological expansion, revisionist accounts stressing American capitalist universalism, and post-revisionist accounts stressing structural bipolarity and mutual misperception. All sides work from substantially the same documentary base—the 1945–47 diplomatic collapses, Stalin’s February 1946 election speech, the Truman Doctrine, Soviet consolidation in Eastern Europe, and the Marshall Plan—yet the question “who started it?” never resolves; scholars merely redistribute weight among Stalin’s security obsession, U.S. economic hegemony, and systemic anarchy. Is this impasse a hidden empirical disagreement about a particular decision or signal, a conceptual disagreement about what “starting” means—intentional aggression, structural provocation, or the first rational escalation— or are historians running together incompatible projects—moral culpability, causal explanation, and institutional inevitability—under the single label of blame? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, multi-layered, or a sign that the question is ill-posed? And given that diagnosis, could any archival discovery, conceptual distinction, or reframing of the question actually move the field, or should we expect the stall to persist regardless?
