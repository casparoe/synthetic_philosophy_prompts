For a third-year undergraduate epistemology exam, draft one essay question on the regress problem—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—together with a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme totaling 25 marks. The question must be unanswerable by merely summarizing the three standard positions. Instead, present students with the following constructed case: an epistemic agent S accepts belief B1 only if B1 is linearly supported by B2, B2 by B3, and so on without termination. However, S also holds that every belief situated at an even-numbered level in this chain belongs to a subset of beliefs that mutually support one another through holistic coherence, whereas beliefs at odd-numbered levels depend exclusively on linear support from the level immediately above. Ask students whether this “staggered hybrid” genuinely escapes the regress, collapses into coherentism or infinitism, or introduces a distinct structural problem—such as a bifurcated justification architecture that lacks any unified account of knowledge or that leaves the odd-level chain ungrounded despite the coherence of the even-level subsets. The exam allows 60 minutes and expects an answer of roughly 800 words; require students to diagnose the logical structure, defend a specific verdict, and address whether the hybrid avoids the characteristic objections to each pure view.

Provide: (1) the exam question itself; (2) a model answer of about 600 words that demonstrates the required reasoning; and (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit conceptual moves, with marks assigned to each (summing to 25), a clear distinction between what an A answer must contain versus a B answer, and a one-line note on the typical student failure for each point. Possible must-hit points include: correctly identifying that coherence is localized to subsets rather than global; recognizing that linear dependence persists unbroken for the odd-level beliefs; assessing whether bifurcation undermines epistemic unity; evaluating whether the hybrid inherits the arbitrariness objection against foundationalism at its transition points; and offering a defended conclusion about whether knowledge is possible in this architecture.

Finally, state how confident you are in the analysis you provide, and explicitly single out the point about which you are least sure.
