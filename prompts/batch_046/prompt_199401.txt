I've been trying to sort out my stance on meat for awhile now and I keep getting stuck on this comparison people make between beef and chicken. There's this scoring framework that a few university dining programs and some animal-welfare nonprofits use — they rank foods by total suffering, counting both farmed animals and wild animals killed in crop production. The logic goes that chicken feed is mostly corn and soy, so harvesting those fields kills tons of rodents and insects, plus you have all the chickens themselves, whereas beef comes mostly from grass and there's only one cow death per alot of calories. So beef scores lower. But here's the weird part: when they count the farmed animals, they weight suffering by cognative capacity — a cow counts more than a chicken — but for the wild animals killed in the harvest, they just count bodies, with no discount for sentience or whether those animals are replaceable (like, if we stopped growing corn, those rodents wouldn't have been born anyway). So the same scorecard is using two different moral frameworks at once.

What philosophical commitments does that hybrid practice presuppose? Does it only make sense if you think wild-animal harm is purely instrumental — just a side-effect of crop farming — while farm-animal harm is intrinsic? Or can a single theory of value license treating a dead field mouse differently from a dead broiler chicken? And if we actually applied the wild-animal logic consistently, wouldn't beef look worse because pasture land displaces wild mammals over the long run, or wouldn't plant-based diets win by minimizing harvest area? I'm trying to figure out whether this beef-over-chicken recommendation is philosophically coherent, or if it's just patching together incompatible views of harm to reach a convenient answer.
