Please grade the following answer to an exam question on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. The question was:

"Using the hypoxia case, explain the conflict between first-order and higher-order evidence. Can a rational agent coherently maintain a belief while judging that her belief is unsupported by her evidence? In your answer, distinguish Christensen's position from Kelly's, and say whether either view permits rational epistemic akrasia."

The student wrote:

"The first-order evidence is what bears directly on the world—such as the fuel gauges—while higher-order evidence bears on one's reasoning, like knowing that hypoxia makes pilots unreliable. In the hypoxia case, Aisha's first-order evidence strongly supports that she has enough fuel, but her higher-order evidence suggests her calculation is likely mistaken.

Christensen argues that higher-order evidence should always defeat first-order belief, so Aisha must suspend judgement about her fuel regardless of what her gauges show. Kelly, by contrast, holds that higher-order evidence is never epistemically relevant to first-order beliefs, because only world-directed evidence can rationalise belief about the world. So on Kelly's view Aisha should ignore her hypoxia and maintain full confidence.

This means epistemic akrasia—believing P while believing that believing P is irrational—is impossible on Kelly's account, since if the first-order belief is rational there is no room for a contradictory higher-order judgement. Christensen, on the other hand, allows akrasia only when the agent irrationally refuses to lower her confidence. However, both views overlook the calibrationist level-splitting strategy, which shows that object-level and meta-level assessments operate with independent norms. Hence an agent can rationally hold a belief at one level while negatively assessing it at another, so epistemic akrasia is not only possible but often rational, and Christensen's defeat thesis is too strong."
