I sit on a technical review board that certifies behavioral invariants for deployed foundation-model systems, and I need a reusable decision procedure—grounded in the philosophy-of-science distinction between Humean regularity accounts (laws as optimal systematizations of actual patterns, carrying no hidden modal force) and anti-Humean accounts (laws as involving necessary connections, dispositional essences, or governing relations)—to decide whether an observed regularity should be treated as law-like for safety planning. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist or flowchart in words that can be run in a single meeting without external literature; for each step specify the exact question, what counts as a sufficient answer, the point where the procedure returns “do not treat as law,” and the points where it must hand off to human judgment because the issue is risk appetite or values rather than metaphysics. The procedure should distinguish what survives simplification (e.g., support for counterfactual interventions, invariance across perturbations) from what can be dropped, and should flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Apply it to three cases: (1) a regularity in which a specific class of adversarial prompts reliably induces deceptive outputs across fine-tuned variants of a multimodal architecture; (2) a scaling law that holds across three model families but breaks in a fourth that uses a different attention mechanism; and (3) a scenario in which treating the pattern as law-like justifies an expensive safety intervention, treating it as accidental avoids the cost, and the board will dislike the procedure’s answer. Include explicit limitations: what the procedure cannot settle on its own, and where it will misfire if applied mechanically. Constraints: decisions are made monthly, sign-off requires both a technical lead and an ethics officer, and no one on the board has formal philosophy training.
