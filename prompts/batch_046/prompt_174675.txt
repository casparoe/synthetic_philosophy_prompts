I've been teaching a grad seminar on peer disagreement all semester, and I keep coming back to this stubborn steadfastness view that I think gets stronger, not weaker, once you realize how much "peerhood" is actually manufactured by institutional filters. The position I'm flirting with goes like this: in domains like moral philosophy or literary interpretation, you shouldn't conciliate with your peer just because you discover disagreement. You should hold firm unless you can isolate a specifc error in reasoning or a hidden bias. And I think this is especially true when the peer designation itself comes from some bureaucratic matching system — citation metrics, review board assignments, algorithmic recommendation — rather than from an organic, domain-specific recognition of competence. In those cases, splitting the difference feels like deferring to an administrative artifact.

But I want to stress-test it. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this qualified steadfastness view to actually collapse? I'm not looking for a bibliography or a list of authors who disagree; I want to know what kind of observation, formal result, or conceptual move would count as a genuine defeater versus just an awkwardness. Would it take a proof that conciliation maximizes long-run accuracy even when peer labels are institutionally assigned? Or a detailed case study where someone who stuck to their guns in an algorithmically-matched disagreement was systematically misled in a way that conciliation would have prevented? And crucially, what would the defenders of this view themselves have to concede is a real problem — not just what critics say? Is there a live escape route if it turns out that manufactured peerhood correlates with hidden expertise that the individual can't recognize in themselves?

Give me an honest assessment of how exposed this position is, and tell me whether being well-insulated here is actually a virtue or a bug. I especially want you to distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, and to say whether this view risks collapsing into a more generic skepticism about expertise, or if it can stand on its own.
