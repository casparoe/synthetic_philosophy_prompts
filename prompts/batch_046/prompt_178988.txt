I've been working through some demanding long-range personal decisions lately—specifically, whether to commit to an intensive, multi-year path that requires severe present deprivation but promises substantial rewards to the person I will be in fifteen years—and I keep noticing a pattern that first struck me in moral philosophy discussions about aggregation. In standard objections to utilitarianism, the separateness of persons is invoked to block trade-offs that distribute severe burdens on one individual in exchange for small benefits spread across many others: the core idea is that there is no overarching "super-person" whose welfare can justify such redistribution across genuinely distinct lives. What puzzles me is that people seem to apply an apparently parallel logic highly selectively within a single life. They readily endure present hardship for future gain—saving aggressively, undergoing arduous training, accepting medical treatment—without treating the future beneficiary as a separate moral party who owes compensation back. Yet in other cases, particularly those involving deep psychological discontinuity—say, an intervention that preserves biological life but erases one's present values, relationships, and core memories—people often resist precisely by saying that the future beneficiary is not "me" in any morally relevant sense, so that my present loss is not genuinely compensated by that stranger's benefit.

Is there an established term for this selective, intra-personal analog of the separateness objection—the tendency to treat temporal stages of one life as morally separate entities under conditions of psychological discontinuity, while treating them as unified under routine intertemporal trade-offs? I am not asking for a survey of personal identity theory generally, nor specifically for Derek Parfit's reductionist account, but rather for the normative pattern itself: the conditions under which people deploy "separateness" reasoning across time rather than across individuals, and whether philosophers have carved out a distinct concept for it. If a term exists—perhaps in literature on prudential rationality, transformative experience, or the ethics of long-term commitment—I want to know how precisely it differs from the inter-personal separateness objection, and where its boundaries lie: for instance, does it apply only to radical transformative cases, or also to ordinary aging and gradual change? If no established term captures this, I would like the phenomenon defined with enough precision to distinguish it both from standard temporal discounting and from metaphysical debates about identity over time, together with an account of whether the parallel with the utilitarian objection genuinely holds or breaks down, and what that tells us about the structure of prudential reasoning.
