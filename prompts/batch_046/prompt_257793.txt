Write a parody of a UK civil-service job advertisement, in the deadpan bureaucratic register of an official vacancy notice (approximately 600 words), for the fictional post of Senior Intergenerational Substitution Analyst at the Ministry of Ontological Planning. The ministry’s remit is to evaluate public policies—climate-adaptation strategies, resource-allocation frameworks, and reproductive-health programmes—solely on the basis of whether they alter the numerical identity of future populations rather than merely affecting pre-existing ones. The advert should treat the nonidentity problem not as an abstract puzzle but as a routine administrative criterion: applicants must be able to explain why a policy that produces happier people who are numerically different from those who would otherwise exist does not, on standard harm-based accounts, wrong anyone, and why this makes obligations to future generations structurally different from obligations to present ones. Include a role summary describing the auditing of population-replacement pathways; a person specification listing the counter-intuitive competences required (for example, fluency in distinguishing better for from better than, willingness to treat non-existence as a baseline that cannot be improved upon, and capacity to approve interventions that knowingly substitute one cohort of future persons for another); an equal opportunities statement that ironically reveals demographic biases in which groups are designated as substitutable; and a brief about us paragraph presenting the replacement of entire generations as unremarkable bureaucratic practice. The satire must exaggerate real features of the nonidentity problem—especially the failure of harm-based reasoning to ground obligations to merely possible people—without distorting the logic; a reader acquainted with the standard philosophical discussion should recognise that the parody is internally consistent and fair. Maintain sustained deadpan irony throughout: never wink at the reader, and never state the objections explicitly, but let them emerge from the bureaucratic language itself. Do not request citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and write the response in British English.
