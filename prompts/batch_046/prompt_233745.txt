Exam Question: The centipede game is standardly used to illustrate backward induction: if rationality and common knowledge of rationality hold at every node, the first player should stop immediately, yielding minimal payoffs. Some philosophers of action have attempted to block this result by distinguishing diachronic consistency—the demand that an agent's choices form a stable plan across successive stages—from synchronic coherence, which requires that beliefs and preferences align at any single decision point. Explain how backward induction operates in the centipede game, and assess whether appealing to diachronic consistency can preserve the rationality of continuing past the first node without abandoning common knowledge of rationality.

Student Answer: Backward induction in the centipede game proceeds by reasoning from the final node backward. At the last decision point, Player 2 anticipates that Player 1 would stop if the game reached the preceding node, so Player 2 stops first. Knowing this, Player 1 anticipates that choice and stops one step earlier, and so on, all the way back to node one. This chain depends on common knowledge of rationality: each player knows the other is rational, knows that they know, and so forth.

Some philosophers block this result by distinguishing diachronic consistency—the demand that an agent's choices form a stable plan across successive stages—from synchronic coherence, which requires that beliefs and preferences align at any single decision point. By appealing to diachronic consistency, a player at node one can justify continuing because she is executing a cooperative strategy adopted at the outset. This preserves common knowledge of rationality, since the players still recognize each other as rational; it merely shows that rationality should be assessed as a temporal pattern rather than as a series of disconnected maximization problems. Synchronic coherence governs alignment within each moment, while diachronic consistency governs the sequence, so the two principles operate in separate domains and do not interfere.

That said, the resolution has limits. If diachronic consistency is interpreted as a form of substantive rationality—evaluating outcomes over the whole sequence—then it collapses into the standard backward-induction framework rather than escaping it. Moreover, Aumann's well-known analysis of agreeing to disagree provides the formal proof that common knowledge entails backward induction in perfect-information games. Still, because diachronic consistency applies rationality differently across nodes, the paradox is dissolved without altering any of the premises of the original argument.
