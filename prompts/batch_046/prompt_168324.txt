Within prioritarianism — the view that benefits matter more the worse off one is, but that equality lacks intrinsic value — the leveling-down objection alleges that any adequate prioritarian rule must sometimes prefer destroying resources that only aid the better off, thereby making everyone worse off rather than improving anyone's position. Rather than asking for a survey of familiar replies, I want you to treat three somewhat under-explored directions as live research agendas and tell me which deserve the most philosophical effort over the next several years.

First, consider whether formally specifying prioritarian social welfare functions with convexity or curvature conditions on the weighting function genuinely dissolves the objection, or merely relocates it into an arbitrary choice of parameter. Second, consider whether the objection has a hidden structural analogue outside standard distributive contexts — for instance, in intergenerational ethics, where reducing the welfare or number of future people might be analogously "leveled down" to avoid relative disadvantage across generations, or in non-human contexts where animal welfare aggregation faces similar curvature choices. Third, consider whether the objection should be read as an institutional design constraint — telling us that prioritarian aggregation rules are unusable for policy evaluation unless modified — or as a deeper reason to abandon prioritarianism for a pluralist or sufficientarian framework.

Please rank these three directions, together with any other open line of work you regard as more important, in order of priority for sustained conceptual research. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria: which questions are upstream of others, how tractable they are with current formal and conceptual tools, how neglected they are relative to the standard literature, and how much practical moral or institutional reasoning would shift if they were settled. Identify the trade-offs between tractability and importance, specify which directions you would deprioritise because resolving them would change little downstream, and state what kind of result or argument over the coming years would cause you to reorder the list. Please write your answer in British English, using British spelling and phrasing throughout.
