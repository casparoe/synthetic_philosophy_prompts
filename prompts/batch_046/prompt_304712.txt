I've been thinking on my own about whether societies built around honor, dignity, or victimhood are actually different in kind, or whether one is just morally better and the others are excuses — and I'm worried I'm just judging from inside my own culture. I want to run a 90-minute workshop for about twelve friends and neighbors, none with any philosophy background, so they can feel why comparing the three is harder than picking a favorite. Design a structured role-play with three rounds, all starting from the same scenario: one participant publicly breaks a shared agreement and insults another in front of the group. In round one, status is restored only through visible retaliation and the group rewards it; in round two, the same offense is answered by suppressing any outward reaction and appealing to an internal standard of personal worth that the group must silently respect; in round three, no response is permitted until the harm is documented in writing and submitted to a neutral arbiter who alone can authorize action. Give every participant a private role card with hidden incentives — cooperation tokens, status tokens, and a hidden personal cost that activates only if they violate their round's protocol — so the rules drive behavior before anyone names the theories. The group must reach a collective decision in each round about whether and how to respond, using only paper tokens and no real money. Include a precise timeline, the materials list, and the payoff scheme. Then write the debrief: map each round onto its corresponding culture, predict exactly what will go wrong the first time you run it — for example, participants refusing to play retaliation because it feels barbaric, or smuggling in arbitration habits from round three into round one — and state frankly which feature of moral superiority the activity cannot reproduce, such as long-term social stability or the subjective experience of being wronged. Finally, take a committed position: build the hidden incentives and the final comparison so they defend one specific conclusion — either that dignity culture is least harmful because it minimizes dependency, or that none can claim superiority without circular reasoning — and justify that choice directly in the rules and token structures you design, rather than leaving the outcome open. Make clear what participants should leave seeing: that each system solves a problem the others create, and that declaring a winner requires importing a value the exercise itself does not supply.
