I'm trying to map my own implicit rules about when to reason from the inside view and when to defer to the outside view, using concrete verdicts rather than abstract commitments. Here are my gut judgments across seven cases.

When I forecast whether I will finish a dissertation chapter by a self-imposed deadline, I discount my detailed work plan and instead weight my historical completion rate for comparable tasks, which is about thirty percent. The outside view dominates. When I forecast whether my close colleague—whose working habits I know intimately—will finish her analogous chapter by her deadline, I rely on her specific schedule and recent productivity rather than the base rate for academics in our field. The inside view dominates. When I evaluate whether a particular psychology study will replicate, I largely ignore my own assessment of its methodology and lean on the field's base replication rate of roughly forty percent. The outside view dominates. When I evaluate whether my own judgment that the study will replicate is correct, I apply the base rate of individual predictions in this domain being accurate—around fifty-five percent—rather than trusting my reasoning. The outside view dominates again, but now reflexively, applied to my own cognition. When I decide whether to undergo a medical procedure with a seventy-five percent trial success rate, but my physician identifies biomarkers placing me in a subgroup with an eighty-eight percent success rate, I adopt the subgroup figure. Here a specialized, mechanistically grounded view dominates. When I decide whether to invest in a venture where I possess deep domain expertise but the base rate for comparable investments is poor, I still defer to the base rate and decline. The outside view dominates. Finally, when I predict whether I will maintain a new exercise routine, I rely on population-level adherence data rather than my current enthusiasm. The outside view dominates.

What principle, if any, captures all seven? I can see at least two consistent rules. One holds that the outside view should prevail whenever the reference class is well-defined and the inside information is not causally independent of the outcome being predicted, except in cases of intimate personal knowledge about others or mechanistic subgroup identification. Another holds that the inside view is legitimate only when one has direct access to causal mechanisms or to another agent's specific mental states, but is systematically toxic when applied to one's own future behavior or to domains where self-deception is common—except that this second rule struggles to explain why I reject my own investment reasoning while accepting my colleague's planning reasoning.

Please state the two (or more) candidate principles precisely, identify which verdict each saves and which it must overturn, and say which further case—perhaps one I have not listed—would best distinguish them. In particular, I am interested in whether there is a coherent account of reflexive reference classes, where the outside view is applied to one's own reasoning about oneself, that avoids collapsing into global skepticism about first-personal judgment or into special pleading for the self.
