Question: Explain the classic statue-and-clay puzzle about material constitution: why does it seem that a statue and the lump of clay composing it occupy exactly the same region at a given time, yet have different conditions for persistence? Then evaluate the following proposal: instead of treating constitution as a static relation holding at a single time, we should understand it through “recycling-tolerant form-potential”—the idea that matter constitutes an object just in case it retains, through continuous reshaping without being destroyed, the dispositional capacity to take on that object’s form again. Does this resolve the coincidence problem, or does it confuse metaphysical constitution with modal properties of the material? In your answer, clearly distinguish (i) the strict identity view, (ii) the non-identity constitution view, and (iii) the four-dimensionalist view that analyzes objects as spacetime worms. Also consider whether this framework applies differently to non-recyclable materials (e.g., marble ground to dust).

Student answer:
The puzzle is that at time t the statue and the clay share a location, but the clay survives being squashed while the statue does not. If they share all properties, including persistence conditions, they should be identical; since they do not, they must be distinct, which violates the principle that two objects cannot occupy exactly the same region at the same time.

The recycling-tolerant proposal says the statue just is the clay when the clay’s form-potential is actualized. This eliminates coincidence, since there is only one thing present—the clay—described in two ways. So the proposal supports the strict identity view (i).

But this conflates constitution with modality. The correct view is (ii): the statue is distinct from the clay yet constituted by it, which allows shared location without identity. The four-dimensionalist view (iii) solves the problem by treating both as four-dimensional worms; because they extend through different temporal slices, like pages in a book stacked separately, they never actually share space at all.

One problem is that if the clay is melted into bronze and loses its potential, the proposal implies the statue never existed in the first place, which is absurd. Also, marble cannot be reshaped without being destroyed, so marble statues have no constituting matter on this account, which is a clear failure.
