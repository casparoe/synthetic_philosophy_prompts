I am trying to determine whether a single principle can distinguish Hobsbawm-style invented traditions from Anderson-style imagined communities, and I want to test a tentative formulation. My proposal is that a collective identity counts as an invented tradition only when two conditions hold jointly: first, a demonstrable discontinuity in social practice across generations, and second, an institutional interest in concealing that discontinuity behind a false genealogical narrative. If the discontinuity is present but openly acknowledged, or if there is no institutional project of genealogical masking, then the identity is merely an imagined community—modern and constructed, but not deceptive in the specifically historical sense.

Here are my verdicts on five cases. First, the Scottish Highland clan tartans and kilt standardized in the nineteenth century and projected backward onto medieval clans: this is an invented tradition, with both conditions satisfied. Second, the Italian nation-state after 1861, built through an explicit political project of forging a national population where prior political consciousness had been local or dynastic: this is an imagined community, not an invented tradition, because the rupture is admitted rather than masked by false ancestry. Third, the Japanese imperial institution’s claim of an unbroken divine lineage reaching back to the traditional founding date of 660 BCE, sustained by state ritual and education despite modern historical consensus that the early chronology is mythical: I want to classify this as an invented tradition, yet I hesitate, because unlike the tartan case there is genuine institutional continuity—the imperial office has persisted for well over a millennium—so the discontinuity is in the narrative rather than in the practice itself. Fourth, the modern Republic of Benin adopting the name and symbolic heritage of the pre-colonial Kingdom of Benin in present-day Nigeria, with which it shares no historical institutional connection: clearly an invented tradition, driven by state-building interest and false genealogy. Fifth, the Iroquois Great Law of Peace, transmitted orally for centuries but rendered in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as a written democratic constitution and then used by activists and scholars as an ancient model of representative government: I treat this as an imagined community or modern interpretive frame rather than an invented tradition, since the underlying council practices were real, and the anachronistic framing does not seem to depend on concealing a total institutional break.

These verdicts pull in different directions. The first and fourth cases fit my principle cleanly; the second also fits, as the negative instance. The third case creates tension: my principle seems to place it with the first because of false genealogy and institutional interest, yet my intuition is that it belongs elsewhere because of persistent institutional reality. The fifth case also tests the boundary, since there is anachronism without, I think, concealment of rupture.

Please do the following. First, state whether my two-condition principle can be repaired—perhaps by distinguishing narrative discontinuity from institutional discontinuity—so that it delivers all five verdicts, or whether it should be abandoned. Second, if repair is possible, present the repaired principle precisely, identifying which case motivates each clause. Third, apply the repaired principle (or, if it is abandoned, the best replacement) to two cases I have not listed: a diaspora community that in the twenty-first century reconstructs a standardized ancestral language from fragmented archival records and teaches it in schools as the authentic mother tongue, despite no living speaker having used that standardized form; and a corporation that invents a founding myth linking itself to a medieval guild for marketing purposes, despite having no legal or organizational continuity with that guild. Fourth, state the verdicts your principle gives for these two new cases. Fifth, be explicit about which of my original five verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether accommodating them forces me to overturn my judgment about the third case, the fifth case, or both.
