My verdicts on whether a relation is genuinely causal, across ordinary experience, Al-Ghazālī-style occasionalism, and deterministic physics. (1) A struck match lights sulfur and flame follows: the match causes the flame. (2) In an occasionalist metaphysics, God directly creates the burning every time fire touches cotton; the fire has no causal power, so God alone is the cause, though I still want to call fire an “occasion” in a non-empty sense. (3) In deterministic physics, the state of a system at t₁ lawfully fixes its state at t₂; I judge this real causation even though the earlier state exerts no “active power.” (4) A surgeon makes an incision and the patient heals, but cells do the actual repairing; I call the surgeon an instrumental or partial cause. (5) A rain dance is performed only when storms are already likely, and rain follows; this is correlation, not causation. (6) Entangled particles show perfect correlation with no physical interaction; not causal. The tension: in (2) the match lacks power and is denied causal status, yet in (4) the surgeon also lacks direct healing power but is granted it, and in (3) determination without power counts as causation while constant conjunction without power in (2) does not. Is there a single principle—perhaps counterfactual robustness across background conditions, or transparency of mediating mechanisms—that captures (1) through (6)? If not, which verdict should I abandon? Apply your principle to one further case: a world where God creates burning whenever a match strikes, but would not create it if the match struck under physically different laws.
