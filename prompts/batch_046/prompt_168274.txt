As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy with any seriousness, I have become fixated on a problem Douglas Hofstadter raises in Le Ton beau de Marot: the translation of a short poem by Clément Marot under self-imposed formal constraints—specifically, the preservation of rhyme scheme, syllable count, and meter across languages whose phonologies do not align. Before I evaluate any theory that claims to explain how such constrained translation succeeds or fails, I want the full desiderata list.

Write the requirements that any adequate theory of formal-constraint poetic translation must satisfy, using the Marot case as the central test bed but framing the criteria generally. For each desideratum, provide the motivating consideration—whether drawn from the translation problem itself, from analogies to invariant-preservation in physical theory, or from broader norms of interpretive fidelity. Specifically address: (i) whether there exists a genuinely invariant poetic kernel—semantic, affective, or structural—that must survive translation regardless of linguistic surface, or whether formal constraint necessarily forces the abandonment of at least one dimension; (ii) which constraints are non-negotiable for adequacy (for example, consistency with the translator's actual linguistic competence, or avoidance of morphological violations in the target language) and which are camp-specific preferences (for example, strict rhyme preservation versus approximate phonological echo); (iii) whether the space of acceptable translations can be modeled as an optimization surface with formal rules acting as potential wells, and if so, what quantity—if any—is being minimized or conserved across that surface; and (iv) which pairs of desiderata appear jointly unsatisfiable, particularly the tension between literal lexical correspondence and formal structure preservation, or between emotional register fidelity and phonological pattern matching.

Distinguish hard failures, in which the theory predicts an impossible translation or violates a linguistic universal, from merely awkward results, in which the translation is technically adequate but aesthetically impoverished. Then, briefly, indicate how three strategic orientations—literalist fidelity to lexical meaning, adaptive-formalist substitution of equivalent formal structures, and what might be called a variational approach that treats translation as navigating a constrained optimization landscape—fare against your list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their underlying cases rather than on scoring the strategies; I want to know what the theory has to be able to do before I ask whether anyone has done it.
