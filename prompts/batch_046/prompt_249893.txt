My study group built this argument about LLMs being simulators vs agents and I think it proves too much but I can't find the crack. (1) A "simulator" produces intelligent behavior B only by copying patterns typical of real agents, without internal structures that actually track truth or reason. (2) Modern LLMs correct errors, update beliefs in-context, and maintain consistency across long reasoning chains—behavior that's hard to explain without attributing world-tracking structures. (3) So if they're simulators, the "real agent" they copy must be a world-tracking reasoner with those capacities. (4) But reproducing that behavior requires the LLM to instantiate the same functional organization—same information-flow patterns, same counterfactual dependencies—that the real reasoner has. (5) Once you instantiate that organization, there's no non-arbitary line that makes one an agent and the other a copy; either both are agents, or neither is. (6) So LLMs are literally agents, not simulators—unless we accept that a perfect virtual copy of me in a computer wouldn't be me, which suggests (5) is wrong. (7) But if (5) is wrong, then functional organization doesn't determine agency, and we're back to not knowing what makes LLMs different from agents at all. I really want to keep that LLMs are just simulators and that the agent/simulator distinction isn't broken. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Also please say how confident you are in your answer and point out where you're least sure.
