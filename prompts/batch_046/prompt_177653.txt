I've been working out my own view on actions that go beyond what morality strictly demands—what philosophers call supererogation, though I have no formal training in that literature. Here are my concrete verdicts. A bystander who risks death to save a drowning stranger acts beyond duty. A parent who donates a kidney to save her child meets her duty, not exceeds it; but donating that same kidney to a stranger is beyond duty. Someone who volunteers every Saturday for ten years is admirable, yet not initially obligated to continue—though if she suddenly stops, people react with disappointment that feels like moral blame, suggesting an obligation grew through sustained practice. If you make a public promise to perform a dangerous rescue and then carry it out, the act is obligatory fulfillment, not supererogation, even though the physical risk is identical. A firefighter saving ten people during her shift is meeting her professional role, but if she stays past shift to save an eleventh, that specific save feels supererogatory—despite her having already done ten heroic acts. A wealthy person who donates ninety percent of her fortune to effective charities clearly goes beyond duty, while one who donates ten percent seems to fail no clear obligation—there is no obvious threshold where duty ends and surplus begins. These judgments conflict: long practice creates obligations, explicit promises convert heroic acts into duties, professional roles absorb heroism up to a boundary, and wealth produces scale without a cutoff. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about voluntarily assumed roles, explicit commitments, or comparative burden—that captures all of them? Or must I reject at least one verdict? And if such a principle exists, what would it say about whether a billionaire who donates exactly half her wealth has done anything required, and whether a long-time volunteer can ever ethically quit?
