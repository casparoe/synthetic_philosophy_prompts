In a discussion about aesthetic testimony, a colleague shut down the idea that critics can convey knowledge of beauty by citing the proverb 'de gustibus non est disputandum' — taste is not disputable. She took it to mean that aesthetic judgments are purely subjective, so there's nothing factual for testimony to transmit. I'm not convinced that's the only reading, and I'm not sure which ones actually support her move. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses this proverb might stand for — e.g., about the nature of aesthetic properties, the accessibility of reasons, the legitimacy of disagreement, or the conditions for knowledge — marking which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which she probably needs? In particular, distinguish readings that make testimony impossible in principle from readings that merely make it unreliable or unnecessary, and say which of these the argument against aesthetic testimony actually requires.
