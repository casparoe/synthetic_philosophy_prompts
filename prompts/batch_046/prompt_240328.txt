I'm a software engineer without any formal training in philosophy, but I've been working through some older texts in an informal discussion group, and I recently encountered David Hume's suppressed essay Of Suicide, which I understand was written in the 1750s and circulated only after his death. The argument is far more nuanced than the caricature I expected: Hume appears to deny that suicide violates any duty to God, to society, or to oneself, provided it does not harm others, and he rejects the analogy that life is an unreturnable gift from a divine creditor. Instead, he treats the preservation of one's life as analogous to maintaining a useful system—something we continue only so long as it serves our overall well-being, and which we may discontinue when it becomes an enduring burden that produces more suffering than satisfaction. He even suggests that a person has a kind of natural authority over their own existence comparable to the authority one has over private property, so that ending one's life is not inherently a crime against nature but rather a decision about whether to maintain a process that has turned net-negative.

What confuses me is a tension I cannot resolve with my own background in distributed systems and identity management. Hume is famous for his bundle theory of the self—the view that the "I" is not a continuous substance but merely a stream of perceptions with no underlying owner—yet in this essay he writes as though there is a single, continuous agent who possesses authority over its life and may legitimately choose to terminate it. If the self is just a transient bundle of impressions, what exactly is the entity that holds the authority to dissolve the bundle? Is Hume implicitly relying on a substantial self here, or can his claim about authority be reconstructed within the bundle framework—perhaps by treating the decision as just one more perception in the stream that happens to have causal power over the others? I also want to understand whether his rejection of the "gift" analogy depends only on the logical weakness of comparing life to a loan, or whether it also presupposes a particular view of human agency that might conflict with his broader empiricism. I am not looking for secondary literature or a reading list; I just want help seeing whether this passage reveals an unacknowledged commitment in Hume's metaphysics, or whether there is a coherent way to read the authority claim without importing a substantial owner of the self.
