My parents left Singapore before I was born, and over the past year our family arguments about democracy have become unavoidable: they defend the PAP’s record—prosperity, order, meritocratic administration—as proof that institutional form is secondary to results, and I have been trying to reconstruct the strongest philosophical version of that view. I have arrived at an argument that I fear proves too much, and I need help finding the exit without abandoning what I take to be true about legitimacy. (1) A state’s claim to legitimate authority rests primarily on the discharge of fiduciary obligations—public order and widespread material prosperity—without which consent is coerced by chaos rather than freely given. (2) When these obligations are fulfilled, procedural mechanisms are instrumental rather than constitutive; democratic accountability is valuable because it tends to secure outcomes, not because it defines legitimacy independently of them. (3) Now consider a regime that fulfills these obligations but maintains what we might call epistemic closure: legal and informational structures that prevent citizens from accessing comparative data or organizing collective dissent, without distorting their direct experience of their own welfare. (4) Under such closure, citizens’ revealed preferences align with the regime’s continuation, and because their direct welfare reports remain accurate, there is no internal deception—only an absence of comparative perspective. (5) Therefore, the regime enjoys dual support: objective welfare fulfillment and subjective preference alignment, even though the alignment is causally shaped by the regime’s control of the epistemic environment. (6) If revealed preferences formed under closure still count as legitimate endorsement—because the citizens genuinely prefer what they have—then legitimacy requires nothing beyond successful governance and successfully managed information. (7) But then the distinction between Singapore’s actual performance legitimacy and a hypothetical authoritarian system that achieves identical GDP and social order through perfect epistemic closure collapses; both satisfy the same conditions. (8) Hence either performance legitimacy is reducible to a technology of outcome production and consent management, or one of these steps contains an error I cannot locate. I am committed to keeping (1) and (2), and I want to preserve the intuition that information control is not merely an instrumental detail but a normative defect in the authority relation itself; yet I cannot see which step to reject without either abandoning substantive legitimacy conditions or conceding that democratic form is constitutive after all. Which step is the weak point, why did it seem plausible, and what does abandoning it cost the overall position?
