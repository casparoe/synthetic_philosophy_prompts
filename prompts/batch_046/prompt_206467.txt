I will present at a philosophy of language seminar next Thursday to an audience of formal semanticists and logicians who specialise in truth-theoretic paradoxes. They are broadly sympathetic to hierarchical or revisionary logical solutions, sceptical of purely pragmatic escapes, and committed to preserving classical bivalence outside paradoxical cases. My thesis is that the Liar paradox reveals not a semantic contradiction but a pragmatic deadlock: a failure of assertibility conditions that arises when a truth-predicate's stability index — its resistance to self-application within a given conversational frame — falls below the threshold required for responsible assertion. I argue that we should separate semantic evaluation, which remains fully classical and deflationary, from pragmatic assertibility, which is indexed to this stability parameter. This allows us to retain deflationary intuitions without ascending a Tarskian hierarchy or revising the underlying logic.

Prepare me for the objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they will come, phrased as a formal semanticist would phrase them.
