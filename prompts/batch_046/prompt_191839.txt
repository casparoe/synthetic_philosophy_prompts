Question: Consider the wrong kind of reasons problem as it arises in both theoretical and practical philosophy. First, explain the structural distinction between a reason of the right kind and one of the wrong kind for holding a particular belief—using, if you like, the familiar case of pragmatic incentives to believe a moral doctrine. Be precise about why a wrong-kind reason is not merely a weak or defeated right-kind reason. Second, apply the same framework to practical reasoning about ends: is there a meaningful distinction between reasons that support adopting an end for its own merit and reasons that support adopting it only for its collateral effects? If so, illustrate with an example; if not, explain why the analogy with belief fails. Third, discuss whether accommodating wrong-kind reasons forces us to reject a broadly instrumentalist picture of practical rationality, or whether the distinction can be preserved within such a picture.

Student Answer: The wrong kind of reasons problem shows up when we have reasons that bear on whether it is good or useful to hold a belief, but not on whether the belief is true. For example, if I am told that believing in free will makes people behave more ethically, that gives me a practical reason to want that belief. But it doesn't give me any reason to think free will actually exists. This is different from having weak evidence: weak evidence still addresses the truth of the claim, whereas this reason addresses only my welfare. So the structural feature is that wrong-kind reasons target the attitude's consequences rather than its representational success.

When we turn to practical reasoning about ends, the same pattern applies. Suppose I consider adopting environmental activism as a central life project. I might have strong reasons related to social approval and career networking, but those don't bear on whether environmental activism is genuinely valuable. Meanwhile, reasons that point to the intrinsic importance of ecological preservation are right-kind reasons for adopting that end. So the analogy with belief holds: in both cases, we can distinguish reasons that address the merit of the state from reasons that address its side effects.

Some philosophers think this distinction collapses unless we accept non-instrumental reasons—reasons that directly justify the belief or end without being mediated by further goals. I think this is correct. If all reasons were ultimately instrumental, then wanting belief because it's pleasant and wanting belief because it's true would both just be ways of promoting my overall satisfaction. There would be no principled basis for treating one as appropriate to belief and the other as a category mistake. Therefore, recognizing wrong-kind reasons requires us to admit that there are genuinely non-instrumental normative relations—truth for belief, and perhaps objective value for ends.

That said, one might object, following Hume's well-known claim that reason serves the passions rather than directing them independently, that all reasoning about ends is ultimately instrumental. If that is right, then there are no genuinely non-instrumental reasons for adopting an end, and the wrong-kind/reason distinction for ends collapses into a distinction between efficient and inefficient means. I think this Humean objection actually supports my view: Hume shows that if we reject non-instrumental reasons, we lose the ability to distinguish appropriate from inappropriate motives for end-selection. So the wrong-kind problem for ends is real precisely because Hume's framework is incomplete.

One further objection is that belief is special because it aims at truth, whereas ends don't aim at anything in particular. But I think this objection misses the point. Even if ends don't have a constitutive aim like truth, we still evaluate them by their substantive merits, and reasons that ignore those merits are still wrong-kind.
