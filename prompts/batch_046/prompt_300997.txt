I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a university-affiliated hospital that serves a large East Asian diaspora community, and we must revise our policy for surrogate decision-making when an incapacitated patient has no advance directive and family members disagree. Our current policy applies a uniform statutory hierarchy—spouse, then adult child, then nearest relative—but the multicultural care office has proposed replacing it with a “role-stratified consultation model” drawn from Confucian role ethics: authority would be distributed among family members according to specific relational roles—spouse as conjugal partner, adult child as filial caretaker, sibling as peer—weighted not only by legal status but by documented performance of care obligations during the patient’s recent illness. Proponents argue that Western autonomy-based surrogacy treats the patient as an isolated rights-bearer and that this model respects her embedded relational identity; critics argue it risks essentializing gender and generational roles, invites intractable disputes over “role-performance” metrics, and may conflict with jurisdictional anti-discrimination norms. Please draft the committee’s written opinion for circulation before next Thursday’s vote. It should recommend a concrete course—full adoption, rejection, or a time-bound pilot with explicit safeguards—include reasoning that takes seriously the philosophical claim that role ethics offers a morally superior framework for this context, and honestly address the strongest practical and moral objection to your recommendation. The memo must be actionable by hospital administration without requiring further philosophical clarification, and it should specify an implementation or review timeline.
