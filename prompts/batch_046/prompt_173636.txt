I'm preparing a podcast interview with a moral philosopher who defends a reconstructed account of supererogation she calls the "risk-budget" model. On her view, obligations are not binary deontic boundaries but baseline allocations of an agent-relative moral-risk budget: each person is required to bear a certain portfolio of costs for others, and acts become supererogatory when they draw on discretionary reserves beyond that baseline, with the "saint" tracking sustained drawdowns and the "hero" tracking acute, high-cost drawdowns. She claims this avoids Urmson's deontic gap without collapsing into consequentialism because the budget is non-aggregative and calibrated to social role rather than global utility. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press this view at its actual pressure points, not generic biography. Include: one question on how the model draws the boundary between genuinely supererogatory rescue and merely expensive but obligatory aid—the "over-demandingness" line—with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that role-calibration fixes the budget; one on whether moral residue (blame or regret for failing to take the discretionary option) survives her framework, since if the act lies outside the required budget there seems to be no normative remainder, with a follow-up for the answer that the residue is only prudential; one on collective or institutional supererogation, asking whether state-level risk-budgets scale, fragment, or require representative agency, and a follow-up if she appeals to democratic authorization; one comparing her account to the counter-obligatory analysis and to scalar-obligation views, specifically asking which feature—agent-relativity, the reserve metaphor, or non-aggregativity—does the real explanatory work; and a final question forcing her to specify what phenomenological or empirical finding could disconfirm the risk-budget framing, anticipating a retreat to conceptual analysis. Structure the sequence so earlier commitments are tested by later ones, keep every question concrete, and do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies.
