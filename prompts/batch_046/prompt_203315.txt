Two members of my philosophy study group got stuck in a dispute over David Benatar’s asymmetry between pleasure and pain, and I need an outside referee. The first, Marcus, laid out the asymmetry precisely: absence of pain is good even without a subject to enjoy it, but absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is a subject deprived of it. He then applied it to reproductive choice. Imagine a couple who can conceive a child who would have a life that is clearly worth living—mostly good—but who will also suffer serious, chronic pain. Marcus argued that because any suffering the child experiences is genuinely bad, while the pleasure the child experiences, though good, does not correspond to any harm avoided in non-existence, procreation introduces a real moral negative without a compensatory moral positive. He concluded that the asymmetry gives us reason never to procreate in such cases, and more generally supports antinatalism, since existence can only add harm or neutrality, never a benefit that non-existence lacks.

The second, Leah, said Marcus was misapplying the asymmetry by treating it as a decision procedure across possible worlds. She insisted that Benatar’s comparison is strictly between existence and non-existence for the same potential being, not between different possible children. Once you ask whether this particular couple should conceive, she said, you are comparing outcomes for different worlds, and the asymmetry doesn’t rank those outcomes because it says nothing about how to weigh goods and harms within an actual life. A net-positive life is genuinely good for the person who lives it; the fact that non-existence involves no deprivation doesn’t diminish that good or turn it into a reason against creation.

Marcus came back with what he called a replacement argument: since the absence of pleasure is never bad, there is no moral cost to leaving happy potential people in non-existence. But the presence of pain is always bad. So every procreative act risks introducing harm with no corresponding moral gain—there is no missing happiness that creation remedies. He added an analogy: if I break your arm but also bake you a cake, the cake doesn’t justify the broken arm unless you consented. Potential people can’t consent to being created with suffering, so the suffering isn’t justified by the pleasure that accompanies it. Therefore, he said, procreation is at best morally neutral and usually harmful, which is enough for antinatalism.

Leah replied that Marcus was sliding from “no reason to create” to “reason not to create,” and that the slide requires treating potential people as having interests in remaining non-existent. But potential beings have no interests; there is no subject in non-existence who is protected or violated. The asymmetry explains why failing to create a happy person is not wrong, but it doesn’t establish that creating a happy person is wrong, because there is no victim when creation occurs and no victim when it doesn’t. She also challenged the broken-arm analogy: in that case there is an actual victim before and after the act, whereas in procreation the “victim” only comes into being through the very act being evaluated.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of Marcus’s and Leah’s main points—the precise statement of the asymmetry, the reproductive application, the replacement argument with the consent analogy, and the objection about potential beings lacking interests—say who is right, partly right, or wrong, and briefly justify your rating. Then say which of them is more right overall, and identify what important distinction or consideration both are missing that would change or sharpen the debate.
