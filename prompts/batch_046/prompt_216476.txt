I'm putting together a graduate seminar on superrationality and I've become slighly obsessed with Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and that bizarre Luring Lottery he arranged through Scientific American — the postcard game with numbers. Over the years I've tracked my old supervisor Dr. Elaine Voss's published verdicts on these puzzles, and I'm trying to work out what rule she's actually running, but her track record is annoyingly compatible with more than one interpretation.

Her rulings, as I understand them, are roughly these: in Newcomb's problem with a genuinely perfect predictor she always one-boxes; in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma between perfect twins she argues both should stay silent, since the reasoning is symmetric; in the Platonia postcard game she insists you ought to send the card, because every copy of you is running the identical calculus; in the actual 1980s Luring Lottery she claimed the real-world randomness broke any meaningful correlation, so rational agents should treat it like an ordinary lottery and enter freely; and in a modified Newcomb case where the predictor is only 85 percent accurate she switched back to two-boxing.

Now here's the case I'm presenting next week — I'm calling it the Echo Chamber. A supercomputer generates 10,000 simulations of you, each deciding whether to pool resources into a shared pot. If more than 90 percent pool, every simulation receives £100; if 90 percent or fewer pool, every simulation receives £10. Crucially, in exactly 1 percent of the runs a random bit is flipped in the reasoning module, so those copies don't share your logic, but you don't know which one you are. Predict Dr. Voss's verdict: is she running pure superrationality that collapses the moment symmetry is imperfect, or is she using some noisy evidential threshold — perhaps cooperating whenever correlation exceeds 90 percent? Identify the closest analogue in her earlier rulings, name the further case that would actually settle which principle she's using, and please answer in British English. Also be upfront about what argument or consideration would change your mind on this.
