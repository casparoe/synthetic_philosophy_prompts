You are an expert in behavioral economics and an accomplished poet. Before writing, please take a moment to brainstorm: how can the physical shape of a poem—its lines, refrains, or accumulation—enact the logic of escalation rather than merely describing it? I am not an academic; I have only been turning over, for a long time, that strange auction in which both the highest and the second-highest bidder must pay their bids, though only one receives the dollar, and how people keep bidding far past any rational return. I want a poem of exactly sixteen lines, divided into four quatrains, in free verse but governed by one strict formal rule: the final line of each quatrain must repeat the same core phrase with exactly one new word appended each time, so the refrain itself grows heavier like an accumulating debt—beginning with "I stay," then "I stay, bidding," then "I stay, bidding higher," then "I stay, bidding higher, still paying." Use this expanding refrain to make the reader feel the trap tightening. The subject is the dollar auction and the escalation of commitment it produces, but do not treat it as a simple parable about greed. Instead, focus on the structural cruelty that makes graceful exit impossible: the loser pays nearly as much as the winner, so stopping is not a release but a nearly identical loss, and every new bid is therefore not an act of hope but a defense against the specific shame of being the second-place payer. Make the mechanism clear enough that someone familiar with escalation-of-commitment research would recognize the logic—especially that rational withdrawal is undermined because the cost of leaving approximates the cost of winning—without naming scholars, citing studies, or using technical jargon. Write in a voice that sounds like a thoughtful non-specialist speaking to someone who has lived through a smaller version of the same trap—staying too long in a failing project, an argument, or an obligation—so the language stays precise but accessible. Do not resolve the tension with a moral or a lesson; let the final line hang with the weight of an unpaid bill. Think carefully, then compose the poem.
