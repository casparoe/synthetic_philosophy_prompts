I am delivering the retirement toast for my father next Friday evening. For thirty-five years he worked as an actuary assessing structural and financial risk, yet he never treated probability as a mere accounting tool. Our family always noticed that he refused to say things like "it will probably rain" or "this investment will probably fail" in the casual, subjective sense; he insisted that there was a real difference between "I am somewhat sure" and "the situation itself carries a tendency toward this outcome." The audience will be divided evenly between his professional colleagues—actuaries, underwriters, and risk analysts who speak in frequencies and confidence intervals—and relatives and friends who simply knew him as an unusually precise, occasionally stubborn man who seemed to treat danger as something physically present rather than personally guessed. I want a five-minute toast that turns on exactly this distinction: the idea that probability might be not just a long-run frequency, nor merely a degree of belief, but a kind of objective disposition or propensity belonging to the world itself, present even in singular, non-repeatable events.

The speech should use this idea to explain concrete habits of his—why he would not comfort us with vague optimism about a one-time medical procedure, why he treated a specific bridge's corrosion rate as carrying its own weight of risk rather than being reducible to his own confidence, and why this made him both exceptional at his job and, at home, sometimes maddeningly resistant to reassurance. The philosophical framing should do real structural work: it should shape what the toast can and cannot say. For example, if probability is partly a feature of the world, then comforting family members by claiming he is "probably watching over us" would violate the very view he held; the speech needs to honor him without smuggling in that kind of subjective or metaphysical comfort. Meanwhile, the professionals in the room should recognize the bones of frequentism, Bayesian subjectivism, and the propensity interpretation without feeling that they are being lectured. Please avoid any citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts or scholars; I need this to stand on its own as an account of who he was, not as a survey of theories. The tone should be warm, precise, and appropriate for a mixed audience that does not share an academic vocabulary.
