I've been reading about arguments over whether Germany can be blamed for the First World War, especially the view associated with the historian Fritz Fischer that German leaders actively sought a broader conflict. I keep noticing a particular rhetorical move that I don't have a word for. Someone will argue that blame shouldn't fall on Germany as a whole because the decision for war was scattered: alliance commitments pulled people in, military schedules made escalation automatic, and no single vote started the fighting. The idea is that responsibility is too spread out across institutions and accidents to stick to one nation. But then, often in the same breath, that same scattered, systemic quality is used to argue the opposite: that there was something distinctively German about how the pieces fit together — a particular strategic culture, a specific bureaucratic willingness to take risks — and therefore the nation carries a special burden. The very feature that was supposed to dissolve blame — everything being distributed and structural — becomes the reason to assign it collectively. Is there an established term for this exact reversal, where structural diffusion is used first to block moral attribution and then to ground it? How is it different from ordinary confusion between explaining what happened and judging who is at fault? And if the concept doesn't exist, what should we call the move, and where exactly does it fail?
