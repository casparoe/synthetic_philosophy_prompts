I'm preparing a taxonomy for a graduate-level metaphysics workshop on the special composition question, and I want an instrument that classifies positions by intuitive judgments about concrete cases rather than by self-identified labels. The standard statue-and-clay framing is too familiar, so the tree should be built from less conventional scenarios that still don't depend on specialized empirical knowledge.

Please design a decision tree with the following structure and constraints.

Root node: The respondent considers whether two entities that share no spatiotemporal overlap but are functionally interdependent—say, a primary computing cluster and its geographically dispersed backup nodes that are activated only upon failure—count together as parts of one composite object. The respondent answers yes or no.

From there, each internal node should present a concrete case or principle question that genuinely divides the logical space: for instance, whether continuous replacement of every physical part over time preserves the identity of the composite; whether parts must be in real-time causal interaction; whether a scattered arrangement of objects that precisely instantiate a complex mathematical relation across disconnected rooms (e.g., nodes spaced at prime-number intervals) composes anything; and whether an object can survive the temporary non-existence of all its parts if their relational structure is preserved in information storage.

The tree should terminate in 6 to 8 leaves, each named after a specific metaphysical position—such as unrestricted universalism, spatiotemporal-contact restriction, functional-unity restriction, mereological nihilism, essentialism about parts, and a gradualist view on which composition is a matter of degree rather than all-or-nothing. For any logical combination of answers that has no named defender, label the leaf "unnamed/unoccupied" and provide a one-sentence diagnosis of whether the view seems incoherent, implausible, or merely unexplored.

Every node must be phrased as a direct question that a student could answer without knowing terms like "mereological universalism" or "contact theory." The tree should not require respondents to recognize position names. Additionally, inject one somewhat niche or unconventional compositional scenario—perhaps involving distributed artificial entities or cross-modal persistence conditions—that forces the taxonomy to accommodate a view not typically mapped in introductory surveys.

Keep the output formal and structured: present the tree as nested branches with each node labeled by its diagnostic question, and append a brief scoring key explaining which answer patterns map onto which leaves. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or references to specific texts. The intended audience is advanced undergraduates who have encountered basic metaphysics but should still be sorted by their reactions to cases.
