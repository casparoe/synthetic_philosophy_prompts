As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy systematically, I find myself returning to a slogan that dominates the modern Stoic revival: the claim, usually traced back to Epictetus but now repeated without citation in self-help essays and therapy manuals, that human beings are disturbed not by external events themselves but solely by the judgments or opinions they form about those events, with the corollary that there exists a sharp dichotomy between what lies within our power—our own judgments, impulses, and desires—and what lies outside it, including our bodies, reputations, and the course of the world. I first encountered this being used, quite recently, in a popular revivalist essay that invoked it to dismiss widespread climate and political anxiety as always and necessarily a voluntary error of judgment rather than a rational response to real danger. Having spent a career studying physical systems in which internal states are tightly coupled to external conditions, and being now aware of contemporary neurobiological and psychopharmacological work—closed-loop deep brain stimulation for mood disorders, real-time fMRI neurofeedback, and precision psychiatric drugs that restructure affective judgment from outside the skull—it strikes me that the boundary between internal judgment and external causation may be far more porous than the slogan allows. Could you therefore lay out, in continuous prose with no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables—simply as one would write in a letter—the distinct precise theses that this slogan could be advancing, from the trivial truism that I cannot will away a hurricane to the radical thesis that all mental suffering is strictly self-authored and therefore morally culpable, indicating for each whether it is defensible, false, or trivial? Then, having presented that menu, I ask you not to remain neutral: take a committed position, with reasons, on which of these precise readings is actually required for the revivalist argument I described to do its dismissive work, and on whether that required reading survives once we take seriously the possibility that modern biotechnology can causally determine the very judgments the Stoic treats as purely internal and therefore fully responsible. I want a definite verdict rather than an open menu left for me to adjudicate, though I want the menu shown first so the landscape is visible; and please avoid any bibliographic references, citation lists, or requests for further reading, since I am working without access to a library database.
