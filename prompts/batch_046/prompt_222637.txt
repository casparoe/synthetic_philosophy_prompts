Having retired from a long career in evolutionary ecology and only recently begun studying philosophical ethics and philosophy of mind, I find myself unconvinced by the scale argument that is commonly advanced in debates over insect sentience. That argument notes that insects exist in staggering numbers—often estimated in the quintillions—so that even an extremely low prior probability of conscious experience assigned to each individual should generate an enormous aggregate moral expectation, perhaps overwhelming the welfare concerns we direct toward vertebrates. I want you to construct a rigorous argument against this conclusion, organized around what I call the phylogenetic conditioning problem: the claim that prior probabilities of sentience ought not to be applied uniformly across the class Insecta, but must instead be conditioned on the presence of specific integrated nociceptive architectures that have evolved convergently only in restricted lineages. Your argument should demonstrate that once this conditioning is combined with a non-linear weighting of epistemic uncertainty—such that high-uncertainty attributions contribute sub-linearly rather than linearly to expected welfare—the total moral weight of insect populations collapses by several orders of magnitude and falls well below that of vertebrate populations, without relying on arbitrary species boundaries or dismissing small organisms a priori. You should also address the objection that such conditioning is merely an ad hoc rescue of human exceptionalism, explaining why the same evidential standards that justify more confident attributions to mammals and birds do not license comparable confidence across all invertebrates. Please present your reasoning as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter or email.
