I'm preparing notes for a philosophy seminar on Moritz Schlick's essay on the meaning of life, and I'm stuck on a conceptual tension that standard introductions tend to gloss over. Schlick is usually read as arguing that life acquires meaning not by serving some external purpose—whether religious salvation, moral duty, or utilitarian maximization—but by being lived as play: activities pursued for their own sake with full absorption, especially in youth and in artistic or creative moments. Reflection, on this reading, destroys the very immersion that generates meaning, and the search for a "purpose" of life is therefore a category mistake that replaces living with instrumental monitoring. What I want to understand is whether this framework can survive what we might call archival consciousness—the modern condition of engaging in an activity while knowing that it is being recorded, documented, or preserved for future evaluation. If Schlick is right that meaning requires a kind of first-order immersion broken by second-order awareness, does the habit of self-documentation (personal archives, narrative tracking, even the implicit awareness that one's life will be interpreted by others) structurally undermine the possibility of meaning? Or is there room for what we could call recursive play—absorption that incorporates awareness of its own conditions without collapsing into purpose-seeking?

Please explain this conceptual issue in detail, working through the distinctions Schlick actually draws rather than a generic "existentialist" version of them. Specifically: (1) clarify how Schlick distinguishes Sinn (meaning) from Zweck (purpose or end), and why he thinks purpose-seeking is not just difficult but conceptually misplaced in this domain; (2) examine whether his critique of reflection targets all forms of self-awareness or only instrumental, goal-oriented self-monitoring, and whether philosophical contemplation itself can count as play on his account; (3) assess whether archival consciousness introduces a genuinely novel problem for his view—one that goes beyond the familiar youth-versus-maturity tension—or whether it merely restates that older problem in technological dress. I am especially interested in the logical space of positions here: does Schlick's view commit him to saying that any life lived with narrative self-awareness is meaningless, or can he accommodate a distinction between destructive reflection and a lighter, playful meta-awareness?

In your response, please clearly separate what is settled in the scholarly interpretation of Schlick's position from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this text—particularly regarding whether Schlick allows for a reflective form of play, and whether his view has implicit resources for addressing mediated or documented experience. Do not present a contested reading as if it were the consensus view, and avoid framing the issue as if there were a single "correct" interpretation that all specialists accept.
