After a long and somewhat frustrating argument with a close friend about institutional reform, I found myself trying to defend a Burkean interpretation of Chesterton’s fence—the idea that inherited norms and structures should not be dismantled until one understands the problems they were originally built to solve—against the charge that it is merely an appeal to ignorance that paralyzes progress. My friend countered that in modern bureaucratic and technological systems, the “fences” are rarely single, legible barriers but rather layered, semi-obscured architectures of rules, where each generation’s patch becomes the next generation’s foundation, and the original rationale is buried under successive modifications. I want to take this layered image seriously rather than treating it as an objection. If we apply Burkean conservatism not to discrete institutions but to these stacked normative structures—where removing one layer may destabilize others whose purposes we no longer grasp—what are the most important implications? In particular, how should this affect our attitude toward “meta-reform,” the redesign of the very mechanisms by which we review and alter rules? Does Chesterton’s principle apply recursively, requiring that we understand not just why a specific rule exists, but why the system that produced and maintained it was structured as it was? And if so, does this generate an epistemic burden so severe that it pushes Burkean conservatism toward a quietism that even Burke might have found impractical, or is there a principled way to navigate partial ignorance without falling into either reckless rationalist reconstruction or total institutional stasis?
