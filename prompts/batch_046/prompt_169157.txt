I'm planning a single ninety-minute seminar for philosophy-of-science first-years who have no background in economic history. The goal is not to teach them the historical answer to industrialization, but to help them see why the framing "why Britain and not China?" — the contrastive question behind what historians call the Great Divergence — may be philosophically ill-posed rather than merely unanswered. Plan the explanatory arc: first, the order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that the framing problem emerges as a philosophical discovery about explanation rather than a historian's quibble; second, the one stripped-down analogue scenario (not Britain or China themselves) that should carry the argument, specifying what properties it must have and what it must lack; third, the misconceptions the students will predictably form — I expect they will treat contrastive explanation as simply "finding the relevant difference," assume that selecting Britain and China is a neutral sampling decision, and look for a single decisive causal factor — together with the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused; fourth, a brief check I can administer near the end that reveals whether they have grasped the framing problem or have merely memorized that "China also had coal"; and finally, what I should explicitly refuse to settle in this session — for instance, whether Britain possessed genuinely unique preconditions or whether industrialization was historically inevitable — and how I should flag that refusal so it does not feel like evasion.
