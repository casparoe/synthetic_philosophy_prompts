I am a high school senior in my school's philosophy and ethics seminar. Last week, two classmates—Jordan and Alex—got into a sustained argument about speciesism and the argument from marginal cases during an open discussion, and they asked me to seek an outside adjudication because neither would concede any ground. I am reproducing their exchange below without taking a side; please treat me only as the reporter.

Jordan began by defining speciesism as the automatic prioritization of human interests over animal interests of equal intensity, and argued that this is structurally analogous to racism: both use biological categories—species and race—that do not, in themselves, track any morally relevant capacity. Jordan cited Peter Singer's well-known position that equal consideration of interests requires extending serious moral weight to sentient nonhuman animals, and concluded that anyone who rejects this extension is committing the same kind of arbitrary boundary-drawing that underwrites racism.

Alex replied that the analogy fails because race has no correlation with cognitive, emotional, or moral capacities, whereas species membership does correlate—though imperfectly—with capacities such as moral agency, reciprocal justice, and long-term planning. Therefore, Alex argued, using species as a boundary can function as a rough proxy for a capacity-based distinction rather than as an arbitrary biological prejudice.

Jordan then invoked the argument from marginal cases: because some humans—infants and severely cognitively disabled adults—lack moral agency yet are widely granted strong moral protection, capacity cannot be the actual basis for human moral status. Jordan claimed this demonstrates that the human boundary is maintained purely by biological membership, which makes speciesism exactly parallel to racism in its arbitrariness.

Alex countered that Jordan was conflating two different issues. The argument from marginal cases undermines a pure, unqualified capacity view, but it does not show that species membership is arbitrary. One could adopt a potentiality or kind-membership view: humans belong to a kind whose normal members possess moral agency, and this kind-membership generates obligations that do not extend to members of other kinds, even if some individuals within those other kinds happen to possess comparable capacities. Thus, Jordan's inference—that the failure of pure capacity proves arbitrariness—does not follow.

Jordan responded that the potentiality defense is just speciesism relabeled: we do not grant rights to a potential athlete on the basis of future athletic ability, so appealing to the potential of human infants is special pleading. Jordan also noted that many nonhuman animals—pigs, crows, elephants—possess cognitive and emotional capacities that match or exceed those of some marginal humans, which undermines Alex's correlation claim.

Alex answered that Jordan was misrepresenting the potentiality argument: it is not about the individual future development of a particular infant, but about membership in a kind defined by its characteristic capacities. Furthermore, Alex argued, Jordan was assuming without argument that comparable capacities must yield comparable moral status, which begs the question against a pluralist view that both capacity and kind-membership can jointly determine moral standing. Alex also pointed out that Jordan seemed to attribute to Singer the claim that animals deserve equal rights, whereas Singer's actual view concerns equal consideration of like interests, not identical rights—a distinction that matters for how the analogy is drawn.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of Jordan's and Alex's main arguments from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Identify any conflations, misattributions, or non-sequiturs you find. Then say which party is more right overall, and explain what both are missing—particularly whether there is a principled way to combine capacity-sensitive and kind-sensitive considerations without collapsing into pure biological preference. Finally, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the specific point about which you are least sure.
