A friend pressed me on whether human self-trust is rationally defensible, and I tried to formalize the worry using the Löbian obstacle. The result feels suspicious because it seems to undermine either our ordinary confidence in our own reasoning or the feasibility of reflective self-improvement in artificial agents. Here is the chain:

(1) A rational agent ought to trust its own future reasoning steps unless it possesses specific, non-circular grounds for doubting them.
(2) If an agent’s reasoning framework is strong enough to represent its own provability relation, then by Löb’s theorem it can derive confidence in its own consistency only if it already assumes that consistency.
(3) So within any sufficiently expressive formal system, non-circular self-certification of consistency is impossible.
(4) Any agent capable of reflective self-improvement must be able to represent and evaluate its own reasoning procedures, hence must operate within such an expressive framework.
(5) Therefore, a reflective agent can never acquire evidence-based, non-circular trust in its own reliability; any such trust must be either formally ungrounded or restricted to systems too weak to model themselves.
(6) Yet human agents evidently do maintain calibrated, empirically supported confidence in their reasoning—through track records, error correction, and cross-checking—without treating that confidence as bare assumption.
(7) So either human cognition lacks the expressive power required for genuine reflective self-modeling, or our empirical calibration does not actually provide the kind of non-circular justification (1) demands.
(8) If human cognition is indeed that weak, then projects assuming that sufficiently advanced systems can reflectively audit and correct their own goals are built on a false model of rational self-trust.
(9) Hence we must reject either (1), that rational self-trust needs non-circular grounding, or (6), that humans possess genuinely justified self-trust, or (4), that reflective self-improvement requires full self-representation.

I want to preserve (1) and (6) if I can, but I suspect the slide happens somewhere between (4) and (5), where I move from formal unprovability to empirical impossibility. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up commit me to? If the argument is actually valid, which of (1), (6), or (4) is the least costly to abandon, and why?
