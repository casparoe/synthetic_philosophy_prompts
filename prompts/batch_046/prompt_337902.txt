I'm preparing a 75-minute graduate seminar session with about ten students on the symbol grounding problem, and I want an in-class simulation rather than a lecture. The session should make participants feel, from the inside, why pure syntactic coordination seems insufficient for meaning, but also why individual sensorimotor grounding may not solve the problem for abstract or normative vocabulary. Rather than replay the standard Chinese Room, I want you to design what I will call a "distributed grounding library": a network of participants in which only a few nodes have direct perceptual access to concrete stimuli, while the rest operate solely with formal rules and messages received from other nodes. The network must collectively classify an ambiguous new object—determining whether it should be treated as "dangerous" or "merely heavy"—where the normative dimension has never been tied to direct experience for most members. The rules, roles, and information bottlenecks should be arranged so that participants reach a stable formal answer before anyone names the philosophical issue, and only in the crisis of coordination do they notice that correct symbol-manipulation can feel empty while direct perceptual access feels incommunicable.

Please provide the full design: physical materials (cards, tokens, or simple props), explicit role assignments with different informational privileges, a step-by-step timeline within the 75 minutes (setup, rounds of message-passing, the introduction of the ambiguous stimulus, and the coordination crisis), and a payoff structure using no real money but real social stakes—perhaps collective access to a shared resource contingent on unanimous classification, with individual nodes able to veto or secede. The debrief must map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the symbol grounding problem—distinguishing syntactic competence, individual sensorimotor grounding, and distributed social grounding—without simply lecturing. Also tell me precisely what will go wrong the first time I run this, how to recover without breaking the experience, and be explicit about which feature of the grounding problem this activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the phenomenological quality of understanding or the causal-covariational relation between symbol and world) and that I must supply through guided discussion afterward. The activity should work for students who know the basic problem but have not read specialized literature on distributed cognition or social externalism.
