Here is the student's answer:

Parfit's original repugnant conclusion compares World A, containing a smaller number of people with excellent lives, to World Z, containing a vastly larger number of people whose lives are barely worth living. Under total utilitarianism, Z is better because the aggregate of barely positive well-being exceeds the aggregate of excellent well-being multiplied by the smaller population. Average utilitarianism avoids this result, since the mean well-being in Z is near neutrality, whereas in A it is very high.

In the modified World Z', each of the 100 billion inhabitants starts with well-being barely above zero, but adding anyone causes a uniform, tiny reduction in every existing inhabitant's well-being—though never below zero. For total utilitarianism, this interdependence means that total welfare is not strictly increasing with population. There is an optimal population size at which the sum of well-being is maximized; beyond that point, further additions reduce the total. Therefore, total utilitarianism is rescued from the repugnant conclusion here: because the total in Z' is bounded and eventually declines, its peak value likely falls below the total welfare of World A, so Z' is not necessarily better.

Average utilitarianism still favors A, because the mean well-being in Z' remains barely positive regardless of population size, given the uniform dilution. The interdependence does not change the mean in a way that benefits Z'. Notably, this dilution structure introduces the mere addition paradox into totalism, since adding people now harms those already present, which is precisely the interaction that the paradox highlights. Meanwhile, because every life in Z' remains positive, critical-level concerns do not arise. Overall, the dilution variant does not reproduce Parfit's original dilemma; instead, it provides totalism with a natural stopping point that prevents unbounded preference for larger populations.
