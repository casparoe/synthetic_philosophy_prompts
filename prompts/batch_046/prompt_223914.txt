For a philosophy of science workshop I am organizing, I would like a sestina on Popper's problem of verisimilitude. The idea is that scientific theories are typically false, yet false theories are not all equally distant from the truth: proximity is measured not by predictive success alone, but by the logical balance of true consequences against false ones (truth content minus falsity content). The conceptual difficulty is that this proximity is not scalar or Euclidean — a theory can be closer to the truth in explanatory structure yet farther away in empirical coverage, so that near and far are dimension-relative rather than absolute.

Please write a strict sestina — six stanzas of six lines, with the six end-words rotating in the canonical pattern (stanza one: 1-2-3-4-5-6; stanza two: 6-1-5-2-4-3; and so on through the remaining stanzas), followed by an envoi of three lines containing all six words — using these end-words in this order: truth, false, near, far, content, error. The form should enact the concept: the cyclical rotation and reordering of the end-words should suggest that approaching truth is not linear progress but a recurring negotiation among dimensions, where truth and false keep returning without ever resolving into a single coordinate. The envoi should leave the tension open rather than synthesizing it — perhaps placing truth and false in proximity without cancellation — so that the poem mirrors the non-metric nature of verisimilitude. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of science recognizes Popper's distinction between truth-content and falsity-content, but it should avoid citing specific texts, relying on post-1990 formal definitions of verisimilitude, or treating the problem as solved.
