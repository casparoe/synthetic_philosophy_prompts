Following a recent family experience in which a physician exercised therapeutic privilege to withhold a terminal prognosis from my father, I have become convinced that modern clinical ethics systematically underestimates the moral force of Augustine’s absolute prohibition on lying. Next Thursday I present to the clinical ethics board of a major university hospital: eight members, six of them principlist bioethicists and palliative-care physicians committed to autonomy-based disclosure norms; one hospital chaplain with a virtue-ethics background but little patience for patristic sources; and one risk-management attorney focused on liability and family litigation. I defend the thesis that Augustine’s prohibition, reconstructed from his treatises on lying and his theory of signs in the work on Christian doctrine, establishes a communicative covenant between physician and patient that makes therapeutic privilege not merely suboptimal but a corruption of signification itself. The physician does not merely utter a falsehood; by deliberately severing the ordered relation between word and reality for the patient’s putative good, she treats the patient’s rational nature as an object to be managed rather than a partner in a shared pact of meaning. I distinguish mendacium—deceptive speech with intent to corrupt the hearer’s understanding—from mere error; I argue that withholding or distorting prognosis fits the gravest of the eight grades of lies Augustine catalogues, but more fundamentally activates a semiotic wrong: it violates the covenant of signification that grounds truthful speech in rational creatures. I concede that autonomy-based frameworks already require disclosure, yet I claim they justify it instrumentally, as respect for decision-making, whereas Augustine grounds it ontologically, as respect for the word-reality bond in beings made to know and be known. This yields stricter obligations: no strategic ambiguity, no gradual disclosure calibrated to emotional resilience, and no family-mediated deception even with the patient’s prior consent.

My sketch for your targeting: first, I establish the semiotic framework using Augustine’s distinction between signs and the things signified; second, I map the eight grades onto clinical communication, showing that benevolent deception always involves both harm to the speaker’s soul and rupture of the communicative covenant; third, I address the autonomy objection by arguing that ontological grounding strengthens rather than undermines patient rights; fourth, I propose a clinical protocol of immediate, unvarnished disclosure paired with sustained pastoral presence, rejecting both abrupt abandonment and managed revelation.
