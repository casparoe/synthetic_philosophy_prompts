I've been working through George Berkeley's economic questions in The Querist on my own—he asks whether money is valuable because of the gold and silver in it, or whether it works more like a token that stands for labor and goods. I'm grading a friend's answer for our informal study group. The question was: Explain Berkeley's comparison of money to a ticket representing labor in The Querist, and assess whether that view commits him to supporting unlimited government printing of paper currency.

Here is her answer:

Berkeley, writing in The Querist around the 1730s, rejects the idea that money is valuable because of its material—gold or silver. Instead, he treats it as a token or ticket that represents the labor people have done and the goods that labor produces. The paper or metal itself is nearly worthless, just like a theater ticket is cheap paper that only works because a system accepts it. From this he concludes that paper money is perfectly legitimate, since the substance doesn't matter.

This economic view is really just an application of his broader philosophy, immaterialism. Berkeley famously held that material objects do not exist independently of being perceived. So gold and silver cannot have intrinsic economic value, because they have no real material substance to begin with. The ticket idea follows directly: if matter is not real in the relevant sense, then wealth cannot be stored in material coins.

Because money is only a symbolic ticket with no natural limit, Berkeley would have endorsed modern fiat systems where governments print currency without a gold reserve. There is no reason to restrict the supply, since the paper itself costs nothing and only needs to represent labor. If more labor is available, more tickets can be issued freely.
