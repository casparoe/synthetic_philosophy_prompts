I'm preparing a moderated Q&A for a philosophy colloquium series. The guest is an unnamed defender of a reconstructed version of Bernard Williams's integrity objection—specifically, the claim that requiring an agent to justify saving a loved one through an impartial moral principle introduces an unacceptable mediating thought (the problem often summarized as having one thought too many), and that this structural flaw extends beyond utilitarian weighing to what she calls proceduralist deontology: any view that demands agents filter personal commitments through a universalizability or impartiality check before acting.

Please draft a sequence of eight interview questions that avoid biography or generic framing and instead press directly on the architecture of her position. Organize your response into exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Conceptual Framing (approximately 90–120 words): Briefly restate the reconstructed objection, identify its two targets—consequentialist calculation and proceduralist filtering—and note one niche implication she draws: that institutional roles (e.g., judges, physicians) may actually require the very reflective distance that personal relationships forbid.

2. Core Questions (approximately 550–650 words): List eight questions in numbered order. Requirements:
- At least three should probe the classic spouse-saving scenario, distinguishing whether the problem lies in the content of the mediating thought, its temporal priority (deliberation before action), or its modal availability (the agent could have acted without it).
- At least two should address her extension to proceduralist deontology, asking how she avoids collapsing into a blanket anti-reflection argument.
- At least one should apply the objection to institutional loyalty—e.g., a judge who feels personal sympathy for a defendant—where impartial procedure is normally a virtue.
- For each question, include a brief note on the answer she is most likely to give, followed by a tailored follow-up that anticipates that reply and makes it harder to deflect.

3. Pressure Points (approximately 220–280 words): Identify three internal tensions—such as how she distinguishes permissible practical reasoning from the forbidden extra step, whether retrospective justification is allowed, and whether small-scale partiality (favoring one's own child's school in a donation) survives the critique. For each tension, provide one sharp, concise follow-up question.

4. Closing Pivot (approximately 90–120 words): One final question that asks what kind of phenomenological or institutional evidence could persuade her that the mediating thought is not always a corruption of integrity but sometimes a necessary structural feature of long-term trust or role-based ethics.

Keep the tone suitable for a live academic audience. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or requests for further sources.
