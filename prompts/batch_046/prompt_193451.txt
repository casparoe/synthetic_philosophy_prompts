Since my mother recently developed a parietal lesion that leaves her able to identify nociceptive stimuli as painful in a purely sensory register yet completely indifferent to them—she does not withdraw, seek treatment, or report any suffering—I have been trying to think through whether the badness of pain is intrinsic to its sensory character or derives entirely from its negative affective dimension. I now hold that pain is bad solely in virtue of its hurting or suffering component, and that cases of pain asymbolia show that nociceptive experience stripped of that affective coloring is not genuinely bad for the subject. I want an honest assessment of what would count against this view. Beyond the familiar objection that asymbolic patients may still harbor unreported distress, what clinical or conceptual possibilities would genuinely undermine the inference? For example, how damaging would it be if there were well-documented cases of intact negative affect paired with severely blunted sensory discrimination, or congenital conditions that dissociate affect and sensation in the reverse direction? Which broader commitments—about intrinsic value, about whether a state can harm a subject who does not mind it, or about the relation between evaluative judgment and feeling—does the view depend on, and which of those dependencies are vulnerable? I also want to know which potential defeaters a committed affectivist would concede as serious versus dismiss as compatible with the view, and I would like the risks ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Please be explicit about where the position is genuinely exposed and where it risks being insulated by ad hoc refinement.
