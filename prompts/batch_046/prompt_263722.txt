For a final-year undergraduate seminar in archaeological theory and philosophy of science—course title Inference from Objects: Beliefs, Institutions, and Material Residue—construct a single take-home essay examination. The exam must be answerable in 1,200 words, carry 40 percent of the module grade, and allow 48 hours. It should not be answerable by reciting standard textbook distinctions between ritual and domestic space; instead, it must require students to navigate an underdetermined material record, defend a specific inferential license, and show where that license runs out.

Present the following hypothetical assemblage—do not substitute a well-known real site such as Çatalhöyük, Stonehenge, or Göbekli Tepe. Site K-14, late fourth-millennium BCE, upper river valley: a circular enclosure formed by twelve upright limestone slabs (approximately 2 m in height, enclosing a 15 m diameter space) contains no formal burials or grave goods. In the fill of three central postholes, excavators found fragments of red-ochre-stained grinding slabs that are broken yet not worn through; 47 unfired clay figurines of highly abstract humanoid form (3–5 cm); and charred seeds of a domesticated grain that is not locally cultivable. Immediately outside the enclosure, standard domestic refuse pits yield intact grinding tools, cooking vessels with local-use wear, and charred seeds of locally grown grains.

The exam question must ask students to evaluate three competing reconstructions of this assemblage:

(a) The circle is a seasonal ritual arena governed by a specialized ritual class; the imported grain represents tribute or offering, the figurines are representations of deities, and the broken grindstones are sacrificially decommissioned tools.

(b) The circle is a communal storage and redistribution center; the grindstones are functionally exhausted tools retired without cosmological significance, the figurines are children’s toys deposited accidentally during communal labor, and the imported grain is surplus acquired through exchange and stored centrally.

(c) The circle marks an exclusionary boundary of a restricted or polluted zone associated with a particular craft or kin group; the deposited objects are not offerings but deliberately placed waste that demarcates the perimeter, requiring no shared cosmological belief and no hierarchical institution.

Require students to take and defend one position, or to construct a hybrid, but mandate that they address two conceptual cruxes: first, the distinction between an intentional trace (material deposited in order to signify or commemorate) and institutional residue (material left as a structural by-product of organized practice); second, the risk of institutional projection—attributing bureaucratic, priestly, or tribute-based structures to societies whose obligations may have been rotating, egalitarian, or task-specific.

Deliver, in your response:

1. The exam prompt exactly as presented to students, including word limit, time allowance, and marking weight.

2. A model answer of approximately 800–1,000 words that adopts a specific reconstruction, demonstrates the required conceptual moves, and avoids mere descriptive cataloguing of the objects.

3. A marking scheme broken into explicit moves—for example, correct deployment of the intentional-trace / institutional-residue distinction; identification of underdetermination between reconstructions (a) and (b); critical evaluation of institutional projection in reconstruction (a); appropriate use of the contrast between inside-circle and outside-circle assemblages; reasoned handling of the imported grain and the unfired figurines without over-reading. Allocate points precisely and specify penalties for characteristic errors, such as treating circular architecture as self-evidently religious; ignoring the domestic refuse comparison; invoking analogies to named real-world monuments without argumentative justification; or conflating brokenness with ritual destruction without addressing wear patterns.

In addition, include a brief meta-reflective paragraph in which you state how confident you are in the model answer’s specific reconstruction of Site K-14, and identify the single inferential step—for instance, the interpretation of the broken grinding slabs or the social meaning of the non-local grain—about which you are least certain, with a brief explanation of why that step remains vulnerable.

Write formally and precisely. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not place any theoretical concept in quotation marks unless you have fetched and verified the exact wording from a source; otherwise paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not depend on knowledge of obscure named theories; frame the conceptual issues in your own terms.
