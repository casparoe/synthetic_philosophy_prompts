My friend and I just finished a long, circular argument about Dewey’s experimentalism—specifically whether treating democratic inquiry as continuous with scientific inquiry necessarily collapses into technocracy. She insists that once you accept Dewey’s idea that democracy solves unsettled situations through cooperative forms of intelligence, experts always end up defining both the problem and the solution. I think there is a way to save Dewey, but we kept talking past each other because we never fixed which defense of democratic experimentalism was actually on the table.

Please work through the following, in these sections and roughly these lengths.

1. Ranked resistance (about 350 words): Rank the five interpretations below from 1 (most resistant to the technocracy objection) to 5 (least resistant). The interpretations are: A, participatory division of epistemic labor—citizens frame the problem and set the success conditions, while experts propose only instrumental means; B, institutional veto—democratic bodies retain formal power to reject expert recommendations without needing technical competence to replace them; C, value-pluralism buffer—democracy is primarily about reconciling incommensurable values rather than aggregating knowledge, so expert authority is largely beside the point; D, social-experimental feedback—policies are treated as hypotheses tested by citizens’ lived experience, with democratic participation supplying the data that validates or falsifies outcomes; and E, deliberative calibration, a view I am introducing here: democratic forums do not generate knowledge or veto experts, but systematically lower experts’ confidence intervals and expose hidden normative premises, functioning as an epistemic humility mechanism rather than an alternative source of expertise. Give one sentence for each explaining why it lands where it does.

2. Diagnostic comparison (about 200 words): Identify the pair of interpretations closest in your ranking and explain whether they can actually be separated in practice, or whether they collapse into one another.

3. Endorsement and vulnerability (about 200 words): State which interpretation you would defend if forced to choose, and specify the single weakest commitment it requires—something that looks implausible on closer inspection.

Do not provide a bibliography or reading list, and do not invoke obscure secondary literature. Just reason through the conceptual architecture.
