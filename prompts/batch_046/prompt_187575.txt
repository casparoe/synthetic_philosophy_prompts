I have come to defend a particular reading of Schrödinger's later metaphysical writings—one that takes his Vedanta-inspired claim about the unity of consciousness not as mystical ornament but as a serious monist thesis. The position is that there exists strictly one consciousness, and what we call individual minds are merely indexical modifications or perspectival angles of that single awareness, comparable to waves arising on one ocean. Because the view never posits a plurality of fundamental subjects, it avoids the combination problem that undermines standard panpsychism; and it treats quantum non-separability not as an independent physical mystery but as a structural analogue of this underlying mental unity. The apparent privacy of experience, on this account, reflects not the existence of separate subjects but the irreducible first-person indexicality of a unitary awareness viewing itself from different positions. What would genuinely count against this position? I want the defeaters mapped and ranked: first, any empirical or formal result that severs the analogy between quantum entanglement and mental unity; second, philosophical arguments showing that a subject-less experience is incoherent or that first-person multiplicity cannot be reduced to indexical variation; third, textual or interpretive evidence that Schrödinger himself intended a far more modest, heuristic, or metaphorical claim. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view risks unfalsifiability—whether its defenders can retreat to a purely phenomenological or heuristic formulation whenever counterevidence arises, and whether that retreat is a vice or a legitimate qualification. Please distinguish what is settled in the scholarship on Schrödinger's engagement with Vedanta from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a disputed interpretation of either the physics or the metaphysics as the consensus among specialists.
