At a recent workshop, two colleagues debated whether an AI system operating under an institutional protocol can successfully perform a commitment-utterance. The system outputs the sentence: I hereby commit this organization to honoring your deletion request within 24 hours. The user treats this as binding. Colleague A argues the utterance succeeds: institutional authority supplies the requisite power, the conventional formula is satisfied, and user reliance is justified, so Austinian felicity conditions are met despite the absence of mental states. Colleague B insists this is an illocutionary misfire: intentionality and sincerity conditions are constitutive, not ornamental, for the act-type committing; without an agent capable of bearing responsibility, the utterance produces only a perlocutionary effect (reassurance), not a genuine commitment. A replies that corporate and state agency are already non-mental, so requiring consciousness here is unmotivated. B counters that corporate agency is always derivative—reducible to human representatives—whereas no identifiable human stands behind this specific token in a responsibility-bearing way.

Please adjudicate move by move: evaluate each claim about felicity conditions, institutional authority, and derivative agency; identify any hidden false presupposition in either argument; and say who is more right overall. Respond in one tightly argued paragraph of no more than 200 words.
