I've just started volunteering at this legal aid drop-in centre near my flat, and last Tuesday I overheard two of the solicitors having it out over coffee about whether anyone ever truly 'deserves' prison. I keep thinking about it because my cousin's up for sentancing next month and I'm suddenly very aware of how casual people are about blame.

Anyway, here's roughly what they said:

(1) Sarah said that even if you reject libertarian free will, punishment can still be justified because some people are competent moral agents who understand rules and can adjust behaviour, and holding them responsible protects society and reinforces norms. She said Dennett's view is that responsibility is like a skill you maintain through practice, not a metaphysical prize.

(2) Tom said that's just desert in disguise. If your 'competence' is itself the result of genetics, upbringing and brain chemistry you didn't choose, then grading punishment by competence is still punishing people for lucky or unlucky wiring. He mentioned Caruso's idea that we should treat dangerous people like carriers of a disease — quarantine them for public safety, but never say they 'deserve' suffering.

(3) Sarah replied that Tom's quarantine model misses the point of moral address. When we blame someone we're not just managing risk; we're treating them as a reasoning being who can respond to reasons, which is exactly what builds that competence in the first place. Without that practice, you don't get less harm, you just get a society that stops seeing people as agents.

(4) Tom said that's circular: you need agency to justify the practice that creates agency. And he asked where the line is — if competence comes in degrees, and every degree is causally determined, then any cutoff between 'blameworthy' and 'just quarantine' is arbitrary. He said Sarah's view collapses into 'punish when it feels right to the community.'

Can you go through there exchange move by move and say who's right at each step and what, if anything, both are missing? I'm not asking for legal advice, just whether the philiosophical moves actually hold up. Also I keep wondering if there's a coherent middle ground where you keep the expressive, reason-responsive part but drop the suffering part entirely — like a kind of graded quarantine that still addresses people as agents — or am I just mixing things up?
