I've slowly conviced myself that the standard rational-deterrence narrative collapses once you take Aumann agreement seriously. If both sides are Bayesians with common priors over escalation dynamics, they should converge on identical risk estimates -- there's no room for the mutual misunderstanding that Schelling-style brinkmanship requires. So nuclear stability isn't a rational strategy; it's maintained by deliberately blocking common knowledge through ambigous red lines, launch-on-warning postures, and organizational noise. The real paradox is that rational belief-updating would dissolve the threat, not preserve it. What evidence or arguments should actually move me, ranked from decisive to merely awkward? Specifically: historical episodes where adversaries converged on risk estimates and that increased danger; formal results that agreement theorems break in multi-agent existential-risk games; or proof that ambiguity is emergent bureaucratic friction rather than engineered. Also, be honest -- has this view already been insulated so that any ambiguity just gets explained away as manufactured? And if it has, is that insulation a problem for a claim of this kind?
