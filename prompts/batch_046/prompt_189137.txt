Design a 90-minute seminar activity for twelve philosophy-of-science graduate students that makes them feel, through real betting stakes, why Solomonoff induction makes Occam's razor mathematically inevitable for computable worlds — and why that inevitability still depends on a hidden choice of representational framework that the invariance theorem only disguises. Split the group into three competing prediction markets of four. Each market receives an identical hidden binary sequence, generated in advance by the facilitator using a short computable rule such as a periodic or linear-feedback pattern but never revealed, and must set prior odds on its next bits. Crucially, each group computes simplicity using a different restricted formal language: Group A may use only regular expressions of bounded length; Group B only deterministic finite-state automata up to five states; Group C only descriptions in a custom mini-language with a fixed set of primitive operations. All groups start with the same point pool — small, non-monetary stakes such as collective contribution to a seminar coffee fund or a shared presentation privilege — and after each revealed bit they update beliefs via explicit Bayes rule applied to their own simplicity metric, then place collective bets. The payoff scheme should reward calibration across rounds rather than a single correct guess, so that groups with poor priors bleed points gradually even when they occasionally guess right. Arrange the rules so that participants discover for themselves: first, that within each framework simpler generators eventually dominate betting; second, that the same sequence can appear highly complex to one group and trivially simple to another; third, that if the true generator is computable all groups eventually converge on it regardless of framework, but only after suffering losses proportional to their initial representational handicap; and fourth, that there is no neutral court of appeal between frameworks except an arbitrary constant factor they cannot justify from within their own language. Include a structured debrief that maps each betting round onto the formal components — universal prior, likelihood weighting, convergence to the true computable distribution, and the UTM-invariance result with its additive constant — and that explicitly names the feature of Solomonoff's full theory the activity necessarily omits: universal quantification over all computable hypotheses, since the groups are confined to their restricted languages. Predict two predictable failures on first run: one group will likely conflate Kolmogorov complexity with intuitive elegance and resist updating; another may claim victory after an early lucky streak and refuse to acknowledge framework-dependence. Say exactly how the facilitator should intervene when the groups realize their simplicity scores are incommensurable. Be frank about what the facilitator must supply in closing remarks that the game mechanics cannot show, particularly the role of the universal prior over all programs. Finally, state clearly what argument or empirical finding about algorithmic induction would change your mind about whether this activity genuinely conveys the formal convergence result rather than merely teaching skepticism about simplicity metrics.
