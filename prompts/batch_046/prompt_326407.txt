I spent three decades in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of mind, and I have become preoccupied with a comparison that appears constantly in the literature and in seminar discussions: knowing another person’s mental state is said to be like interpreting the readout from a calibrated scientific instrument—say, a cloud chamber or a mass spectrometer. The instrument never reveals its internal mechanism to direct inspection; one sees only traces, peaks, and noise, from which one infers unobservable entities. The analogy is deployed to defend a theoretical-inferential account of other minds and, more pointedly, to argue that skepticism about other minds is epistemically on a par with skepticism about electrons or magnetic fields: if consistency permits belief in unobservable physics, it should permit belief in other minds.

Yet my laboratory experience makes me suspicious of the parallel. Instruments can be disassembled, recalibrated against independent standards, and repaired when they malfunction; their outputs are intersubjectively reproducible in ways that first-person mental reports are not. Conversely, mental-state attribution seems to involve something absent from instrument-reading—namely, the possibility of direct correction through dialogue and a kind of first-person authority that does not map onto any feature of detector maintenance. So the analogy may be smuggling in a metaphysics of mind as purely theoretical posit, while quietly omitting structural differences that matter for the anti-skeptical conclusion it is meant to license.

Could you audit the analogy with precision? I want an explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the instrument, the trace or readout, the unobservable entity, the calibration protocol, the observing scientist, and the community of users. Then distinguish the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from those that genuinely undermine the argument: in particular, does the capacity for direct verbal correction and the asymmetry of first-person access break the parallel in a way that revives the skeptical worry, or can those features be accommodated within the instrument model? Does the analogy implicitly treat mental states as theoretical entities in exactly the same sense as subatomic particles, and if so, is that treatment defensible once the differences in error-correction are on the table? Finally, if the instrument analogy fails to carry the conclusion that other-mind skepticism is unreasonable, is there a more apt comparison—perhaps drawn from signal interpretation in noisy environments, or from the validation of complex engineering models—that captures our epistemic situation without importing misleading metaphysics? Please treat the reasoning on its merits, without appealing to bibliography or citation.
