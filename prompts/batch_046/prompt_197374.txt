I'm taking a course on philosphy of language and we've been stuck on Quine's Two Dogmas for ages. From what I gather, Quine thinks the analytic-synthetic split is basically a myth because all beliefs face the tribunal of experience together, and there's no clear line between meaning-constitutive truths and empirical ones — everything is revisable in principle, even logic. My tutor says this holism means you can't isolate a single statement and call it analytic without smuggling in a whole web of theory.

But then at the pub my mate came up with this idea she calls 'default entitlement tokens'. She reckons Quine is right that nothing is immune to revision, but wrong that this kills the distinction entirely. On her view, analytic claims aren't metaphysically special; they're just assertions that come with a built-in epistemic licence — you can deploy them in reasoning without offering fresh empirical evidence, not because they're unrevisable, but because the linguistic community treats them as baseline permissions. So 'all bachelors are unmarried' works like a token you cash in automatically, whereas 'all swans are white' doesn't, even though both could technically be overturned.

I'm going back and fourth trying to figure out if this token-idea is genuinely different from Quine's pragmatism, or if it's just Quine's holism dressed up in fancy language. Could you compare the two? Specifically, does reconceiving analyticity as a pragmatic entitlement-status rather than a semantic boundary actually escape Quine's critique, or does his holistic argument swallow it anyway? I'd also be curious whether either view can explain why we feel so differently about revising a logical law versus revising a scientific generalisation, since that intuitive gap is what started the whole debate for me.
