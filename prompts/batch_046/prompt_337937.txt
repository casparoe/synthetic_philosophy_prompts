In philosophy of computation and mathematical logic, the debate over the epistemic status of the Church-Turing thesis has persisted for decades without clear resolution. On one side stand those who treat the thesis as a definitional or conceptual claim: that Turing-machine computability captures the pre-theoretic notion of effective calculability, so that any purported counterexample—whether analog, quantum, or hypercomputational—either fails to meet the conditions of being genuinely effective or changes the subject rather than refuting the thesis. On the other side are those who treat it as an empirical claim about the structure of physical law, holding that it asserts all physically realizable computing processes are Turing-computable and could in principle be falsified by a well-characterized physical device. Between them lies a less-discussed position that interprets the thesis not as a claim about semantics or physics but as a normative boundary governing what counts as a legitimate method of mathematical proof—essentially a regulative ideal that preserves the surveyability of computation rather than describing a natural kind.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not primarily about physics or about the semantics of computation, but about hidden disagreements over revisability conditions: what kind of evidence—formal, experimental, or conceptual—would be required to overturn the thesis, and whether effective calculability refers to a single natural kind or fragments into incommensurable concepts tied to human practice, physical causation, and algorithmic transparency. The sides appear to talk past one another because defenders often invoke algorithmic transparency—the requirement that a procedure be surveyable step-by-step—while critics invoke physical realizability, yet neither side explicitly defends the criterion it uses to exclude the other's examples.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal—centered on ambiguous uses of effective and computation—or substantive but obscured by mismatched standards of evidence? Are the participants answering different questions—one about the boundaries of human cognitive practice, one about the limits of physical law, one about the norms of proof—under a single heading? Or is the thesis genuinely ill-posed, attempting to unite descriptive, normative, and conceptual claims that should be separated?
