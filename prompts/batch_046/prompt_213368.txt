Write a single final-exam essay prompt for an upper-level moral philosophy seminar (90 minutes, 40 marks) that tests whether students can apply the concept of supererogation to a non-standard case without reciting textbook taxonomies. The scenario should be precisely constructed: a senior engineer employed by a public-utility company is asked to enter a structurally unstable facility during an emergency to perform a manual shutdown that is not within her contractual duties, carries a significant risk of death, and is not legally required; refusal would result in catastrophic harm but no professional sanction, while acceptance is widely praised as heroic. Ask students to defend or reject the claim that her performing the rescue is genuinely supererogatory rather than obligatory, and require them to address at least two of the following conceptual tensions in their answer: (1) the threshold problem—whether the boundary between sufficient and heroic sacrifice is sharp or gradient, and how that affects classification in institutional contexts; (2) institutional supererogation—the idea that organizational roles can generate demands that feel obligatory to role-occupants but appear heroic to outsiders, and whether this asymmetry undermines the act's supererogatory status; (3) the excuse-function concern—whether labeling an act supererogatory necessarily provides a moral excuse for those who decline it, and whether that functional consequence should influence the metaphysical classification of the act itself. Provide a model answer of approximately 800 words that demonstrates the specific argumentative moves a top answer must make: distinguishing evaluation of the act from evaluation of the agent; explaining why gradient sacrifice does not automatically collapse supererogation into obligation; assessing whether role-internal expectations can generate obligations without legal or contractual backing; and evaluating whether the excuse-function is a feature or a defect of the category. Include a marking scheme that assigns marks for conceptual precision (10), argument structure (15), and critical engagement with the scenario (15). List the specific mistakes that should cost marks: conflating supererogation with mere moral permissibility; treating the scenario as a straightforward consequentialist calculation of lives saved versus risk taken; invoking an undefined appeal to common-sense intuition without analyzing the competing intuitions in the case; failing to distinguish institutional expectation from moral obligation; or asserting that the category is useless without addressing whether the excuse-function is separable from the classification. The question should be unanswerable by summary alone and should separate students who understand how the concept operates under pressure from those who have only memorized its standard definition. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists.
