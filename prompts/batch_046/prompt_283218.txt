I'm constructing a research agenda—approximately three years, interdisciplinary philosophers and legal theorists, focused on conceptual architecture rather than empirical case studies—on how the category of genocide functions in moral, legal, and political discourse. The familiar backdrop is the divergence between Lemkin's original 1940s formulation, which treated genocide as a multidimensional process encompassing cultural, economic, biological, and physical dimensions of group destruction, and the 1948 Convention's narrower formulation centered on specific intent and protected-group membership.

Rather than rehearse the broad-versus-narrow debate, I want to prioritize among four specific conceptual lines of work that are currently underdeveloped:

First, taxonomic reconstruction: whether recovering Lemkin's multidimensional framework as an operational taxonomy—rather than a historical curiosity—can resolve persistent boundary problems in international law, or whether multidimensionality inevitably undermines legal determinacy.

Second, the normative structure of categorical refusal: whether the strategic avoidance of the genocide label by states and international bodies constitutes a distinct moral wrong (a failure of recognition that compounds the original harm) or merely an epistemic and rhetorical failure with no independent normative weight.

Third, scalar versus threshold architecture: whether atrocity classification should be reconceived along a scalar continuum measuring the intensity and multidimensionality of group-destructive practices, rather than as a binary legal trigger; and specifically, whether such a scalar reconception preserves or dissolves the category's distinctive normative force.

Fourth, cross-category contamination dynamics: how the conceptual expansion of adjacent categories—such as ecocide, politicide, and gender-based persecution—affects the boundaries and operational criteria of genocide when these categories share causal mechanisms but lack the protected-group logic central to the Convention definition.

Assume you can direct the effort of this small group. Which two or three lines should they pursue, in what sequence, and why? Your answer should defend an explicit ordering using criteria you specify—such as conceptual tractability, dependency relations among the questions, practical payoff for legal practice or moral theory, and the risk of dead ends. Identify which line is most upstream (the settlement of which most affects the others), which is most tractable given purely conceptual methods, and which you would deprioritize because resolving it would not substantially alter downstream work. Also specify what kinds of conceptual or institutional developments over the next few years would cause you to reorder the priorities.
