I was discussing commodification with a friend late into the evening, and we kept circling the same problem: we can both point to clear cases where putting a price on something corrupts it, yet we disagree about where the line sits and whether there is a single principle behind our judgments. I would like to see whether my own verdicts cohere, or whether I am holding incompatible intuitions that only feel consistent because I have never laid them side by side. Here are my judgments on seven concrete cases, with one pair that especially troubles me.

First, a regulated transnational market for living kidney donation—in which sellers receive long-term healthcare, education vouchers, and rigorous anti-coercion screening—strikes me as morally permissible, perhaps even obligatory given the lives saved, provided the structural safeguards are genuine. Second, algorithmic surge pricing for bottled water and insulin during an acute natural disaster, calibrated in real time to predicted mortality risk of the buyer, feels not merely unfair but structurally repugnant and worthy of prohibition. Third, open vote-selling in a municipal zoning referendum, with property developers purchasing citizens' ballots and the votes being legally transferable, is corrupt in a way that damages democratic equality. Fourth, hiring a professional "grief proxy" to attend a funeral and express authentic-seeming sorrow on behalf of absent relatives—supplied with biographical details but with no family member present—strikes me as deeply wrong, even though it is technically labor for hire. Fifth, paying a stranger to queue overnight for a limited government business permit that is legally transferable seems entirely acceptable, no different in kind from hiring a courier. Sixth, a verified carbon-offset scheme in which wealthy individuals pay low-income workers elsewhere to reduce emissions on their behalf, with the reduction audited and credited, feels permissible and perhaps praiseworthy. Seventh, paying a close acquaintance to serve as one's best man at a wedding solely to maintain the appearance of deep friendship, with scripted emotional displays and no underlying bond, feels wrong—though I am genuinely unsure whether it belongs with vote-selling or with the grief proxy.

The pair that unsettles me is the kidney market and the best-man hire: both involve money entering domains usually governed by intimacy or bodily integrity, yet I judge one acceptable and the other repugnant. Meanwhile, the grief proxy and the line-stander both involve purchasing emotional or temporal labor, yet I treat them very differently. Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the corruption of a specific social meaning, the presence of a good that resists instrumentalization, or the distinction between substitutable and non-substitutable roles—that captures all seven verdicts? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause handles which case, predict its verdict on a case I have not listed (say, a market in which parents pay teachers to write personalized, emotionally supportive letters to their children that the parents then present as their own), and tell me honestly which of my judgments it cannot accommodate without strain. If no single principle works, present the two best competing accounts, show what each saves and what each sacrifices, and advise me which verdict I should be most willing to overturn for the sake of coherence.
