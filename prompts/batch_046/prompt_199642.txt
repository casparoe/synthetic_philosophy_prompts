The administration consistently explains these tools as ways to eliminate mechanical labor so that teachers can devote themselves to deeper mentorship and creative instruction. Yet the teachers' contracts, class sizes, standardized testing schedules, and salary structures remain completely unchanged; the technology has been inserted into an identical institutional framework, as though faster evaluation automatically produces human flourishing regardless of who controls the time that is saved or whether that saved time is redirected back into intensified administrative demands rather than returned to the worker. This pattern seems to carry hidden philosophical commitments about technology, labor, and freedom. Please reconstruct the implicit theory that makes this practice seem reasonable: does it assume that technological change is neutral and automatically beneficial, that labor-saving devices expand human freedom even when social relations stay fixed, or that automation can be separated from questions of ownership and control? Say whether the practice is genuinely coherent with those commitments or quietly presupposes contradictory ones—perhaps treating machines as mere instruments in one context and as liberating agents in another. Distinguish what the institution strictly requires philosophically from what merely fits it comfortably, and tell me what school policy would have to change if we replaced the underlying view with one that holds automation necessarily reshapes the social relations of education rather than simply accelerating the old ones. Please write your response in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a thoughtful letter or email.
