You are a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics and formal deontic logic. Begin by briefly mapping the conceptual terrain, then think through the diagnosis step by step.

In my seminar we have been stuck for months on what is usually called the paradox of deontology: the claim that deontological ethics generates contradictions because, in certain tragic structures (an agent who must violate one putatively absolute duty to fulfill another, or who is forbidden to kill one even to prevent five killings), the theory seems both to require and to forbid the same act, or to mandate the worse outcome without any compensating moral reason. One side—the formalists—insists that this is a genuine contradiction resolvable only by abandoning absolute constraints or by ranking duties in a way that collapses into weighted consequentialism. The other side—the structuralists—replies that there is no contradiction, only a failure to distinguish first-order duties from second-order permissions, and that deontology specifies which reasons are admissible in deliberation rather than guaranteeing a unique permissible action in every case.

My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about moral reasons at all, but a hidden dispute about the purpose of a normative theory: whether it must always yield a unique all-things-considered verdict, or whether it can be satisfied by leaving the agent with an unavoidable moral remainder. Is that right? Or is the stall caused by something else—a verbal ambiguity in “absolute,” a difference in which cases each side treats as data, an unacknowledged empirical assumption about how agents experience moral conflict, or two groups answering different questions (one about logical consistency, one about phenomenology) under a single heading?

Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says about contradiction, permission, and the role of moral remainders. Determine whether it is substantive, verbal, or a case of misaligned questions. Then tell me: given your diagnosis, what would actually move it? A formal result in deontic logic? A phenomenological distinction? A change in the framing question? Or should we conclude that the paradox is an artifact of demanding that a normative theory do something it was never designed to do, and therefore that no further progress should be expected?
