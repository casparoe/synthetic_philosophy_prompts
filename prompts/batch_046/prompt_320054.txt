I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm organizing a 90-minute workshop for about twelve colleagues—also engineers, none with philosophy training—in a conference room with whiteboards and a projector. I want them to feel the pull of Good's theorem on the value of information: roughly, that if you face a genuine decision with more than one possible action, and you have a free opportunity to observe something before acting that might change which action is best, then taking that observation can never lower your expected payoff, and it will strictly improve it whenever there's any real chance it alters your choice. I do not want a lecture up front; I want the idea to emerge from what they do, and I only want the theorem named in the final debrief.

Please design a fully runnable game-plus-debrief for this group. The setting should be a simulated release-engineering scenario across several rounds. In each round, every participant must choose whether to deploy feature A (high reward, but risky if a hidden latency regression is present) or feature B (safe, low reward). The hidden state—whether the regression exists—is determined by a random draw the facilitator makes each round. Before committing, each participant may request a noisy "canary" observation: a simulated monitoring signal with a known false-positive and false-negative rate that reveals partial information about the hidden state. Requesting the signal costs zero points, but participants must physically raise a card to do so; doing nothing and deciding immediately is the default path, so inertia favors skipping observation.

To keep this from being a generic decision-theory textbook example, inject a specific niche twist drawn from actual engineering practice: participants must also choose the resolution of any observation they request. They can ask for an instant binary "healthy/unhealthy" flag, or for a granular multi-metric scorecard that takes thirty seconds of simulated "analysis time"—costing them the chance to participate in that round's payout if they take too long. Over successive rounds, they should discover that the granular scorecard occasionally reveals a subtle pattern that the binary flag misses, allowing them to switch actions when the flag alone would not have changed their mind; yet even the binary flag, being free and occasionally decisive, should never hurt them on average.

The rules and payoff matrix should be arranged so that participants begin by skipping observation to "move fast," then gradually realize—through accumulated point losses—that they are leaving value on the table. Include small real stakes, such as points convertible to coffee vouchers, so the incentive is tangible but low-risk.

The debrief should do four things. First, map concrete moments from the game onto the conditions of the theorem: identify the round where skipping the free signal caused a predictable loss (the decision was non-trivial), the round where granular resolution changed the optimal action (the ability to condition on observation), and the round where a noisy signal arrived but did not change the choice (expected value is still non-negative, but not strictly positive). Second, explain—without formal proof, using only the game's point totals—why rational conditioning on free information cannot reduce expected utility, and why it strictly improves outcomes whenever the signal has any chance of altering the best action. Third, predict what will reliably go wrong in execution—analysis paralysis from over-requesting granular data, groupthink in card-raising, treating the "free" binary flag as genuinely costless while ignoring the hidden opportunity cost of the resolution choice, or base-rate neglect when interpreting noisy metrics—and suggest brief facilitator interventions for each. Fourth, be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: the formal result requires a precise probability space and a consistent utility function, which the card game only approximates; moreover, the theorem assumes Bayesian updating, whereas real engineers may suffer confirmation bias or anchoring, and the game does not model those cognitive failures unless you deliberately build them in, which this design does not.

Please avoid naming the theorem until the debrief's final minutes, do not include any bibliography, reading list, or citation request, and do not ask for verbatim quotations from philosophical texts. Write the instructions so that everything can be executed with only the cards, score sheets, and whiteboard markers available in the room.
