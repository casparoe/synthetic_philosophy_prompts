I'm a retired physicist who's only gotten into philosophy in the last few years, and I sit on our university's human-subjects review board. We recieved a proposal that has the panel split right down the middle, and I need to draft our decision memo before Thursday.

The study is from the psych department. The PI wants healthy volunteers to go through a guided argument over two seperate sessions—basically a modern version of Zhuangzi's butterfly dream combined with the point that if waking and dreaming are phenomenally indistinguishable, then your justification for thinking you're awake collapses in exactly the same way. They don't just present it as history; they walk people through it step by step, then have them do decision tasks where they have to rely on perceptual evidence. The idea is to see if accepting the argument actually changes behavior, or if people just compartmentalize.

Half the board thinks this is routine—it's like giving people a logic exercise, and they debrief afterwards. The other half thinks it's unethical to actively induce skepticism about basic reality, especially since the PI admits you can't really unconvince someone who takes it seriously. There's also a worry that Zhuangzi wasn't even doing epistemology—he was talking about transformation or somthing—but I don't want the memo to hinge on my amateur reading of Chinese philosophy.

I need a memo recommending approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with concrete reasons the PI could actually respond to. It should take the harm argument seriously without just dismissing it as anxiety, and it should include an honest treatment of the strongest point on the other side. Can you draft it?
