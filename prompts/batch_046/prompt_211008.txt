Write the opening remarks — about eight minutes — for the dedication ceremony of the new Institute for Political Entitlement and Food Security. The audience is deliberately mixed: economists and historians of famine who know Amartya Sen’s entitlement approach in detail; philosophers of politics who have argued over whether the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward famine are best understood as distributive failures rather than natural disasters; and the family of the institute’s benefactor, who built his career in global grain logistics and who will sit in the front row, together with university administrators and graduate students. The benefactor has asked that the ceremony not become a lecture, but the scholars present will expect the remarks to take a real position rather than offer ceremonial platitudes.

Your remarks should take Sen’s core claim — that these three famines were not primarily failures of food availability but collapses of exchange and direct entitlements produced by political structures — and make it do structural work for the occasion: it should determine both the kind of gratitude you can extend and the kind of warning you must issue. In particular, use the distinction to frame the institute’s mission, but introduce a conceptual refinement that is not standard in textbook summaries. Distinguish between what might be called “distributive famine,” in which food is present but access is blocked by price, policy, or market failure (the pattern Sen emphasized in Bengal and Ireland), and “epistemic famine,” in which the political system not only blocks access but systematically suppresses the information that starvation is occurring, so that the failure of entitlement is compounded by a failure of recognition — a pattern visible in the bureaucratic reporting structures during the Great Leap Forward. Explain both categories to the non-specialists without condescension, and show the specialists that you are extending rather than merely summarizing the framework. Because the guiding idea is that famine is a political crime rather than a natural disaster, you cannot fall back on language of tragedy, fate, or nature’s cruelty to comfort the room; you must find a way to honor the benefactor’s work in logistics — genuinely about moving food — while insisting that logistics alone cannot prevent famine without the political will to make suffering visible. Keep the tone ceremonial yet intellectually rigorous, and do not include any bibliography, citation, or reading list.
