I am a high school student writing an independent study paper on how people compare well-being across different social groups when arguing about fairness and public policy. I have noticed a recurring argumentative move that I have not been able to match precisely to any concept I have read about, and I would like to know whether philosophers or social scientists have named it, and where its boundaries lie.

Here is the pattern, described through two situations I keep encountering. First, in debates about educational funding, advocates for poorer districts insist that objective deprivation—lack of textbooks, crowded classrooms—makes those students worse off, even when the students themselves report feeling content or optimistic. The subjective reports are treated as unreliable or adapted. Yet in debates about mental-health resources, the very same speakers treat subjective distress—anxiety, depression—as the authoritative measure of welfare, so that a materially secure student who reports severe suffering is judged worse off than a materially deprived student who reports none. The metric for comparing welfare seems to flip: objective conditions govern when comparing across class lines, but subjective experience governs when comparing within a privileged group.

Second, in cross-national comparisons of quality of life, people often invoke happiness surveys to rank countries, but only selectively. When citizens of a materially poor country report high satisfaction, commentators dismiss the data by saying those citizens have lowered expectations or have adapted to deprivation, so the reports do not reflect true welfare. But when citizens of a wealthy country report high anxiety despite material abundance, those reports are taken at face value as evidence of genuine suffering, with no corresponding suspicion that wealthy people have inflated expectations. In both cases, the principle of adaptation is deployed not consistently, but specifically to prevent a comparison that would challenge the speaker's prior moral conclusion—usually that the group they favor deserves more attention.

My question is not simply about adaptive preferences or about the well-known difficulty of interpersonal utility comparison in economics. Those concepts describe single frameworks. What I am describing is a pattern of strategic framework-switching: using objective metrics for Group A and subjective metrics for Group B, or invoking adaptation for Group B but not for Group A, in order to block a ranking that would undermine one's policy preference. Does this configuration have an established name—perhaps in philosophy of welfare economics, moral psychology, or political theory? If not, what are the nearest established concepts, and exactly how does each differ? I want to understand not just the label, but the carving: what belongs inside the phenomenon, what is a nearby but different failure mode, and whether there are boundary cases where the switch is justified rather than question-begging.
