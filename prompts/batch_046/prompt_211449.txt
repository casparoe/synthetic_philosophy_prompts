Exam question (approximately 350–450 words expected):

In the Political Treatise, Spinoza defines democracy not as majority voting but as the form in which the multitudo retains sovereign power collectively, expressing its potentia through law. He also insists that democracy is the most natural form, since it least departs from the equality of natural right, yet he excludes women, children, foreigners, and dependents on the grounds that they are not sui iuris.

Address the following: (1) How does Spinoza’s notion of democratic law as the expression of the collective potentia of the multitudo differ from an aggregative or preference-summing model? Here, draw on his idea—developed in the Ethics—that reason produces common notions which can ground a shared understanding of the common good, and consider whether democratic deliberation for Spinoza is better understood as rational convergence than as bargaining among interests. (2) Spinoza excludes certain groups from citizenship, sometimes appealing to natural differences in strength and prudence and sometimes to existing power relations. Is there an internal tension between calling democracy the most natural form and these exclusions? In particular, if natural right is strictly coextensive with power (potentia), does the exclusion of the powerless follow consistently, or does it smuggle in a normative distinction that his own metaphysics cannot support?

Student answer:

Spinoza’s democracy is not like modern voting where people just add up preferences. Instead, the multitudo holds power together, and the law is the expression of its collective potentia. This is closer to nature because in nature everyone has equal right to preserve themselves, and democracy doesn’t transfer that right away like monarchy does.

Spinoza also thinks reason gives us common notions—ideas that are true for everyone—so democratic deliberation isn’t bargaining; it’s people coming together to see what is rationally required for the common good. This means democracy works best when citizens are rational and agree on shared truths, not when they compromise.

On the exclusions, Spinoza says women and servants aren’t sui iuris. Some critics say this contradicts democracy being natural. But actually there’s no tension. Spinoza’s natural right is just power: if you don’t have power, you don’t have the right to rule. Women in Spinoza’s society lacked power, so excluding them is just recognizing reality. Spinoza doesn’t need a separate moral argument; the metaphysics of power does all the work. So democracy remains the most natural form for those who actually possess natural right.

Compared to Hobbes, Spinoza is more democratic because Hobbes thinks sovereignty must be absolute and undivided, whereas Spinoza allows sovereignty to be divided among the citizens in a democracy. This division is what makes democratic law an expression of collective will rather than a single command.
