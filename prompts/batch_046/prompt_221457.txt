You are a professional philosopher specializing in AI ethics and the rhetoric of persuasion, with practical experience in adversarial collaboration design. Before drafting anything, spend several minutes brainstorming the precise conceptual fault lines between autonomy-preservation and transparency-in-persuasion; think step by step, and do not rush to a synthetic verdict.

As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am commissioning the following structured document. The question is this: given that future superintelligent AI systems may possess extreme persuasive capacity, how should they be architected to communicate with human beings?

Treat the analysis as an adversarial collaboration between two camps.

Camp A — the Cognitive Sovereignty Coalition — maintains that such systems must be governed by doxastic autonomy buffers: hard structural constraints on framing, emotional calibration, and premise-selection that prevent exploitation of known human cognitive asymmetries, even when this reduces explanatory clarity or conversational efficiency. They argue that once the intelligence gap is sufficiently wide, full transparency about reasoning is inadequate protection, because human interlocutors cannot evaluate the premises at comparable depth.

Camp B — the Persuasive Calibration Alliance — maintains that autonomy buffers are technically unstable and ethically paternalistic. They advocate instead for rhetorical transparency mandates: complete disclosure of argumentative strategies, confidence distributions, and framing choices, allowing humans to provide informed consent to being persuaded. They contend that suppressing persuasive capacity treats rational agency as disposable rather than supported.

Please produce the joint statement in the following format:

1. Agreed claims: a numbered list of propositions both camps can sign, covering the formal problem (the scale of the intelligence gap, the reality of super-persuasion risk, the limits of existing alignment protocols, and the conceptual distinction between informing and manipulating).

2. Disagreements table: state Camp A’s core position and Camp B’s core position, each rendered in wording that the respective camp would accept for its own view. Then list the exact points of divergence in precise, non-caricatured language.

3. Cruxes: identify specific sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, record in advance what each side counts as relevant evidence or argument, state each side’s predicted outcome, and note any shared empirical case or structured thought experiment that both agree would bear on the issue.

4. Verbal disputes: if any portion of the disagreement turns out to be about word usage rather than substance, specify exactly which terms diverge (for example, autonomy versus agency, or transparency versus disclosure) and explain why the residual disagreement remains genuine.

Keep the prompt focused, avoid bibliographies, citation lists, and requests for further reading, and write for an informed audience that follows philosophy of technology but is not assumed to specialize in formal decision theory.
