Hey, I'm a grad student in philosophy (mostly ethics, but I'm poking around social ontlogy) and I need help putting a name on something I keep running into. You can treat me like you're advising an expert on collective intentionality — please think carefully, maybe do a quick brainstorm of edge cases before you lock in an answer, because I want to know exactly where the boundaries are and not just get a bare label.

So here's the pattern I've noticed in a few different places. First, at a stadium after a big match: thousands of people leave at once, but nobody decides "let's all use gate B." People just drift toward the nearest exit, adjust to crowd density, and suddenly there's a massive flow through B and none through A. Ask anyone why they went that way and they say "it was closer" or "everyone else was going there" — not "we chose B." Second, language change: over generations a word shifts meaning, no speaker ever votes on it, yet the community now "uses" the new sense as if it were shared. In both cases there's a clear collective result that looks coordinated, even norm-governed (people get annoyed if you go against the flow or misuse the word), but there's no shared intention behind it — just a bunch of individual responses that mesh.

My question is whether there's an established concept for this specific kind of thing: a stable, collective-level pattern that behaves like an intentional action but lacks any actual shared mental state or author. I know "joint action" and "we-mode" are nearby, but those seem to require that participants actually think of themselves as part of a "we" doing something together. Here the "we" is only visible from outside. Is there a name for that gap — maybe emergent coordination without authorship, or distributed agency where nobody holds the plan? If there isn't a standard term, what should we call it, and how do we draw the line between this and real joint action? Also, is it just the same as ordinary social emergence, or is there something philosophically distinct about the fact that these patterns can be normative for the individuals involved even though nobody authored them? Don't worry about citations or a reading list — I just want the conceptual carving, especially what this is not.
