I'm writing this after completing a graduate seminar in international humanitarian law, during which I volunteered with an organization that archives survivor testimony from conflicts where prosecutors have consistently declined to apply the genocide framework despite overwhelming evidence of mass killing. The institutional practice that now preoccupies me is the legal taxonomy—rooted in Raphael Lemkin's original conception and codified in the Genocide Convention and ICC jurisprudence—that reserves genocide for cases demonstrating a specific intent (dolus specialis) to destroy a protected group as such. In practice, this means that a campaign of extermination driven primarily by territorial consolidation, resource extraction, or the suppression of political opposition is classified as a crime against humanity or war crime, even when the scale, methods, and victim suffering are indistinguishable from recognized genocides. At the same time, advocacy networks and certain state actors treat the genocide designation as a singular moral and legal threshold that activates mechanisms like the Responsibility to Protect, while other classifications carry no comparable obligation. I find this divergence troubling because the experiential reality for survivors seems invariant across these categories.

What philosophical commitments about collective harm, group ontology, and the moral relevance of perpetrator intent does this classification practice presuppose? Does it implicitly require a metaphysically robust view of protected groups—perhaps that they possess a non-aggregative moral status or a collective personality that can be targeted for destruction independently of harm to individual members—or can the distinction be defended on purely instrumental grounds about which forms of violence are most amenable to deterrence and international prosecution? And if we replaced the intent-based taxonomy with one that classified atrocities solely by the irreversibility and scale of individual harm, irrespective of whether perpetrators selected victims based on group identity, what concrete institutional mechanisms—beyond labeling and rhetorical mobilization—would have to be redesigned?
