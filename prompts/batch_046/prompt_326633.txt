A fellow graduate student summarized Nietzsche's doctrine of eternal recurrence for me in roughly the following terms. She said that in his middle and late writings Nietzsche advances it first as a cosmological claim: if time is infinite and the total stock of possible configurations of force is finite, then every state must eventually recur, yielding an exactly identical repetition of one's life. She said that its philosophical point is nevertheless not predictive physics but an existential test — the thought experiment asks whether one can affirm one's life so completely that one would welcome its eternal repetition, which distinguishes noble affirmation from resentful denial. She then argued that this makes eternal recurrence structurally parallel to Kant's categorical imperative, except that it assesses the worth of an entire life rather than the universalizability of a single maxim. Finally, she suggested that recent cyclic models in theoretical cosmology have made the underlying physics more respectable than it was in Nietzsche's day, so philosophers who treat the idea as empirically obsolete are being prematurely dismissive.
