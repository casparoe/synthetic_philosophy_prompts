I am a retired physicist who has recently taken up philosophy in earnest. I am drafting a proposal for a four-month independent research programme, to be pitched to a small interdisciplinary foundation, on the hedonistic imperative: the view that biotechnology ought eventually to be deployed to abolish suffering in all sentient beings.

Please write the proposal itself—formal, polished, in British English, roughly 600 to 800 words. State the precise research question, explain why current ethical treatments leave it unresolved, set out the conceptual approach (not an empirical literature review) and justify that choice against more obvious alternatives, describe what a useful deliverable would look like and what would count as the project failing, and keep the scope honest for four months.

Do not include a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations. You may refer to general positions and arguments without precise attribution; avoid putting anything in quotation marks from a specific text, and do not lean on titles or dates of particular works.

My half-formed idea is this: standard debates treat suffering as a uniform experiential commodity to be minimised, but from a scientific standpoint aversion looks more like a multi-functional control signal—carrying sensory, predictive, and motivational information. The proposal should ask whether these functional roles can be conceptually dissociated from negative valence, and whether such a dissociation reveals the hedonistic imperative to be either incoherent or far more modest than its advocates suppose. The deliverable should be a conceptual taxonomy that maps types of aversive experience onto whether they are candidates for biotechnological elimination, preservation, or transformation, together with explicit conditions under which the abolitionist programme collapses into ordinary harm reduction. Keep the framing technical enough that it demands genuine conceptual work rather than repetition of familiar utilitarian talking points, but do not invoke obscure theories or highly specialised neuroscientific findings that a small language model would be unlikely to have properly retained.
