I am preparing for a discussion in my philosophy of mind course about the transition from classical dualism to contemporary distributed models of consciousness, and I keep returning to a notoriously dense passage from Descartes' correspondence and physiological writings of the 1640s, in which he proposes that the pineal gland serves as the singular point of convergence for sensory impressions, allowing the immaterial soul to inspect the unified scene of experience as if seated in a private theater. The passage is difficult partly because Descartes moves quickly from anatomical speculation to metaphysical conclusion, and I am struggling to see whether the theatrical metaphor is merely illustrative or structurally essential to his account of the soul-body union.

Given this, I would like help understanding exactly what is at stake in Dennett's Cartesian theater critique and his multiple drafts alternative. In particular, I am trying to grasp whether Dennett's model undermines the theater metaphor primarily by denying that there is a single anatomical location where consciousness happens, or whether the deeper problem is the assumption of a unified temporal frame—what we might call a single "now"—in which all drafts must be assembled for the self. Does the multiple drafts model eliminate the observer entirely, or does it fragment the observer into parallel editorial processes that never converge? I want to avoid treating this as a simple historical comparison; rather, I am interested in how the theatrical imagery in Descartes' passage conceals a commitment to centralized temporal binding that Dennett's distributed architecture makes unnecessary. Any explanation that clarifies this conceptual dependency without appealing to secondary literature would be greatly appreciated.
