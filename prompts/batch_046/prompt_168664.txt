I've been working through the modern Stoic revival in a philosophy of psychology course, and I keep returning to a conceptual tension in Epictetus's dichotomy of control. The classical division is sharp: our judgments, impulses, and desires fall within our power, while everything else—our bodies, possessions, reputations, and external events—lies outside it. Yet contemporary Stoic-inspired interventions, particularly the cognitive restructuring drawn from CBT and the acceptance protocols in ACT, seem to operate in a middle zone. They treat habitual emotional reactions and attentional patterns as neither fully within direct voluntary command nor entirely beyond influence, since they can be gradually reshaped through metacognitive practice without being instantaneously willed away. This makes me wonder whether the modern revival implicitly relies on a trichotomy—direct control, indirect influence, and complete externality—rather than the classical binary.

If we take that middle category seriously, I want to understand whether it is conceptually coherent with Stoicism or whether it collapses back into the original dichotomy once we distinguish between our assent to an impression and the impression itself. Specifically, how does the Stoic claim that virtue is the only good interact with therapeutic goals of reducing emotional suffering? Does framing distress as something to be modified through practice—rather than merely accepted through correct judgment—represent a departure from Stoic ethics, or does it reveal that the dichotomy was always meant to govern our assent rather than our initial affective responses? Please explain this conceptual issue in American English, being as precise and technical as the distinction requires.
