Two philosophers, Ava and Ben, have been arguing about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. They agree on the framing: a genuine dilemma requires that an agent faces two all-things-considered moral obligations that conflict irreducibly, with no permissible resolution and no possibility of full compensation or cancellation.

They fix on a specific case. A museum curator, acting in her official capacity, is the sole guardian of two artifacts in a burning archive. One is a unique manuscript containing a lost argument in value theory that, if destroyed, eliminates a line of reasoning from the historical record. The other is a set of ritual objects that constitute the only material link between a living community and its ancestral practices; if destroyed, the community loses the physical basis for ceremonies central to its collective identity. The curator can save only one. She saves the manuscript, executing the rescue without negligence or avoidable error.

Ben argues that this is a genuine moral dilemma. He says the curator had a binding obligation to preserve the manuscript and an equally binding obligation to preserve the ritual objects. Because the values at stake—epistemic preservation and ancestral relational continuity—are incommensurable, there is no common metric by which one obligation overrides the other. The choice is therefore arbitrary from a moral point of view. After saving the manuscript, she correctly feels moral residue: not mere sadness, but guilt that she violated an uncanceled obligation to the community. That residue is appropriate, which proves the obligation was real and unfulfilled.

Ava denies this is a genuine dilemma. She grants that both artifacts generate serious reasons, but insists that practical reason can still rank them through second-order considerations tied to the curator's institutional role. The museum's charter prioritizes the preservation of irreplaceable knowledge; that charter gives her an overriding reason to save the manuscript. Once she acts on that overriding reason, she has done everything morality requires. The guilt Ben describes is not moral residue but a form of non-moral regret—an emotional response to an unavoidable loss, not evidence of a moral failure. If Ben's view were correct, she adds, then every hard choice involving incommensurable goods would be a moral dilemma, which collapses the concept into triviality.

Ben responds that invoking the institutional charter merely relocates the conflict rather than resolving it. The charter gives her one reason, but the community's claim gives her another, and both remain valid and uncanceled. The fact that she acts on one reason does not erase the normative force of the other. That is precisely the structure of a genuine dilemma: an obligation remains in force even when it is justifiably overridden, leaving a remainder that counts as a moral cost.

Ava counters that Ben confuses an unfulfilled reason with a violated obligation. In practical reasoning, once reasons are weighed and a course of action is determined, the reasons on the other side are not violated; they are set aside by the structure of decision-making. She points out that we do not think someone who keeps a promise to save one life at the cost of another has violated an obligation to the second life, provided the choice was justified. The remainder Ben identifies is therefore just the tragic structure of finite agency in a world of scarcity, not a distinctively moral failure.

Ben replies that promises and life-saving choices are different because they involve goods that are at least commensurable in principle—lives measured against lives—whereas here the goods are genuinely incommensurable, so weighing is impossible without distorting both. He also claims that Ava's analogy fails because in standard conflict cases the overridden reason is canceled by the greater weight of the other; here, no greater weight exists, so cancellation never occurs.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: say who is right about each specific claim, note where each side mischaracterizes the other, and identify anything both are missing—particularly regarding whether moral residue can serve as independent evidence for a genuine dilemma, whether institutional roles can dissolve dilemmas rather than merely relocate them, and whether incommensurability genuinely blocks weighing or only makes it more difficult. Then give an overall verdict on whether this case is a genuine moral dilemma and on which party is more right overall.
