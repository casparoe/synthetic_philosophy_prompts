Write a dialogue between two philosophers—one who holds that genuine moral dilemmas are possible and one who denies it—centered on the following case: a lone archivist responsible for an irreplaceable, climate-sensitive oral-history collection is caught in a sudden structural collapse and can either rescue a trapped survivor or seal the vault that preserves the archive, but cannot do both. The defender of dilemmas should argue that the archivist faces obligations of genuinely incomparable kinds—saving an immediate life versus safeguarding intergenerational cultural memory—so that whichever choice is made, the agent correctly bears moral residue (appropriate regret or guilt) because no single ordering of reasons resolved the conflict. The opponent should reply that this appearance of incommensurability is actually a failure to apply a unified practical framework with sufficient precision, and that the remaining regret is best understood as grief for a sacrificed good rather than as proof that the reasons were unranked; they should also press the objection that true incommensurability would paralyze rational choice rather than produce a tragic but correct decision. Ensure that both interlocutors are equally competent and that each directly addresses the other's specific claim—particularly whether post-decisional moral residue tracks an unresolved conflict between values or merely registers the unavoidable cost of rational trade-offs. The dialogue should avoid citations, named theories, bibliographies, or appeals to authority; keep the argument focused on the conceptual structure of the disagreement and on whether a choice can be both fully rational and permanently stained by appropriate remorse.
