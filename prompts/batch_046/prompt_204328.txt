For our upper-level seminar on political myth and collective memory, I need a final-exam essay prompt—ninety minutes, approximately 1,000–1,200 words, thirty marks—together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The course treats lost-cause narratives (the Confederate Lost Cause, the Masada myth, the Kosovo Polje narrative, and the Dunkirk “miracle”) not as historical summaries but as living rhetorical structures. I want the question to be unanswerable by pure recitation of these cases. To keep it in the weeds without requiring obscure scholarship, build the prompt around the conceptual problem of “mood migration”: the rhetorical slide in these myths from counterfactual conditionals about past defeat—for instance, “if the supply line had held, the siege would have broken”—to indicative conditionals about present identity or future collective action—for instance, “if we organize with the same resolve, we will prevail.” Ask students to examine whether this migration is conceptually coherent given the philosophical distinction between indicative conditionals (governed by epistemic and evidential constraints, projecting forward from what is known) and counterfactual conditionals (governed by similarity-based selection over nearby possible worlds). Specifically, require them to argue whether preserving the distinction—keeping the past strictly counterfactual and separated from present indicative claims—undermines or actually strengthens the myth’s normative political force, and whether the blurring is essential to the myth’s efficacy. Provide: (1) the exam question itself with time and word constraints; (2) a model answer outlining five or six specific argumentative moves a top response must make; and (3) a marking scheme allocating points across those moves (total thirty), with a one-line note on the characteristic error that should cost marks at each stage. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and do not frame the answer around memorized labels alone.
