Write a dialogue, approximately 700–900 words, between two philosophers—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—discussing the procreation asymmetry in an advanced seminar on practical ethics. Vasquez defends the asymmetry: she holds that an agent has a moral reason not to bring into existence a child whose life would contain serious suffering, but lacks any corresponding reason to bring into existence a child whose life would be genuinely good. Chen rejects the asymmetry, arguing that if the prospect of suffering carries negative moral weight, then the prospect of comparable happiness should carry symmetrically positive weight, and that the standard defense of the asymmetry relies on an unjustified presentist bias about the conditions under which a being can be wronged.

In developing her position, Vasquez should articulate and defend what she calls the modal proximity thesis: the claim that the moral salience of a potential welfare state depends not on its bare logical possibility, but on its modal proximity to actualization through the agent’s present causal pathways. On her view, when an agent stands in a specific causal relation to actualizing a suffering life, that potential suffering acquires moral weight because the pathway is open; by contrast, when an agent merely refrains from opening a pathway to a happy life, no parallel moral relation is established, and therefore no reason is generated. Vasquez should present this as a refinement of the standard person-affecting intuition, not as a rejection of it.

Chen should respond directly to the modal proximity thesis. He should argue either that it collapses into a distinction between acts and omissions that cannot bear the normative weight Vasquez assigns it, or that it implicitly presupposes a circular account of potential personhood—one that defines moral relevance by reference to the very causal relations that are supposed to justify it. He should also challenge Vasquez to explain whether her framework yields implausible results in related cases, such as obligations to rescue existing children versus obligations to create new ones, and whether modal proximity can be specified with enough precision to do real normative work.

The exchange should be genuinely dialectical: each speaker must engage with the specific claims just made by the other, revising or sharpening their position in response. Neither character should be a strawman; both should display familiarity with the conceptual landscape of potentiality, moral status, and the ethics of creation, but they should not invoke obscure named theories that require specialist bibliographic knowledge. The dialogue should conclude not with superficial agreement, but with a clear articulation of the precise point at which their disagreement remains unresolved.

Please do not include citations, footnotes, reading lists, or references to specific texts by title and date. The dialogue should be self-contained and suitable for evaluation by a grader who has no access to external sources.
