I've been trying to steelman a tension I keep encountering in rationalist and secular-humanist discussions of morality, and I'd like a rigorous essay that resolves it rather than splitting the difference. The controversy is whether religious commitment actually produces better moral outcomes than secular ethical frameworks. But "more moral" is ambiguous, so I want the essay to distinguish three dimensions precisely: (1) observable prosocial behavior in monitored or low-cost settings; (2) normative coherence — the consistency and generalizability of one's moral reasoning when pushed to edge cases and extreme resource trade-offs; and (3) trust-resilience under anonymity, meaning the stability of cooperative norms in one-shot, high-stakes interactions where reputation tracking, legal enforcement, and third-party observation are all absent. The core question is whether secular societies can sustain dimension (3) without the internalized sacred-value architecture that religious traditions typically provide, or whether institutional substitutes — transparency mechanisms, contract design, cultural signaling — can fully replicate that function. Weigh the familiar empirical pattern that highly secular societies score well on standard prosocial metrics against the conceptual argument that such metrics systematically miss the high-anonymity failure modes sacred framing is specifically evolved to cover. Reach and defend a definite verdict: either secular moral communities can, in principle, engineer equivalent trust-resilience through institutional and cultural evolution, or there is an irreducible functional role for transcendent normative framing in sustaining cooperation under extreme anonymity. Be explicit about which of the three dimensions you treat as morally decisive if they conflict, and explain what your conclusion implies for how secular moral communities ought to be designed.
