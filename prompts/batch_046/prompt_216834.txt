Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Mara and Elias, who are members of a medical-ethics board debating a proposed triage protocol. The protocol allows a physician to invoke an unexplained personal-conflict exemption that removes her from a case; an algorithm then allocates scarce life-saving resources purely by expected lives saved. Mara defends the exemption by drawing on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarian reasoning. She argues that if a surgeon must justify saving her critically ill child rather than five strangers, she is compelled to treat her deepest commitment as one factor in a calculus — precisely the problem of having one thought too many — and that the exemption protects her ground project by letting her act from attachment without translating it into public reason. Elias opposes the exemption, conceding Williams's psychological point but arguing that institutional morality requires transparency and that Williams mistakes private deliberation for public justification. He offers a two-level alternative: clinicians internalize rules that generally license special attachments in defined contexts, so no opaque case-by-case exemption is needed. The exchange must be genuine back-and-forth. Mara should directly answer Elias's worry that unexplained exemptions invite arbitrariness and collapse the distinction between personal and institutional reasoning; she should also argue that even Elias's internalized rules force the agent to adopt an alienating, external perspective on her own commitments. Elias should directly answer Mara's claim that any required justification is alienating; he should examine whether his two-level view actually avoids that alienation, or whether applying a general rule in a particular case still introduces the very distance Williams criticizes. Neither should be a strawman; both should grant the force of the other's point before pressing their own.
