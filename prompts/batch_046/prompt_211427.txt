I'm a phd student in modern european intellectual history, writing my diss on genocide and modernity (mostly around Bauman and the Historikerstreit stuff, but I'm not a specialist in holocaust studies per se). I've noticed a recurring arguement move in the literature that I don't have a word for, and I'm wondering if there's an established concept or if I'm just seeing ghosts.

The pattern goes like this. First instance: a scholar insists the Holocaust is unique because of some specific mechanism — say, the fusion of industrial bureaucracy with total racial ideology, the kind of thing Bauman highlights. Fair enough, that's a narrow, defensible claim. But then in the very next breath they use that uniqueness to block any comparision with colonial atrocities or other modern state violences, even though those comparisons don't deny the bureaucratic-racial fusion; they just ask whether analagous mechanisms appear elsewhere. Second instance: someone defends uniqueness on the grounds of the total, universal intent to destroy Jewry everywhere — again a specific claim. Then they extend it to reject comparing the Holocaust with Rwanda or other group-targeted destructions, comparisons that don't touch the universal-scope claim at all. In both cases, when you point out the slide, they retreive to the narrow claim: "I only meant unique in that specific dimension, not incomparable in general." But the retreat doesn't erase the blocking work the uniqueness claim already did.

It feels structurally different from ordinary strategic ambiguity, because the narrow claim is sincerely held and does real argumentative labor. It's not quite motte-and-bailey either, since the "motte" isn't a weak fallback but a genuinely substantive thesis that gets weaponized. Does this specific architecture — using a defensible narrow uniqueness claim as a shield for an indefensible broad incomparability thesis, then retreating to the narrow claim under pressure — have a name in genocide studies or philosophy of history? If not, what's the best way to carve its boundaries, especially distinguishing it from the standard uniqueness-vs-comparability debate itself?

Please weigh the nearest concepts I might be conflating it with, and don't just drop a bare label — I care more about the carving than the name. End with your bottom line in a box: \answer{...}
