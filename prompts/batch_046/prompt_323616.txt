I’m preparing to close the final session of a philosophy-of-mind seminar that has spent several weeks with Schrödinger’s later essays, especially his Vedanta-influenced argument that consciousness is not genuinely plural. He compares the appearance of separate minds to white light split by a prism: the division belongs to the instrument, not to the source, so that what we call individual awareness is really one consciousness seen through different refractive conditions. For our closing, I want a poem—specifically a sestina, six stanzas of six lines followed by a three-line envoi—that uses the rotating end-words one, many, prism, light, veil, and same to mirror that metaphysical structure.

In the opening stanza, many and prism should dominate the line-ends, establishing the familiar image of multiplicity and division; through the successive rotations, one and same should gradually take over those terminal positions, and in the envoi all six words must appear together, with one and same paired in the final line while prism and veil occupy the middle positions, so that the form itself enacts the idea that separation was always in the medium, never in the source. The tone should avoid both mystical triumphalism and a reductive materialist dismissal: leave the tension between the physical-optics image and the claim of ontological unity unresolved. It should be precise enough that someone who knows Schrödinger’s prism analogy recognizes it without being named in quotation marks, and it should work as a poem even for a reader who has never encountered Vedanta.
