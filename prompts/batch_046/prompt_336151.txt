I'm putting together a 5-month grant proposal for a small philosophy nonprofit. I want to tackle Scanlon's contractulism and the aggregation problem, but not the usual way. The standard debate asks whether Scanlon can justify saving five over one, and people answer yes or no by tweaking the 'reasonable rejection' test. I think the whole framing is off: contractualism isn't a weighing machine, it's about whether a principle can be justified to each affected person. So the real puzzle isn't 'how many?'—it's whether a harm that is individually tolerable for every single person can become collectively intolerable in a way that generates a shared reason for rejection, without anyone having a large individual grievance.

My proposed question: is there a coherent account of 'joint reasonable rejecability'—where the structure of distribution itself creates a standing to reject that isn't reducible to summed individual standings?

Write me a one-page proposal. It should have: the precise question stated so it could come out yes, no, or partly; why Scanlon's own framework and the usual responses (denying aggregation, or importing consequentialist calculus) leave this specific gap open; my aproach—designing a set of distributed-harm scenerios and testing whether a principle permitting them can be rejected by the affected group as a collective without being rejectable by any member, and if so, what formal properties that rejection has; what counts as success (a principled distinction between summed and emergent rejection) vs failure (the concept collapses into individual aggregation or is incoherent); and an honest scope check—why 5 months is enough for the conceptual architecture but probably not for full formalization.
