You are delivering the opening remarks at a workshop titled "Symbiotic Strange Loops," co-hosted by a philosophy of mind group and a neuroscience institute. Half the audience are philosophers familiar with Hofstadter’s argument that the self is a pattern—specifically, that after his wife’s death portions of her "I" persisted inside his own cognitive loop—while the other half are neuroscientists who study inter-brain EEG synchronization but have little patience for metaphysics. The retiring organizer being honored, Dr. Elena Voss, has spent her career modeling continuous bidirectional predictive-coding resonance across paired cortical architectures—essentially testing whether one self-model can maintain coherence distributed across two brains rather than confined to one. Write a five-minute welcome that uses Hofstadter’s distributed-"I" idea as a structural constraint, not decoration: you must genuinely wrestle with the fact that, if the workshop’s premise holds, greeting the room as discrete individuals and celebrating Voss as a single, bounded self may be conceptually unstable. The speech should land for both audiences—the philosophers should recognize an accurate engagement with loop theory and personal identity, the scientists should grasp the concrete mechanism of cross-brain predictive resonance without feeling patronized—without asking for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Invent and deploy one concrete, vivid scenario showing what it would actually feel like for an "I" to span two brains, and make that image do real argumentative work in framing why this workshop matters.
