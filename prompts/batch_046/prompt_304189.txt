Construct a thought experiment that isolates the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—what common feature, if any, is shared by bodily pleasures, intellectual satisfactions, and malicious joys—by engineering a single ordinary-life scenario in which phenomenal-feel hedonism, attitudinal hedonism, and a representational (content-based) theory of pleasure deliver three genuinely different verdicts on whether the protagonist is experiencing pleasure. The scenario must be realistic and confined to one paragraph: inject a niche but empirically grounded condition, such as acquired musical anhedonia paired with intact reward-seeking behavior, or a localized interoceptive deficit that severs predictive reward signals from conscious affect, but do not invoke philosophical zombies, perfect predictors, brain-manipulation devices, or exotic neuroscience. After presenting the case, do the following check: state the mental state or behavior in question; specify the evaluative method each of the three theories applies; identify the nearby possible worlds that matter for each verdict; and explain exactly why each theory reaches its conclusion. Then identify which structural feature of the scenario forces the divergence—whether it is a dissociation between phenomenal quality and motivational pull, or the presence of representational content that lacks corresponding affect—and show how a defender of the theory that comes out least intuitive would attack the case. Finally, say whether removing that structural feature collapses the disagreement back into a standard Mill-style distinction between higher and lower pleasures, and confirm that the disagreement is generated by the theories themselves rather than stipulated by the scenario. The case should be suitable for an undergraduate seminar and should not depend on any specific bibliographic source.
