I've been thinking about that old seperateness of persons objection to utilitarianism — you know, the Rawls-style one — but I'm trying to defend a weird version of it for a course I'm in. Instead of saying persons are metaphysically distinct in some deep ontological way, I'm arguing that the real force comes from a constraint on rational aggregation: any aggregation rule has to be one that each agent could rationally accept ex ante without knowing which "person-slot" they'll occupy. Total-utilitarian rules fail this because they allow one slot to be sacrificed for big enough gains elsewhere, and no rational agent could consent to that risk if they might be the sacrificed one.

Please play a sharp utilitarian opponent — not a strawman, someone who actually knows the literature, will concede small distributive points but never abandons aggregation. Stay in character until I say 'stop', keep answers to about a paragraph, and don't break character to explain things. Here is my opening: if the seperateness objection is just this contractualist constraint on aggregation rules rather than metaphysics, then utilitarianism doesn't fail because it ignores "boundaries" between people, but because it adopts a rule that violates epistemic symmetry — no one behind a veil of modal ignorance could endorse being the potential sacrifice. How do you respond without just saying the total welfare is still larger?
