As a journalist covering philosophy and cognitive science, I have been tracking a peculiar stasis in the literature on Plato's Meno problem: why is knowledge more valuable than true belief that is merely correct? The debate now divides, in my observation, into two camps that refine their positions constantly yet never converge. The first, which I will call the modal-security camp, argues that knowledge adds value through counterfactual robustness: a known belief remains true across nearby possible worlds and therefore functions as a durable resource for reasoning and action. The second, the achievement camp, insists that the surplus value is normative rather than instrumental: knowledge is true belief that manifests the believer's own intellectual competence, giving the agent a kind of epistemic credit that accidental belief lacks. Both sides have absorbed standard counterexamples—environmental-luck cases for achievement, fragility cases for security—and sharpened their responses, yet the exchange feels like two parallel monologues rather than a single dispute.

My working hypothesis is architectural rather than substantive: the security camp evaluates beliefs as tools, asking about reliability and robustness, while the achievement camp evaluates believers as agents, asking about responsibility and credit. They may not be disagreeing about one thing called "value" at all, but answering different questions under a shared heading.
