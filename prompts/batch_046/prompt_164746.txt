As a journalist covering science and philosophy, I have observed a recurring pattern in interdisciplinary writing that I cannot map neatly onto the standard vocabulary I know. In explanations of collective phenomena—murmurations of starlings, distributed neural circuits, institutional boards—scholars routinely speak as though the group itself acts: the flock turns, the circuit recognizes, the committee decides. In these contexts the collective is treated as a genuine causal agent, not as a mere figure of speech. Yet when the same writers are pressed on what actually exists—whether in an ontological aside or in response to a reductive challenge—they retreat to saying there is literally nothing present except the individual parts arranged in space and interacting by local rules, and that the collective name is only a convenient label for tracking regularities. The shift is not framed as a formal dual-level theory with explicit translation rules; it is an unmarked slide between granting the group enough reality to do explanatory work and denying it any composed reality. I have seen this in both popular science and specialized literature, and what marks it for me is the absence of any stated criterion for when a collection of parts should count as one thing rather than many. Does this specific oscillation—relying on collective agents in explanation while refusing them the status of composed objects—have an established name? If the nearest concepts are instrumentalism about groups, or the view that higher-level entities genuinely emerge, how exactly does the described pattern differ from each? And if no established term captures it precisely, could you propose a precise label and definition, with clear boundary conditions—such as whether it applies to inanimate aggregates like crystals or weather systems, and whether it requires that the parts be organized toward a shared end? I am not looking for a bare name; I want the carving done precisely: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
