After a long, circular argument with a close friend about whether anyone can ever be in a situation where every available choice genuinely violates a binding moral obligation, I want a precise diagnostic instrument—not a literature review. Build me a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts positions on whether genuine moral dilemmas exist, designed so that a reader can answer each branch node without already knowing the philosophical labels. The tree must be constructed from the logical space of commitments first, with named positions placed only at the leaves, and it should be answerable by someone without web access.

Requirements: write in American English, keep the tone formal and polished, and do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or verification of specific texts.

Structure and content:
- Start with a concrete case node (for example, a Sophie’s-Choice-type scenario or a professional duty that necessarily harms an innocent party) that asks whether the agent does something morally wrong regardless of choice.
- Include six to eight branch nodes, each phrased as a direct question or commitment that separates siblings. The nodes must cover: (1) whether two obligations can be simultaneously all-things-considered binding; (2) whether the conflict is logical (the obligations genuinely contradict) or only practical (the world prevents joint satisfaction); (3) whether rational agent-regret or moral residue is appropriate after choosing the least bad option; (4) whether such conflicts require incommensurable value domains or can arise inside a single unified normative framework; (5) whether an ideal, complete moral theory could in principle resolve every apparent conflict; and (6) a somewhat niche separator: whether the overridden obligation retains binding force (the “bindingness retention” thesis) or is merely a psychological trace with no remaining normative pull.
- The leaves must include at least: strict deontic consistency/monism; value-pluralist tragic choice with residue; epistemic-gap view (real for agents, dissolved by perfect theory); practical-conflict-only view (joint unsatisfiability without logical contradiction and without residue); existentialist/radical-choice view (groundless decision with no remaining obligations); and virtue-ethical resolution (apparent dilemmas dissolved by practical wisdom/phronesis). Add any other combinations the logic produces.
- For every unoccupied combination in the logical space, briefly diagnose why it is empty—whether incoherent, conceptually unstable, or merely unexplored.
- Explicitly flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, explaining which shared answer pattern collapses them.
- The instrument should sort people by what they believe about cases and commitments, not by which label they prefer, so no node should ask “do you think you are a deontologist?”
- Keep the audience in mind: philosophy-curious but not necessarily trained; avoid assuming familiarity with technical terminology unless it is defined in the node itself.
