I just spent the evening arguing with a friend about whether human extinction is bad primarily because of the deaths it involves, or because of something Samuel Scheffler calls the afterlife conjecture — the idea that much of what we presently value depends on our tacit assumption that humanity will continue after we are gone. Tomorrow I am giving the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop called Continuity and Value; half the room are moral philosophers who will recognize Scheffler's framework immediately, and half are urban planners and architects who think in terms of legacy and long-range design but have no background in this literature. I want the speech to treat the idea structurally rather than decoratively: it should shape what can be said about extinction by showing that losing the future might hollow out present meaning, not merely eliminate future lives. To do this, I need the speech to work through a distinction my friend insisted on — between narrative anchoring, in which our projects gain significance from being embedded in a story that extends beyond our deaths, and existential dependency, in which we literally cannot value our present lives without that future. Only one of these supports Scheffler's conclusion that extinction is uniquely bad in a non-aggregative way; the other collapses into an extended form of egoism. The speech should navigate this tension for both audiences, letting the philosophers see the conceptual machinery at work without losing the planners in jargon, and it should avoid any bibliography or citation list since the point is to make the idea do live work. It should be roughly five minutes, should not sermonize about climate or technology, and should leave open whether accepting extinction is irrational or merely a different temporal orientation — though it should make clear why infrastructure and long-range planning are not just prudence but expressions of a present-tense moral psychology.
