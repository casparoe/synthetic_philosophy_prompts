I've been discussing Sartre's ontology of radical freedom with colleagues in a seminar on post-war French philosophy, and I find myself stuck on what looks like a tension in his account of bad faith. In his early ontological work, Sartre describes the café waiter who plays at being a waiter, treating this as a paradigm of mauvaise foi. But the critique seems to presuppose that there is some hidden, authentic self—perhaps the pure for-itself prior to all social determination—that the waiter suppresses by identifying with his role. Yet Sartre's entire ontology denies any such essential human nature: consciousness is defined precisely by its lack of coincidence with itself, by nihilation. So if there is no true self behind the mask, then identifying with a social function cannot be a betrayal of authenticity; it is simply one free project among others. In fact, it seems that any sustained identification—whether with a profession, a political commitment, or even the original project Sartre mentions—would have to be accused of the same spirit of seriousness, since each treats a contingent facticity as if it possessed the density of being-in-itself. I am trying to see whether Sartre's later analysis of the practico-inert offers a way out: perhaps material and social structures provide a kind of objective inertia that is not self-deception but genuine constraint, allowing some commitments to be authentic because they acknowledge this inertia rather than deny transcendence. But then I worry that this collapses Sartre's early existentialism into a form of determinism he explicitly rejects.

Given this, I want to ask: where exactly does my reasoning fail? Is the error in thinking that Sartre's critique of the waiter requires an essentialist self, when in fact bad faith consists not in denying a hidden essence but in denying one's own transcendence by treating facticity as destiny? Or is there a deeper problem with my attempt to salvage authenticity through the practico-inert? More specifically, if radical freedom truly means we are condemned to be free in every moment, how can any long-term project avoid being either a flight from freedom or a mere illusion of continuity? What observation, textual detail from Sartre's ontology, or conceptual distinction—perhaps between the original project and seriality—would force me to abandon my suspicion that the concept of authenticity collapses once we take nihilation seriously? I ask this sincerely: I want to know which argument or passage would change my mind, and where my reasoning is most vulnerable—whether in my interpretation of bad faith, in my use of the practico-inert, or in my assumption that radical freedom must apply uniformly to every temporal slice of consciousness rather than to the global structure of a life.
