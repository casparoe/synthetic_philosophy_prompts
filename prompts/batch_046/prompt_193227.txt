I just finished a long and exhausting argument with a close friend about whether dramatizing dead people violates something genuinely moral, or whether it merely offends our taste for accuracy and our sympathy for survivors. She insisted that because the dead cannot suffer, any obligation we call owing them must reduce to either epistemic duty to the living audience or to protection of living relatives from distress. I tried to articulate a middle position: that there is a distinct wrong in treating a completed life as unfinished raw material for present narratives, specifically by attributing to the deceased a philosophical stance they explicitly rejected, thereby distorting the internal coherence of the life that was actually lived. She countered that this is just aesthetic preference dressed up as ethics, and that if no one is harmed and no false belief is spread to a living audience that matters, the filmmaker has done nothing wrong. We ended without agreement, but the core disagreement stayed with me: is there a non-metaphysical obligation to preserve the narrative shape of a dead person's life, separate from factual accuracy and separate from harm to survivors?

This is now urgent rather than abstract. I serve on the Posthumous Representation Committee of the National Archive of Civic Memory, and I must circulate a written opinion recommending a concrete decision before Thursday's meeting. The case involves a proposed dramatic feature about a public-health reformer who died in 1987 and whose private papers—journals, unfinished essays, and correspondence with a sister who died in 1993—were donated in 2021. The filmmaker requests full archival access. The script invents extended dialogue between the reformer and her deceased sister based on ambiguous letter fragments, and more significantly, it attributes to the reformer a philosophical position—strategic pessimism about collective action—that the papers show she considered in an unpublished draft and explicitly rejected. The filmmaker argues that biography is always reconstruction, that the real person is epistemically inaccessible, and that our committee should limit itself to verifying that no living persons are defamed.

We must recommend whether to approve access unconditionally, deny it, or approve it subject to binding conditions—specifically, a requirement that any philosophical stance attributed to the deceased be supported by clearly affirmed or published sources, and that invented dialogue involving the dead sister be framed explicitly as speculative within the film. The memo should specify a timeline for script review and a mechanism for enforcement. It must treat the filmmaker's argument seriously: acknowledge that dramatic coherence requires selection and compression, and that imposing narrative conditions risks being the kind of paternalism my friend described. At the same time, it must determine whether there is a genuinely distinct obligation to the dead—one that does not depend on attributing consciousness, interests, or suffering to the deceased, and that is not reducible to accuracy-to-audience or protection-of-survivors—which justifies the conditions. If such an obligation exists, the memo should name it precisely, explain how it applies to this case without collapsing into either metaphysical fantasy or aesthetic preference, and justify the concrete recommendation. If it does not exist, the memo should say so clearly and recommend unconditional approval with only standard legal review.
