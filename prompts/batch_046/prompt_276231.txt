Compare Derek Parfit’s resolution of the hitchhiker case—where it can be rational to cultivate and act on a disposition to pay the driver even when, upon reaching one’s destination, one has no further instrumental reason to do so—with the alternative “diachronic narrative commitment” view, according to which the rational defect in breaking one’s word lies not in the causal efficacy of the disposition but in the disruption of the practical identity that makes one’s past planning and present action intelligible as parts of a single agent’s life. Focus your comparison on two issues: first, whether the narrative account genuinely avoids the charge of rational irrationality or merely redescribes the same dispositional structure in psychological rather than strategic terms; second, how each view handles the precise moment of arrival, when the hitchhiker’s present self faces the choice to pay or flee, and whether either view can explain why the rational agent should feel the pull of the promise as a reason rather than as a mere habit or causal tendency. Keep the analysis conceptual rather than historical: do not list readings, and do not invoke specific textual editions unless they are necessary to the argument.
