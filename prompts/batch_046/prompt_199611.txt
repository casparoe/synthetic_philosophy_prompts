A historian of moral change, working in the line of Bernard Williams and Kwame Anthony Appiah, holds four verdicts about why harmful norms disappear: dueling in Britain ended not through moral argument but through an inversion of honor—gentlemen eventually lost status by dueling; foot-binding in China collapsed when it became a symbol of national backwardness that damaged collective honor, with reformers gaining voice only after that shift; Atlantic chattel slavery was abolished mainly because industrial capitalism and strategic interests made it obsolete, with moral abolitionism supplying public language rather than driving force; and trial by ordeal vanished after ecclesiastical authorities withdrew clerical participation, dissolving the thick concept of divine judgment through physical testing rather than being argued away. Now consider the rapid disappearance in Western societies between roughly 1965 and 1985 of the norm that an unmarried pregnant woman should be concealed, her child placed for adoption in secret, and family reputation preserved through silence. If this historian wants to remain consistent with their track record, is the closest analog the honor-inversion pair (dueling, foot-binding), the interest-driven case (slavery), or the conceptual-dissolution case (ordeal)? Should they explain the shift as an instance of “asymmetric honor collapse”—where the practice first lost honor for the dominant family before it could gain honor for the woman—or as a structural change driven by contraceptive technology, legal reform, and women’s entry into the labor market? Give the verdict that best coheres with the four precedents, identify the closest analog, and state honestly what principle the historian would be adopting with each of the other two choices. Finally, propose one further case—such as the decline of blood-feud arbitration in Albania or the abandonment of corporate resignation rituals in Japan—that would best reveal which rule they actually run.
