I am preparing a conceptual fiction exercise for my high school philosophy seminar, and I would like you to write a short story—roughly 800 to 1,200 words—related to meritocracy and desert. Rather than a familiar tale about equality of opportunity, I want the narrative to inhabit a more obscure logical extreme: a near-future administrative system that practices what I will call counterfactual desert filtering. In this system, rewards are not assigned by outcomes but by an exhaustive audit that attempts to strip away moral luck, innate temperament, and all unchosen advantages, retaining only those actions that genuinely flow from an agent's uncoerced will. Follow one citizen or bureaucrat who realizes that, applied consistently, this filter eliminates nearly every basis for reward, yet the society still requires incentives to function. The story should explore the resulting social dynamics—particularly a kind of strategic self-effacement, where individuals conceal talents or preferences to avoid being penalized for undeserved dispositions—without reducing the idea to a simple dystopian cliché.

Please avoid references, citations, or a reading list; the piece should rely entirely on reasoning. After the story, include a brief author's note that identifies where the narrative takes liberties with actual philosophical positions—such as luck egalitarianism, the free-will debate, or theories of moral responsibility—and states which contested assumption the story's implicit verdict depends on (for example, whether counterfactual choice is the correct criterion for desert, or whether social cooperation can be sustained without desert-based motivation). Finally, state clearly what argument or consideration would change that implicit verdict and lead to a different conclusion about whether the system is unjust or merely rigorous.
