I have been reading Nietzsche's scattered remarks on digestion, climate, and bodily regimen — particularly his insistence that philosophical thinking is inseparable from the state of one's stomach, his warnings against alcohol and heavy cooking, and his tendency to present his own physical suffering and his strict dietary rules as continuous with, rather than separate from, his philosophical project. In discussing these passages with others, I keep encountering the same interpretive gesture: one interlocutor will cite Nietzsche's lifelong migraines, digestive collapse, and eventual mental breakdown as evidence that his philosophy of strength, self-overcoming, and the affirmation of life is merely a compensatory fantasy constructed by an invalid body. Another interlocutor will do precisely the opposite, treating his disciplined avoidance of stimulants and his meticulous attention to diet and climate as proof that his philosophy was genuinely embodied and therefore carries an authority that purely abstract systems lack. In both directions, the bodily practice is not treated as biographical background but as an argument about the philosophical content itself — either a reductive causal explanation or a legitimating credential.
