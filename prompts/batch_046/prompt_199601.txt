I was arguing with my roommate about why anyone listens to sad music or watches movies where literally everyone dies. We sketched out this arguement and now I'm stuck on whether it's subtly broken or actually proves we can't enjoy art the way we think we do. (1) If I enjoy a tragedy, it's because the experience as a whole is good for me. (2) The experience includes feeling genuinely sad — not just recognizing characters are sad, but actually feeling grief or pity, like I'm really choked up. (3) So if that sadness is part of why the experience is good, then sadness itself must contribute positively to my well-being in that context. (4) But sadness never contributes positively to well-being; it's a negative mental state by definition. (5) So the sadness in tragedy must not be real sadness — maybe it's pseudo-sadness or some aesthetic-distance version that feels like grief but isn't. (6) But then I'm not actually feeling grief at the play, which feels false — I really do tear up. (7) So maybe (4) is wrong and sadness can be instrumentally good. (8) But if sadness can be good for me, then I should seek out small real-life sadnesses too, like missing a flight or losing a bet, since those also produce sadness without huge costs. (9) I don't seek those out; I avoid them. (10) So either sadness is never good (back to 5-6) or there's something special about artistic framing that transforms negative affect. But if framing can do that, it should apply to every negative emotion, meaning I should enjoy real fear if it's "framed right," which doesn't track either. Where does this break? I kinda want to keep (2) because it feels true I'm not faking the tears, but then (8) seems unavoidable. Is there an equivocation between contributing to a good whole and being good in itself?
