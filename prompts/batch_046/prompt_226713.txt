I'm auditing the coherence of my own position on suffering, aggregation, and value comparison before I finalize an argument, and I want an external consistency check rather than a lecture. Here is the package I currently hold:

(1) I am a negative utilitarian in the strict sense: suffering is the dominant or sole moral disvalue, and the production of happiness has no direct moral weight except instrumentally, insofar as it prevents suffering.

(2) I endorse a lexical threshold for extreme suffering: no quantity of pleasure, absence of suffering, or distribution of mild discomfort elsewhere can justify allowing, causing, or failing to prevent an instance of severe, torture-level pain. This boundary is categorical, not merely a matter of extreme weighting on a continuous scale.

(3) I think pleasure and pain are fully commensurable: they lie on a single cardinal hedonic continuum, differing only in sign, intensity, and duration, so that in principle any amount of one can be exactly canceled by a corresponding quantity of the other.

(4) I accept unrestricted total aggregation across persons: the moral evaluation of a world-state is the simple sum of individual welfare values, with no averaging, no distance-based discounting, and no person-affecting restrictions on who is included in the sum.

(5) I maintain an asymmetry regarding potential people: there is a strong moral reason not to bring into existence beings who will suffer, but no corresponding reason—positive or negative—to bring into existence beings who will be happy.

(6) On hard cases, I judge that it is morally required, and sometimes obligatory, to allow mild chronic discomfort in an arbitrarily large number of people—each well below the lexical threshold—if that is the only available means of preventing a single instance of extreme suffering, regardless of whether the summed total of that mild discomfort exceeds the disvalue of the single extreme case in raw hedonic units.

(7) I claim that the lexical boundary in (2) is best explained not by any incommensurability between pleasure and pain, but by a deontological side-constraint against instrumentalizing persons, which operates inside an otherwise consequentialist framework.

Please examine these commitments pairwise. For each pair that is in genuine tension—not merely apparent—rate the severity, specify exactly what pulls against what, and state the cheapest revision that restores consistency without abandoning the core of negative utilitarianism. For pairs that only look tense but dissolve on inspection, explain briefly why they are compatible after all. In particular, check whether (3) undermines the coherence of (2); whether (4) together with (6) eventually collapses the protection that (2) is meant to provide; and whether (7) can genuinely sit with (1) without rendering (2) redundant. Order the real tensions by severity, and then identify the single revision that would clean up the most problems at once, noting what it costs.
