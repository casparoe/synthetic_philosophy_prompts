I'm writing to ask for help drafting a research proposal for a course on comparative moral philosophy. The assignment is a proposal for a term paper of roughly five thousand words due at semester's end, and I want to examine a question that sits at the intersection of Buddhist ethics and consequentialist theory but is rarely addressed in the standard literature. Specifically, I want to ask whether the Theravada Abhidharma taxonomy of mental factors—particularly the distinction between root conditions and proximate conditions, and the weighting of mental efficacy over hedonic outcome—contains a structurally consequentialist calculus that has been obscured by the usual emphasis on intentionalism. The precise question is whether one can derive from this causal ontology a formal decision procedure for ethical action that weighs karmic results without collapsing into utilitarianism, and whether the Mahayana notion of skillful means then operates as a rule-consequentialist constraint rather than as an ad hoc suspension of moral rules. Existing treatments tend to classify karma either as pure agent-centered intentionalism or, conversely, treat skillful means as an unprincipled exception, but neither camp examines whether the Abhidharma framework already embeds an outcome-sensitive structure that could ground a hybrid moral theory. I propose to approach this through conceptual reconstruction: first articulating the implicit causal model within the mental-factor taxonomy, then mapping its components onto modern distinctions between agent-centered and outcome-centered morality, and finally testing whether the resulting framework can address standard objections to consequentialism—such as the separateness of persons—without importing utilitarian assumptions about aggregate welfare. A successful result would be a clearly articulated formal structure with explicit conditions for karmic optimality; failure would be demonstrating that the taxonomy is too indeterminate to support any calculus, or that skillful means collapses the distinction between rule and outcome evaluation entirely. I believe this is tractable within one semester if I restrict myself to conceptual analysis of standard Abhidharma categories and avoid extensive philological or historical work across all Mahayana schools, though it risks being too large if I also try to integrate contemporary cognitive science or cover every regional variant of the doctrine. Please write the full proposal in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write in a single letter or email: state the precise question, explain why current answers leave it open, describe the proposed method and why it is preferable to obvious alternatives like a direct historical comparison with Mill or a purely textual exegesis of the Lotus Sutra, specify what a result and a failure would look like, and include an honest assessment of what might make the project too big for the available time.
