If the reversal-of-fortune argument uses settler mortality as an instrument to isolate the causal effect of extractive institutions on modern national income—thereby purging geographic confounders from the institutional coefficient—why do development economists still treat culture as an independent explanatory channel rather than as either an institutional byproduct or a variable confounded by the same ecological instruments? Or is the real issue not empirical but philosophical: that instrumental-variable identification secures only local treatment effects over discrete causal steps, and that applying such coefficients to explain wealth differences across centuries requires a counterfactual-stability assumption about institutional transmission that history, lacking replicable interventions, cannot validate? In other words, does the residual role of culture in these debates signal a genuine causal pluralism, or does it expose the limits of extending identification strategies from short-run natural experiments to long-run structural explanations?
