I'm participating in an eighteen-month clinical protocol in which my auditory processing pathways are being replaced, one functional layer at a time, with synthetic interfaces. The design ensures there is never a moment of unconsciousness or discontinuity: each prosthetic layer assumes the precise computational role of the tissue it replaces, and my perceptual baselines—pitch discrimination, timbral recognition, emotional response to harmony—remain statistically identical to my pre-intervention measurements. By construction, there is no discrete event of replacement, only a continuous gradient.

Fourteen months in, I have resumed composing. I find that the harmonic and textural choices that now feel intuitively right are ones I would have actively rejected before the trial began—structures I once experienced as cold or incoherent now seem to carry the emotional weight I previously sought in consonant, lyrical lines. My partner, who is fully informed about the protocol, insists this is not a philosophical problem: because every substitution preserved functional equivalence, there was never a point at which I ceased to be myself; my changing taste is therefore no different from any adult's gradual aesthetic evolution. Yet my discomfort is not a fear of death or discontinuity. It is a sense that these preferences are not authored by the same narrative continuity that shaped my earlier work. If I complete this symphony while the remaining layers are being integrated, am I extending my own artistic project, or am I allowing a gradually installed, functionally equivalent but architecturally foreign system to co-author under my name? Given that no objective test can detect a rupture, should I suspend the remaining replacements in order to protect what feels like narrative ownership of my aesthetic judgment, even if that means forgoing perceptual capacities I might come to value?
