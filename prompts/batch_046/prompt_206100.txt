I chair the curriculum revision board for our university’s interdisciplinary humanities core, and we must submit a binding recommendation to the faculty senate by the end of the month. Our current required sequence—titled “Classical Foundations,” “Medieval Transition,” and “Modern Achievement”—has been criticized by a coalition of students and junior faculty who argue that its chronological arc implicitly frames every earlier intellectual tradition as an incomplete stage on the way to contemporary Western norms. In response, a working group proposes dismantling chronology entirely in favor of three thematic modules—“Cosmologies,” “Accountability,” and “Material Practice”—each taught with texts drawn from ancient, medieval, early modern, and recent sources arranged in parallel rather than in succession. The working group’s chair claims this eliminates teleology; however, a senior historian on our board has raised a more subtle concern, noting that if instructors select pre-modern texts for these modules mainly because they resonate with current debates—say, using a twelfth-century treatise on natural law only to illuminate modern constitutional theory—we may preserve the very distortion we claim to cure, merely hiding it behind thematic labels. Meanwhile, several students have submitted a counter-proposal insisting that removing all chronological scaffolding deprives learners of the ability to see how specific problems were transformed rather than solved, and they suggest retaining the linear structure but renaming the middle unit “Plural Modernities in Formation” rather than “Transition.”

The board is divided. One faction wants to approve the thematic restructuring with the condition that each module include at least one text taught with minimal modern framing; another faction believes any structural reform is cosmetic unless we abandon the idea of a required “core” altogether. I need a short written opinion—no more than two pages—that recommends a concrete course architecture, includes a clear decision on whether chronology should survive in any form, and honestly addresses the strongest argument from the side that loses. The recommendation should be something the senate can vote on directly, not a call for further study, and it should avoid treating either pedagogical coherence or historical alterity as obviously disposable. Please draft this memo for circulation before Thursday’s meeting.
