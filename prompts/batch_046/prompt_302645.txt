I've been trying to understand Dennett's idea that consciousness isn't a Cartesian theater — there's no central screen where everything comes together — and that instead we have a "multiple drafts" model in which different parts of the brain produce competing narratives without any single viewer. What puzzles me is the moment when one draft gets treated as the official report of experience. Even if we eliminate the audience and the screen, there still seems to be a selection mechanism that elevates one narrative above the rest and makes it authoritative. I want to ask whether this selection point isn't just the Cartesian theater relocated: not a theater with a spectator, but an editorial office that decides which draft reaches awareness. If that office is genuinely distributed, perhaps the model escapes the problem; but if it functions like a hidden gatekeeper, haven't we smuggled the theater back in under the name of editing? I'd welcome comments on whether this is a fair criticism or whether I'm misunderstanding how drafts stabilize and compete. In particular, does the multiple drafts model really avoid a unified point of convergence, or does it merely disguise that convergence as a publication process? And if there is genuinely no editor at all, how should we explain the dominance of one narrative without implicitly appealing to an audience that picks the winner?
