Construct a taxonomy, in the form of a branching decision tree with six to eight leaves, that sorts positions in the philosophy of mind debate over eliminative materialism specifically as it applies to beliefs and desires. Design the instrument for an advanced undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind: every internal node must be a concrete claim or thought-experiment that a student could answer yes/no (or select among options) without already knowing which philosophical label the answer corresponds to, so that the tree classifies people by what they actually hold rather than by the name they prefer.

Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of named philosophers. Begin with the question of whether a mature neuroscience could in principle predict and explain human behavior without ever invoking propositional attitudes. From there, branch on whether such explanatory success would justify (i) complete ontological elimination, (ii) mere pragmatic suspension of the concepts, or (iii) retention of the concepts with heavy reconstruction. Next, include a branch that separates symmetric targets (both beliefs and desires eliminated together) from asymmetric targets (only one eliminated, or the two treated differently). Then distinguish “replacement” views—which posit a specific alternative ontology, such as high-dimensional dynamical-state descriptions or predictive-processing architectures, to do the explanatory work—from “quietist” views—which deny that any unified replacement structure answers to the folk-psychological concept and hold simply that nothing corresponds to it. You must also include a branch addressing whether fragments of folk psychology—such as context-bound “propositional fragments” lacking full generality, or instrumental desire-ascriptions retained in legal and moral practice—can survive the elimination of the full-blown attitudes.

The leaves should include at least the following positions, located precisely in the space your branches create: (1) global replacement eliminativism (both beliefs and desires eliminated, with a specific neuroscientific ontology substituted); (2) belief-only quietist eliminativism (beliefs eliminated without replacement, desires retained in revised form); (3) an asymmetric revisionism that eliminates propositional beliefs but reconstructs desire as a non-representational motivational vector; (4) a non-eliminativist revisionism that accepts the neuroscientific critique but reconstructs belief and desire within a predictive-processing framework rather than discarding them; (5) a pragmatic instrumentalist view that treats belief-desire talk as a useful fiction with no ontological commitment; and (6) one additional leaf representing an unoccupied or under-defended combination of your branches, which you should label “unnamed” and briefly diagnose—is the gap due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect? Wherever your tree genuinely cannot separate two of the named leaves, flag the pair explicitly and explain why the branching questions fail to discriminate them.

Take a definite, committed stance on which of your branch distinctions are genuinely divisive versus merely verbal, and defend your ordering of the nodes—do not present the tree as if every branching choice carries equal weight. Give explicit reasons for why you placed the symmetric/asymmetric split before (or after) the replacement/quietist split, and for why you think the fragment-survival branch belongs where it does. Your response should contain no bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources; it should be answerable entirely through conceptual reasoning.
