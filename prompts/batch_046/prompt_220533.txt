As a high school student in an interdisciplinary logic seminar, I recently heard an instructor compare Russell’s analysis of definite descriptions—especially his treatment of “the present King of France is bald”—to a structured database query aimed at a missing record: instead of attributing baldness to some ghostly object, the query returns a null value that the system processes according to its logical rules. The instructor claimed this shows Russell’s quantificational paraphrase is essentially modern null-handling, and that Meinongian object theories are therefore as obsolete as pre-relational data models.

Please audit this analogy systematically. Give an explicit correspondence table: what in Russell’s framework corresponds to the query string, the database schema, the null return, the evaluation rules, and the issuing agent? Then identify where the mapping breaks—for example, does Russell’s distinction between primary and secondary occurrence have any counterpart in query evaluation? Does the analogy hide the fact that Russell eliminates the definite article entirely, while a database query preserves the syntactic form of the search term?

I am especially interested in whether the analogy silently imports commitments from negative free logic, where empty singular terms receive a special semantic value rather than being dissolved into quantifier scope, since many database systems do precisely that with null pointers. If so, does the analogy actually support Russell, or does it unintentionally vindicate a Meinongian or free-logic treatment of empty reference?

Finally, assess whether the conclusion that Meinongianism is obsolete survives once these disanalogies are explicit, or whether the analogy was carrying the argumentative weight all along.
