I'm sitting on this working group that revises mediation protocals for international water treaties, and there's this weird asymmetry baked into the template that nobody really talks about. When upstream infrastructure expands the total pie—like a new dam—mediators scale each country's share proportionally to its "utopia point," basically the max it could claim if it ignored the other party entirely. So if one nation's ideal claim was twice the other's, it gets twice the surplus. But when drought shrinks the pie, the protocol doesn't scale down proportionally; instead it locks both sides into their prior absolute allocations and makes them share the deficit symetrically. I mentioned this over coffee with a collegue and she said it's probably just path dependency, but I think there's a deeper philosophical commitment hiding in there.

What theory of distributive fairness does that pattern presuppose? It seems to treat entitlement as anchored to each party's maximal feasible claim, so that gains should respect that ratio, while losses are treated as a collective burden to be split evenly regardless of those same claims. Does that mean the protocol is implicitly committed to individual monotonicity—this idea that expanding what's available should never leave a party worse off—while rejecting that same logic for contractions? And is there a hidden view about bargaining power, maybe something like Kalai-Smorodinsky style reasoning, that makes this coherent, or is it just stitching together two incompatible intuitions about what people deserve independant of agreement? I want to understand whether the practice actually needs this particular theory, or if it's compatible with other accounts that don't treat utopian entitlements as morally basic.
