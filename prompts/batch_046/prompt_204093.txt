Draft the joint statement that should result from a sustained adversarial collaboration between a conciliationist and a steadfastist about peer disagreement in normative epistemology. Define the conciliationist position with precision: upon discovering that an epistemic peer assigns a credence to proposition P that differs from one's own, rationality requires revising one's credence toward equality, because the disagreement itself constitutes symmetric higher-order evidence about the reliability of both reasoners. Define the steadfastist position with equal precision: one may rationally maintain one's original credence if one possesses an independent, non-circular basis for judging that one's own reasoning process is more reliable than the peer's with respect to P, and if that judgment survives reflective scrutiny of the disagreement itself.

Structure the document exactly as follows. First, a numbered list of at least six substantive claims both sides can endorse—covering, for example, the conditions that must hold for two agents to count as epistemic peers; the distinction between first-order evidence about P and second-order evidence about reasoning reliability; the irrationality of dogmatically ignoring a peer's view; the fact that conciliation need not entail complete abandonment of one's position; the relevance of domain-specific expertise asymmetries; and the shared commitment that the debate is normative rather than merely descriptive. Second, present a disagreements table in rigorously neutral wording: each row states the conciliationist's view and the steadfastist's view in language that each party accepts as an accurate representation of its own position, avoiding caricature and loaded terminology. Third, isolate exactly two cruxes—sub-questions such that resolving one in a particular direction would rationally compel movement from the opposing camp. I suggest the cruxes are: (i) whether a subject can ever have genuinely non-circular access to her own cognitive reliability in precisely those cases where peer disagreement calls that reliability into question; and (ii) whether applying conciliationism consistently across all domains entails an implausible epistemic spinelessness, particularly when experts with asymmetric training disagree about technical predictions.

For each crux, construct a concrete hypothetical case study—specified in sufficient detail that both sides agree entirely on its descriptive features, yet predict opposite normative judgments about what the rational agent should do. Then record, in advance and in each party's own voice, the specific predicted judgment and the reasoning step that generates it. Finally, append a brief residue section that lists what the debate is not about—e.g., it is not about whether disagreement ever carries epistemic weight, nor about whether one should listen to others, nor about the psychology of belief formation—so that the remaining disagreement is made exact, localized, and empirically or conceptually testable in principle.

Write in formal, polished philosophical prose. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works; do not invoke empirical studies or statistical results that would require external verification. The deliverable should read as a document both parties could sign, with the residual disagreement isolated rather than diffused across ambiguous terminology.
