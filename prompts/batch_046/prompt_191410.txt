I have been trying to think through, on my own terms, why my personal choices—eating meat, driving a car, or buying particular goods—are often said to make no moral difference to large-scale harms like climate change. The reasoning I keep encountering is that any single act is causally negligible: a factory farm or power plant operates at a scale where my one purchase or trip is imperceptible, so I do not cause any additional harm by participating. At the same time, I have tried to work out an alternative framing: perhaps the wrongness is not in triggering a specific harmful event but in being part of a collective practice that produces harm, where individual actions are constitutive rather than causally decisive. I want to compare these two conceptual pictures—the one that denies individual efficacy on purely causal grounds, and the one that shifts the analysis to participation in a harmful collective structure—across three specific contexts: personal carbon emissions, voting in a large democratic election, and consumer demand in supply chains where prices aggregate enormous numbers of tiny signals. In particular, I am unsure whether the participatory framing genuinely answers the causal-negligibility worry or whether it simply moves the discussion away from causation into something like social meaning or symbolic endorsement. Could you lay out precisely where each framework succeeds and fails conceptually, especially in the supply-chain case, where my purchase might alter a price by an amount far smaller than any rounding error yet the market mechanism is built precisely to register such infinitesimal differences? Please treat this as a conceptual analysis rather than a request for empirical data, author citations, or reading lists.
