I am convening a three-year interdisciplinary working group—philosophers of metaphysics together with conservation scientists and materials engineers—to determine where conceptual work on material constitution should go next. The familiar statue-and-clay problem, with its standard options (identity, relative identity, temporal parts, or non-identity constitution relations), has been thoroughly examined in static cases, but I believe the pressing open questions now arise from artifacts whose material substrate is designed to change from within rather than merely suffer external replacement. Think of architectural installations built from living or self-regenerating substrates—mycelium structures, bio-concrete that metabolizes cracks, or monuments subjected to long-term conservation protocols in which every mineral component is eventually substituted with geologically distinct but chemically identical material, with institutional practice continuing to treat the result as the original work. These cases are not peripheral applications; they challenge whether constitution is essentially diachronic, whether it can be intermittent when substrate is temporarily absent during regeneration, and whether the constituting relation binds an artifact to a specific material aggregate or to a sustained functional and formal profile that can survive total material turnover.

Given this framing, I want you to propose and defend a prioritization of the open conceptual lines of work that these dynamic cases open up—not a literature survey, but an agenda with resource constraints in mind. Our group has philosophers with training in logic and metaphysics but no laboratory access; we have three years; and our aim is to produce conceptual frameworks that conservation practice and art ontology could actually use. Which questions should we treat as upstream, in the sense that resolving them restructures the others? Should we prioritize developing a pluralist theory of constitution (different relations for biological, artifactual, and hybrid cases), refining a distinction between formal and material causation adequate to regenerative processes, or abandoning the one-object/one-substance model in favor of a process-based ontology? For each line you rank highly, specify the tractability conditions—what kind of argument or formal tool would advance it, and why philosophers rather than empirical scientists are positioned to do the work. Conversely, identify which apparent questions are overrated or likely dead ends: for example, does extending classical essentialist criteria of artifact identity to metabolically active substrates genuinely solve the persistence problem, or does it merely relocate the ambiguity? Rank your top three priorities, defend the ordering with explicit criteria—downstream dependency, tractability, and practical relevance to conservation ethics—and tell me what kind of argument or counterexample over the next few years would cause you to reorder them.
