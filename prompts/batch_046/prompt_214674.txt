I retired a few years ago from a long career in experimental condensed-matter physics, and since then I have taken up philosophy with some seriousness, particularly the work of Kierkegaard on modernity, the press, and the abstraction he called the public. Recently, in conversation with former colleagues now serving on regulatory boards, I have become puzzled by an institutional protocol that seems widespread in scientific governance: the standardized public-consultation procedure used by agencies overseeing technical proposals—such as genetic-modification protocols, nuclear-waste siting, or climate-engineering pilot studies—wherein any citizen may submit written comments, these submissions are aggregated into a quantitative sentiment profile, and the final regulatory report is required to address that aggregate as if it were a single interlocutor. The protocol assigns formal equality to every submission regardless of the contributor's technical competence, and it treats the summed output as expressing the view of something called the public, even though no individual stands behind the aggregate position and no participant is held personally accountable for the outcome.

This strikes me as structurally analogous to Kierkegaard's worry that the press manufactures an abstraction—the public—which allows individuals to hold opinions without commitment, flattens qualitative distinctions among judgments, and dissolves individual responsibility into an anonymous mass. Yet I am unsure whether the practice actually presupposes a philosophical theory of democratic knowledge—perhaps that truth or wisdom emerges from the aggregation of perspectives—or whether it can be defended on purely procedural grounds of legitimacy and inclusion without any epistemic claim at all. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of value, knowledge, and collective identity that this design requires, to distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what merely accompanies it, and to say whether it is philosophically coherent to maintain the procedure while denying that there exists a unified public will or that crowd aggregation has cognitive value. Furthermore, please indicate the extent to which Kierkegaard's critique of the press and of public opinion applies not only to journalism but also to this kind of bureaucratic consultation mechanism.

Please state how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point about which you are least sure—whether that concerns Kierkegaard's own position, the interpretation of the institutional practice, or the compatibility of the two.
