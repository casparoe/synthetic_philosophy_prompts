I was arguing with my flatmate about whether formal logic can ever really talk about uncountable things, and I think I've accidentally built an argument that proves far too much. Here are the steps, numbered so you can point at the weak link.

(1) The downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem says that if a first-order theory has an infinite model, it also has a countable model.

(2) ZFC proves Cantor's result: there's no bijection between the natural numbers and their power set, so the power set is genuinely uncountable.

(3) So ZFC has a countable model M. Inside M there's a set P that M takes to be the power set of ω, and M correctly proves, within itself, that P is uncountable.

(4) But since M is countable, every member of P also lives in M, and from outside we can just list them all. That gives us a function f: ω → P in the real meta-universe, even if f isn't inside M.

(5) So in reality there IS a one-to-one correspondence between ω and P, despite ZFC's proof that there isn't one.

(6) That means ZFC proves something literally false in the world where we apply it — unless we say it's false only inside M, but then mathematical truth becomes model-relative in a way that undermines objective maths.

(7) Worse, I can run the same move on any theory with size distinctions: physics with continuous space, a model of possible worlds with uncountably many worlds, even a philosophical account of belief-spaces over an uncountable range. Each gets a countable model where externally the continuous or uncountable structure is actually discrete and countable.

(8) Therefore every theory that distinguishes sizes is self-undermining: it proves distinctions that vanish the moment you embed it in a countable model.

(9) That can't be right — maths and physics work fine. So one premise must be wrong, but I can't see which. Is it (4), because the outside enumeration isn't a real set? Is it (5), because internal and external notions of bijection don't compete? Or is the mistake elsewhere?

Please tell me exactly which step fails, why it looked convincing, and what giving it up costs us — particulary whether we have to accept that mathematical truth is relative to a model, or whether we can keep objectivity and just limit first-order logic's reach.
