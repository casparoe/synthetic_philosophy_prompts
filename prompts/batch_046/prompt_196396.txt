Two colleagues, neither of whom is me, argued over coffee about Berkeley's master argument. (1) The first said the argument is decisive: to conceive of a tree existing without being perceived is already to perceive the conception, so unperceived matter is literally unthinkable and therefore impossible. (2) The second replied that this mistakes the vehicle of thought for its content; we think about distant galaxies or the inside of a closed book through abstract structural descriptions without direct sensory presence, so conceivability clearly outstrips current perception. (3) The first then offered a refined case: consider a computer-vision pipeline that stores a high-dimensional latent encoding of a room—structured representational data that is never rendered to a screen or observed by any conscious subject. Engineers treat this encoding as representing an unobserved scene, yet if Berkeley is right, treating it as an unperceived object should be incoherent. Since the design is perfectly coherent, the master argument fails for representational states that lack phenomenal presence. (4) The second countered that the encoding is not genuinely unperceived: the system's own read-write operations access it internally, and Berkeley's ontology already accommodates ideas in non-human minds and in God. Thus the encoding is either an idea in some mind or meaningless syntax; it does not escape Berkeley's framework but merely relocates the perceiver. The argument therefore confirms, rather than undermines, that representation requires a representer.
