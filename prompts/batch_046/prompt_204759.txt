At a recent roundtable on well-being and artificial minds—an event I was covering for a feature on how philosophers are rethinking value in an age of synthetic cognition—two thinkers, Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen, became locked in a sustained disagreement over what they termed tranquilism: the thesis that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, while positive states such as joy, love, or creative achievement possess at best instrumental weight. Vasquez defended the position; Chen pressed it. Their exchange unfolded in three distinct rounds.

In the first round, Vasquez proposed that the ethically perfect life is not one of flourishing but of sustained lucidity without desire or distress—something closer to deep meditative absorption than to conventional happiness. Chen objected that on this account a human in such a state is morally indistinguishable from a rock or a comatose patient, since all three lack craving and suffering. Vasquez replied that the critical difference is the presence of consciousness without suffering—what she called bare awareness—which is sufficient to avoid collapse, and that this does not require assigning any positive value to pleasure, meaning, or achievement.

In the second round, Chen argued that Vasquez was smuggling intrinsic value back into consciousness itself: if bare awareness is worth preserving, then conscious existence has non-instrumental value, which immediately opens the door to valuing the positive states that typically accompany it. He then introduced a concrete case drawn from current machine-learning architecture: imagine an artificial predictive-processing system engineered with zero reward signals and zero aversion signals—pure homeostatic equilibrium, no affect whatsoever. If tranquilism is correct, Chen said, such a system, lacking both suffering and craving, should have exactly the same moral status as Vasquez’s enlightened meditator. Vasquez accepted this implication, insisting that once suffering and craving are eliminated, there is no further ethical demand to inject joy, ambition, or attachment into either biological or synthetic minds.

In the third round, Chen pressed that this acceptance carries radical consequences. If only absence matters and conscious existence is valued solely negatively, then the best state of affairs is one with no sentient beings at all—strict anti-natalism—since non-existence entails no suffering and costs nothing in terms of lost goods. Vasquez agreed that anti-natalism follows from her view, but Chen pointed out that she relies on an asymmetry: she treats the absence of suffering in a non-existent being as ethically favorable (or at least not a loss), yet denies that the absence of joy in such a being counts against non-existence. Vasquez responded that suffering is positively bad in a way that joy is not positively good—joy, she claimed, is merely the temporary cessation of craving experienced as pleasure—so there is no parallel obligation to preserve it. Chen countered that this is not an independent argument for the asymmetry but a restatement of tranquilism itself, leaving the view circular when used to defend its own most extreme implications.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. For each of the three rounds, indicate whose move is stronger, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether Vasquez has an independent defense of the asymmetry that does not presuppose her conclusion, and whether Chen’s artificial-system case genuinely tests tranquilism or merely reveals that the view needs an explicit theory of consciousness to avoid counterintuitive results. Keep your analysis focused on the arguments exactly as made; do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
