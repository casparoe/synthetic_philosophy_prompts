I've been playing with backward induction and the centipede game, and I think I've pushed it into a place where it proves too much. (1) In the centipede game, rational players should stop immediately because they foresee defection at the final node. (2) The same reasoning works for any finite cooperative chain where everyone knows everyone else is rational. (3) So in real-world cases like multi-year research collabrations or open-source maintainer chains - where there's no binding contract and anyone can walk away - rational agents should never start, since they'd predict the other side will deffect at the last useful step. (4) But people obviously do start these things and they work out okay. (5) Therefore either backward induction is not a general rationalty principle, or all that cooperation is systematically irrational. (6) I don't want to bite the bullet on (5), so I'm trying to reject (2). But (2) looks like just applying the same logic to a different domain. Where exactly does it go wrong? Is there a hidden assumption about common knowledge of rationality that fails outside the lab? Or is there a difference between 'defect' in a game and 'walk away' in real life that saves (2) but makes backward induction less scary? Which step should I drop, and what's the cost?
