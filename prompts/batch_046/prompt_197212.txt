Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on the collapse of moral norms such as slavery and dueling: Camp A, the interest-and-institutional camp (holding that these norms dissolve primarily when material incentives and enforcement structures change, with public moral rhetoric functioning largely as ex-post rationalization, in line with Bernard Williams's skepticism that rational argument drives large-scale moral change); and Camp B, the honor-reconfiguration camp (holding, in the spirit of Kwame Anthony Appiah's analysis, that norms collapse when adherence becomes incompatible with the honor code of dominant groups, so that continued practice brings shame rather than status).

The statement must be written in American English, using formal academic prose. It must not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.

Structure the document as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims (at least six) that both representatives can sign. These should cover: the conceptual distinction between normative extinction (cessation of practice) and normative delegitimization (widespread moral condemnation); the historical timing of abolition and dueling's decline relative to legal prohibition and economic restructuring; the inadequacy of purely philosophical argument to explain rapid normative shifts; the existence of episodes of double-bookkeeping, in which elites privately maintain a practice while publicly denouncing it; the observation that some norms disappear quickly while others linger for generations despite similar legal and economic conditions; and the need for an explanation that can account for both the speed of collapse in some cases and the long normative lag in others.

2. A disagreements table with three columns — Issue, Camp A formulation (worded so Camp A accepts it), Camp B formulation (worded so Camp B accepts it) — covering at least: whether honor-language tracks or drives institutional change; whether honor-shifts can occur independently of material-interest shifts and causally precede them; each camp's diagnosis of the central error in the opposing view; and whether the phenomenon of normative lag — the interval between a practice becoming costly or shameful and its actual disappearance — supports one mechanism over the other.

3. A cruxes section identifying three sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify the empirical or conceptual test both sides agree is relevant; Camp A's predicted finding; and Camp B's predicted finding. Include at least one crux that hinges on a specific, non-obvious historical comparison — for instance, whether the rapid post-1865 decline of dueling in the American South correlates more closely with the destruction of planter-class economic autonomy or with a reconfiguration of Southern honor codes that made dueling appear cowardly rather than courageous; and whether the British Slave Trade Act of 1807 preceded or followed the economic conditions that would have made the trade unprofitable, and what that temporal ordering implies about causation.

4. A brief concluding paragraph stating whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive, identifying which portion and explaining why.

Inject into the collaboration the conceptual tool of normative lag, defined above, and ask both camps to specify in advance what pattern of lag they would expect under their own account and under the opponent's.
