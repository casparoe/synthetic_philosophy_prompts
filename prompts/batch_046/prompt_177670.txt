I'm serving on the advisory board for the Borges Foundation's digital archive initative, and we have to vote by Thursday on a grant proposal called the Circular Archive. The idea is to digitize Borges's late notebooks and organize them not by date but as a branching, hyperlinked network where every entry connects to every other, modeled on his view that linear time is mostly an illusion of succession and that past and present can be treated as co-present in some metaphysical sense. The tech team says this is the only curatorial approach that takes Borges seriously; our head archivist says it's anachronistic imposition that erases the actual labor of writing and makes it impossible for scholars to trace how his ideas developed in real historical time.

I need you to act like a senior philosopher of history with some archive ethics background, and please think step by step before you write. First, brainstorm for a moment: what's the real philosophical tension between curating for "philosophical resonance" versus curating for empirical fidelity, and is there a version of curatorial eternalism that doesn't force the Foundation to take a metaphysical stance on time itself? Then draft the board's decision memo — it should recommend approve with conditions, reject, or approve outright, with a concrete implementation timeline (say, within the next funding cycle). The memo needs to take the archivists' objection seriously: explain whether arranging texts as simultaneous commits us to endorsing eternalism about time in general, or whether we can bracket that claim and still justify the structure. Include at least one specific condition that protects linear scholarly access, and make sure the reasoning gives both sides something they could actualy respond to rather than just restating our mission. Start by outlining the strongest argument for each side, then move to the recommendation.
