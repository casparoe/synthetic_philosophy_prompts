As a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun serious study of philosophy—currently auditing a graduate seminar on medieval Islamic philosophy and meeting monthly with an informal reading group of scientists turned humanists—I have become preoccupied with Avicenna’s flying man argument in a way that the standard commentaries do not seem to address. The argument, as I understand it, asks us to imagine an adult human created fully formed and suspended in a void, with no sensory contact whatsoever, and claims that such a being would nonetheless be immediately aware of his own existence, though unaware of any bodily parts or spatial position. The usual interpretation treats this as a proof of the soul’s substantial independence from the body: self-awareness is intrinsic and requires no corporeal mediation.

What strikes me, however, is a structural parallel that the philosophical literature largely ignores. In classical physics, we learned to distinguish measurements that depend on an observer’s reference frame from quantities that remain invariant across frames—what we now call gauge-invariant observables. Avicenna seems to treat first-person existence as precisely such an invariant: it persists regardless of the frame constituted by bodily sensation or spatial embedding, whereas all bodily properties are frame-dependent and therefore absent. If this analogy is sound, then the flying man argument may not simply demonstrate an immaterial soul; it may tacitly presuppose a particular model of self-reference—one that treats the first-person subject as a coordinate-independent datum. And if modern physics and philosophy of science have shown that apparently frame-independent quantities often conceal hidden relational structures, then the argument’s apparent simplicity might dissolve upon closer inspection.

I need the proposal to state the precise research question: does Avicenna’s argument for the soul’s immateriality depend on an implicit analogy between first-person awareness and frame-independent physical measurement, and can that analogy be used either to defend or to undermine the argument’s conclusion? I also want you to explain why the existing interpretations—whether in the Aristotelian commentary tradition, in modern analytic philosophy of mind, or in comparative studies of self-consciousness—leave this specific question open, rather than resolving it. The proposal should outline the actual method: what I would do in each of the three months, what texts or conceptual tools I would examine, and which alternative approaches I am deliberately not taking (for instance, I am not proposing an empirical study of sensory deprivation, nor a full historical reconstruction of Avicenna’s physics). It should specify what a successful result would look like—perhaps a reconstructed version of the argument that either exposes its hidden physicalist commitments or shows that the frame-invariance analogy fails—and what would count as the project failing, such as discovering that the analogy is merely metaphorical and yields no philosophical leverage.
