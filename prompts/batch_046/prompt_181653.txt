I am preparing a feature on rationality and what some call "epistemic restraint," and a science writer I interviewed offered this gloss of I.J. Good's result on the value of information: "Good showed that, for any decision problem, a rational agent should always prefer to observe any available evidence before acting, because the expected utility of an observe-then-choose strategy is strictly greater than that of choosing blindly. The proof assumes nothing about the content of the evidence, so it applies equally to information about past events, future outcomes, or causally irrelevant states. Consequently, 'strategic ignorance'—deliberately avoiding information to protect one's preferences or commitments—is not merely imprudent but formally irrational, since more knowledge can never reduce the maximum expected utility of a decision." The writer draws from this that ethical arguments for "the right not to know" in medical or personal contexts are undermined by decision theory. Before I quote this in my piece, I want an accuracy audit. Which elements correctly represent Good's result, which conflate it with broader claims about knowledge and rationality, and where specifically does the inference to "strategic ignorance is formally irrational" break down? In particular, does the theorem require that the information be actionable, costless, or pre-decisional, and are those conditions met in the ethical cases the writer invokes?
