okay so i just got dragged onto this participatory budgeting committee in my town (long story, don't ask) and we're rewriting the charter for who gets to vote on local infrastructure spending. the town has tons of seasonal farmworkers with temporary visas, a big refugee resettlement center, and remote property owners who live elsewhere but pay taxes here. naturally i went down the democratic theory rabbit hole and now i'm stuck on the boundary problem — who actually belongs to the demos?

i've been reading the usual camps: the all-subjected crowd says if you're under the authority of a decision you should have a seat; the all-affected-interests people say anyone whose welfare gets impacted gets included; and the stakeholder folks tie membership to long-term reciprocal obligation or some kind of sustained stake. but in every seminar i find, the debate just loops. the subjected side points to coercion and autonomy; the affected side points to moral equality of interests; the stakeholders talk about democratic reciprocity. nobody moves, and the arguments all feel like paralell tracks that never intersect.

i started wondering if the stall is actually verbal — like we're all using "the demos" to mean different things: one group asking who should have legal authority, another asking whose interests need representation, and a third asking who can be held accountable for outcomes. or maybe it's substantive but hidden behind an empirical disagreement about whether temporary workers are actually "subject to" local zoning law in the same way citizens are, or whether remote owners have "interests" that are affected in a meaningful sense. there's also that classic recursive problem — defining the demos seems to require a prior demos to decide — and i can't tell if that's a formal dead end or just a red herring.

but i also cooked up this niche distinction that i can't stop thinking about: "direct jurisdictional exposure" versus "systemic spillover exposure." direct exposure is when the decision literally regulates your behavior — like if you're a resident and zoning applies to your house. systemic spillover is when the decision reshapes the environment you depend on but doesn't directly govern you — like a downstream community hit by a water project, or maybe even future generations represented by some climate proxy. i think this might cut through the deadlock, but i'm not sure if it's doing real work or just renaming the same old camps.

so what's the real structure of this disagreement? is it verbal, with each camp answering a different membership question under one heading? is there a hidden formal issue — maybe the boundary can't be non-circular — making the whole thing ill-posed? or are we stuck at bedrock intuitions about belonging and naturalness that nobody can argue past? and if your diagnosis is right, what would actually move it? a formal result? an empirical study about how temporary residents experience coercion? or should i accept that my direct-vs-systemic distinction is the progress we need and build the charter around it?

also — and this might be a long shot — do you think the stall is partly because the literature treats the boundary problem as a one-time constitutional question, when in practice it's a continuous governance problem? like, maybe we're asking "who is in the demos?" when we should be asking "how does membership shift as the decision shifts?" i feel like that reframing might dissolve some of the impasse, but i can't tell if i'm onto something or just tired and overthinking this because my committee meeting is tuesday. definatly let me know if my distinction is useless.
