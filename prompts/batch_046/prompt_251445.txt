Here is an argument I built for a seminar and now find suspicious because it ties the lottery paradox and the preface paradox together in a way that threatens to dissolve both at once. (1) Consider a fair lottery with N > 1/t tickets, where belief requires probability exceeding threshold t. For each ticket i, the probability it loses exceeds t, so I should believe ticket i will lose. (2) If rational belief is closed under conjunction, I should also believe the conjunction that all tickets lose, contradicting the rational belief—required by fairness—that exactly one ticket wins. (3) The standard resolution denies closure: I may rationally believe each individual loss without believing the universal conjunction, avoiding contradiction. (4) Now apply the same structure to the preface: an author rationally believes each specific claim P1 through Pn in her book, and also rationally believes—given ordinary human fallibility—that at least one Pi is false. (5) If closure is denied, she can consistently hold every Pi and also hold that not all Pi hold, with no contradiction arising. (6) But apply this denial back to the lottery: if closure is unavailable, I may consistently believe each specific ticket loses and also believe some ticket wins, removing the tension entirely. (7) Conversely, if we preserve the lottery paradox by retaining closure and solving it instead through a raised threshold or a non-aggregative belief rule, we must apply that same uniform policy to the preface case. (8) Doing so forces the author to treat her preface admission as irrational—she must either abandon all specific beliefs or deny her own fallibility—both of which seem wrong. (9) So any domain-general policy for rational belief in large sets either eliminates the lottery problem or eliminates the preface problem. (10) Yet both seem independently puzzling. (11) Therefore either rational belief is impossible across large domains, or there is an asymmetry between statistical lottery beliefs and inductive preface beliefs that my argument has missed. I would rather preserve the intuitive force of both paradoxes than abandon either, but if I must choose: where is the hidden asymmetry, which step should be rejected, and what does giving it up cost?
