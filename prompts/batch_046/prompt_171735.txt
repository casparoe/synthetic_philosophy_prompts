I have recently been appointed an independent trustee of a family charitable foundation, and the experience of making long-term commitments to beneficiaries whose future circumstances I cannot predict has made the philosophical question of why promises bind personally urgent rather than merely abstract. I am not seeking a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want a defended agenda that can guide practical judgement.

Standard accounts of promising tend to treat the relation as dyadic: a specific promisor, a specific promisee, and a directed obligation to perform some particular act. Yet in fiduciary roles I have become aware of a tension that these accounts rarely disentangle with sufficient clarity. There is the directed obligation — the explicit pledge made to an identifiable beneficiary — and there is what I shall call institutional residue: a second-order normative pressure not to act in ways that erode the background conditions of credible commitment-making within the institution itself, even when no individual beneficiary can plausibly claim to have been wronged by the particular act. These two forces can come sharply apart. I might honour every explicit pledge to current beneficiaries while restructuring the foundation in a manner that renders future trustees' promises systematically incredible, thereby damaging something that is not reducible to the sum of individual directed duties.

With this framing in mind, which open questions about the nature of promissory binding — and about institutional or fiduciary commitment more broadly — matter most for someone in my position? Please fix the audience (a practitioner with limited time, not a career academic) and the end (better judgement about when to hold firm, when to renegotiate in good faith, and when the preservation of institutional credibility ought to outweigh the performance of a specific pledge). Prioritise the questions, defend the ordering, and make explicit the criteria you are using — importance, tractability with conceptual rather than empirical methods, and the extent to which other questions depend on resolving this one. I also want to know what kind of practical case or conceptual clarification over the next few years would cause you to reorder them.

Furthermore, please rank the main alleged implications of taking promissory binding seriously for trusteeship and institutional ethics, distinguishing those that genuinely follow from those that are overrated or depend on a confusion. In particular: does bindingness necessarily entail that changed circumstances never release the promisor? Does it generate enforceable rights for third parties who were never the original promisees? Is institutional residue genuinely a distinct normative source, or is it only an aggregate of directed obligations? For each, explain whether accepting it would actually change my practice, and identify which implications rest on conflating moral obligation with legal enforceability.

Treat the deliverable as agenda-setting rather than exposition: I need to know where limited intellectual effort should be directed and why, not a bibliography or a historical overview.
