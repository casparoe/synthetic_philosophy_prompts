I'm closing a graduate seminar on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia—the specific deadlock that arises when your first-order judgment (for instance, that a long deductive proof is valid) is undermined not by a discovered error in the proof, but by a second-order belief about your own condition (that you are severely sleep-deprived and have a documented tendency to miss hidden premises in exactly this kind of formal reasoning). The discussion kept circling the same structural problem: the higher-order doubt doesn't identify a flaw in the argument, yet it seems irrational to simply ignore it, and neither level yields to the other. To end the session, I'd like a villanelle—strictly nineteen lines, with two refrains in the standard A1 b A2 rhyme scheme—written from the voice of the reasoner caught in that loop. One refrain should carry the first-order conviction, something like "The chain holds firm; each step is right," and the other should carry the higher-order suspicion, something like "But tired minds have failed before tonight." The poem should make the philosophical work happen through its form: the refrains must keep returning, alternating dominance, and finally meet in the closing couplet without resolving the tension into either pure confidence or pure skepticism. Please keep it serious and polished, accurate enough that someone familiar with the debate recognizes the logical structure rather than any named author, and avoid all references to specific papers, philosophers, or bibliographies—this is meant to be read aloud in a room with no screens.
