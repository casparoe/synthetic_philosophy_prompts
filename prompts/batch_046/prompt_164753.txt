I sit on my university's Committee for Archival Ontology and Epistemic Classification, and I need to circulate a formal recommendation memo before our session next Tuesday. The concrete case concerns what we have provisionally named the Restored Resonance Collection: a group of reconstructed hollow-body acoustic resonators from the early medieval period, recovered in fragments, whose original wood species, internal bracing geometries, and precise cavity dimensions are now permanently inaccessible. Non-destructive imaging and comparative analysis allow us to propose probable reconstructions, but the original manufacturing parameters and material constitutions are irrecoverable, and our cataloging protocol requires that every entry carry a stable ontological identifier because that identifier governs preservation priority, research-access tier, and attribution of cultural origin in all downstream institutional partnerships.

Last night I had a long and fairly intense argument with a close friend who works in conservation science, and it crystallized a split that has been forming among us. One faction holds that we should identify each object strictly by its reconstructed, measurable properties—the position one might call ontological actualism about artifacts—because those are the only properties we can verify and defend in peer review. Another faction, closer to my friend's view, maintains that identification must track the original, now-inaccessible manufacturing intentions and material constitutions, treating the reconstructed instruments as derivative entities with a different ontological status altogether; on this view, assigning them the same identifier as the originals risks what she called epistemic laundering in material-science claims. There is also a minority proposal that has received little formal attention: a branching-ontology framework in which the catalog maintains a primary practical identifier tied to the reconstructed object, paired with a formal shadow entry for the inaccessible original, the two linked by a dependency relation that can be revised should new archaeological evidence emerge.

I need the memo to recommend one of these three positions, or a workable modification, with explicit criteria the committee can apply when evaluating future acquisitions of a similar kind. It should specify a concrete course of action—including a timeline for revising our catalog schema and notifying partner institutions—and it must treat the strongest consideration on the losing side with real intellectual honesty rather than dismissive reassurance. In particular, please engage seriously with the worry that collapsing reconstructed and original objects into a single ontological entry will corrupt downstream research that relies on our catalog for claims about medieval material culture, and with the counter-worry that multiplying entries for every epistemically opaque artifact will render our database administratively useless and conceptually unwieldy.
