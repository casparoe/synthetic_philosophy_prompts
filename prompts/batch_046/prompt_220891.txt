I've been thinking about lifeboat ethics — the kind of reasoning people use when resources are so scarce that keeping everyone alive means everyone dies, like deciding who gets into the last boat or whether to share food when there's not enough for all. I recently read Yann Martel's Life of Pi, and I'm wondering whether the novel actually challenges that logic rather than just illustrating it.

The story has Pi Patel alone on a lifeboat in the Pacific with a Bengal tiger, Richard Parker, after a shipwreck that kills his family and other survivors. At first Pi considers killing the tiger to secure space and supplies. Instead he feeds the tiger, establishes daily routines with him, and eventually saves the tiger's life when they reach Mexico — even though the tiger immediately vanishes into the jungle with no acknowledgment or reciprocity. Meanwhile, earlier in the boat, Pi kills the cook who murdered Pi's mother and a sailor.

Read the novel through the lens of lifeboat ethics, but with a specific twist: don't just ask whether Pi should have killed the tiger to survive. Ask whether the novel suggests that in absolute confinement, moral obligations don't shrink as resources shrink, but instead expand toward the dangerous, vulnerable other. How does Pi's relationship with Richard Parker compare to his relationships with the human survivors? Does the novel imply that standard lifeboat reasoning — treating survival as a zero-sum calculation — misses something essential about dependency, recognition, and shared space?

I'm writing an essay for a general audience and want to avoid reducing the book to an example or distorting the ethics to match the plot. Does Life of Pi ultimately vindicate a stricter lifeboat view, or does it show that the framework misses what it actually means to survive with another being?
