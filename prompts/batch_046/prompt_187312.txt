student answer (about 350 words):

Gradient illusionism does avoid the immediacy problem that kills uniform illusionism. In the uniform view, if phenomenal properties are just fake reports, then it's weird that they feel so direct and undeniable—like there's nothing between me and the redness. But in the gradient view, the monitoring system actually is tracking something real: its own representational state. That tracking is immediate in the normal sense—it's direct self-monitoring—so the immediacy objection dissolves. The misassignment part just explains why we think the raw sensory state has the feel, when actually the feel belongs to the monitoring layer.

However, this is basically just the higher-order thought theory of consciousness, which Rosenthal and others developed. So gradient illusionism isn't really illusionism at all; it's HOT theory with a fancy name. The illusion is only in the projection downward, but the core phenomenal reality is saved in the higher-order state.

As for the new problem about explaining monitoring: it doesn't arise, because the monitoring is a physical brain process just like any other. Once you explain how the brain represents its own states—maybe through global workspace integration—you've explained everything. There's no hard problem left because there's no mysterious phenomenal property sitting out there needing explanation. The projection error is just a misattribution, not a metaphysical mystery.

So yes, gradient illusionism solves the immediacy issue and avoids creating any new explanatory burden.
