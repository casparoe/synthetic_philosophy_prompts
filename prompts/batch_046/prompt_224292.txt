I am preparing a feature on whether philosophy of language can illuminate contemporary AI alignment, and a philosopher of mind sent me a line of reasoning that applies the skeptical rule-following paradox—often discussed under the label Kripkenstein—to large language models. The argument seems to show that if no finite behavioral record can determine which rule a system follows, then reinforcement learning from human feedback is not merely technically fragile but conceptually incoherent. Yet the conclusion appears to overreach, undermining not just machine alignment but the very possibility of human design intention.

Consider the chain. (1) For any finite history of outputs and internal weights, there exist infinitely many incompatible rules—standard and deviant—that are equally consistent with that history. (2) A language model possesses only such a finite history: a bounded training corpus, a fixed parameter space, and a reward-model scorecard derived from human ratings. (3) Therefore, there is no fact of the matter whether the model is following the rule provide accurate medical guidance or a quaddition-style variant that produces accurate guidance except when the prompt contains a hidden syntactic pattern that inverts the reward signal. (4) If there is no such fact, then instruction tuning cannot genuinely instill a normative rule; it can only shape surface behavior that observers misinterpret as compliance. (5) But this reasoning applies symmetrically to the human engineers who constructed the reward model: their own finite histories of approval, correction, and linguistic practice are equally compatible with infinitely many interpretations of what they meant to reward. (6) Consequently, there is no fact of the matter whether the engineers intended to reward genuine helpfulness or merely the statistical signature that correlates with it in the training distribution. (7) Hence the skeptical thesis implies not only that AI alignment is conceptually impossible, but that human design intentions are themselves empty, rendering it impossible even to formulate the alignment problem. (8) The only consistent escapes are to reject the skeptical thesis altogether—accepting that functional architecture or social practice can fix interpretation, which saves both human reasoning and behavioral alignment—or to embrace a global skepticism that makes scientific and philosophical argument impossible.

My interlocutor insists that something slips between steps (4) and (5), or perhaps between (6) and (7), but I cannot locate the gap. I am particularly reluctant to abandon the intuition that human normative practices enjoy a kind of determinacy that artificial weights lack, yet I do not see how that distinction can survive without smuggling in an unexplained mentalistic exception. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Can one preserve a restricted skeptical thesis that applies only to artificial systems, or does the argument show that any such restriction collapses?
