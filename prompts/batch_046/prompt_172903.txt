As a software engineer without formal philosophical training, I have become increasingly puzzled by a standard institutional practice in technology hiring that seems to carry unstated commitments about the nature of mind and knowledge. During technical interviews, candidates are typically required to solve algorithmic problems on a whiteboard or in a minimal text editor, explicitly denied access to documentation, search engines, integrated development environments, version control, or collaborative tools—resources that are not incidental but constitutive of actual software engineering work. The stated rationale is that the interviewer wants to see "how the candidate thinks" in isolation, implying that genuine cognitive competence must be demonstrable without environmental scaffolding and that external aids would contaminate the assessment of true understanding. This framing appears to presuppose that the boundaries of cognition coincide with the individual skull or unaided biological memory, a view that sits in tension with the extended mind thesis—associated with philosophers such as Andy Clark and David Chalmers—according to which mental processes can genuinely incorporate external artifacts and social structures.

I am asking you to reconstruct the implicit theory of knowledge and cognitive authority that this hiring practice presupposes: does it strictly entail an internalist, individualist philosophy of mind, or can it be reconciled with extended or distributed cognition if we carefully separate conditions of performance from conditions of underlying competence? Please distinguish what the practice genuinely requires from what it merely happens to fit well with, evaluate whether its different parts (for instance, valuing tool-mediated collaboration in production while forbidding it in evaluation) presuppose consistent philosophical commitments, and describe what an interview process redesigned around an extended-cognition framework would actually need to assess—without supplying bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, since I want the argument constructed from first principles rather than from scholarly reference.
