Exam question:
Chase and Sanborn argue that the concept of qualia is incoherent because it requires properties—intrinsicness, non-relationality, and ineffability—that cannot be jointly satisfied. Evaluate whether this incoherence can be dissolved by appealing to "structural transparency": the thesis that phenomenal character is not an extra feature appended to representational content, but is fully constituted by the organizational properties of that content. In doing so, address the "modal redundancy" concern: if two experiences can share every structural property yet differ phenomenally (as in standard inverted-spectrum scenarios), then phenomenal properties appear either redundant with respect to structure or inexplicable within a structural framework. Does this concern revive Chase and Sanborn's charge, or can it be neutralized without abandoning qualia altogether?

Student answer:
Chase and Sanborn maintain that qualia are incoherent because they must be intrinsic, non-relational, and ineffable. A property that is intrinsic and non-relational cannot depend on anything outside itself, including other mental states or worldly relations. Ineffability then follows naturally, since if no relations link a quale to anything communicable, it remains private.

The structural-transparency proposal attempts to preserve qualia by identifying phenomenal character with the organizational features of representational content—say, the contrastive relations among color representations—rather than with an added "feel." This seems to avoid incoherence, but it does so only by sacrificing non-relationality, because organizational properties are inherently relational. Hence the structural-transparency theorist must concede that phenomenal properties are not non-relational after all.

This concession activates the modal-redundancy problem. In an inverted-spectrum case, the organizational structure of experience is preserved while the phenomenal character is swapped. That demonstrates that phenomenal properties are redundant to structural properties: the structure is present, yet the phenomenology is absent or altered, so structure already does all the explanatory work and qualia become superfluous. Consequently, Chase and Sanborn's incoherence charge is vindicated. Either one accepts that qualia do not exist (eliminativism), or one accepts that phenomenal properties are nothing over and above representational structure (representationalism). There is no stable intermediate position.
