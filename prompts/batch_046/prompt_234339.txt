i'm on a county health ethics board and we're constantly getting proposals that move harms and benefits around between different people -- like cutting mental health budgets to fund elder flu shots, or forcing nurses into overtime to protect immunocompromised wards. I want a reusable decision procedure my team can run to check if we're falling into the seperateness of persons problem that critics of utilitarianism point out -- you know, treating different people like they're just buckets in one shared pool of welfare.

Please write the procedure as a checklist: ordered questions, what counts as a yes/no/maybe, where it hard-stops with a rejection, and where it has to hand off to a human because it's values not facts. It needs to distinguish intrapersonal tradeoffs (same person over time) from interpersonal pooling. Then run it on three cases:
1. Shifting 30% of mental health funding to elder flu vaccines.
2. Mandatory double shifts for nurses to shield high-risk patients.
3. Paying healthy volunteers to take an experimental risky vaccine so vulnerable people avoid exposure entirely.

One of these should come out with an answer that's going to annoy the board. Also flag exactly where the procedure runs out of road and can't substitute for actual judgment, especially around "offsetting" one harm with a different benefit. Keep it practical -- we have about 15 minutes per proposal and no philosophy PhDs in the room. And if there's a weird edge case where the procedure says "technically ok" but it still feels like we're using one group as a resource for another, call that out explicitly.
