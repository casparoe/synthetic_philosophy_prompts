A common analogy in debates over the Great Man theory of history compares an indispensable leader to the keystone of a masonry arch: the arch is assembled from many stones—structural conditions, institutions, ideologies—and held together by mortar—bureaucratic habit, economic interest, cultural resentment—but the keystone is the single wedge-shaped block that converts downward weight into lateral stability, and if it is removed the arch collapses. Proponents of a middle view use this image to argue that without Hitler, interwar Germany might have produced some form of authoritarian nationalism—the arch could still be built—but the specific configuration of total war, state-directed genocide, and the Führerprinzip would have collapsed, leaving only diffuse, substitutable authoritarianism. Can you lay out the explicit correspondence table, mapping keystone, voussoirs, mortar, abutments, downward load, and architect onto the political and social elements of 1930s Germany; identify precisely where the architectural properties fail to carry over, such as whether a keystone is engineered to be irreplaceable by design whereas political succession is a built-in feature of regimes, whether an arch collapses instantly rather than undergoing gradual deformation, and whether the analogy silently imports an intentional designer that structural explanations reject; distinguish the disanalogies that genuinely weaken the middle-position conclusion from those that are merely ornamental; and assess whether the conclusion—that Nazi Germany as actually constituted required Hitler specifically—survives once those failures are corrected, or whether a different analogy, drawn perhaps from chemical catalysis, ecological succession, or distributed information networks, would do better justice to the interaction between contingency and structure?
