My opening move challenges the refined position I just described. I want to press you on what I am calling the scale-invariance trap. Every grand narrative, including those revised to absorb Graeber-Wengrow’s critique, seems to depend on identifying causal mechanisms—agricultural surplus generating hierarchy, or cognitive revolutions generating symbolic culture—that are claimed to operate similarly across radically different social scales, from band-level foragers to continental empires. If the mechanism is genuinely scale-invariant, then the narrative should predict comparable outcomes wherever it appears, which it manifestly does not; if it is not scale-invariant, then the macro-pattern dissolves into local contingencies that the narrative was meant to explain away. I claim this is structural, not accidental: any narrative long enough to be grand must smuggle in scale-invariance to bridge its gaps, and any narrative honest enough to reject it collapses into anti-narrative particularism. Do not simply deny that particular narratives have fallen into this trap. Show me how your refined grand-narrative position avoids it without merely renaming the problem.
