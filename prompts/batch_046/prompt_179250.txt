I am preparing material for an interdisciplinary seminar that pairs formal decision theory with literary form, and I need a short story that makes the St. Petersburg paradox feel like an unavoidable social reality rather than a classroom curiosity. Set the narrative in the floating archipelago of Novgorod, where the economy runs not on currency but on lifespan contracts that replicate the classic gamble: each successive coin-flip doubles the years gained, producing an infinite expected value, yet every resident instinctively caps their wager at a few months of life. To move beyond a generic illustration, please invent and embed within the world a specific local institution—the Covenant of Saturated Utility—by which citizens who have entered too many contracts become hollow traders: biologically alive and aware, but legally stripped of all future years they could stake, wandering the docks with a strange, quiet contentment.

Write a restrained, literary story about Elara, one such hollow trader, and her encounter with a young mathematician who argues that rationality demands she sell whatever remains—her present, her memories, her very capacity to choose—for one final contract whose expectation is unbounded. Through their interaction, show rather than explain why the paradox persists in human psychology: the curvature of lived utility, the weight of risk aversion, and the particular serenity of having nothing left to lose should all resist the mathematician's calculus. End with Elara making a concrete, embodied choice that dramatizes the gap between expected value and what a life can actually bear. Do not include citations, footnotes, named philosophical treatises, or bibliographic references; the philosophical ideas should operate as atmospheric and structural forces within the fiction itself.
