In my course on twentieth-century political philosophy, we have been working through Simone Weil’s essay on the abolition of all political parties, and ever since then a particular phrase has been circulating in my study group as a kind of conversational stopper: “parties are a necessary evil.” Whenever someone raises a reformist objection—say, that parties could be democratized, made more transparent, or constrained by law—the response is that yes, parties are corrupt, but they are a necessary evil, as if that single clause automatically neutralizes Weil’s abolitionism. I am skeptical that the phrase is doing any precise philosophical work, and I want to take it apart rather than accept it as a slogan.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that “parties are a necessary evil” might actually be expressing? I am especially interested in readings that engage Weil’s specific arguments rather than generic cynicism about politics: for instance, the claim that parties are instrumentally indispensable for democratic representation despite being morally corrosive; the claim that they necessarily destroy the capacity for “attention” in Weil’s technical sense, turning receptive, non-possessive judgment into collective possession and thereby making justice structurally impossible; the claim that human social psychology makes party affiliation unavoidable, even if it systematically transforms the desire for justice into a machinery of domination; or the claim that the evil of parties is a quantifiable cost that democratic goods reliably outweigh. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial or substantive, whether it is defensible on its own terms, whether it actually conflicts with Weil’s specific analysis of how parties manufacture collective passions, and—crucially—whether accepting that reading allows a reformist to reply to her without simply begging the question. I want a menu of disambiguations I can bring back to the group, not a single interpretation, and I want to know which of these readings, if any, could be held consistently with the idea that parties do not merely misdirect justice but structurally convert it into something else.
