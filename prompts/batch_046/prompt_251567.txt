I have recently found myself in a position that began as a practical decision but has grown into a philosophical puzzle. For nearly a decade I have cultivated an elaborate but now increasingly unprofitable intellectual project—part research orientation, part personal worldview—and the available evidence strongly suggests that abandoning it for a leaner, more productive framework would yield far greater results going forward. The conventional counsel is straightforward: sunk costs carry no rational weight, and I ought to switch without regret. Yet my resistance feels different from mere stubbornness; the years already spent seem to matter not as wasted resources to be mourned, but as part of what makes the present commitment genuinely mine. I tried to formalize this intuition, but the resulting argument now strikes me as proving far too much.

(1) Rational decision-making ought to depend only on the expected future consequences of the actions now available, not on resources already expended; otherwise one could justify any wasteful persistence simply by citing prior loss.

(2) If (1) is correct, then my decade-long investment provides no rational reason in itself to continue the project; it should be ignored exactly as a rational gambler ignores prior losses.

(3) But the same reasoning applies to every sustained commitment—artistic mastery, moral character, deep friendship—where past investment creates the very conditions that make present persistence meaningful. If past expenditure never counts, then loyalty, perseverance, and narrative continuity are all, in principle, rational errors.

(4) One might reply that in these cases the reason to continue is not the sunk investment itself, but the future goods—trust, skill, integrity—that the accumulated capital now makes possible. Yet if a genuinely superior alternative exists—one that promises more trust, more skill, or greater integrity—then even this reply collapses back into (1), and abandonment remains required.

(5) Therefore either we accept (1) and condemn all long-term commitment as irrational whenever a more efficient path appears, or we reject (1) and allow that past expenditure can itself constitute a reason to persist.

(6) But if we reject (1), then there is no principled boundary between honoring a decade of intellectual labor and throwing good resources after a plainly failed venture; any refusal to adapt can be reframed as honoring one’s sunk narrative.

(7) So the argument forces an uncomfortable choice: either rationality demands perpetual flexibility, thereby undermining the conditions for deep goods that require sustained commitment, or rationality permits sunk-cost reasoning, thereby stripping us of any principled objection to wasteful stubbornness.

The conclusion feels wrong—surely there is a middle path—but I cannot locate the slip. Is the flaw in (4), where I conflate the value of accumulated capital with the value of the investment as such? Or is (1) the weak point, and if so, what does surrendering it cost us in decision-theoretic terms? If I must choose, I would rather preserve the idea that long-term commitments carry rational weight than preserve the idea that rationality never looks backward; but I want to know exactly what I am paying for that preference. Which step should I reject, why did it seem plausible, and what does each alternative commit me to?
