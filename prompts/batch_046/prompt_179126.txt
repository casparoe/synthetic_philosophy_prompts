I'm organizing a seminar for advanced students in moral philosophy, and we've been working through a position I'm calling strong tranquilism: the claim that only the absence of craving and suffering carries intrinsic value, that positive states such as joy, love, or intellectual fulfillment are at best instrumentally valuable as stabilizers of that absence, and that any framework treating them as independent goods commits a category error. We are pairing this with Aldous Huxley's Brave New World, and I want a philosophical reading that takes both the novel and the position seriously rather than reducing the text to a cartoon illustration. Read the World State's regime—soma, hypnopaedic conditioning, the abolition of art and religion—not merely as a failed utopia but as an institutional attempt to realize strong tranquilism. Does Huxley depict a society that has genuinely eliminated craving, or does he show that suppressing first-order craving produces a second-order dependency on the mechanisms of suppression themselves? Focus particularly on the Savage's refusal of soma and his suicide: is this a demonstration that tranquilism is psychologically impossible for beings with reflexive self-awareness, or does it reveal that the State never achieved true tranquility because it substituted manufactured contentment for the bare absence of disturbance? I also want you to consider whether the novel's aesthetic horror—its evident attachment to Shakespeare, to passionate attachment, to tragic choice—constitutes an internal argument against strong tranquilism, or whether the honest critic must say that Huxley is simply importing a positive theory of human flourishing that the philosophical position he dramatizes explicitly rejects. Where does the work illuminate something about the stability of purely negative value that abstract argument misses, and where does it distort the position to make it more vulnerable than it needs to be?
