Design an in-class structured role-play and deliberation for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar of about 14 students with 75 minutes available, intended to make participants feel the force of Nietzsche’s distinction among monumental, antiquarian, and critical history—specifically that each mode serves life and simultaneously harms it—without relying on prior lecture or exposition. Use a concrete but nationally obscure scenario: a town council must decide the fate of a single monument commemorating an 1892 industrial accident, now threatened by demolition for a transit development. Divide students into three groups of four or five, each assigned one mode and required to enact, not merely argue for, both its service and its specific disadvantage: the monumental group must physically alter or obscure part of a printed model or image of the monument (using markers, paper, or tape) to render it inspiring for present action, thereby experiencing the distortion and necessary forgetting monumental history demands; the antiquarian group must document every visible feature and resist any alteration, enacting the paralysis and horizon-narrowing that comes from treating the past as sacrosanct; the critical group must actively undermine the meanings the other groups have constructed, including physically tearing or covering parts of their own written commitments if those commitments have become oppressive to present life, thereby experiencing the self-destructive violence critical history does to identity. Provide explicit interaction rules that shape speech—monumental members speak only in future-oriented imperatives, antiquarian members only in origin-focused questions, critical members only in negations or exposure of hidden interests—plus a three-phase timeline: 15 minutes of embodied enactment, 30 minutes of negotiation with a forced final vote, and 20 minutes of guided reflection. Include materials (a large printed image or physical model, constraint cards, writing surfaces). The debrief should map specific actions in the room onto Nietzsche’s claims about each mode’s utility and harm for life, predict what will predictably go wrong the first time (the critical group will likely dominate through easier intellectual negation, the antiquarian group may remain passive, and the monumental group may confuse constructive alteration with mere vandalism), and specify how the facilitator should correct these imbalances (for instance, requiring the critical group to damage their own prior statements). Be explicit about which feature of Nietzsche’s view—his physiological conception of life as requiring a protective, non-historical horizon that critical history necessarily breaches—cannot be reproduced through classroom rules alone and must be supplied conceptually by the facilitator afterward. The core philosophical tension should emerge from the rules, roles, and forced choices, not from the facilitator naming it in advance.
