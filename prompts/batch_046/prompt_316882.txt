Take Cheselden's 1728 case of the boy who gained sight after cataract surgery — basically the real-world version of Molyneux's problem. I want a careful mapping, not just a retelling: what he actually reported about recogizing shapes he already knew by touch, about misjudging distance, about thinking the moon was close enough to grab. Then run it through the empiricist vs rationalist answers — Locke's side that cross-modal ideas need learning, Leibniz's side that the mind already has the geometry. Where does the case actually land? Both sides usually claim Cheselden supports them, so show me where they're misreading his notes, and whether there's a third option — maybe something about haptic scaling not transferring unless the visual system gets active exploration first — that neither Locke nor Leibniz could have framed. Finally, does his failure to instantly recognize a cat he knew by touch really damage rationalism, or is that using a wrong idea of what 'immediate recognition' was supposed to be? Keep it to what's in the reported details, not later philosophers' summaries.
