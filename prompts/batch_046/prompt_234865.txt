Me and a friend were eavesdropping on two coworkers arguing about directness over lunch. One, who's Dutch, said Japanese indirectness is basically institutionalized lying—like saying 'that might be difficult' to mean 'no,' which just hides accountability behind social ritual. The other, who grew up partly in Osaka, said that's wrong: Japanese indirectness isn't hiding the message, it's distributing it—both people already know what's being said, so spelling it out is actually redundant and can feel aggressive. Then they argued about white lies. She said telling someone their cooking is 'interesting' corrupts the information pool because you've inserted falsehood for emotional convenience; he said conversation isn't a database, it's a coordination game, and a white lie can be a cooperative move that keeps the relationship intact for actual honest talk later. She said if you allow 'cooperative falsehood,' you've lost any principled line against manipulation. He said her view assumes everyone wants raw data in every interaction, which is its own kind of cultural blindness. Can you walk through thier arguement step by step? For each move—indirectness as hiding vs distributing, white lies as corruption vs coordination—say who's closer to right and what both are ignoring. Don't just crown a winner.
