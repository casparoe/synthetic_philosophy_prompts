Here are my intuitive verdicts on cases in which an outcome is unavoidable, and I wish to know whether one principle can capture them all.

Consider a merchant who learns he is fated to meet Death in Samarra and flees Damascus to avoid him. I judge his flight rational as an attempt, not foolish, even though it was destined to fail.

Consider a patient told that her illness is one hundred per cent fatal regardless of treatment, who stops chemotherapy to spend her remaining months with family. I judge that fully rational — not a defeatist surrender to fate.

But consider a town told that global temperatures will reach two degrees Celsius by 2100 irrespective of its local emissions, which therefore abandons all mitigation. I judge that irrational, despite the fixed global trajectory.

Consider a student told by an infallible oracle that she will fail tomorrow’s examination whatever she does tonight. I judge that studying remains rational if it improves understanding, but not if its sole aim is to pass.

Consider a prisoner told that the judge will impose a ten-year sentence regardless of whether he apologises to his victims, who then refuses to apologise. I judge that he errs — the apology still holds value for the victims and for his character, even though it cannot alter the sentence.

These verdicts pull in different directions: accepting a fixed end and redirecting effort seems right for the patient, yet allowing a fixed outcome to suppress action seems wrong for the town and the prisoner. The distinction, therefore, is not merely “pursue side benefits when the primary goal is blocked”.

Please state the precise principle that best fits this pattern. For each clause, cite the case that motivates it. Say honestly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then apply it to the case I keep returning to: a pilot informed that her aircraft will crash in exactly thirty minutes regardless of any manoeuvre, who decides not to warn the passengers on the grounds that panic serves no purpose since the crash is unavoidable. I feel this decision is wrong, but I am uncertain whether the wrongness is of the same kind as the prisoner’s refusal, or of a different kind. Finally, predict what your principle says about one case I have not listed: a general who knows his army will be defeated regardless of tactics, but can choose whether to surrender immediately or fight to the last soldier.
