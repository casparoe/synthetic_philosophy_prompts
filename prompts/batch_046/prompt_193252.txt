I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on social ontology and the metaphysics of institutions, and I am co-designing a session for a graduate seminar on the evolution of norms—about fourteen students, ninety minutes, meeting in a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard. A few days ago, over coffee with a friend outside academia, I tried to explain why David Lewis treats conventions as arbitrary equilibria sustained by common knowledge rather than as explicit agreements, and I realized that the standard examples—driving on the right, linguistic meaning—are so overused that students treat them as obviously correct rather than genuinely contingent. To push back against that complacency, I want a structured, runnable activity in which the group itself generates a coordination equilibrium, then watches the salience of its original cue decay, and—without being instructed—develops an implicit secondary protocol for interpreting the decayed signal. I have been thinking about this layered stabilization as "normative scaffolding," a somewhat niche evolutionary feature I want the exercise to make palpable: the idea that primary conventions rarely collapse outright but are propped up by meta-conventional practices about how to read them, so that normative change is path-dependent and stratified rather than a direct switch.

Please design the full activity, specifying the physical layout, the division of roles (for example, "archivists" who hold corrupted historical records, "messengers" restricted to three-word communications, and "adjudicators" who must ratify group choices without discussing intent), and a phased timeline with real but small stakes—say, points that accumulate toward a collective coffee voucher or a visible scoreboard. The rules and payoffs should be arranged so that participants first converge on an arbitrary equilibrium in round one, then face an ambiguous or degraded coordination cue in round two that makes the original choice unstable, and then—crucially—find themselves inventing an unwritten rule about how to treat the ambiguity in order to preserve coordination. Only in the debrief should the facilitator name these moments as convention formation, salience decay, and normative scaffolding. The debrief must map each phase onto the relevant conditions—arbitrary equilibrium, mutual expectation, common knowledge of precedent—while also explaining the evolutionary layer. Be explicit about what will predictably go wrong in execution, such as students importing an "obviously rational" solution that destroys the felt arbitrariness, and describe how the facilitator should suppress those cues in real time. Finally, include an honest admission of which theoretical feature the activity cannot reproduce experientially—almost certainly the infinite regress of common knowledge reasoning (I know that you know that I know)—and state clearly that the facilitator must supply that analytical layer verbally after the simulation ends.
