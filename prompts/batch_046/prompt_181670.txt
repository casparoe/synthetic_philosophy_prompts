I am applying for a six-month independent research fellowship at a legal-philosophy institute and need a polished two-page proposal. My half-formed idea concerns self-amending rules in constitutional and legal systems — specifically, the familiar puzzles about provisions that limit their own amendability, or that specify procedures for altering the very rules of alteration. Most existing treatments, whether in the logical tradition associated with Alf Ross or in contemporary constitutional theory, treat these difficulties as problems of linguistic self-reference or logical consistency: can a rule coherently apply to itself? I suspect the debate has stalled because both sides implicitly conflate two distinct normative relations: the authority a rule confers upon an institutional agent to initiate change, and the validity it confers upon the outcome of that change. If we separate authority-conferring rules from validity-conferring rules — treating authority as a relational property held by actors and validity as a status assigned to results — then some classic paradoxes dissolve, but new structural difficulties appear around what I call procedural closure: the point at which a system's authority-structure becomes unable to recognise amendments that lack the correct procedural lineage, even if those amendments have already achieved widespread social efficacy.
