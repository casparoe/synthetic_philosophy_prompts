I'm a doctoral student in moral and political philosophy advising a legislative drafting office whose explanatory materials for proposed bills routinely employ behavioral framing—default descriptions, loss-gain asymmetry, and social-norm references—to improve public comprehension, yet oversight committees and advocacy groups repeatedly dispute whether such framing crosses the line from legitimate persuasion into manipulation. I need a reusable decision procedure that staff without philosophical training can run in under fifteen minutes on any proposed communication: specify the ordered operational tests, the evidentiary threshold that satisfies each, the precise point at which the procedure yields a definitive classification, and the precise point at which it must hand the decision to human judgment because the dispute is one of values rather than facts. Ground the procedure in the principle that influence is legitimate only when its pathway is both transparent and counterfactually reversible—so that upon learning the framing mechanism the recipient can integrate that knowledge into deliberation without self-alienation—while treating sub-rational heuristic capture as lacking this reversibility, rather than relying solely on deception or coercion. Apply the procedure to two proposals: (a) a pension-reform leaflet that describes opt-out enrollment as the standard path with a small-print disclosure of the opt-in mechanism, and (b) a vaccination-campaign notice that presents mortality statistics in relative-risk terms without absolute base-rate context to encourage uptake, noting that advocates will likely reject the verdict for (b). Present the entire response as exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding text. Finally, state explicitly what empirical finding or conceptual argument—for instance, that transparency invariably eliminates framing efficacy, or that reflective endorsement cannot be operationalized—would lead you to revise or abandon the boundary you draw.
