Consider four analytical frameworks for classifying mass atrocities as genocide: (1) Raphael Lemkin’s original 1944 formulation, which encompassed cultural, social, and economic destruction alongside physical killing; (2) the definition adopted in the 1948 UN Convention, which restricts genocide to acts committed with intent to destroy a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group in whole or in part, excluding purely cultural destruction; (3) a hypothetical "coordinated-cultural" framework that treats systematic destruction of a group's cultural institutions and linguistic transmission as genocide only when coupled with direct state policy and demographic suppression, but not when arising from diffuse structural conditions; and (4) an "intent-maximalist" framework that extends the 1948 definition to cover cases where perpetrators foresee group destruction as a certain consequence of their actions, even without explicit genocidal purpose. Rank these four frameworks from 1 (most suitable for international criminal adjudication) to 4 (least suitable), explaining in one or two sentences for each why it occupies that position. In your ranking, weigh specifically the tension between moral inclusiveness—capturing harms that victims experience as existential erasure—and juridical precision—providing tribunals with discernible elements of proof that do not collapse into general crimes against humanity.
