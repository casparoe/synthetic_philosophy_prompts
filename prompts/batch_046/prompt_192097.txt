Write a poem of exactly fourteen lines, arranged as an octave (lines 1–8), a quatrain (lines 9–12), and a closing couplet (lines 13–14), in unrhymed iambic pentameter with a clear volta after line 8. The octave should present the KK principle as an ascending ladder of self-knowledge: line 1 asserts first-order knowledge of a single proposition (that the wall is crimson), line 2 asserts second-order knowledge of that same proposition, and so on, with each successive line grammatically embedding one additional nested "I know that..." so that line 8 asserts eighth-order knowledge, always preserving the identical base claim. At the volta, shift to Williamson's slowly brightening room: the speaker stands at the precise boundary where the wall's crimson is just barely visible. In the quatrain, show why the requirement that knowing allows a margin of discrimination from nearby error-possibilities blocks the corresponding second-order knowledge here, even while the first-order knowledge holds, again using the same base proposition. The couplet should not resolve whether luminosity survives; instead, it should leave the ladder broken at its second rung, so that the poem's formal ascent enacts the very failure it describes. The verse should be precise enough that someone familiar with the anti-luminosity argument recognizes the argument's anatomy, and readable as poetry by someone who is not.
