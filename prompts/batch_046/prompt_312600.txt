I am writing an essay for my philosophy of history module, and I have noticed a recurring pattern in historiographical argument that I struggle to name precisely. The pattern arises when historians attempt to avoid presentism by judging past actors according to the norms and epistemic resources actually available to them. In practice, however, they apply this contextual charity selectively: they defend a figure's commitment to a now-discredited framework — for example, humoral medicine or geocentric astronomy — on the grounds that it was the best-supported theory within the actor's own conceptual scheme, yet they do not extend the same charity to other beliefs held with equal confidence at the time, such as racial or gender hierarchies, which they treat as failures of moral reasoning that should have been overcome even then. The result is not straightforward presentism, because the historian explicitly disavows judging the past by present standards; nor is it mere inconsistency, because the selection of which norms to treat as binding seems systematically correlated with the historian's own unstated moral commitments. I have observed this in debates about early modern physicians who ignored anomalous clinical data: commentators excuse the neglect as rational within humoral theory, but then condemn the same physicians for ignoring equally anomalous social data about, say, the efficacy of folk remedies, without explaining why one kind of anomaly should have triggered revision and the other should not, except by importing modern scientific values. Does this specific configuration — a structured oscillation between contextual justification and modern critical evaluation, with the boundary drawn according to contemporary moral sensibilities rather than internal historical consistency — have an established name in the philosophy of historiography? How does it relate to presentism, to the principle of charity in interpretation, and to what is sometimes described as double standards in historical judgement? If it lacks a standard label, I would be grateful for a precise delineation of what it is and is not, together with boundary cases that distinguish it from legitimate contextual explanation.
