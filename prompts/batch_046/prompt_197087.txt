I sit on my university's Strategic Risk and Governance Panel, and we must vote next Thursday on a proposal from the Office of Institutional Planning to adopt a formal 'Historical Analogy Protocol'. Under the protocol, any senior strategic recommendation made during an institutional crisis must be tagged with one of three canonical analogies — the Munich appeasement of 1938, the Vietnam quagmire (1965–73), or the Thucydides trap describing rising-power versus ruling-power dynamics — and must include an explicit mapping of structural correspondences between the historical case and the present situation. Proponents argue that this disciplines reasoning by forcing advisers to surface hidden premises; critics contend that it institutionalises the very fallacy it claims to cure, since selecting an analogy determines the normative conclusion before evidence is weighed, and that the three canonical cases each import tacit value commitments — Munich a morality of preemptive firmness, Vietnam a morality of strategic restraint, the Thucydides trap a structural determinism about conflict — that the protocol smuggles in under a guise of neutrality.

I am a software engineer by training and have no formal background in philosophy or historiography, yet I find the debate unexpectedly difficult to adjudicate. I keep thinking about the problem in terms of feature selection in predictive modelling: choosing Munich, Vietnam, or Thucydides as your canonical feature set pre-structures the decision boundary, and the protocol risks institutionalising an overfitted model of history in which disanalogous causal details are suppressed like training-set leakage. I am unsure whether this comparison illuminates the issue or merely imports technical jargon that obscures it. Could you please draft the panel's written opinion — addressed to the Vice-Chancellor and intended for circulation before Thursday's meeting — recommending either adoption with conditions, adoption with amendments, or rejection of the protocol? The memo should treat seriously the strongest argument for adoption (that structured analogies make normative premises explicit and therefore contestable) and should engage honestly with the strongest counter-argument (that historical analogies suffer from irreducible 'selective salience', suppressing causally decisive differences, and that formalising them risks locking the institution into a small, theory-laden feature space).

Please write in British English. In your reasoning, please be explicit about what would change your recommendation: identify the specific argument or empirical consideration that, if shown to be mistaken, would lead you to reverse your verdict, and flag the precise point in your reasoning where failure is most likely. Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; if you refer to particular authors or works, paraphrase without quotation marks. The deliverable should be a formal document with a clear, actionable bottom line rather than personal guidance.
