I'm preparing a closing exercise for an advanced undergraduate seminar on metaphysics of time. We've spent the past weeks on Borges's arguments that if personal identity is only a fiction sustained by memory, then the experience of temporal succession collapses into a kind of eternal present in which all moments coexist—like the books arranged on the shelves of his infinite Library—while human consciousness can access them only one at a time, stitching them into narrative. My students kept returning to one tension: Borges denies the reality of linear time in essays that borrow from Berkeley and Hume, yet he describes this denial with an almost nostalgic attachment to the illusion. I want a poem that captures this precise tension without resolving it, and I want the form to do philosophical work rather than merely decorate the theme.

Please write an 18-line poem divided into three sestets. Adopt this structural constraint: the final word of each line in the first sestet must open the corresponding line in the second sestet, and in the third sestet those same six words must appear in exactly reverse order, one per line, with no other repetition of them elsewhere in the poem. The speaker should be the "remembering self"—the fictional agent Borges describes as constructing continuity from simultaneous moments—rather than Borges himself. The imagery should draw on the infinite Library and the Aleph, the point containing all points, but the argument should live in the form: the recycling of words should enact the claim that memory rearranges co-present elements rather than recording a sequence, and the final inversion should feel like the moment when linear time is revealed as a reversible fiction. It should be accurate enough that a reader familiar with Borges's denial of time recognizes the Berkeleyan/Humean move and the role of identity, and it should work as poetry even for someone encountering the idea for the first time.
