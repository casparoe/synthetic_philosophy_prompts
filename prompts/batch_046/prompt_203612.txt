Here is an argument I have been working through that seems to show that combining epistemic contextualism with pragmatic encroachment collapses into contradiction whenever knowledge attributions are made across contexts with mismatched practical stakes. I want to preserve both the contextualist intuition about shifting standards and the encroachment intuition that high stakes genuinely demand stronger evidence, but one of these premises must contain a hidden slide.

(1) Epistemic contextualism holds that the truth-conditions of "S knows that p" depend on the context of attribution, specifically on which error possibilities are salient to the speaker.

(2) Pragmatic encroachment holds that whether S knows p depends on the practical stakes for S in acting on p.

(3) These two dimensions are independent: an attributor can occupy a context in which error possibilities are highly salient and practical stakes are enormous, while the subject S occupies a context in which both salience and stakes are minimal.

(4) Consider S, an engineer who personally believes a bridge is safe based on a routine inspection. For S, hidden structural flaws are not particularly salient, and the personal stakes of being wrong are ordinary.

(5) By (1) and (2), in S's own context, S knows the bridge is safe.

(6) Now consider an oversight board B that must certify the bridge for public use. For B, hidden structural flaws are highly salient, and the stakes of erroneous certification are extreme.

(7) By (1), B correctly says, "S does not know the bridge is safe," since B's context sets a stringent epistemic standard.

(8) Crucially, by (2) applied without restriction, the enormous stakes of B's evaluation determine the conditions S must satisfy to count as knowing, regardless of whose action is at issue.

(9) Therefore, S absolutely fails to know the bridge is safe, which contradicts (5).

(10) One might block the contradiction by insisting that knowledge is purely relative: S knows-relative-to-S's-context and fails-to-know-relative-to-B's-context.

(11) But if (10) is correct, then (8) is false—stakes do not determine S's epistemic status across attributive boundaries—so pragmatic encroachment cannot apply across contexts in the way (8) assumes.

(12) Alternatively, reject (1) and adopt invariantism, preserving cross-contextual stake-sensitivity but losing the contextualist explanation of ordinary bank-case shifts.

(13) Or reject (2) and adopt pure contextualism, preserving attribution-relativity but losing the explanation of why subjects in high-stakes situations genuinely need stronger evidence.

(14) Thus the combination seems to force a choice: either knowledge is not consistently evaluated across contexts, or one of the two motivating views must be abandoned.

I am especially reluctant to surrender both (5) and (7), since each seems correct in isolation. Which step contains the hidden equivocation—perhaps a slide between the attributor's salience-conditions and the subject's practical stakes? And if I keep both (1) and (2) but reject the inference in (8), what exactly must I pay? Do I need a principled boundary that fixes whose stakes and whose salience conditions govern a given attribution, and if so, how is that boundary justified without collapsing back into invariantism or pure contextualism?
