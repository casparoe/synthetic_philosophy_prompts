I'm commissioning a feature essay on a stubborn philosophical problem in the foundations of science. Write an essay examining whether Wigner's thesis on the surprising power of mathematics becomes more acute—not less—when we restrict our attention to the success of continuous formalisms in describing apparently discrete or granular physical domains. The controversy to resolve is this: does the fact that smooth manifolds, differential equations, and continuous symmetries so precisely model quantum and possibly Planck-scale phenomena strengthen the case for a Platonic view in which mathematics genuinely mirrors an underlying continuous reality, or does it instead reveal a methodological selection effect, in which scientists adopt continuous tools because they are computationally tractable and empirically adequate at macroscopic scales, regardless of deeper ontological structure? Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides, address at least one objection that a selection-effect explanation must answer—such as the predictive success of continuous mathematics in domains where no macroscopic approximation was initially available—and reach a justified conclusion. The essay should be rigorous but accessible to an educated general reader, and it should not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the reasoning must carry the argument on its own.
