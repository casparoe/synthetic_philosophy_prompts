I have been helping a local volunteer group plan a permanent exhibit for our town's bicentennial, and none of us has formal training in history or museum design. Our committee keeps splitting over what belongs in the display: some members want to center dramatic, well-documented moments, such as the 1952 river flood that destroyed the old mill district and triggered a local political crisis, while others argue we should emphasize the far slower processes that shaped the settlement—how the valley's soil and rainfall patterns constrained building over centuries, and how two largely unnoticed revisions to regional water-rights law gradually shifted irrigation control away from individual farms. I recently heard someone mention an idea—perhaps the phrase was longue durée—about treating history as operating at different speeds: the nearly immovable pace of geography and climate, the intermediate pace of social and economic structures, and the rapid pace of individual events and people.

I want to translate this into an actual decision procedure that our five-person committee can run on any proposed exhibit topic without getting lost in abstraction. Please lay out the steps in order, specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, indicate where the procedure should stop with a clear rejection, and mark the points where the question is genuinely one of values rather than facts and therefore requires a human judgment call. Then apply the procedure to the two proposals we are debating—the 1952 flood and its immediate political aftermath versus the 150-year documentation of wetland drainage and water-rights change—showing exactly where each lands. I am particularly concerned with the case where the procedure might reject the human-centered story that visitors will actually care about, or where it might approve the slow-structure story even though it offers no clear narrative hook; I want to see where the framework breaks down and what it forces us to admit about our own priorities.
