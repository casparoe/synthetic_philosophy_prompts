Since being appointed co-executor of my parents' estate alongside my two siblings, I have found myself unable to stop reflecting on the boundary between genuinely acting together and merely coordinating in parallel. I want to know whether my intuitions about joint action and collective intentionality form a coherent pattern, or whether some must be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases.

A. Two strangers push a stalled car from an intersection without speaking, each seeing the other do so, both intending only to clear the road. Verdict: this is joint action, though minimal and spontaneous.

B. Three siblings serve as co-executors of a will and must jointly approve the sale of a family house. One votes yes with conviction, one votes yes solely to avoid being sued by the others, and one abstains but signs the document on her lawyer's advice. The sale proceeds. Verdict: all three participate in the collective intentional act of selling, including the reluctant voter and the abstaining signer.

C. A hospital ethics committee votes unanimously to withdraw life support from a patient. Each member reaches the decision through incompatible reasoning—religious conviction, consequentialist calculation, and pure procedural compliance—and they never discuss motives with one another. Verdict: this is still collective intentionality; they jointly decided, even though no shared mental content exists beyond the outcome itself.

D. A neighborhood group chat agrees that every household will turn off its lights for one hour to protest energy waste. One household participates accidentally, having always turned off lights at that hour regardless. Another household intends to join but forgets entirely. Verdict: the intentional households form a joint action; the accidental one does not; but regarding the forgetful household, I keep oscillating—perhaps its prior commitment keeps it within the collective act in a weakened sense, or perhaps the absence of any concurrent mental state dissolves its membership.

E. Two colleagues collaborate on a research article. One believes the central thesis is true and significant; the other believes it is false but writes skillfully to advance her career. They divide sections, edit each other's prose, and submit under both names. Verdict: this is not genuine joint action but parallel individual action disguised as collaboration, because the apparent shared goal is not genuinely shared.

F. A corporate board votes to lay off hundreds of workers. Every board member privately judges the decision immoral, yet each votes yes because she believes the others will do so and wishes to preserve board unity. The layoffs are executed. Verdict: this IS collective intentionality—the board jointly decided—even though no individual mentally endorses the decision.

The tension I cannot resolve lies between E and F (and C). In E, divergence in the private mental attitudes toward the content of the goal seems to destroy jointness; yet in C and F, divergence in private reasons or moral evaluations does not destroy it. Meanwhile, B suggests that minimal mental endorsement—reluctance, abstention—still counts, while D suggests that a missing mental state—forgetting—may or may not count. Is there a single principle that preserves A, B, C, and F, explains why E fails, and fixes my ambivalence about D? Or does saving every verdict require an implausible multiplication of conditions?

Please state the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, with precision: identify which clause responds to which case, and show how it distinguishes mental divergence that undermines jointness from mental divergence that does not. Then apply it to resolve the status of the forgetful household in D. Also apply it to a case I have not listed: two chess players conduct a game by correspondence over months; one dies after recording his strategy and leaving detailed notes for the remaining moves. The survivor completes the game exactly according to those notes. Does the survivor now act jointly with the deceased, or does he act alone?

Finally, please say honestly which of my verdicts—A through F—the principle cannot accommodate without distortion, and whether any simpler principle forces me to overturn one of them.
