I'm a doctoral student in comparative philosophy focusing on metaphysics of the self, and after a recent seminar on cross-cultural moral psychology I have been engaged in an extended, somewhat circular argument with two colleagues whose views have crystallized into opposing camps. One defends a liberal-individualist position: the person is ontologically prior to social relations, possessing a bounded, unitary core of agency and moral responsibility that exists independently of any particular social role. The other defends a Confucian-relationalist position: the self is irreducibly constituted by its embeddedness in specific, hierarchical roles—parent and child, elder and younger, ruler and subject—such that talk of a pre-relational, role-independent substrate is conceptually incoherent, like searching for an empty center where no center can exist.

Rather than asking you to adjudicate between them, I want you to draft the joint statement that should emerge from a proper adversarial collaboration: a document both parties could sign in good faith. The document should include: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims—covering the phenomenological reality that individuals experience themselves both as distinct agents and as role-embedded members; the logical distinction between relations that are constitutive of an entity (without which it is not that kind of thing) and relations that are merely formative or instrumental; the empirical observation that patterns of self-construal vary across cultural contexts without being deterministically fixed; and the shared rejection of both extreme atomism (the self as socially causeless) and extreme fusion (the self as lacking any boundary whatsoever); (2) the precise residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side accepts—specifically, whether relational roles are constitutive of personal identity or only formative of it; whether moral autonomy requires independence from social roles or can be fully exercised within them; and whether the persistence of personal identity over time requires a minimal, role-independent substrate capable of surviving total role-abandonment; (3) the cruxes—particularly whether there exists a minimal self that persists through the deliberate severing of all familial and social ties, and whether moral praise or blame can be coherently directed at an agent abstracted from every relational position; (4) for each crux, a concrete scenario or conceptual test both sides agree bears on the question—such as the case of an adult who deliberately abandons all recognized social roles and lives for years in sustained, unacknowledged anonymity—together with each side's explicit prediction of what that case demonstrates about psychological continuity and moral agency. Do not reach a verdict or declare a winner; make the disagreement exact and rigorous. You may present the disagreements in a table for clarity, and please maintain a formal, academic register appropriate to a philosophy working paper.
