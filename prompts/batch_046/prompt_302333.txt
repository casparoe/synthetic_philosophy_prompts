As a novelist constructing a narrative about the quiet machinery of the British state, I keep returning to a peculiar institutional habit shared across constitutional monarchies: in the United Kingdom, every Act of Parliament requires the formal assent of a hereditary sovereign who, by unwritten convention, never withholds it; in Sweden, Norway and Japan, the monarch formally appoints governments and promulgates statutes, yet always in strict obedience to parliamentary arithmetic. The arrangement preserves a non-elected person as the constitutional fountain of all executive and legislative authority, while simultaneously stripping that person of every capacity for independent judgement. What philosophical theory of sovereignty, representation, or democratic legitimacy does this bifurcation presuppose? Must it rely on a coherent distinction between symbolic and substantive authority, or does it quietly depend on a pre-democratic conception in which legitimacy flows downward from a crown rather than upward from consent? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and say whether the same institutional form could survive if its underlying theory were replaced by a consistently republican one—where all formal authority derives directly from the electorate—or whether specific rituals and legal fictions would then have to be abandoned.
