I serve on the University Research Integrity and Joint Authorship Appeals Panel. We must issue a formal written opinion by Friday on Case 2024-09, which turns on collective intentionality and joint action in scientific collaboration. Four laboratories conducted a shared study on distributed moral judgment under a protocol that required each site to enter an explicit “joint commitment to co-publish” before any data collection began. Site D now petitions to withdraw all of its data and to remove its two investigators from the author list, arguing that the principal investigator failed to disclose a secondary hypothesis concerning cross-cultural blame asymmetry. Site D claims this omission vitiated the shared intention that constituted the collective action of co-authorship, rendering the joint commitment void ab initio. The other three sites respond that disclosure obligations were satisfied by the general protocol description; that Site D’s researchers actively contributed to analysis, interpretation, and drafting; and that allowing unilateral withdrawal on the ground of retroactive misapprehension would destroy the conditions of shared agency—specifically, that a we-intention, once enacted through coordinated contribution, cannot be dissolved by one party’s private reassessment without undermining collaborative trust.

Please draft the panel’s decision memo. Recommend a concrete, actionable course—approve full withdrawal, approve withdrawal with conditions (e.g., retention of anonymized data with revised attribution), deny withdrawal, or order a structured mediation with a fixed deadline (for instance, 30 days)—and state the bottom line clearly so that the researchers can respond to it. The reasoning should engage the philosophical issue precisely rather than ornamentally: address whether collective intentionality in research generates obligations that survive a participant’s claim that the original we-mode commitment was formed under defective consent, or whether such a defect genuinely dissolves the joint commitment from the start; distinguish we-mode contribution from mere I-mode coordination; and consider whether retroactive defeasibility is compatible with the stability required for joint action. You must also treat honestly and charitably the strongest consideration favoring the side that loses—whether that is the protection of individual autonomy and informed consent against coerced collective identity, or the preservation of collaborative integrity against strategic withdrawal. Write for an institutional audience that must act, not as an abstract essay, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources.
