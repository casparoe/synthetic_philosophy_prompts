A colleague presents the following reasoning about mind uploading and gradual neural replacement, claiming it shows that any substrate-independent, continuity-based defense of uploading collapses into absurdity. I want to know exactly where the slip is.

(1) If personal identity survives the complete loss of original biological matter, it must be carried by psychological continuity rather than by bodily continuity.

(2) Psychological continuity is not merely mental-state similarity; it requires the right kind of causally connected chain linking earlier mental states to later ones — a diachronic causal history, not just a static pattern.

(3) In gradual, in-vivo replacement of neurons by functionally identical synthetic analogues, each new unit satisfies what we might call the in-vivo inheritance condition: it takes over the causal role and local dynamical trajectory of its biological predecessor inside the living system, so the global causal chain of mental states is never interrupted.

(4) Therefore, gradual replacement preserves psychological continuity, and the entity after full replacement is numerically identical to the original person.

(5) Now consider an instant scan-and-upload: a remote silicon system is instantiated with the exact same global functional architecture and mental-state profile as the biological brain had at time t.

(6) The internal relations among mental states in the uploaded system are structurally identical to those in the gradually replaced brain; since psychological continuity is constituted by the pattern of these relations, the uploaded system stands in the same continuity relations to the pre-scan original as the gradually replaced entity does.

(7) So if gradual replacement yields identity, instant upload must also yield identity — the only difference is whether the functional preservation is distributed across time or compressed into a single instant.

(8) But now suppose both occur: the biological brain is gradually replaced, producing entity A, while a scan taken at the midpoint is instantiated elsewhere, producing entity B. Both A and B are psychologically continuous with the original in precisely the same structural way.

(9) Personal identity is unique: one original cannot be fully identical to two distinct successors.

(10) Hence the inference in (3)→(4) must fail; gradual replacement does not actually preserve identity after all.

The colleague concludes that this proves any continuity-based account of uploading is self-defeating, because it either denies that gradual survival is possible or accepts that identity can be duplicated. I feel something slipped between (5) and (7), or perhaps between (8) and (10), but I cannot name whether it is an equivocation on “continuity,” a modal slide from “can occur together” to “must conflict,” or a genuinely costly rejection of uniqueness.
