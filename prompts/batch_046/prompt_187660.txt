I am preparing the syllabus for an advanced graduate seminar on historiographical method, and in a recent discussion with colleagues I found myself arguing that microhistory is not merely macrohistory at a smaller scale but involves a genuinely different ontology of causation. To sharpen this position, I want to step back from particular case studies and specify exactly what we should demand of any theoretical framework that claims to integrate history from below with the history of elites and states. I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of named schools; I need the criteria themselves, especially because I suspect that some of the conditions people routinely impose on such a theory may be jointly unsatisfiable.

Please write the full list of desiderata that an adequate theory of historical explanation must meet if it is genuinely to accommodate microhistorical agency—including individual lives documented only in fragmentary traces, local customary practices, and what I will call infrapolitical temporalities: the slow, sub-state rhythms of negotiation and resistance that operate below formal political institutions yet are not reducible to private domestic life—without reducing those phenomena to epiphenomena of state formation, class structure, or elite decision-making. For each requirement, state the motivating case or conceptual consideration that makes it necessary: for instance, the obligation to distinguish absence in the archive from absence in history; the need to preserve scalar incommensurability, by which causal mechanisms at the village, household, or parish scale are not treated as miniature replicas of macro-structural forces; and the need to respect temporal heterogeneity, since social domains may change at incompatible rhythms that cannot be synchronized into a single national narrative. Clearly mark which desiderata you regard as non-negotiable for any serious account and which reflect the particular commitments of one camp—say, structuralist state historians versus interpretive microhistorians. Most importantly, identify whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable given established constraints—for example, whether full preservation of micro-agency can coexist with a determinist macro-structural explanation, or whether avoiding methodological nationalism necessarily sacrifices explanatory parsimony. If some combination proves impossible, flag that impossibility as a substantive result in its own right. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their justifications rather than on scoring specific theorists, though you may briefly indicate how a structural-functionalist orientation and a dense source-critical microhistorical orientation fare against the list.
