For my philosophy-of-science seminar, our instructor has been judging whether specific unrealistic assumptions in scientific models count as defensible idealizations or unacceptable distortions. Her verdicts across the cases we have discussed so far are these: she accepts the ideal-gas law applied to low-density nitrogen, despite treating molecules as point masses with no intermolecular forces; she accepts the perfect-competition model applied to liquid commodity markets, despite homogeneous goods and perfect information; she accepts Newtonian bridge-stress analysis that ignores quantum effects and thermal expansion; she accepts a linear-filter model of primary visual cortex for predicting responses to simple oriented gratings, despite omitting recurrent connections; she accepts a well-mixed SIR epidemiological model for forecasting aggregate city-level infection curves, despite ignoring social-network clustering; she accepts a uniform-ocean-mixing climate model for predicting global mean temperature trends, despite ignoring regional current variation; and she rejects that same climate model when it is applied to regional hurricane-frequency forecasting. Next week she will evaluate a new proposal: a macro-level model of scientific citation dynamics that treats every researcher as an identical node with a uniform citation rate, ignoring disciplinary boundaries, institutional prestige hierarchies, and topical clustering, yet predicts the aggregate exponential growth of published papers and total citations over a thirty-year span with high accuracy. The model's proponents present it not merely as a forecasting instrument for aggregate growth but as an explanatory account of how citation networks evolve. Predict her verdict on whether this idealization is defensible. Identify the competing principles that could organize her earlier judgments — for instance, a Friedman-style predictive-vindication criterion (the F-twist), a causal-negligibility or explanatory-scope-restriction criterion, and a robustness-to-perturbation criterion — and for each state whether it accepts or rejects the citation model. Give your best guess about which principle she is actually running, with reasons drawn from the pattern of her earlier rulings, and specify one further hypothetical case that would decisively distinguish the principle you favor from its closest rival.
