hey so i just found out im gonna be the executor for my grandmas estate and have to split it between my cousins and my aunt, and its made me think about weighing one persons claim against a bunch of other people. my friend sent me this argument about Scanlons contractualism and aggregation that seems like it proves the theory cant work at all, but i feel like theres a hidden slide somewhere i cant name.

1. Contractualism says an act is wrong iff its permitted by a principle that no affected party could reasonably reject.
2. Reasonable rejection is comparative: you reject principle P only if theres some available alternative Q thats better for you and not worse for anyone else in a relevant way.
3. Now take distributing a fixed estate: giving the whole thing to my aunt saves her from losing her house, but splitting it ten ways gives each cousin almost nothing useful.
4. For each cousin, theres a principle Q where they recieve the full amount; Q is better for them, and the others are just in the same boat they started in.
5. So each cousin can reject the principle that gives everything to the aunt.
6. But the aunt can reject equal split on the exact same grounds, since theres a Q where she gets it all.
7. Therefore every possible distributive principle is rejectable by someone affected.
8. So contractualism condemns every division of the estate, which is obviously absurd.
9. Thus contractualism collapses once you have more than one person involved.

where does this actually go wrong? is it the move from (4) to (5), where "not worse for anyone else" secretly fails because in Q the other cousins are definitely worse off? or is the real problem that (1) treats principles as applied case-by-case instead of ex ante? i want to keep that individual claims really matter and cant just be summed away, but i also dont want every family dispute to paralyze the theory. which step should i reject, and whats the cost of giving it up?
