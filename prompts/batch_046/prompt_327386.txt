Student Answer: The situationist critique, mainly pushed by Harman and Doris, uses experiments like Milgram's obedience study and the follow-up Good Samaritan experiment by Batson to show that behavior tracks situational pressures more than stable global traits. Virtue ethicists like Aristotle and MacIntyre assume people have unified character traits—courage, honesty, generosity—that stay constant across contexts, but Mischel's data shows consistency coefficients around .30, too low for global virtues. One reply is the local-trait defense that Harman developed: instead of global courage, people have narrow situation-specific traits like courage-around-friends that are stable within limited domains. This keeps consistency but gives up Aristotle's link between virtue and overall flourishing, or eudaimonia. The other reply appeals to practical wisdom, which lets the virtuous agent adapt correctly to each context. But this makes virtue ethics circular—if someone acts badly we just say they lacked wisdom, and if they act well we credit wisdom—so it can't really be tested. Overall the critique lands hard, though local traits might survive as a psychological model rather than a full moral theory.
