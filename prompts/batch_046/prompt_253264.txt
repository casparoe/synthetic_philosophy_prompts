I am a doctoral researcher in the philosophy of history, and in a recent departmental seminar I heard the following summary of the debate between Leopold von Ranke's injunction that the historian should show how things actually happened, and the sceptical responses developed by E. H. Carr and Hayden White. According to the speaker: Ranke believed that perfect objectivity is achievable if the historian suppresses personal judgement and allows the archival record to speak unmediated, producing a direct mirror of the past; Carr rejected this as a form of naive empiricism, insisting that all historical facts are selected and interpreted through present concerns, so that objectivity is impossible, though he retains what one might call an epistemic humility—the past remains a single reality that we can only approach approximately; White, however, pushed the argument onto ontological ground, claiming that historical writing is governed by deep linguistic tropes—metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche and irony—that do not merely colour our access to the past but literally generate distinct pasts, yielding an ontological pluralism in which documentary evidence functions only as raw material for emplotment, with no independent constraining power. On this basis, the speaker concluded that Ranke's ideal is not merely unattainable but conceptually incoherent: if narrative form constitutes the past rather than representing it, then there is no meaningful normative distinction between history and fiction.

I suspect this account is too neat, and that it conflates methodological, epistemic and ontological claims that the original authors keep carefully apart—particularly in attributing to White a literal world-creating pluralism and in treating Carr's scepticism as leaving the past entirely untouched. I also wonder whether the inference that the failure of pure objectivity entails the collapse of any distinction between historical and fictional narrative actually follows once the premises are corrected. Could you audit this summary for fairness? Specifically, please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what is a subtle distortion—paying particular attention to Ranke's own hermeneutic and theological commitments (which are often omitted in popular accounts), to Carr's actual attitude towards empirical evidence and the historian's present-mindedness, and to White's precise view of the relationship between documentary record and narrative form. I would also value your view on whether the speaker's conclusion survives once these distinctions are restored, and whether the labels 'epistemic humility' and 'ontological pluralism' genuinely capture the respective positions of Carr and White.
