Consider Simone Weil’s implied judgements on four forms of collective organisation. First, large national political parties competing for state power: she treats these as corrupting machines that manufacture collective passions, suppress independent reasoning, and ought to be abolished. Second, a temporary local workers’ association formed for an immediate economic grievance, with rotating leadership, no permanent bureaucracy, and no claim to govern others: she regards this as legitimate, because it remains rooted in direct lived experience and avoids domination. Third, a hierarchical religious institution that demands doctrinal assent and exercises moral authority over its members: she judges it structurally analogous to a party, substituting collective loyalty and emotional unity for the individual’s search for truth and justice. Fourth, a voluntary study circle without collective decision-making power, where members may disagree openly and no binding position is enforced: she accepts this as compatible with the obligation to seek truth, provided it does not harden into an organised faction.

Now consider a new form: a citizens’ deliberation cell, selected by lot for a fixed term of eighteen months, tasked with producing a single binding advisory recommendation (not law) on a specific technical domain such as regional water governance. It is state-funded and given privileged access to expert testimony; its rules forbid internal blocs, party-style campaigning, or collective manifestos; members must sign a consensus report, though each may append an individual dissenting note; and once its recommendation is delivered, the body dissolves.

If we want to be consistent with Weil’s track record, what should her verdict be? Identify the two or three plausible rules that jointly explain her four prior judgements, say what each predicts about the citizens’ cell, and name which prior case is the closest analog for each rule. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, state plainly what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice. Finally, specify one further hypothetical case — a different collective form — that would best distinguish the competing rules.
