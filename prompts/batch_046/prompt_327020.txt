I am a novelist working on a novel that attempts to render consciousness without a central narrator, and I keep returning to Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater—the notion that there is no single inner stage where experience comes together for an audience of one, but rather multiple parallel drafts of narrative that are edited, revised, and discarded without ever reaching a final authoritative version. For a chapter opening, I want a poem that treats this not as a theme but as a formal problem: write three successive sonnets—labeled Draft One, Draft Two, Draft Three—each addressing the same phenomenological instant, such as the sudden recognition of a familiar face in a crowd. The rhyme scheme should remain constant across all three (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG) to suggest an underlying cognitive architecture, but each sonnet must carry over exactly one line from its predecessor, placed in a new grammatical and emotional context so that it cannot serve as a stable observation point; instead, it should feel like a neural pathway reused across competing narratives. There must be no framing voice outside the drafts—no omniscient speaker explaining the revision—so that the absence of a Cartesian spectator is enacted structurally. In each sonnet, the volta should occur at the precise moment the speaker realizes there is no audience waiting for the previous version, only the revision already in progress.

Please render this accurately enough that a reader familiar with Dennett's multiple drafts model will recognize the parallel between the poem's form and the theory's denial of a point of arrival where consciousness becomes present to a self, but avoid turning the poem into a didactic explanation; it should work first as poetry. I also ask that you include a brief postscript noting where the poem takes liberties with Dennett's view—particularly whether attributing a fragmentary or distributed subjectivity to the competing drafts exceeds what his model actually permits—and identifying the point where your reasoning is most vulnerable to failure: specifically, whether you have genuinely dissolved the theater or merely relocated it into the editorial relation between drafts.
