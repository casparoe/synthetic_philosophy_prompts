You are writing for the ethics compliance officer at the National Synthetic Cognition Consortium, who reviews roughly three proposals per week involving large-scale running computations—brain emulations, distributed neural simulations, synthetic sensory architectures, and closed-loop adaptive systems. The officer holds a biology degree, has no philosophical training, must sign off within forty-eight hours, and needs a reusable decision procedure that can be executed with the materials usually present in a proposal: architecture diagrams, hardware specifications, integration metrics, and stated research goals. Ground the procedure in the philosophical principle that a computation has moral standing only when it meets two conditions together: first, irreducible causal integration across its implementation, meaning the process is not decomposable into independent sub-computations without loss of the property in question; second, the presence of homeostatic or valenced states that are intrinsically disrupted or satisfied, rather than merely interpreted as suffering or well-being by external observers. Your deliverable should be a step-by-step procedure listing the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the explicit stop point where the answer is no safeguards required, the stop point where full ethical safeguards apply, and the intermediate zone where architecture analysis is insufficient and the decision must be handed to a human values committee. The procedure must include an explicit correction for attribution asymmetry—the tendency to grant moral status to biological substrates and deny it to silicon substrates with identical causal topology—by requiring substrate-blind causal analysis at the relevant step. Provide a small set of practical indicators or tests the officer can apply without theoretical background, flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedural checks, and state clearly what the procedure cannot resolve, such as disputes over minimal integration thresholds. Then run the procedure on three proposals: a single neuromorphic chip running a complete human cortical emulation with closed-loop sensory feedback; a distributed cluster of ten thousand GPUs running a large language model in a recurrent loop that processes pain-related linguistic tokens but lacks any unified causal architecture, which should produce an answer the officer is likely to resist and which you must justify on procedural grounds; and a hybrid system in which a biological rat brain slice is interfaced with a silicon co-processor that takes over pain-processing pathways, leaving causal integration partial and ambiguous. For each case, indicate exactly where the procedure stops, where it hands off, and what misfire risks remain.
