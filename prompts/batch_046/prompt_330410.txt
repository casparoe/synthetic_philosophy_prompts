At a recent philosophy seminar a participant dismissed an objection to strict deontology by repeating the maxim "the ends do not justify the means," treating it as if it automatically resolved the paradox of deontology — in particular, as if it settled whether a deontological constraint can ever be overridden when the stakes reach catastrophic magnitude (say, breaching a minor procedural safeguard to avert an existential threat). Before I accept that this sentence resolves anything, I want to see it disambiguated. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could express, separating tautological readings from substantive normative claims and from empirical claims about moral reasoning? For each interpretation, say whether it actually addresses the paradox, whether it allows any exception at catastrophic scale, and whether it entails that one ought to permit a preventable disaster rather than violate a constraint. Present the readings as a menu rather than defending a single one, and identify which interpretation the seminar interlocutor was likely invoking — and whether that interpretation genuinely supports dismissing the objection.
