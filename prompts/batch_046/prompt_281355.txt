I serve on the works council of a 200-person manufacturing plant and have no formal training in economics, yet every quarter I must help vote on automation proposals within 48 hours. I have been thinking about Marx's analysis of machinery: some technology extends a worker's own capacity, while other technology takes over the rhythm of labor and fragments skill so that the worker must follow the machine's pace rather than set their own. Management has now proposed an "adaptive throughput optimization" system that reads real-time biometric fatigue data and automatically adjusts assembly-line speed, with no worker override. Please turn that distinction into a reusable decision procedure my council can apply in under an hour: state the ordered questions, what counts as sufficient evidence for each, the precise conditions that produce a "no," and the steps where the answer is necessarily a value judgment rather than a technical fact. Run the procedure on three cases: robotic arms that lift heavy parts; the adaptive biometric pace system; and an AI vision checker that requires workers to spend two unpaid hours weekly correcting its errors. Ensure that one case yields an approval my members will likely dislike. Finish by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot settle and where it will probably misfire.
