I'm preparing a focused research agenda for a philosophy seminar that treats germs, climate regimes, and volcanic eruptions not as background conditions but as "historical actors" in their own right—entities that reshape societies, economies, and political orders without being reducible to human intention. The framing is deliberately provocative: it risks collapsing into environmental determinism if we are not careful about how agency, causation, and narrative explanation interact. Rather than asking for a survey of existing literature, I want a defended prioritization of the open conceptual questions that actually matter for this project, ordered according to which are upstream of the others, which are tractable for a philosopher working without specialized laboratory or archival training in epidemiology or geology, and which carry real weight for historiographical practice rather than being purely metaphysical puzzles.

In particular, I'm weighing three clusters. The first concerns the metaphysics of distributed agency: whether attributing action to non-human entities requires something like intentionality, or whether occupying a functional role in a causal network—disrupting supply chains, altering agricultural yields, selecting for immune resistance—is sufficient. The second is a narrative-structural problem: how to integrate such actors into explanatory histories without rendering human societies merely reactive to them, since that reintroduces the determinism the framework seeks to avoid. The third involves normative implications for responsibility and explanation—if a volcanic winter or a plague wave reshapes the conditions for a revolution or a labor restructuring, does that diminish the agency of the human actors involved, or does it demand a multi-layered model of causation in which different kinds of agents operate at different explanatory scales?

Please rank these clusters—or substitute others you think are more central—according to which should be tackled first within the constraints of a single semester of sustained conceptual work. Defend the ordering by specifying which questions depend on answers to others, which are currently stalled because of ambiguous conceptual tools rather than missing empirical data, and what progress on each would actually change how historians write about cases like the 1816 climate crisis or plague-driven labor dynamics in late medieval Europe. I want the reasoning exposed: what makes one cluster upstream, what evidence or conceptual clarification would shift its priority, and which lines you would actively deprioritize because they either dead-end or fail to alter the determinism debate in any meaningful way.
