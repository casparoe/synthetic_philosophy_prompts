I've been working through recent discussions of value incommensurability and hard choice—particularly the claim that two life paths can stand in a relation of parity, being neither better nor worse nor precisely equal, without that relation reducing to mere epistemic uncertainty about a hidden ordinal ranking. In my own case, I'm weighing a commitment to pure mathematics against one to clinical medicine: the values that animate each path—intellectual abstraction and universal structure on one side, embodied care and immediate moral urgency on the other—seem genuinely resistant to translation into any shared evaluative unit.

My difficulty arises when I try to apply formal decision theory. Standard models require preference completeness, but incommensurability appears to violate completeness in a substantive rather than informational way. Some recent work suggests we can restore completeness by introducing a second-order preference for "resoluteness"—the value of being the kind of agent who makes firm commitments despite first-order parity. Yet this seems to smuggle in a hidden metric: if I choose mathematics because I value resoluteness, haven't I simply elevated one first-order value—say, the aesthetic coherence of a mathematically centered life—into a disguised tie-breaker?

So my question is whether there is a coherent way to model hard choice without either (a) presupposing a hidden common scale that dissolves the parity, or (b) treating the choice as rationally arbitrary. Specifically, can we construct a decision procedure in which the act of commitment itself generates a new, non-derivative reason—one that does not function as a hidden weight on the original values but transforms the choice situation entirely? If such a procedure exists, what distinguishes it from mere rationalization? And if it does not exist, does that imply that all hard choices are ultimately non-rational in the strong sense, rather than rational in some non-comparative mode?
