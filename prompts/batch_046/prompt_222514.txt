In a philosophy of mind seminar I led recently, a student proposed an analogy to capture Dennett’s worry about artificial beings that are behaviorally indistinguishable from human persons yet lack the right kind of origin. Rather than comparing them to counterfeit currency—the usual image—she likened them to a perfect forgery of a Vermeer painting: every visible feature matches the original, but the object’s status depends on provenance and authorship rather than appearance, and once the forgery is exposed we do not say the original’s value was illusory, only that this particular object never possessed it. She applied this to advanced AI, arguing that even a system that passed every behavioral test for personhood would remain a counterfeit person because it lacks the evolutionary and biological history that is constitutive of genuine personhood, just as the forgery lacks Vermeer’s hand regardless of its surface qualities.

Please audit this analogy with precision. Provide an explicit mapping between the artistic case and Dennett’s framework—what corresponds to the original artwork, the forgery, the authentication process, the market’s valuation, and the revealed provenance; identify where the correspondence breaks down, especially regarding whether Dennett’s functionalism about the intentional stance allows origin to function as a hidden property or whether it could in principle be engineered into an artifact; examine whether the analogy smuggles in the normative premise that origin is morally decisive over functional equivalence, a premise that may sit uneasily with Dennett’s broader views; and say whether, once these disanalogies are on the table, the analogy genuinely licenses treating behaviorally equivalent AI as counterfeit people, or whether it collapses into a different argument about consciousness or moral status. Respond in a single paragraph of no more than 250 words.
