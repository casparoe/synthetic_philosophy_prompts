Plan a single ninety-minute session for an advanced undergraduate philosophy reading group that has not yet studied G. E. Moore. The goal is to teach his view that friendship and aesthetic appreciation are intrinsic goods, centered on his thesis of organic unity—specifically, the idea that combining friendship with a shared aesthetic experience can yield a whole whose value exceeds the sum of its parts, even when the individual pleasure in the experience is reduced. Set out the order of introduction: what distinction must be in place before organic unity can be introduced, and why the failure of simple hedonism should precede rather than follow the discussion of beauty. Choose one concrete carrying scenario—evaluate whether comparing a solitary museum visit with one shared with a close friend (where conversation slightly distracts from the painting but deepens the overall encounter) successfully illustrates the claim, and specify what that scenario must include and lack to avoid being read as mere social preference. Map the predictable misconceptions—particularly that Moore is reducing friendship and beauty to pleasant feelings, that organic unity is an additive rule ("friends plus art equals bonus points"), and that any social context automatically elevates aesthetic value—and indicate the exact point in the sequence where each should be defused. Provide one end-of-session check that distinguishes true comprehension of organic unity from rote agreement with Moore's valuation of friendship. Finally, explicitly note which element of Moore's broader argument in Principia Ethica should be acknowledged but left unexplained, with a brief justification for the omission. Write the answer in American English.
