Write a dialogue between two grad students, Mara and Jules, arguing about Plato's views on drunkenness — not the generic "he hated wine" take, but the actual tension between Laws I-II and the Symposium.

Mara's position: Plato is totally consistent. The Laws sets up drinking parties as a kind of character stress-test. You let people get tipsy under a sober supervisor, and you see who loses self-control. She thinks Alcibiades in the Symposium proves the point: he's completely wasted, Socrates stays stone-cold sober, and the contrast shows exactly why you'd want that supervisory framework. For Mara, alcohol is basically a diagnostic tool, and the Laws gives you the protocol.

Jules' position: That's a misreading that imports modern psychology. He says in Laws the drinking party is mainly about civic bonding and ritual cohesion, not individual testing. And in the Symposium, Alcibiades' drunken speech is framed as genuinely revelatory — he tells truths about Socrates that sober guests avoid saying. If Plato thought drunkenness just exposed pre-existing flaws, why center the dialogue's emotional climax on a drunk guy's praise? Jules also thinks Mara is ignoring that the Laws allows drinking precisely because it builds social trust, not because it's running a lab experiment on souls.

Make them actually talk to each other, not past each other. Mara needs to defend her stress-test idea against Jules' "social ritual" objection. Jules needs to answer why Socrates' sobriety matters so much if alcohol isn't diagnostic in Plato's eyes. Then inject this twist: have Mara suggest that Plato's concept of methē — being "drunk" — isn't limited to alcohol. She should argue it covers any state where you surrender rational guard, including the kind of philosophical eros that drives the Symposium's speeches. Jules should push back hard: if you collapse alcohol-drunk and love-drunk into the same category, you erase the Laws' careful regulation of wine and turn Plato into some kind of mystic about losing control being good.

Keep it casual, like they're in a coffee shop. Maybe include a typo or two — like writing "recieve" or mixing up "their/there" somewhere. Don't ask for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; just the dialogue itself.
