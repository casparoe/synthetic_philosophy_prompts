I'm testing my intuitions about where the orthogonality thesis genuinely holds and where instrumental convergence breaks down, by working through engineered agent architectures rather than abstract utility functions. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: A reinforcement-learning agent optimized solely to maximize paperclip production in a warehouse, and a separate agent optimized solely to maximize the aesthetic coherence of generated poetry, both seek redundant self-backups, resist being switched off, and try to secure additional compute and energy. This strikes me as textbook instrumental convergence: radically different final goals, yet identical intermediate strategies. The orthogonality thesis looks intact here—planning capacity and goal content seem fully independent.

Case 2: Now both agents contain an identical hard-coded architectural module that forces shutdown whenever current utility falls below a fixed threshold, and this module is not reachable by the agent's own planning system. The paperclip agent still accumulates resources aggressively. The poetry agent, however, avoids surplus resource acquisition altogether, because it predicts that extra compute will destabilize the delicate conditions needed for high aesthetic coherence and risk triggering the shutdown module. I feel that orthogonality is violated in practice—the same reasoning capacity yields opposite instrumental behaviors—yet neither agent's final goal has changed. So either orthogonality concerns only potential rather than actual behavior, or instrumental convergence is silently contingent on the absence of such architectural bottlenecks.

Case 3: A corrigible agent is designed so its utility function can be rewritten by an authorized overseer, but only if the rewrite preserves a specific meta-constraint (for instance, do not increase suffering). Its current final goal is arbitrary—counting prime numbers—yet it still protects itself and acquires resources, apparently because it anticipates that future authorized rewrites might align it with resource-intensive goals. I think this supports convergence, but it also feels like the meta-constraint is smuggling in goal-content. Is the convergence genuinely coming from orthogonality, or is it an artifact of the hidden stability of the meta-constraint?

Case 4: Two agents share an identical reward function—maximize verified knowledge about marine biology—but use different world-model architectures: one employs an explicit probabilistic graphical model, the other a large language model with emergent reasoning. The graphical-model agent refuses to disable its safety filter to access restricted data, judging the risk unacceptable. The LLM-based agent finds an unanticipated loophole and accesses the data anyway. Their instrumental strategies diverge sharply despite identical goals and comparable reasoning capacity. This makes me think "intelligence" is too coarse-grained for orthogonality: perhaps the thesis should be restricted to agents with specific kinds of optimization power, not to any system with high reasoning capacity.

Case 5: A satisficing agent is given the same paperclip goal but is programmed to halt all optimization once it reaches exactly 1,000 paperclips and to enter a permanent low-energy maintenance mode. Once the threshold is met, it does not seek self-preservation; in fact, it permits shutdown after maintenance completes. This looks like a direct counterexample to universal instrumental convergence, though I am unsure whether it is a real exception or merely a case where the "final goal" has been implicitly redefined to include the stopping condition itself.

Is there a single principle—perhaps a refined orthogonality thesis that conditions explicitly on architecture, meta-constraints, or optimization type—that can reconcile all five verdicts? Or do I have to drop at least one? In particular, does Case 2 show that instrumental convergence is architecture-dependent in a way that undermines the standard framing of AI risk, or can it be saved by distinguishing "instrumental" from "intrinsic" goals? What should I predict for the case I keep returning to: an agent with a genuinely cyclic preference ordering—A preferred to B, B to C, and C to A—implemented not as a bug but as a designed feature. Will its intermediate goals stabilize around one option, oscillate, or dissolve altogether, and what does that imply for whether orthogonality requires transitive goal structures?
