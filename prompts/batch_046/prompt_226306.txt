Consider the institutional pattern in major museums and university art curricula: works by artists later revealed to have committed serious moral wrongs are almost never deaccessioned or removed from syllabi on aesthetic grounds—the formal analysis of composition, influence, and technique proceeds unchanged—while the same institutions routinely rescind honorary titles, rename galleries, or reject new acquisitions from living artists accused of comparable conduct. The artwork remains in the canon; the artist's institutional presence is purged. What philosophical commitments about the ontology of art and the nature of aesthetic value does this split presuppose? Must one hold that the work is an autonomous object bearing value independently of its creator's agency, while institutional honor is essentially relational and therefore morally tainted? Or is the pattern compatible with moralism about art—say, that moral flaws can diminish aesthetic value—provided one adds auxiliary assumptions about tractability, historical distance, or the difference between appreciation and endorsement? Reconstruct the implicit theory that best explains why the object survives but the name does not, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it, and say whether it is philosophically consistent. Then describe what a curatorial and pedagogical practice built on a genuinely integrated view—where serious moral failure in the artist could diminish the aesthetic value of the work itself—would actually have to change: not just wall text or disclaimers, but the criteria for inclusion, the structure of formal analysis, or the distinction between studying a work and honoring its maker.
