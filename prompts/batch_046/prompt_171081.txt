I have been discussing distributive justice frameworks with colleagues, and we have begun applying them to an unconventional domain: the governance rules of collaborative research platforms. To test my own intuitions, I want to adapt Sen's liberal paradox—where individual liberal rights over personal spheres can conflict with Pareto-optimal social outcomes—into a setting that moves away from the familiar consumption-based examples. In my modified scenario, two researchers share a joint database architecture. Each researcher possesses what we might call an epistemic liberal right: the authority to decide whether a particular contested methodological assumption is adopted within their own local analytical pipeline. However, the shared platform enforces global consistency conditions, meaning that if Researcher A integrates the assumption into her local pipeline, the database automatically propagates related structural changes that constrain Researcher B's independent pipeline in ways B considers harmful—without B having any direct veto over A's local choice. The result is a Sen-style tension: respecting both researchers' liberal rights over their epistemic domains seems to prevent reaching a globally Pareto-superior state where both could work with a unified, consistent dataset.

Given this framing, please rank the following four resolution strategies into tiers—S-tier, A-tier, B-tier, and C-tier—according to how successfully each resolves the paradox while preserving both a meaningful notion of liberal rights and some form of collective rationality, specifically within this epistemic-collaboration context rather than the standard physical-consumption context. The strategies are: (1) restricting liberal sovereignty to domains that have no technical externalities on shared infrastructure; (2) redefining Pareto improvement to require unanimous consent across all affected epistemic domains; (3) instituting a hierarchical rule that grants lexical priority to local epistemic rights but allows for side-payment or compensation mechanisms at the platform level; and (4) denying that methodological commitments fall within the legitimate scope of liberal rights altogether, treating them instead as purely strategic choices subject to collective optimization. For each tier assignment, include one sentence explaining whether the strategy genuinely dissolves the tension or merely relocates it within the architecture of collaboration.
