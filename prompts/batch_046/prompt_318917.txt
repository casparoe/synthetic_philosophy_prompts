I'm trying to work out where I actually stand on whether a work's moral badness can ever be part of what makes it artistically good, rather than just a flaw you tolerate. Here are my gut verdicts, and they're a bit all over the place.

There's a novel with a brilliant plot structure that quietly endorses eugenics through who gets to survive — I'd still call it a great novel, but the endorsement is an artistic defect, not something that adds value.

Then there's a painting that depicts torture with this cold, clinical detachment, so you realise you're being made complicit in the gaze — I think that complicity is exactly the point, and it makes the painting better art, even though it's morally revolting.

A comedy routine that's technically perfect but spends half its time mocking a specific disabled person by name — I'd say it's bad art full stop, because the cruelty is the actual structure, not just content being represented.

There's an opera with gorgeous music that was commissioned by a war criminal and celebrates his victories — the music's still beautiful, but I wouldn't call the opera good art as a whole. That said, if I just hear the overture on its own, I do call that bit good.

Then there's this film shot using exploited child actors, where the real fear is visible in their performances and gives the scenes an eerie authenticity — I'm genuinly torn. Part of me wants to say that authenticity is an artistic achievement, part of me thinks it makes the film more disgusting, not redeemed.

Is there a principle that captures 1-4, or do I have to drop one? What's the real difference between case 2, where the moral rot seems to strengthen the art, and case 5 where I'm stuck? And is my split verdict on the opera in case 4 consistent, or am I just special pleading?

If there's a principle, I'd like it stated precisely — what clause handles each case, what it says about something I haven't mentioned, like an artist who deliberately injures someone to get the right brushstroke — and an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't save. If there isn't one principle, what are the competing ones and which verdict does each have to sacrifice?
