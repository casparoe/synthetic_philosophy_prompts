Write a short story that dramatises the philosophical concept of supererogation—acts that go beyond what moral duty strictly requires—by situating it within an invented institutional framework called the Registry of Reciprocal Obligations. In this system, every fulfilled obligation is logged as a closed account with no residue, but any contribution that exceeds duty is recorded not as a credit to the agent but as an unsettled moral overhang: a surplus that the agent is ethically prohibited from reclaiming, yet which leaves the recipient with a lingering structural indebtedness that subtly distorts all subsequent interactions between them. Your protagonist, a senior archivist named Elspeth, should undertake one clearly supererogatory act—voluntarily accepting permanent professional disqualification in order to prevent a falsified record from destroying a junior colleague’s career—after which she must navigate the social and psychological consequences of the Registry’s refusal either to erase the overhang or to allow her to convert it into moral standing. The narrative should avoid framing her choice as conventional heroism; instead, it should explore how the impossibility of claiming credit transforms her status within the community, particularly when the colleague, now indebted yet barred from expressing gratitude through any institutional channel, begins to treat her with a mixture of reverence and alienation. You may invent specific rituals or linguistic conventions of the Registry as needed, but keep the philosophical tension central: the act is genuinely good, genuinely unnecessary, and genuinely damaging to the agent’s moral position in ways that a simple calculus of benefit and cost cannot capture. Present the story in British English, using continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though writing a single uninterrupted letter.
