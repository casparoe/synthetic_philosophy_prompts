I understand that Hume denies beauty is an objective property of objects, locating it instead in the sentiment of the perceiver; yet he also insists that not all sentiments are equal, proposing an ideal critic whose refined judgment sets the standard. Recently, while looking into the neuroscience of aesthetic experience, I encountered arguments—drawing on predictive-processing models—that what we call delicacy of taste might just be lower noise in hierarchical Bayesian inference, allowing certain brains to minimize prediction error more efficiently when viewing particular forms. This made me think: if Hume's ideal critic is essentially a statistical outlier with optimized perceptual machinery, then hasn't modern neuroaesthetics shown that his standard is not a normative ideal at all, but a descriptive cluster of biological parameters? More specifically, if cross-cultural studies consistently show preference peaks around specific fractal dimensions and symmetry ratios, shouldn't we interpret Hume's framework as an early, pre-scientific attempt to describe these neural regularities? And if so, does the philosophical debate about objectivity versus subjectivity in beauty become obsolete—replaced by an evolutionary account of why our brains converge on the same patterns? I'd like an answer in American English that explains whether this reading of Hume is coherent, or if there's a conceptual mistake in treating his normative argument about correct judgment as if it were competing with empirical explanations of why people agree.
