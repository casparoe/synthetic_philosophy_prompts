I have been thinking on my own about that old logical puzzle Lewis Carroll set up, where a tortoise keeps pressing Achilles to supply yet another rule every time Achilles tries to apply an inference. It seems to me that anyone who claims to have justified deduction — not merely described how people reason, but shown why they are entitled to do so — ought to be able to pass a clear set of tests. Before I read any philosophers on this, I want the specification written out.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of the justification of deduction must satisfy. For each requirement, state the specific case or consideration that makes it necessary — for instance, the tortoise's infinite regress, the fact that competent reasoners apply rules without being able to articulate them, or the need to distinguish knowing how to apply a rule from knowing that the rule is correct. Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect the prejudices of particular camps, such as whether circularity is always fatal, whether psychological facts can enter into logical justification, or whether the account must cover every valid inference form or only a minimal base. If you can see a subset of these desiderata that are jointly unsatisfiable — perhaps because blocking regress requires a premise that invites circularity — say so explicitly, since that tension is what I want to understand.

Then, very briefly, indicate how three familiar directions fare against your list: a view that treats logical rules as justified by their place within a formal system; a view that grounds justification in the psychology of human reasoning; and a view that holds deduction to be self-justifying or constitutive of rationality, requiring no further defense. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves rather than on the scorecard; I am trying to learn what to ask of such a theory, not which philosopher wins.

Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. I want the conceptual map, not directions to the library.
