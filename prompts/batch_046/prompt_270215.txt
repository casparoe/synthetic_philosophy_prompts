A close friend is directing an indie film about her recently deceased uncle, a local labor organizer with no wider public profile. She intends to merge several minor real incidents into a single invented confrontation with a factory manager and to put sharply worded speeches into his mouth that capture sentiments he held but never actually expressed. She grants that he cannot suffer harm, but she describes her unease as an “epistemic debt”: she worries she is corrupting the shared local record that family and historians will later treat as authoritative. She asks whether there is any coherent obligation to the dead that does not depend on consciousness, and whether labeling the film “inspired by true events” rather than “based on a true story” changes the moral calculus. What is the philosophical structure of her dilemma, and is her idea of an epistemic debt to the dead defensible?
