I have been turning over, without any formal training in philosophy or biology, that old analogy in which an ant colony is compared to a single mind — I believe it comes from a dialogue called the Ant Fugue, where an ant named Aunt Hillary imagines her colony as one thinking creature. I want to know what it would actually take for a theory to settle whether a colony like that really has a mind of its own, rather than being only a swarm of simple parts. So please lay out the desiderata: the full list of requirements any adequate account of ant-colony cognition — whether affirmative, negative, or partial — must satisfy. For each requirement, name the specific observation or thought-experiment that motivates it, such as colonies solving maze-optimization problems that no individual ant understands, maintaining colony-level behavioral patterns despite total worker turnover each season, or functioning with no central nervous system at all. Mark clearly which demands are non-negotiable and which are camp-specific preferences — for instance, whether a mind must involve unified subjective experience, whether every mental state must be reducible to individual ant mechanics, or whether functional information-integration is sufficient on its own. Identify any pair or subset of these desiderata that appear jointly unsatisfiable, especially if one camp insists on irreducible phenomenology while another insists on complete decomposability into ant-level rules, and say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out rival views. Finally, briefly score the three main stances — the superorganism view, the distributed-cognition view, and the view that the mind-analogy is purely metaphorical — against your list. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I need a framework I can work with on my own. Keep the weight on the criteria, their motivating cases, and their internal conflicts, not on a history of who argued what.
