Me and my friend got into it last night over a couple beers, arguing about Gödel's rotating universe and whether he actually proved time isn't real or just accidentally broke physics. We tried to formalize our disagreement and now we've got this chain that feels like it proves way too much, but I can't see where the sleight of hand is. We even started calling it the "Gödel mirror problem" — where the loop creates a mental reflection with no physical difference.

(1) Gödel's rotating universe allows closed timelike curves, so an observer can loop back and literally occupy the same spacetime point as an earlier version of themselves.
(2) At that point, the physical state of the local region is identicle — same particles, same fields, same brain chemistry if you scan them.
(3) But the looped traveler remembers taking the journey, and the "earlier" self hasn't experienced it yet.
(4) So at one spacetime point you have two mental states — one with the memory, one without — paired with exactly the same physical state.
(5) That violates supervenience: mental differences with zero physical differences.
(6) Gödel seemed to think the rotating universe specifically undermines the objectivity of time, making past and future as unreal as distant places, kind of like Kant said.
(7) But the problem in (4)-(5) has nothing special to do with rotation or circular time; you could reproduce it in flat Minkowski space by cloning a brain and implanting false memories in one copy.
(8) So if Gödel's argument works, it doesn't just show time is unreal — it shows mental states don't depend on physical states in any universe, which is a way bigger deal.
(9) And if mental states float free of physics, then Gödel's own mathematical reasoning — which he trusted as objective — was just mental noise with no physical or logical grounding.
(10) Therefore either the rotating universe doesn't actually allow self-intersection with identical physics, or Gödel's philosophical conclusion is totally disconnected from his metric, or we've accidentally proved that physics and math are both impossible.

I'd really like to keep (6) — I think Gödel genuinely was after an idealist conclusion about time — and I'm not attached to (9) since it sounds melodramatic. But tell me which premise or step is actually the weak link, why it looked okay when we were writing it, and what each standard position — maybe the 4D block-universe view, the growing-block view, and the presentist view — has to pay to reject it.
