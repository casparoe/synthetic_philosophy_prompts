I am delivering the opening remarks this Thursday for an interdisciplinary workshop titled "Explanation Without Before and After." The audience is divided evenly: philosophers of physics and cosmologists who work with Gödel's rotating-universe solution and its implication that closed timelike curves undermine a globally directional, objective time; and scholars from literature and ethics who have never encountered that cosmological model. Two months ago, my brother—who left physics for another career—died suddenly. He had long insisted that linear narratives of grief and recovery are consoling fictions, and I find myself structurally unable to invoke any progressive story about healing or moving forward in these remarks. I want Gödel's challenge to objective temporal flow to do real structural work, not decorative work: the speech should use the possibility of circular time in rotating universes to frame the workshop's central problem—whether scientific explanation necessarily presupposes an asymmetric temporal order—while implicitly honoring my brother's refusal of linear consolation. The remarks should run approximately six minutes, be rigorous enough that the physicists recognize the reference without becoming a lecture on general relativity, and remain fully accessible to the non-specialists without patronizing them. Crucially, they must avoid false comfort, afterlife imagery, or any suggestion that loss is followed by closure. Please write the opening remarks. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the speech should stand entirely on its own.
