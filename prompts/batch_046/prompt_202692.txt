I am revising our university's speech-review guidelines and need a practical evaluation protocol grounded in Mill's harm principle for cases where the alleged harm is not direct emotional or physical injury, but what I call deliberative corrosion: speech that degrades the shared epistemic conditions—evidence norms, mutual accountability, and informed choice—without targeting any particular individual. Turn Mill's self-regarding/other-regarding distinction into a step-by-step procedure that a non-philosopher faculty panel can apply in thirty minutes to a specific campus event or publication. The protocol must include: (1) a filter separating direct harm from structural corrosion; (2) a marketplace-of-ideas exemption that asks whether the speech, despite its corrosive effect, contributes to long-run error correction; and (3) an explicit flag for indeterminacy in Mill's framework, especially when strategic misrepresentation serves a putatively corrective purpose. Apply it to three cases: (a) an invited speaker who denies well-established scientific consensus without engaging counter-evidence; (b) a student publication that circulates fabricated data to provoke debate about research ethics; and (c) a case where your procedure recommends restriction but that result conflicts with strong liberal intuitions—identify the precise judgment call. Be explicit about what the protocol cannot settle and where it risks misclassifying protected dissent as harmful corrosion.
