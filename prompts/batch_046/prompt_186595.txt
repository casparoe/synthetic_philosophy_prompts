You are a philosopher of religion and political theory with particular expertise in Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor and its implications for the ethics of authority. Before giving your final answer, please take a moment to brainstorm carefully: lay out the competing interpretations of the Inquisitor's core claim—that human beings are structurally incapable of bearing the burden of radical freedom without suffering, and that legitimate authority should therefore absorb the costs of choice on their behalf—without assuming there is only one way to formalize it.

My dissertation advisor applies this framework to modern institutional design, and I want to test the consistency of her judgments. Her verdicts so far are these. First, she endorses a welfare architecture in which the state assigns every adult a vocation and domestic partnership, while protecting an inviolable private sphere of religious practice and artistic expression; she argues this removes the terror of material and social choice without touching the soul's capacity for transcendence. Second, she endorses a democratic system that preserves all formal rights and electoral mechanisms, but deploys algorithmic recommendation and social architecture so thoroughly that citizens rarely encounter genuinely opposing worldviews—dissent remains legal but is rendered psychologically invisible; she calls this the purest modern expression of the Inquisitor's method, since freedom is technically present but weightless. Third, she endorses a pervasive therapeutic and educational culture that teaches citizens to interpret every life outcome as the product of systemic forces rather than personal agency, thereby dissolving guilt and the anxiety of responsibility, even though no institution makes decisions for anyone; she regards this as adequate, because the burden of explanation—and therefore of choice—has been relocated onto the collective structure itself. Fourth, she rejects a voluntary community in which each member must make a single irrevocable vow to one of several paths, with full knowledge of the permanent consequences, and then live within that constraint for life; she says this forces the weak to suffer for the sake of the strong, which is precisely the cruelty that Christ's gift of freedom demands and the Inquisitor refuses.

Now consider a new proposal: a constitutional order in which all economic and familial outcomes are determined by a transparent, publicly audited lottery, with no governing authority and no individual discretion. Citizens are taught to regard outcomes as pure fortune, and a comprehensive pedagogical system ensures that no one experiences regret or the sense that they could have done otherwise. The system explicitly denies that anyone—neither state nor citizen—bears responsibility for any result.

Predict my advisor's verdict on this lottery order. Identify at least two plausible rules that jointly fit her first three endorsements and her rejection of the fourth, explaining precisely what each rule takes to be the Inquisitor's essential criterion—whether it concerns the phenomenological absence of anxiety, the structural relocation of decision-making to an identifiable authority, the preservation of an illusion of freedom, or something else. Say which rule you think she is actually running, with reasons drawn from the pattern of her judgments. Then state what the lottery case predicts under each surviving rule, and name one further hypothetical scenario—different from both the lottery case and her earlier four—that would most sharply distinguish the interpretations that remain standing after this test. Be explicit about where they diverge, and ground your reasoning only in the internal logic of her own precedents, not in external authority.
