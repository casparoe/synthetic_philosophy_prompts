In philosophy of mind and metaphysics, interpretations of Berkeley's master argument have been deadlocked for decades between two camps that cite the same passages in the Dialogues and the Principles yet never converge. The modalists read Berkeley as relying on a tacit inference from the limits of conceivability to metaphysical impossibility: if I cannot form the idea of a tree existing without being perceived, then an unperceived tree is impossible. The intentionalists deny that any modal premise is present; they claim the argument turns solely on the intentional structure of ideas, since every idea necessarily has a perceiver as its intentional object, so an idea of an unperceived object is conceptually incoherent rather than metaphysically impossible. Both sides agree on the textual evidence, both reject the other reconstruction, and neither has moved. My hypothesis is that the stall is not about Berkeley's text but about a hidden disagreement over whether conceptual incoherence can ever ground a metaphysical conclusion—masked by shared vocabulary about ideas and conceiving. Is this diagnosis correct, or is there a substantive disagreement about mental representation, a formal disagreement about the logic of conceivability, or an empirical disagreement about the cognitive architecture of imagination doing the work? Diagnose it concretely: is this a verbal dispute over what conceiving means, a case of two groups answering different questions—historical interpretation versus philosophical validity—under one heading, or a disagreement about bedrock standards of philosophical argument that neither side can justify to the other? Be specific about what each camp actually says, not just labels. Then say what, if anything, would count as progress: a rigorous distinction—for instance, between phenomenal unperceivability, where no sensory access is available, and structural unperceivability, where the representational format itself excludes the content—that dissolves the ambiguity; a formal result about modal epistemology; an empirical finding about imagination; or should we conclude the question is ill-posed because it conflates epistemic limits with ontological claims? I want an honest structural diagnosis, not a survey of interpretations.
