my advisor grades history papers on whether they achieve "objectivity" and her track record is weird but not random: she praised a study of medieval guilds that traced contracts with zero moral language; she tore apart a dictator biography that judged actors by modern human rights norms; she approved a Marxist labor history that opened with "this is my framework" and never let the frame choose sources; she rejected a cultural history that used novelistic narration while claiming neutrality; she passed with conditions an oral history where the researcher had family ties to the events but quarantined that in a seperate section.

now a new proposal: a 1970s energy policy history written by a climate activist. it relies only on declassified government docs, but uses "negative space cataloguing" — listing every topic the archive silently omits and treating those silences as structurally weighty without speculating on intent. it also applies "prospective retrospection," framing each decision as viewed from 2050, though the author calls this pure rhetoric, not a moral stance. the paper ends with an explicit call for present-day policy reform.

if she wants to stay consistent with herself, whats the verdict? which precedent is the closest analog? if her past calls genuinely underdetermine it, what principle is she adopting by accepting vs rejecting? and what further case would actually tell us whether she's running a Ranke-style "no presentism" rule, a Carr-style "standpoint transparency" rule, or a Hayden White-style "narrative form reveals ideology" rule?

also, which structural feature of this proposal is actually doing the work — the activist conclusion, the 2050 framing, or the cataloguing method — and if you strip out the framing but keep everything else, does her answer flip?
