I am preparing an essay for my philosophy of medicine course on the conceptual coherence of recognizing brain death alongside circulatory death. My current view is that the whole-brain criterion is biologically unstable. We declare a patient dead only once all brain functions—including brainstem reflexes—have irreversibly ceased, yet we routinely maintain that patient on mechanical ventilation with a spontaneous heartbeat and warm, perfused tissues for days in order to preserve organs for transplantation. If the heart can sustain circulation and tissue oxygenation without any neural direction, then neural tissue cannot be the "integrative center" of the organism in any robust biological sense; the cardiovascular system is evidently capable of maintaining organic integration independently. From this, it seems to follow that these patients are not biologically dead at all, but are kept in a state of suspended animation by technology, and that "brain death" functions as a legal convention rather than a biological discovery. Given this, shouldn't we eliminate the brain-death category entirely and revert to a strict circulatory-respiratory standard—treating neurological assessment not as a criterion for declaring death, but merely as a prognostic tool for predicting imminent cardiac failure?
