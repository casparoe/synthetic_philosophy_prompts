I am working through decision-theory readings for an independent study project, and I have become stuck on expected-value fanaticism—the view that rational choice should maximize expected value without any upper or lower bound, so that a payoff large enough multiplied by a non-zero probability, however small, can swamp every finite alternative. Rather than testing the view on another toy problem like the St. Petersburg paradox, I want to see whether it can be disciplined about mapping onto a real historical decision, and whether the episode in turn exposes a gap in the theory.

Consider the decision faced by Manhattan Project leadership in July 1945, immediately before the Trinity test. The salient facts, as commonly reported, are these: prior theoretical work had raised the possibility—rapidly calculated to be astronomically unlikely—that a nuclear detonation might ignite atmospheric nitrogen and destroy all life on Earth. Bethe and others showed the risk to be effectively negligible, yet not demonstrably zero in a strict mathematical sense. At the same time, the anticipated benefits—ending the Pacific War, demonstrating strategic superiority, avoiding a costly invasion—were enormous but plainly finite. The test proceeded. Analyze this episode as a case study in expected-value reasoning. What should consistent EV maximizers have done, given a catastrophic tail risk with non-zero probability multiplied by an effectively infinite negative payoff? Does the theory explain the decision to proceed only if we interpret the scientists as treating the probability as literally zero rather than merely negligible, or does it miss something about how agents treat lexicographic or existential risks? Conversely, does the fact that they went ahead despite a non-vanishing catastrophic tail show that EV fanaticism is descriptively inadequate, or that it requires a hidden threshold clause—perhaps an inverted form of Pascal’s mugging, where an enormous loss at negligible probability should paralyze action, yet human decision-makers reject that paralysis? I am especially interested in where the mapping breaks down: who counts as the agent, whether collective institutional choice changes the calculus, and whether the theory is simply silent about how to weigh extinction-level outcomes against finite goods. Please treat this as a conceptual analysis rather than a historical inquiry; I am not asking for sources or disputed archival details, but for a careful account of what the theory illuminates, what it misdescribes, and what the Trinity decision reveals about its limits.
