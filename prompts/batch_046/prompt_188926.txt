I've been working through the conceptual relationship between Hobsbawm's invented traditions and Anderson's imagined communities, and I think the standard comparison misses a structural ambiguity that matters for how we evaluate institutional legitimacy. Hobsbawm treats tradition-invention as an elite strategy of concealment: novel practices are ritualized and backdated so that continuity with the past masks present power. Anderson treats the nation as an imagined community sustained by cognitive framing—print-capitalism, modular sovereignty, and the shift from sacred kingship to horizontal comradeship—which sounds less like deception and more like a distributed representational scheme. The conceptual issue is whether these are two species of the same phenomenon or genuinely different mechanisms. If invention requires hidden fabrication, then an institution whose origins are fully transparent—say, an open-source governance protocol or a forecasting tournament with published rules—cannot be an invented tradition, even if participants eventually treat it as if it had always existed. But if what matters is not concealment but the functional substitution of a constructed origin for a naturalized one, then explicit design might still produce the same legitimacy effect, just without false consciousness.

Please explain the conceptual distinction: what exactly separates an invented tradition from an imagined community at the level of mechanism rather than scale? And does the critical framework survive the case of explicitly designed institutions—where no one is fooled about origins—because the legitimacy still depends on treating the construct as timeless, or does transparency dissolve the Hobsbawmian critique entirely? I want the structural logic, not a bibliographic survey; I'm trying to see whether the framework applies to artificial coordination mechanisms the way it applies to nation-states.
