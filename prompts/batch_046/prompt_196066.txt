I'm a third-year PhD in moral phil and I've been stuck on something about indirect normativity that I want to turn into a six-month writing project, maybe for a small dissertation grant. The basic puzzle is this: indirect theories say we should follow rules or cultivate traits that don't directly maximize value, and they usually justify this by asking what fully rational agents would endorse after idealized deliberation. But here's the thing—if the deliberation is totally transparent, the agent sees that the rule is only instrumentally good, and that seems to undermine the kind of motivational grip the indirect theory needs. So I'm wondering whether we can construct a model of idealized deliberation that justifies indirect norms without requiring what I'm calling "deliberative opacity," where the agent somehow can't see through to the instrumental justification. Or does every workable model just collapse back into direct normativity?

What I want funded is basically one paper that frames the question precisely: is there a coherent "deliberative architecture"—by which I mean a specific pairing of epistemic access, motivational stability, and normative authority—that lets an agent rationally endorse indirect rules while knowing full well why they exist? The existing literature, at least the parts I know, treats cognitive significance as a single thing and either assumes transparency is always available or smuggles in opacity without defending it. I think splitting the deliberator's knowledge into three seperate layers might show that only one combination survives, but that combination forces us to accept a pretty weird view about self-knowledge in practical reasoning. My method would be mostly conceptual: build the taxonomy of architectures, run them through standard puzzle cases like the honest-shopkeeper variant but modified for indirect rules, and check which ones avoid collapse. A useful result wouldn't be "indirect theories win" but a clear map of which psychological assumptions you're buying if you want indirectness to work.

If the project fails, it fails because every architecture either collapses into direct evaluation or requires a psychology so unrealistic that no actual agent could inhabit it. I can do this in one semester since I'm not running experiments—just sharpening the framework and seeing where the cracks are. I don't need a bibliography for this pitch; I'll fill in the citations to Railton, Parfit, and whoever else myself. Does this sound like a tractable angle, or am I missing an obvious reason why the transparency problem is already solved?
