Psychiatric nosology sorts people into discrete categories—depression, PTSD, bipolar disorder—even though epidemiological data show symptoms distributed continuously without sharp boundaries. Clinicians treat these boxes as real enough to guide treatment and billing, and patients often report that receiving a diagnosis is validating because it supplies a framework for interpreting their own experience. Given Hacking’s claim that human kinds are reshaped by the act of classification itself, what philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Must the categories track pre-existing natural kinds despite the continuous distribution, or can the institution be justified entirely by looping effects—that labeling alters self-conception in ways that make the category causally real? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether moving to purely dimensional assessment—scoring mental health on continuous scales with no discrete boxes—would destroy the clinical and personal value diagnosis now provides, or whether that value could survive without the categorical ontology.
