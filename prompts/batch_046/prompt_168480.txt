As a novelist accustomed to inventing characters whose actions are explained by beliefs and desires I never take to be literally inside them, I have watched the philosophical stalemate over eliminative materialism with a particular frustration. For decades, eliminativists have argued that folk psychology is a stagnant theoretical framework destined for replacement by neuroscience, while realists about propositional attitudes have insisted that belief and desire are indispensable explanatory kinds that no brain-science vocabulary can capture. The exchange has not moved: the same objections about predictive success, about the semantics of mental content, and about the continuity of scientific practice are repeated, and no empirical result or formal proof seems capable of ending it. My hypothesis is that this is not a hidden empirical disagreement but a deeper divergence about what counts as an adequate explanation—perhaps even about whether belief names a natural kind or a hermeneutic tool—so that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading. Is that right? Or is the deadlock maintained by bedrock intuitions about interiority that neither side can argue for? I want an honest diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is—substantive, verbal, taxonomic, or ill-posed—supported by what each side actually says, together with an assessment of what, if anything, could break it: a neuroscientific discovery, a conceptual distinction, or perhaps a clarification drawn from narrative practice, where we routinely deploy mental-state attributions as useful fictions without ontological commitment. Please do not survey the literature; diagnose the structure of the impasse, and say whether progress should be expected at all.
