Modern digital publishing platforms and algorithmic newsfeeds are architected to maximise real-time engagement—quantified through likes, shares, and velocity metrics—while systematically eroding authorial presence and contextual framing. The audience is thereby treated not as discrete individuals who must personally appropriate truth, but as an aggregated, anonymous mass whose collective reaction is the sole arbiter of value. In light of Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public—particularly his analysis of how anonymity dissolves individual responsibility and how the public functions as a phantom that replaces qualitative commitment with quantitative aggregation—reconstruct the implicit philosophical anthropology and epistemology that this institutional design presupposes. Does the practice necessarily commit to a view of the self as essentially social and reactive, or is it compatible with an account of the individual as prior to the collective? Distinguish what the architecture strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, assess whether its parts presuppose consistent commitments about truth and authorship, and specify what structural alterations to the feedback and attribution mechanisms would be required if the underlying theory of the public were replaced by Kierkegaard's. Present your analysis in British English.
