I serve as the evaluation officer for a metropolitan transit authority, and each month I receive proposals to alter routes, frequencies, or fares based on consultants' claims that they have inferred underlying passenger preferences from ridership counts, fare-card taps, complaint logs, and occasional surveys, using methods drawn from inverse reinforcement learning. Because my team lacks the statistical training to audit the underlying reward-modeling machinery, I need you to write for me a reusable decision procedure — expressed entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, tables, or enumerated lists, as though it were a single long letter of instruction — that we can apply to any proposal without having to rebuild the reasoning from scratch. The guiding principle should be that observed travel behavior reveals an underlying preference structure only when a specific set of identifiability conditions hold, and that in public transit these conditions are systematically undermined by what I will call structural invisibility: passengers adapt their expectations and travel patterns to whatever network already exists, suppress complaints about chronic failures once those failures become normalized, and can never reveal preferences for service configurations that are not currently available. Your procedure must present, in strict sequential order, the questions my team is to ask of any proposal, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the precise point at which insufficient evidence causes the procedure to terminate with a rejection of the inference claim, and the distinct points at which the remaining ambiguity is not factual but normative — for instance, whether accommodating preferences that have been distorted by long-term service degradation is itself a legitimate policy aim — at which stage the question must be handed to our authority's values committee rather than resolved technically. The procedure should be explicit about its own failure modes: identify one category of proposal for which it will systematically produce a false negative and one for which it will systematically produce a false positive, and explain the mechanism behind each. Then apply the procedure in detail to three concrete proposals: first, a recommendation to reduce late-night bus frequency on a low-ridership corridor, justified by the inference that passengers do not value that service; second, a recommendation to add express service along a corridor where current ridership is high but survey data show extreme dissatisfaction with crowding; and third, a recommendation to eliminate a poorly used route serving a low-income neighborhood with no alternative connections, where ridership is low but community testimony describes a hidden dependency on the route for essential travel. For each case, show the step-by-step reasoning through your procedure, flag the exact moment where the decision is handed off to values rather than settled by inference, and ensure that in at least one case — I anticipate it will be the third — the procedure produces a recommendation that conflicts sharply with the intuitive moral response my team is likely to feel, then explain what that conflict reveals about the hard limits of inferring human preferences from behavior alone. Finally, state clearly what kinds of proposals, data environments, or social conditions lie entirely outside the competence of this procedure, and do not include any bibliography, reading list, citation, request for further sources, or reference to specific authors or papers by name.
