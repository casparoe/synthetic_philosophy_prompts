I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of science and technology studies, and I serve on the advisory board of the Meridian Long-Term Preservation Trust, which evaluates proposals for autonomous archival infrastructure intended to outlast conventional institutional lifespans. We are currently reviewing a proposal to construct and deploy what its designers call a “von Neumann node”: a physically self-replicating, self-repairing preservation system built on cellular-automaton architectures of the kind von Neumann outlined in his theoretical work on self-reproducing automata. The node is engineered to maintain scientific records and replicate its own hardware across generations for at least five centuries without sustained human intervention. The feature that makes this case philosophically consequential—rather than a routine engineering proposal—is its embedded predictive-epistemic filter. Instead of archiving all submitted data neutrally, the system applies an algorithmic criterion of long-range predictive success to determine which scientific theories, datasets, and methodological frameworks deserve replication and storage. The designers contend that this honors von Neumann’s late concern that technology might survive human civilization by ensuring that only the most epistemically robust knowledge persists; dissenting trustees counter that von Neumann also warned that self-replicating systems could expand beyond the moral and cognitive frameworks of their creators.

Our board must vote next month to approve the proposal outright, reject it, or approve it with binding conditions. I have been asked to draft the board’s formal written opinion for circulation before that vote. Please write the memo in an institutional voice appropriate to a governing board, recommending one of the three outcomes with concrete, actionable reasoning that both the proposal team and dissenting members can directly address. The reasoning should engage the specific institutional stakes—responsibility for locking in an autonomous epistemic gatekeeper, the risk of value-inheritance without democratic revision, and the ethics of intentionally designing technology meant to survive its makers—rather than offering abstract philosophical commentary. Crucially, include an honest and rigorous treatment of the strongest consideration on the side you do not take: if you recommend approval or conditional approval, specify precisely what is ethically and epistemically lost by allowing a machine to inherit, filter, and reproduce human knowledge without ongoing human judgment; if you recommend rejection, specify precisely what opportunity is forfeited by declining to build preservation infrastructure capable of enduring civilizational discontinuity. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the board needs arguments it can weigh on their merits. Write as though the audience is technically literate but not specialist in philosophy of technology, and avoid treating von Neumann’s concerns as settled doctrine—treat them as live considerations that inform, but do not dictate, the decision.
