In our upper-level African philosophy seminar, two classmates—Maya and Julian—got into a sharp disagreement about whether Ubuntu entails a metaphysical dissolution of the individual or merely an ethical emphasis on interdependence. Maya began by arguing that the Ubuntu proverb describing a person as constituted through other people is not merely a moral exhortation but an ontological claim: personhood is literally constituted by communal relations, which means that liberal talk of pre-social individual rights is conceptually incoherent within an Ubuntu framework. Julian replied that this overreads the tradition; he said the saying is about moral development and social recognition—how one becomes fully human—rather than a denial of individual existence. To support this, he pointed out that in traditional Ubuntu practice, exile is considered the gravest punishment precisely because it severs the relations the individual needs to flourish, which only makes sense if there is a distinct subject who suffers that loss.

Maya countered that Julian was begging the question by presupposing exactly the individual subject he was trying to defend; she insisted that the suffering of exile is itself relationally constituted, so there is no pre-relational self that endures harm. She also noted that South Africa’s Constitutional Court has invoked Ubuntu in dignity jurisprudence, which suggests it does substantive metaphysical and legal work rather than functioning as mere ethical advice. Julian responded by distinguishing what he called constitutive relations, where parts retain independent identity but form a larger whole—like distinct notes in a chord—from fusional relations, where parts lose all independent standing. He argued that Ubuntu requires only the former: relations enable and shape personhood without dissolving the individual into the collective, and that the Court actually uses Ubuntu to enrich interpretations of individual dignity rather than to erase the rights-bearer. Maya then said that Julian’s chord analogy smuggles in pre-social individuals, since in Ubuntu moral standing is not possessed by the notes outside the chord at all; she claimed his distinction saves liberal individualism by disguising it as relationality.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, treating the four successive contributions (Maya’s opening, Julian’s reply, Maya’s counter, Julian’s response) as distinct moves. For each, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is right about which specific point—whether Ubuntu is metaphysical or ethical, whether exile proves an individual subject, what role Ubuntu plays in constitutional interpretation, and whether the constitutive versus fusional distinction holds—and identify anything important that both parties miss about how relational personhood actually functions.
