I've been drafting an essay for a rationalist audience that tries to dissolve the boundary between the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. The core claim is that both arise from the same structural feature: a rational agent is required to hold beliefs that are individually well-calibrated but jointly violate a closure principle—aggregation across independent events for the lottery, and consistency for the preface—because the agent lacks the computational resources to verify the joint entailment. I argue that once we model belief as a resource-bounded partition of epistemic space rather than a logically closed set, the tension in both cases reduces to a trade-off between local calibration accuracy and global coherence costs. I then sketch a resolution inspired by statistical learning theory: treat high-confidence beliefs as hypotheses in a model class with a VC-dimension bound, so that holding N independent high-confidence beliefs is rational only if the complexity penalty allows it.

I'd like focused comments on three points. First, is the unification genuinely illuminating, or does it paper over the fact that the lottery involves probabilistic aggregation across independent events while the preface involves self-reference and consistency? Second, does the VC-dimension analogy do real conceptual work, or is it merely decorative formalism that obscures the epistemology? Third, are there standard objections in the literature—say, from Kyburg, Foley, or Makinson—that this framing misrepresents or fails to address?

Please respond with attention to conceptual coherence and accuracy rather than style or bibliography.
