I am a PhD student in philosophy of biology and ethics, and next week I present to a joint seminar of comparative cognition researchers and moral philosophers. My thesis is that the scale argument for insect sentience—multiplying vast numbers by a low prior probability of phenomenal consciousness—fails to yield actionable moral obligations because it implicitly treats sentience as a uniformly distributed binary property across invertebrate taxa. Once we replace that uniform prior with taxon-specific, graded probabilities (for instance, comparatively high for decapod crustaceans and certain hymenopterans, near-vanishing for dipteran larvae), the expected-value aggregation fragments into epistemically incommensurable sub-populations; the argument therefore collapses not into a denial of sentience, but into a paralysis of comparative weighting that undermines its own practical conclusion.

The audience comprises: two empirical researchers committed to precautionary moral consideration of bees and cephalopods; a philosopher of science who is sceptical of ethical arguments that outrun available measurement frameworks; a decision theorist who accepts expected-value reasoning but is alert to “probability laundering”; and several environmental-ethics graduates broadly sympathetic to extending moral status to invertebrates yet hostile to unreflective utilitarian aggregation. Please prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as these specific interlocutors would phrase them. For each, indicate what I should concede, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the objection I am hoping nobody raises—specifically, the charge that my fragmentation strategy either covertly relies on a discredited essentialism about “insect nature” or, conversely, that it generalises to marginal human cases in ways I must reject. Include a brief reply strategy for each, suitable for a five-minute defence, and sketch the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.

If my framing rests on a confused premise, or if this task as posed cannot be done well, say so plainly rather than complying politely. Write in British English.
