I have noticed a recurring argumentative move in discussions of chaos and determinism for which I lack a term. A speaker asserts that the universe is fully deterministic. When a critic cites long-range weather unpredictability, the speaker replies that determinism holds only at the microscopic scale. If the critic then invokes quantum indeterminacy, the speaker shifts once more: determinism concerns the structure of physical law, not the predictability of outcomes at any particular scale. Each reply is locally defensible, yet together they insulate the original claim from empirical challenge without ever explicitly abandoning it.

This does not appear to be a standard auxiliary hypothesis, since no new empirical mechanism is added; nor is it straightforward Popperian immunization, because defenders typically concede that the predictive failures are genuine, they simply relocate the thesis. Is there an established concept for this specific pattern of successive scale-shifting or domain-restricting defenses of determinism? If so, how does it differ from ordinary ad hoc rescue and from simple equivocation on determinism? If no established label exists, can you coin one, define it precisely, and supply boundary cases that separate it from conventionalist stratagem?

Please indicate how confident you are in identifying—or denying—an established concept for this pattern, and single out the specific comparison, whether to auxiliary hypotheses, conventionalism, or equivocation, about which you are least certain.
