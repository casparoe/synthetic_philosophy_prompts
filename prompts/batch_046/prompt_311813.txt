I am a doctoral candidate in moral psychology presenting next Thursday to an interdisciplinary workshop on autonomy and influence. The audience includes two Rawlsian political philosophers committed to the publicity condition, a social psychologist who takes situationist critiques of second-order capacities seriously, and a behavioral economist who insists that any normative distinction must be operationalizable. My thesis is that the standard autonomy-based line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion collapses once we recognize that reflective access is not uniform across influence pathways. I defend a structural criterion I call the filter-access condition: influence is manipulative not when it bypasses rationality entirely, but when it selectively disables the target's second-order monitoring of the influence mechanism itself, typically by saturating pre-reflective affective commitments that are structurally insulated from revision. I apply this to algorithmic recommendation architectures and to classical rhetorical techniques that exploit temporal pressure rather than false belief. Crucially, take a committed position: either defend the filter-access condition as genuinely distinct and defensible against these objections with concrete arguments, or concede that it collapses and explain precisely where and why the collapse happens.
