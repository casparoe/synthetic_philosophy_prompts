I am preparing to moderate a colloquium interview—recorded for a philosophy podcast—with a philosopher who has recently defended the view that honoring sunk costs is not invariably irrational. Her position turns on a critique of what she calls the "time-slice" model of agency implicit in standard decision theory: she holds that rational agents are not isolated momentary maximizers but are subject to "commitment-integrity constraints," which generate forward-looking reasons to maintain continuity with past investments that are constitutive of one's practical identity. To avoid collapsing all persistence into rationality, she distinguishes "resource-sunk" costs, which ought indeed to be ignored, from "identity-sunk" costs—such as sustained mastery of a craft or long-term relational commitments—which she analyzes not as backward-looking loss-avoidance but as conditions of diachronic narrative coherence.

Please draft a sequence of eight to ten interview questions that press this view at its actual joints rather than inviting restatement. Specifically, I want: (i) an opening question that requires her to individuate "identity-sunk" costs without reducing them to subjective preference or contingent life-plans, paired with a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to objective structures of practical identity; (ii) a question about the hard boundary case—say, an abusive or demonstrably destructive commitment—together with a follow-up anticipating her probable distinction between authentic commitment-integrity and pathologically distorted persistence; (iii) a question probing whether her constraints are genuinely distinct from a long-run consequentialist calculus that values stability for its welfare benefits, including the follow-up she is most apt to offer; and (iv) a concluding question asking what kind of discovery—conceptual or empirical—would actually count against her view, for instance a demonstrated dissociation between narrative coherence and agent welfare, rather than being accommodated as another case study. The tone should be formally precise but accessible to an educated non-specialist audience, and I want no generic biographical or methodological questions.
