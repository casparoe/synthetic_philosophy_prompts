I hold a qualified Jamesian position against Clifford's evidentialism: Clifford's prohibition applies cleanly to ordinary propositional assent, but misses a distinct class of practical stances I call framing commitments—orientations that regulate inquiry without functioning as empirical hypotheses. For instance, adopting the stance that human dignity is non-negotiable may be permissible in the absence of decisive evidence, since it operates as a condition for moral reasoning rather than as a belief about the world. What would count against this? I want decisive defeaters separated from merely awkward ones: arguments that framing commitments collapse into standard beliefs, findings in moral psychology that erode the distinction, concrete cases where such commitments cause epistemic harms that Clifford's norm would have prevented, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by redefining "belief." Rank these by how damaging they are, say which ones a committed defender should concede rather than absorb, and assess whether that insulation is a genuine problem for a view of this kind.
