I've spent a lot of time thinking about David Lewis's account of convention—how people solve coordination problems when they all know that they all know the right solution, like driving on the right side of the road. Lately I've been stuck on a different kind of case: large-scale interaction through platforms where the rules that shape outcomes are hidden from the users themselves. People still fall into stable patterns—who speaks first, how long they wait, what they treat as fair—but they can't check whether everyone is facing the same hidden payoff structure, and they may not even realize there's a hidden structure at all. It feels like a convention, but Lewis's common-knowledge requirement seems broken in a structural way, not just accidentally. So I'm trying to figure out which open questions in this area actually deserve attention if you want to understand how norms evolve when information is systematically inaccessible.

Should the priority be revising the common-knowledge condition so it tolerates partial or design-blocked awareness? Or is the deeper issue whether these patterns are conventions at all, rather than emergent routines shaped by invisible optimization? There may also be a separate problem about how norms stabilize when participants can't verify the underlying game they're playing. I'd like you to rank the three or four most important conceptual problems here and defend the ordering. For each, explain whether it sits upstream of the others, whether it can be advanced by philosophical argument alone or needs empirical input, and what kind of finding or counterexample would cause you to reorder them. I'm preparing this for a discussion group with no formal background, so I want a clear, defended agenda—not a bibliography, not citations, and not a survey of every theory in the field. Which lines of work matter most, and why?
