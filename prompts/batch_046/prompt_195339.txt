Design one 90-minute essay question for an upper-level philosophy of religion or metaphysics final exam on Buddhist anatta (no-self) and personal identity. Do not ask students merely to summarize the five-skandha reduction or to compare Buddhism with Hume’s bundle theory. Instead, embed the following conceptual scenario in the prompt: imagine a philosopher, M. R. Chen, who defends a two-tier “aggregative resonance” account of anatta. At the conventional level (sammuti-sacca), Chen claims that what we call a person is not a self but a dynamically sustained pattern—aggregative resonance—of causal-functional dependence among the five skandhas (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness), producing a stable pseudo-center that supports conventional ascriptions of responsibility and continuity. At the ultimate level (paramattha-sacca), Chen holds that even this resonance dissolves upon analysis into discrete, momentary dharmas with no enduring structure. Ask students to evaluate whether Chen’s framework enables Buddhist reductionism to address a Parfit-style fission problem—specifically, a case in which a single psychological-causal stream divides into two functionally integrated successors—without either (i) covertly reintroducing an essentialist self that survives division, or (ii) collapsing into an eliminativism that renders conventional moral responsibility incoherent. The question should require students to operate at both the conventional and ultimate levels, to compare Chen’s resonance with standard Abhidhamma causal-series explanations of continuity, and to state whether resonance adds genuine explanatory power or is merely a redescription.

Provide a model answer of approximately 600 words. Then provide a marking scheme out of 25 marks that distinguishes an A-range answer (22–25) from a B-range answer (16–19) by specifying the exact moves required at each level: (1) precise articulation of Chen’s two-tier distinction and the role of aggregative resonance (5 marks), (2) rigorous application to the fission case showing how resonance behaves under division (5 marks), (3) comparison with standard Abhidhamma causal-series accounts and identification of what resonance adds or fails to add (5 marks), (4) evaluation of the essentialism charge—whether the pseudo-center functions as a hidden self (5 marks), and (5) evaluation of the moral-responsibility/eliminativism charge at the conventional level (5 marks). For each criterion, include a one-sentence note on the characteristic failure mode (e.g., describing resonance as “like a soul,” conflating the two levels, or ignoring the fission mechanics).
