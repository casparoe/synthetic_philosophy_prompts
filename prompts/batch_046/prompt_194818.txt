I'm a retired biologist who only recenty got into philophy, and I'm giving the eulogie for my old mentor who died last month at 87. He spent forty years on sponges and cnidarians — things with no brain to speak of — and he was a sincere Epicurean: he really held that death is nothing to us, not as a slogan but as something that shaped how he lived. He left a note asking nobody say he's watching over us or in some better place. The room will be half scientists who knew his work, half family who don't care about neurons. I need a short speech that uses that view structurally — it should comfort people without saying anything false by his lights, and explain why grieving is okay even though he isn't suffering or missing us. Please weave in his idea that human death is continuous with the non-experience of simpler organisms, not a special horror that needs solving. Don't quote texts or cite anyone; I don't have his exact words, so don't put anything in quotation marks attributed to him. Write it casual enough for my niece, but with enough substance that the biologists nod.
