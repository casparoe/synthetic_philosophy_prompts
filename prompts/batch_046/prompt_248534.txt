Analyze the July 2020 open letter published in Harper's Magazine—warning against illiberal social coercion and signed by writers, academics, and public figures—and the subsequent distributed backlash against several signatories, including institutional disinvitation, petition campaigns for professional removal, and widespread social withdrawal of cooperation, through the lens of cancel culture, deplatforming, and social sanction understood as punishment. Do not treat the backlash as a single authoritative judgment; instead, examine it as a polycentric punishment event that lacks any centralized sentencing authority. Before applying the theory, distinguish carefully between two forms of sanction that I want you to hold apart: targeted deplatforming, meaning the removal of a specific venue, platform, or opportunity intended to alter a signatory's future conduct or expression; and ambient social cooling, meaning the diffuse, non-targeted withdrawal of collegial and public engagement that functions primarily as expressive condemnation without a defined endpoint or explicit behavioral demand. For each form, map precisely who held punitive power, what conduct was being sanctioned, and whether the sanction operates primarily as retributive, deterrent, rehabilitative, or expressive punishment; then assess which standard conditions of legitimate punishment—recognized authority, proportionality between sanction and offense, procedural due process, and public transparency of rules—are satisfied, violated, or rendered conceptually inapplicable by the dispersed, non-state nature of the event. Next, run the analysis in both directions: say what the punishment framework explains well about the dynamics of the backlash, such as the expressive function of collective withdrawal or the deterrent effect on potential future signatories, and where it misdescribes or is silent, such as whether distributed sanctions can ever satisfy the authority condition; and in turn say what the episode reveals about the limits of applying state-centered punishment theory to decentralized social sanction. Finally, take a committed position with explicit reasons: is this episode best understood as a legitimate exercise of collective moral accountability in which distributed sanction replaces missing institutional mechanisms, or as a structural malfunction of punishment logic when authority, proportionality, and due process are fragmented across an uncoordinated public? Be disciplined about the mapping—identify the players, the options, and the payoffs rather than forcing the theory to speak where it has nothing to say—and explicitly mark any point where the conceptual framework is silent or indeterminate rather than inventing an answer for it.
