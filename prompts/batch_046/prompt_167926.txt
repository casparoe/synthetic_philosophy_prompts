I'm prepping a seminar on whether explaining an atrocity ever lets the agent off, and my study group cooked up this arguement that seems to trap us between letting everyone off and turning everyone into a monster. I want to see if there's an exit I missed.

(1) Any full explanation of why someone took part in mass killing has to fall into one of two bins: either the situation did the work (peer pressure, orders, bureaucratic diffusion) or the agent's own stable character did it (hatred, cruelty, deep ideological commitment). There's no third box. (2) Now suppose the explanation is mostly situational, like Browning's picture of Reserve Battalion 101. Then theres a nearby possible world where the commander is sick that morning and the men go home; in that world the same guys don't kill anyone. So the act wasn't modally robust -- it wasn't tied to who they are across situations. (3) If an act isn't modally robust in that way, blaming the agent is like blaming a leaf for falling left instead of right. The character wasn't in the driver's seat. (4) So if Browning is right, the perpetrators aren't really morally responsible; they're just ordinary people caught in a bad flow.

(5) On the other hand, if the explanation is character-based, like Goldhagen's willing executioners, then these people had a disposition to murder that would have shown up in lots of different settings. But then they aren't "ordinary" in any interesting sense -- they're essentially monstrous, and Goldhagen's claim that ordinary Germans did this collapses. (6) So we're stuck: either situation explains it and nobody is to blame, or character explains it and nobody is ordinary. (7) But we want to say that some perpetrators were both fairly ordinary and still blameworthy. (8) Therefore one of (1)-(3) or the hidden link between (2) and (3) has to go.

My instinct is I want to keep (1) and (7), which means I probably have to attack the slide from (2) to (3). But I can't see exactly where the modal-robustness thing sneaks in an extra premise. Is there a hidden assumption about what responsibility requires? Or is (1) the real problem -- maybe there's a third kind of explanation involving agential identification with the situation? Where does this go wrong, and what does fixing it cost?
