I'm putting together a long-ish piece on AI alignment for a science magazine, and over coffee with a friend who works in robotics I ended up in this rabbit hole about low-impact systems. I think I've landed on a bunch of views but they feel like they're pulling in different directions and I can't tell which ones I actually have to drop. So here's where I'm at, roughly:

First, I definately think the idea of a value-neutral impact measure is worth saving. If defining avoid side effects requires us to already have solved value alignment — like knowing exactly which outcomes count as good or bad — then low-impact AI is basically just redundant, it's not a seperate design goal.

But at the same time I keep noticing that any time you try to specify what a side effect is, you're already sneaking values in through the back door. Is shifting the temperature of a room by half a degree a side effect? That depends on whether anyone cares about the temperature, right? So side effects can't be purely physical.

That said, I'm still attracted to this physical-baseline idea: measure the AI's causal footprint — energy used, atoms displaced, that kind of thing — and compare it to what human activity in that region already does. That feels like it should work without invoking values.

Except then I run into this thermostat case I keep thinking about. An AI running climate control has almost zero physical footprint, but it's clearly reshaping the world in a massive way compared to not running at all. And whether that counts as impact seems totally value-laden: if you value stable temperature it's designed-in, if you don't it's an intrusion.

Then there's the counterfactual baseline issue. I want to say an AI is low-impact if the world with it running is close to the world without it running. But constructing that counterfactual requires a full causal model of everything, which is basically smuggling in a specification of what matters and what doesn't. So I'm back to values.

Lately I've also been playing with this idea I call the shadow footprint — the ways an AI blocks other actors from taking actions in the same space, not just its own direct effects. So a low-impact system shouldn't just be physically small, it should avoid crowding out human decisions. But measuring that crowding-out requires knowing which human choices we value preserving, so once again I'm importing values.

And yet I still want to defend low-impact design as a real, practical target for small-scale systems — not just a philosphical mirage. I think there's something there about constraining the scope of action rather than the content of values.

So do these hang together? Or am I helping myself to the physical-baseline view and the shadow footprint in ways that the value-neutrality claim and the thermostat case rule out? Which commitment is the weak link, and what's the cheapest repair? Also, if I drop the value-neutrality requirement, does that kill the whole concept or just change its role?
