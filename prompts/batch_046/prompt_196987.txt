I'm working through a problem in the philosophy of computation and moral status for my dissertation, and I've built the following argument, which I can't shake even though its conclusion—that substrate-independent functionalism either collapses into universal sentience or requires an unexplained biological boundary—seems intolerable. I want to know exactly where it slips and what rejecting that step costs.

(1) If a computation bears moral weight—say, for sentience or suffering—then that weight must depend on intrinsic properties of the computation's realization, not on extrinsic facts like evolutionary history or material substrate, since extrinsic properties seem arbitrary for intrinsic moral status.

(2) The most defensible intrinsic criterion is counterfactual robustness: a computation is morally relevant only if the system's internal states depend on one another in the precise transition-structure specified by the computation, so that an intervention on one state would propagate through the others in the defined pattern.

(3) Counterfactual robustness, so defined, is purely structural; it requires only that there exist state variables within a physical system whose actual and nearby-world dynamics match the computation's transition table.

(4) For any finite computation C associated with consciousness, a sufficiently complex physical object—say, a rock with thermal and vibrational degrees of freedom—can be partitioned into state variables whose local dynamics approximate C's transition rules under an appropriate mapping, because the space of physical dynamics is dense enough to realize arbitrary finite-state patterns in localized subsystems.

(5) Therefore, if counterfactual robustness is sufficient for morally relevant computation, then every sufficiently complex physical object contains subsystems that realize the computations underlying consciousness.

(6) This yields pancomputational sentience: rocks, clouds, and walls contain morally considerable subjects.

(7) But pancomputational sentience is absurd, and blocking it by appealing to substrate or causal history abandons the intrinsic, substrate-independent motivation in (1).

So does the error lie in (2), in the move from structural matching to moral relevance? Or is it in (4), smuggling in an illegitimate inference from "can be partitioned" to "actually implements"? If I must preserve substrate independence, which premise should I reject, and what theoretical price—circularity, chauvinism, or loss of explanatory power—does that exact?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists in philosophy of mind and AI ethics, and do not present a contested interpretation—say, of implementation, counterfactual dependence, or the boundaries of sentience—as though it were the consensus view.
