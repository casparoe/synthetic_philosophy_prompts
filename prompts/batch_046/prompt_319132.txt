I am working on a chapter about first-person self-location for my dissertation in philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to the conceptual architecture of Dennett's brain-transplant scenario—the one in which a philosopher's brain is removed to a vat while a duplicate is implanted in his body. Most literature treats this as a puzzle about whether identity follows the brain or the body, but I am trying to isolate a more specific tension: the protagonist asks "where am I?" using a first-person indexical that seems to presuppose a single, unified point of view, yet the scenario splits the conditions for legitimate self-attribution between psychological continuity, which remains with the original brain receiving radio signals, and sensory-motor integration, which remains with the body walking around. Could you explain the conceptual issue here—specifically, whether this ambiguity shows that first-person self-location operates with a different logic from third-person identity judgments, and whether we should understand the "where" as a spatial claim, a functional claim about the locus of agency, or a narrative claim about which center of gravity governs the self-model? I am not looking for a summary of Dennett's own answer; rather, I want to understand whether the puzzle genuinely threatens substrate-independent accounts of the self, or whether it merely demonstrates that indexical belief requires an embodied anchor even when psychological continuity is preserved in a separate substrate.
