Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps in philosophical aesthetics concerning the paradox of tragedy — specifically, why audiences voluntarily seek out and report satisfaction from artworks that centrally depict profound, unmerited suffering. The two camps are: (1) the Transformative-Balance Camp, which holds that enjoyment is possible only because negative affect is genuinely converted, counterbalanced, or superseded by higher-order goods (cognitive understanding, emotional release, or moral affirmation), leaving the final experiential state net-positive or at least fully integrated; and (2) the Residual-Form Camp, which holds that enjoyment arises directly from the appreciation of formal coherence and narrative meaning, that the suffering depicted retains an unassimilated normative weight they term "moral remainder," and that this remainder is not dissolved into pleasure but coexists with it as a structurally necessary, non-compensated feature of the experience.

The document must contain:
1. A numbered Agreed-Claims section. Include claims both sides can sign about: the phenomenological report that audiences feel both distress and satisfaction; the inadequacy of explanations that treat the distress as merely instrumental or illusory; the observation that audiences repeatedly return to the same tragic work rather than substituting novel suffering; and the shared commitment that any adequate theory must explain why the suffering is not merely tolerated but actively sought out.
2. A disagreements table with two columns, each phrased in wording its own camp would accept. Cover at least: (a) whether the negative emotion is retained in the final experiential state or fully transformed; (b) whether "moral remainder" — defined as the audience's sustained recognition that the depicted suffering is undeserved and unredeemed — is a necessary structural feature of tragic appreciation or a mischaracterization of unresolved emotional tension; and (c) whether repeated exposure to a single tragedy strengthens an integrative mechanism or deepens an unreconciled dissonance.
3. A Cruxes section identifying at least three precise sub-questions such that resolving one would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete comparative case or structural feature both sides agree is relevant — for instance, a tragedy in which suffering is explicitly redeemed by a clear moral lesson versus one in which suffering is structurally unredeemed — and record in advance each camp's prediction about how audience response and reported satisfaction should differ between the two cases. Include one crux specifically on whether a narrative that eliminates moral remainder (e.g., suffering that is clearly deserved and narratively inevitable) produces a different quality of enjoyment or fails as tragedy altogether.
4. A brief concluding assessment stating which disagreements are genuinely empirical, which are conceptual, and whether any portion of the dispute reduces to a verbal disagreement about the meaning of "enjoyment" or "negative affect."

The statement should be formal and polished. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke highly specialized theories or authors beyond the framing given above; reference only widely recognized narrative structures if needed for the comparative cases, and paraphrase without quotation marks. Ensure that the injected framing — particularly the role of "moral remainder" and the comparative redeemed-versus-unredeemed structure — requires constructing original reasoning rather than reproducing a standard summary of catharsis.
