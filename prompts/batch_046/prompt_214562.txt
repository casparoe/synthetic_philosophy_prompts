I'm reading Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay on the meaning of life for a course in early twentieth-century philosophy of value, and I am trying to extract his implicit criteria from the cases he treats. He appears to tie meaning exclusively to activity pursued for its own present sake—what he calls Spiel—while withholding it from any action directed at a future end, however worthy. From this I derive the following pattern of verdicts he would presumably endorse: a child building sandcastles purely for the joy of building lives meaningfully; a monk praying solely in order to secure salvation does not; a researcher pursuing a problem only for professional prestige does not; a parent nursing a sick child out of present, unconditional affection does; and a musician playing alone, with no audience or recording, purely to inhabit the music, does. Now consider a constructed case designed to test the boundary. A retired logistics planner spends a decade in her basement constructing a meticulously accurate scale model of a medieval city, complete with functioning miniature plumbing. She keeps rigorous engineering ledgers, corrects structural errors, and holds a clear vision of the completed whole, yet she insists she works solely for the daily pleasure of solving spatial problems; she never intends to exhibit the model, and she claims she would feel no loss if she died with it unfinished.

Predict Schlick's verdict—would he regard her life as meaningful?—and identify the precise rule that best explains the pattern across all six cases. Then specify which structural feature of the case is doing the work, what both rules converge on if that feature is removed, and what a single further hypothetical case—one that keeps intrinsic motivation constant but varies only the presence or absence of a long-term internal goal—would reveal about whether Schlick's concept of Spiel can accommodate disciplined, project-based activity. Finally, please state what would change your mind: which interpretation of Schlick's distinction between play and work, if shown to be more faithful to the 1927 text, would overturn your prediction, and where your reasoning is most vulnerable to misreading his concept of youth or present-orientation.
