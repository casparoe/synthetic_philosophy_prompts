I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I serve as the methodological consultant to a regional public-health funding board that reviews small pilot grants quarterly. The board receives four to six proposals per cycle—typically $50,000 to $200,000, with a thirty-day decision window—and my role is to supply a written epistemic assessment in about forty-five minutes for each proposal. The board members are epidemiologists and clinicians; they understand study design and basic statistics but have no background in formal epistemology. Increasingly, we receive proposals whose anticipated results would largely confirm mechanisms already well supported in the literature—what Bayesian epistemology treats as the problem of old evidence—yet apply them to marginally different populations, delivery formats, or co-intervention settings. I need a reusable decision procedure my board can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation against funding on purely epistemic grounds, and where it must hand the decision to a human judgment about values, equity, or institutional mission rather than facts.

Please ground the procedure in the Bayesian problem of old evidence—specifically, the challenge that evidence already incorporated into prior beliefs should not rationally shift posterior belief—while also incorporating an operational extension I am calling a contextual depreciation check. This check asks whether the prior probabilities for the target mechanism have lost relevance since the original confirming studies were conducted, due to demographic shifts, changes in standard-of-care, or alterations in the causal environment. Treat this depreciation check as a niche but defensible addition that must survive simplification for non-philosophers.

The deliverable should be an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, with explicit stop points. For each step, state the question, the criterion for a sufficient answer, and whether a negative result ends the review or triggers a value-judgment handoff. Flag the cases the procedure cannot settle—particularly where urgency, equity, or prestige might justify funding despite negligible epistemic return—and be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as formal rules.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

1. A forty-person trial replicating a well-established analgesic pathway in adults, using a delivery vehicle with a minor formulation change, with an expected effect size identical to prior meta-analyses and no meaningful contextual shift.
2. A study testing a nutritional intervention previously confirmed in stable, housed populations, now delivered to unhoused adults in a shelter system where medication adherence, sleep architecture, and co-morbidity profiles differ substantially.
3. A proposal to replicate a well-characterized cognitive-behavioral sleep intervention in college students—a population already extensively studied—with an expected effect size matching prior work and no contextual depreciation, but bundled with a high-profile campus wellness initiative that the board is politically inclined to support.

In at least one case—specifically, the one where the board’s intuitive preference conflicts with the procedure’s recommendation—the answer should be the one the board will not want to hear, and the procedure should explicitly state what it cannot do in that situation.

Please write the answer in American English, without bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not include requests for sources; the board has no access to them during review.
