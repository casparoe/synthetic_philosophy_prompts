ok so i'm writing up my views for a pragmatism seminar and want a consistency check before i present them. here's roughly where i've landed, probably with some internal messiness:

(1) democracy is best understood as cooperative social inquiry, not preference aggregation—policies are working hypotheses tested by social consequences, and ends are themselves hypotheses we revise in light of experiement. (2) this means fallibilism runs all the way down: there are no moral or political truths that sit outside the inquiry process and act as untouchable, a priori constraints. (3) direct participation by affected citizens is epistemically essential, not just symbolically valuable—lived experience supplies data that experts and representatives miss, so cutting people out distorts the inquiry. (4) but in technically dense domains (epidemiology, climate modeling) democratic deliberasion should be largely deferential to expert bodies; citizens frame the problem and set values, but shouldn't second-guess complex models. (5) the justification for democracy is purely instrumental—it works better as a problem-solving method, not because collective self-rule expresses some deep intrinsic dignity or autonomy.

(6) still, basic liberties—free speech, association, dissent—function as non-instrumental safeguards; they prevent inquiry from being captured by majorities or power, and i'd protect them even if suppressing them temporarily improved outcomes. (7) there are objective failure conditions: if a policy produces widespread avoidable suffering, that's not just "a different value choice" but a genuine experimental failure, regardless of how inclusive the democratic process was. (8) lately i've been thinking the 'public' in dewey should be reconceived through distributed cognition—democratic inquiry isn't really mass deliberation but an interlocking network of institutions (courts, peer review bodies, regulatory agencies) that correct each other's biases; citizens contribute mainly through these mediated structures rather than direct input on every technical issue.

do these hang together? i'm especially worried about (3) vs (4)—if lived experience is necessary data, why sideline citizens in technical evaluation?—and (5) vs (6), since pure instrumentalism seems incompatible with treating rights as non-instrumental guardrails. also (2) and (7) feel like they might clash: if nothing sits outside the process, how can suffering be an objective failure? and does (8) quietly undermine (3) or actually resolve it? please audit the package: rank the tensions by severity, say for each which commitment has to bend and what the cheapest revision is, and flag any that are only apparent. don't just say "it depends on priorities"—tell me where the set is actually unstable. and is there any hidden commitment in here—say, about moral knowledge or the nature of expertise—that i haven't made explicit but that my views depend on?
