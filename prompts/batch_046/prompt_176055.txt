As a retired physicist now working through the philosophy of social science, I have noticed a recurring explanatory structure in treatments of Weber’s Protestant ethic thesis that I do not know how to classify. A writer first claims that an ascetic cultural value caused a specific institutional form—say, the rationalization of capital accounting. Then, when accounting for why that value persisted across generations, the same writer appeals to the selective pressures and incentive structures generated by the institution itself. The two explanations are not woven into an explicit feedback model with an entry condition or a mechanism for breaking the circle; instead, culture is granted causal priority in one context and structure in another, as if they were interchangeable primary causes. I have seen this in several discussions of Weber, but I am unsure whether it is recognized as a distinct failure mode. Does this pattern—mutual causal substitution between culture and institution without a specified loop mechanism—have an established name in the sociology of explanation? How precisely should it be distinguished from Weber’s own idea of elective affinity, and from ordinary reciprocal causation? If no precise term exists, what would be the clearest way to define its boundary conditions, particularly the absence of an explicit mechanism that would allow one to test where the circle begins?
