Next Thursday I am presenting a paper at our philosophy of decision theory seminar, and I would like a speaker’s prep sheet for the question period. My thesis is that the strategy-stealing assumption in Newcomb-style problems depends on an implicit asymmetry: the predictor is treated as possessing fixed epistemic access to the agent’s strategy, whilst the agent is treated as strategically free, yet if we model both parties within what I call a reciprocal commitment frame—where each party’s predictive model is updated in response to the other’s anticipated update—this asymmetry collapses, and the standard evidentialist justification for one-boxing loses its force. I am not denying that evidential reasoning can be rational in general; I am arguing specifically that the strategy-stealing formulation illicitly imports a one-directional dependency that dissolves once strategies are indexed to temporal stages of mutual modelling.

The audience is precisely the mixture that will not allow this to pass unchallenged. There are three committed evidential decision theorists who have worked through the formal derivations of choice-theoretic analogues to the Born rule and who regard causal decision theory as a conceptual dead end; two causalists who believe Newcomb’s problem is dissolved once we draw the correct causal graph; a philosopher of science who is deeply suspicious of any framework that multiplies entities beyond the standard Bayesian network; and a visiting economist concerned with mechanism design, who will care less about metaphysics and more about whether my reciprocal frame is implementable or merely decorative. The session allows forty-five minutes with roughly twenty minutes for questions, so I need concise, defensible replies rather than extended digressions, and I need to know in advance where my position is softest—particularly on whether I have merely relocated the asymmetry rather than dissolved it, and on whether the reciprocal frame can be operationalised in any actual predictive practice.

Please draft the objections in the order they are likely to arise, ordered by how damaging they are, and phrase each one in the vocabulary that subgroup would actually use. For each objection, indicate what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest, and provide a reply I can deliver in under ninety seconds for the four most dangerous objections. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—the one that exposes the gap between my formal framing and any existing predictive institution—together with an honest reply that admits where the framework remains speculative rather than operational. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I need strategic preparation, not citations.
