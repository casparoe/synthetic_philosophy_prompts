For a long-form piece I am preparing on levels of explanation in collective biology, I have been examining recent models of slime-mold pathfinding that invoke distributed gradient encoding: individual cells respond solely to local chemical concentrations, yet the colony-level network topology appears without any single cell representing the overall route. This has led me to apply a familiar argument from the philosophy of mind to this biological case. Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion problem holds that if every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause, then any higher-level cause—whether a mental state or a collective decision—is either redundant or violates the closure of the physical domain. Transposed to the slime mold, it seems to follow that either the local biochemical gradients do all the causal work, rendering swarm-level pathfinding a purely descriptive convenience, or we must attribute downward causal powers to the colony that conflict with biochemical closure. Consequently, I am inclined to think that biological emergence is caught in the same dilemma as mental causation: it must collapse either into eliminative reduction or into something like vitalism about group behavior. Is this the right way to understand the philosophical stakes, or does the analogy with mental causation rest on a false presupposition about what emergence in biology actually claims?
