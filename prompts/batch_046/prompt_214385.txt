I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy of mind, and I am preparing the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on self-deception. I want one essay question that can be answered in about ninety minutes and that cannot be resolved by reciting the familiar paradox—that if the deceiver and the deceived are identical, self-deception appears to require simultaneously believing p and not believing p with full awareness. To force students past this rehearsal, the question should introduce what I will call the protocol-blindness scenario: a researcher designs a double-blind clinical trial, knows the randomization algorithm in full, but deliberately partitions her own cognitive access so that she treats any inference she makes about a particular subject's treatment assignment as epistemically disqualified and refuses to integrate it into her working beliefs. The exam prompt should ask whether this partitioned state counts as self-deception in any philosophically interesting sense, and whether explaining it commits us to a divided-self model, a deflationary reinterpretation of belief, or a rejection of the claim that deception necessarily involves an intention to mislead. I want a model answer of approximately 600 words, together with a marking scheme structured around five must-hit analytical moves, each assigned marks that sum to 20, plus a one-line note on how candidates typically fail each move. The scheme must distinguish an A answer—which explicitly shows why the standard paradox is inadequate to the protocol-blindness case, draws the functional analogy to interpersonal deception without collapsing the two, and critically evaluates at least two of the three theoretical options while identifying a specific cost for each—from a B answer that merely restates the standard paradox and applies it superficially to the scenario. Please write the exam question, the model answer, and the marking scheme.
