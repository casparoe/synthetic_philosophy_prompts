Since retiring from a career in experimental physics, I have been working through Bernard Williams's moral philosophy in a more sustained way than my earlier, casual reading allowed, and I find that two of his best-known arguments do not line up as neatly as introductory texts suggest. The first is the integrity objection: a utilitarian calculus can require an agent to sacrifice the ground projects that give his life its point and coherence, treating those commitments as merely one factor among others in an aggregate welfare computation. The second is the argument usually associated with the phrase one thought too many, in which a man saves his wife from drowning rather than a stranger, and the moral failure is not in the choice but in the presence of an explicit deliberative step—say, a calculation of special obligations or overall welfare—between the perception of danger and the act of rescue. Many readers present these as twin pillars of Williams's attack on impartial moral theory, but I want to understand whether they are genuinely independent structures or whether one reduces to the other.

My current, somewhat tentative framing is that the integrity objection concerns the distribution of practical authority—specifically, which commitments are protected from being overridden by external moral demands—whereas the one-thought-too-many case concerns the procedural form of reasoning, particularly the corruption that occurs when explicit justification intrudes into relationships that should be governed by direct, unmediated response. Yet I worry this distinction collapses under scrutiny. If ground projects are protected precisely because they constitute the agent's practical identity, then the spouse-rescue case might be protected by the very same identity-constituting structure, which would make the second argument a localized instance of the first rather than a separate critique. On the other hand, if the spouse case is genuinely about the inappropriateness of deliberation rather than about the protection of identity-constituting commitments, then one could imagine an agent with no special ground project involving his spouse who nonetheless ought not to calculate—suggesting the arguments have different scopes and possibly incompatible normative bases.

Can you compare these two Williamsian arguments in terms of their underlying logic, the specific kinds of moral theory each threatens, and whether a coherent reading exists on which they remain genuinely independent? I am especially interested in whether there is a principled way to separate questions about which projects deserve protection from questions about how reasoning should proceed within intimate relationships. Please also indicate what specific argument or counterexample—perhaps a defense of impartial reasoning within marriage, or a reinterpretation of ground projects as themselves requiring justification—would cause you to revise or abandon the distinction I have drawn, and point out where in your analysis the reasoning is most likely to fail.
