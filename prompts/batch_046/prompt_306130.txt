I sit on the exhibit review board for our regional science museum. Next month we're voting on a proposal titled 'Reconstructing Deep Time: Chronology, Megaliths, and Lost Civilizations' — it's an interactive gallery that presents Fomenko-style statistical reconstructions of medieval and ancient timelines, frames ancient-astronaut explanations for megalithic construction as 'testable alternatives,' and uses Atlantis specifically not as myth but as a methodological case study in demarcation: how do we tell a lost-civilization narrative from legitimate archaeology? The curators argue this teaches critical thinking and the philosophy of historical method, comparing it to how we display outdated scientific theories like phlogiston. Our advisory historian and one board member say it's different — Fomenko's chronology isn't just outdated, it violates well-established astronomical and textual constraints in ways that make it pseudohistory, not revisionism, and framing it as an open question gives it credibility. Our policy allows approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions (clear labeling, contextual framing, maybe a required companion panel). Please draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend one course with a concrete timeline — if approve-with-conditions, specify what conditions and by when they must be implemented. Take the curators' pedagogical argument seriously rather than just restating that the claims are false; engage the actual demarcation issue — what distinguishes legitimate historical revisionism from pseudohistory, and whether displaying a false theory inside an institutional frame can actually teach demarcation or inevitably endorses it. Keep it readable for non-philosophers but don't avoid the conceptual hard part.
