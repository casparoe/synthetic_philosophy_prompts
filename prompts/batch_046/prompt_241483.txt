In the fiction workshops I have taught in and the editorial guidelines I have encountered, there operates an unspoken rule: a premise may violate physics—ghosts, time loops, architectures that defy geometry—and still be welcomed as serious fiction, so long as the reader can form a vivid mental image of it. Yet when a premise involves what instructors call an "inconceivable" configuration—say, a consciousness that genuinely lacks any self-reference, or a society that organizes itself without any operative concept of causation—the manuscript is rejected not as fantasy but as a failure of imagination, as though the author has demanded that the mind perform an operation it structurally cannot execute. Curiously, these same workshops insist that a writer must be able to inhabit anyone—tyrants, mystics, minds separated by centuries or moral universes—and they treat psychological distance as never an excuse for flatness. I am puzzled by the asymmetry: physical impossibility is treated as a genre convention, radical psychological otherness as a professional duty, but certain conceptual or metaphysical structures are treated as hard boundaries on what fiction may legitimately ask a reader to entertain. What implicit theory of conceivability, and of its relation to metaphysical possibility, does this three-part practice presuppose? Does it assume that conceivability tracks possibility only within specific domains, or that there exists a distinctive modal imagination that collapses at conceptual boundaries while remaining intact across physical ones? Reconstruct the philosophy embedded in the practice, distinguish what it strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and indicate whether it is philosophically underdetermined—compatible, for example, with views that separate epistemic limits from modal limits, or with views that deny conceivability any evidential role in modal reasoning altogether. Then say what workshop pedagogy would have to change if that underlying view were abandoned. Please separate what is settled in the philosophical literature on conceivability and possibility from what remains genuinely contested among specialists in modal epistemology, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
