I am a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science drafting a two-page research proposal for a one-year fellowship in conceptual studies of consciousness. The proposal takes as its point of departure the classic thought experiment associated with The Mind's I—encoding Einstein's brain in a book—where every neural connection and representational state is symbolically preserved across static, readable pages. The standard literature divides over whether such an object could be conscious: functionalists typically answer yes if the causal-functional architecture is preserved regardless of substrate; biological or substrate-dependent theorists answer no because paper and ink lack the relevant physical properties; and some integrated-information accounts hold that the answer hinges on whether the encoding preserves a non-zero value of phi across the system. I think all three approaches miss a deeper structural issue, because they treat the book's lack of dynamics as merely an absence of processing speed that a sufficiently rapid reader could in principle compensate for, rather than as an absence internal to the substrate itself.

What I want to investigate is whether consciousness requires not merely representational content ordered in time or functional sequencing among states, but what I will call endogenous temporal binding: representational modifications that are causally produced within the substrate by its own immediately prior physical states, without mediation through an external interpreter. In the book, page 200 symbolically represents a brain-state that follows the state represented on page 199, yet the physical ink and paper at page 200 do not depend causally on the physical substrate at page 199 except through the perceptual and cognitive processing of a reader. Even a perfect functional encoding therefore relocates the causal topology of the system from the object to its user. My proposal asks precisely whether this absence of endogenous temporal binding necessarily disqualifies static encodings from consciousness, or whether a sufficiently complex representational structure could instantiate binding non-causally through pure symbolic architecture alone.

The proposal must accomplish four tasks within a realistic one-year scope with no empirical data collection. First, it must state the question with sufficient precision to yield a determinate answer. Second, it must show why existing functionalist, biological, and information-theoretic answers leave the question open—specifically, by demonstrating that they conflate symbolic temporal ordering with causal temporal dynamics. Third, it must outline the method: a conceptual analysis of the conditions for temporal binding in prominent theories of consciousness, assessing whether those conditions are substrate-relative or purely formal, followed by an application to the static-encoding case that determines whether any book-like structure could satisfy them. Fourth, it must specify what success and failure look like—success being a clear argument that either static encodings are necessarily disqualified or that a specific class survives, and failure being a demonstration that endogenous temporal binding collapses into ordinary functional sequencing and therefore adds no new constraint. Please write the response in American English, take a committed position with explicit reasoning rather than neutrally surveying options, and do not include any request for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, which I will supply myself.
