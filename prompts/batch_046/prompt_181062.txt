Specify the desiderata that an ontology of social generations—e.g., "Baby Boomers," "Generation X," "Millennials," "Gen Z"—must meet to be adequate: neither a mere demographic sorting device nor a biological natural kind. For each criterion, state the motivating case or empirical constraint: the boundary-arbitrariness problem (why 1980 rather than 1981?); the frequent dominance of within-cohort variance over between-cohort variance in attitudinal and behavioral data; the demographic necessity of separating cohort effects from period effects and life-cycle age effects; the persistence of collective self-identification even when statistical clustering is weak; and the phenomenon of "generational drift," in which the social meaning of a cohort label shifts as its members age without collapsing the cohort into a simple age-grade. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which reflect camp-specific commitments (e.g., sharp essential boundaries versus fuzzy, event-anchored membership; mind-independent existence versus purely institutional status). Identify any jointly unsatisfiable subset—especially between treating generations as kinds with deterministic membership conditions and accommodating continuous, context-sensitive historical socialization. End with a brief evaluation of three positions against the list: a strong reificationist view, a pure constructivist/marketing-category view, and a "practical kind" account that permits fuzzy boundaries anchored in shared formative events but rejects essentialism. Keep the weight on the criteria and their conflicts, not on the scorecard.
