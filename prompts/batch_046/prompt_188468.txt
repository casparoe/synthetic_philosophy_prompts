I work at a large fulfillment center that recently automated its entire physical sorting and packing operation. Rather than reducing staff, the company reassigned warehouse workers to what it calls “narrative logistics”: we write brief, imaginative descriptions of each package’s journey—small poetic fragments that customers read when they track an order. Management presents this as the natural outcome of technological progress: the machines now perform the necessary manual labor, freeing human beings to do creative, meaningful work. I ought to feel elevated, they suggest, from physical drudgery to artistic expression.

But the experience does not feel like freedom. My writing is governed by algorithmic metrics—engagement scores, sentiment analysis, efficiency targets measured in fragments per hour. The “creativity” is constantly A/B tested to maximize customer retention, and every word I produce belongs to the company. The exhaustion has changed its form, yet it is no less relentless, and my imagination seems to have been placed on a different kind of assembly line, monitored with even finer precision than the old conveyor belts.

Is management correct that this arrangement represents a genuine emancipation through automation? Or is there a philosophical mistake in assuming that once machines take over manual labor, whatever human work remains is automatically “free”? I am not asking for career guidance; I want to understand whether my discomfort points to a real distinction between being released from toil and being moved into another form of labor that merely wears the mask of freedom—especially when that labor is still owned, measured, and required.
