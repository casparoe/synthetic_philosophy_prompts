Me and a few friends have been arguing about Parfit's teletransporter for months and it's gone nowhere. We all agree on the phyiscal set-up - scan the brain, destroy the body, rebuild elsewhere with identical psychology. One lot says I'm dead and it's just a copy with false memories; the other lot says I survive because what matters is psychological continuty, not this particular lump of meat. We've both read the standard replies (branching, fission, the replication problem) but nobody shifts an inch.

My hunch is we're not actually disagreeing about metaphyscis at all - we're disagreeing about what kind of reason psychological similarity gives me to care about the future person. The anti-teletransport camp treats bodily continuty as a prerequisite for rational self-concern, whilst the continuty camp treats it as just one factor among many. But I'm not sure.

Here's the twist that's been bugging me: imagine the scanner doesn't destroy the original, it just makes a perfect copy. Then both live for thirty years. The original becomes a monk in Nepal, the copy stays in London as a banker. They share every memory up to the scan date, then diverge completely in values, projects, and emotional behaviour. If I ask the original monk 'are you the same person as the banker?', he says no - they've grown apart like twins. But the banker might say yes initially, then gradually feel less connection.

So is the debate stalled because it's really two debates - one about legal or biological 'identity', and one about the gradient of prudential concern? Or is there a hidden factual disagreement about whether psychological continuty comes in degrees? And if it's the latter, is there anything - an empirical finding about brain plasticity, a formal model of identity over time - that could actually move the needle? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on who's right, plus an honest take on whether progress is possible or whether we're just stuck with bedrock intuitions about 'me-ness' that no argument can shift.
