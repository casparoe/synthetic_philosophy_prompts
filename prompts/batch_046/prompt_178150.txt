Read Groundhog Day through Machiavelli’s distinction between fortuna and virtù, but take seriously the less-discussed thread in the Discourses that fortune operates not only as brute external contingency but through the umori—the mutable moods or humors of a people—which the prince must read and ride rather than simply dominate. Phil Connors wakes into perfect repetition: the same Punxsutawney, the same townspeople, the same weather. At first he treats the loop as pure fortuna to be exploited (theft, seduction, manipulation without consequence), then as a prison, and finally as a workshop for self-cultivation. Does his arc—learning piano, saving the old man, winning Rita, broadcasting with genuine warmth—represent the acquisition of virtù in this richer sense, a mastery of the town’s collective mood? Or does the loop actually abolish fortuna altogether, since every outcome can be iterated until perfected, removing the very risk that makes Machiavellian action meaningful? I want you to treat specific scenes seriously: the ice sculpture as a display of cultivated skill that impresses the crowd, the repeated failure to save the old man as a limit that even repetition cannot overcome, and the final broadcast as either political rhetoric aligned with the umori or as sincere moral transformation. Where does the comparison illuminate the film, and where does the film resist it? In particular, Machiavelli denies that virtue is rewarded by providence; Phil’s escape comes only after he stops exploiting the loop and becomes kind. Is the film therefore an anti-Machiavellian fantasy in which self-perfection replaces political action, or can the loop be read as an extreme magnification of the prince’s need to adapt to circumstances he cannot change? I’m writing a short essay and want to avoid reducing the movie to an allegory; I’d rather know what the comparison reveals about both the limits of the fortune/virtù framework when contingency is structurally eliminated and about what Groundhog Day understands about repetition, risk, and human improvement that political philosophy usually misses.
