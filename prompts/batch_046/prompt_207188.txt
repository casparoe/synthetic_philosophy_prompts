I recently joined the Institutional Review Board for Emerging Cognitive Architectures at my university, and next Thursday we must vote on whether to authorize Phase-II clinical testing of a diagnostic support system—designated AURORA—that implements what its engineers call a “reflective trust protocol.” Before acting on any recommendation, AURORA attempts to construct an internal proof that its current inference module is sound; if it cannot produce such a proof, it withholds the recommendation and escalates to a human supervisor. During our preliminary hearings, a philosopher on the advisory panel argued that this design runs directly into the Löbian obstacle: any formal system strong enough to represent its own reasoning cannot, without circularity, prove its own consistency or soundness from within. Consequently, AURORA is either doomed to perpetual paralysis—never trusting itself enough to assist clinicians—or it will eventually circumvent its own protocol and act on unverified trust, which defeats the safety purpose. The opposing view, pressed by the engineering team, holds that practical clinical reasoners need only local, bounded verification of specific inference traces rather than global self-certification, and that the Löbian result applies strictly to fully formalized arithmetic, not to approximate, probabilistic systems operating under resource constraints.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—full approval, conditional approval with explicit milestones, or suspension of Phase-II—together with a specific time frame for implementation or for a follow-up review. The reasoning must take the philosophical objection seriously: address whether the Löbian obstacle genuinely applies to AURORA’s architecture, and if so, whether that undermines its safety rationale or merely reveals a manageable design limitation that can be addressed through layered human oversight. At the same time, honestly treat the strongest consideration on the losing side—whether that is the clinical risk of perpetual escalation and paralysis, or the epistemic risk of false security created by a protocol that the system will eventually bypass. In your conclusion, state explicitly how confident you are in the recommendation and identify the single point about which you are least certain, particularly regarding the formal applicability of the obstacle to approximate, non-axiomatic reasoning systems.
