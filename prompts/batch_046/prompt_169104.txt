I am preparing the interview schedule for a special episode of my philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher—let us call him Dr. Elias Vorn—who has recently been defending an unconventional reading of the scale argument in insect ethics. Rather than treating the astronomical cardinality of insects as multiplying a small individual moral weight into an overwhelming aggregate obligation, Vorn argues that moral consideration is subject to what he terms a resolution constraint: our normative practices are structured to address individuated subjects, and when the potential subject pool exceeds the granularity at which agency can meaningfully distribute concern, the scale argument does not amplify obligations but transforms their kind. On his view, the low per-insect probability of sentience combined with vast numbers produces not a massive distributive duty but a systemic, background-level obligation to preserve ecological conditions, because attempting to apply distributive moral arithmetic across billions of non-identifiable beings collapses into practical incoherence. He further maintains that this position avoids both the moral-catastrophe conclusion that insects dominate our ethical landscape and the dismissive conclusion that they matter not at all, by locating their moral status in a gradient zone where welfare is real but not individuated.

I want eight to ten interview questions that move well beyond asking him to restate this framework. Finally, include a sequence that makes his systemic-rather-than-distributive conclusion progressively harder to maintain: begin with the standard interventionist objection about why not simply fund pesticide reduction, then move to whether his view licenses indifference to localized, high-confidence cases of insect suffering, and end with a question about what practical decision procedure could distinguish legitimate systemic concern from rationalized neglect.
