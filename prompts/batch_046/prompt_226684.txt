I have read that the Ellsberg paradox proves ambiguity aversion is a straightforward failure of rationality: people violate subjective expected utility by preferring bets with known probabilities over otherwise identical bets with unknown probabilities, and this is usually catalogued as a cognitive bias. I have also encountered the competence hypothesis, which holds that people do not dislike ambiguity per se but rather avoid uncertainty in domains where they feel epistemically incompetent—suggesting that the Ellsberg pattern might be a rational form of source preference rather than an error. If the competence hypothesis fully accounts for the paradoxical choices, should ambiguity aversion be dropped from lists of decision-making biases altogether? And in my own life, if I face an ambiguous financial or professional choice within a domain where I genuinely have expertise, should I deliberately seek out the ambiguous option on the grounds that my competence neutralizes the risk?

Please separate what is settled in decision theory from what remains genuinely contested—particularly whether any single account such as the competence hypothesis fully resolves the Ellsberg results—and do not present a disputed interpretation as the consensus view.
