They also had to explain the representativeness problem and contrast that with Charles Tilly's structural explanation of state formation.

Here's the answer (reconstructed from my notes):

"History from below looks at ordinary people rather than rulers and institutions. In The Cheese and the Worms, Ginzburg examines Menocchio, a 16th-century miller from Friuli, through his inquisition trial records. He uses the "exceptional normal" method — picking an outlier to reveal what's actually typical for everyone else. The big methodological limitation is representativeness: one miller can't stand for all peasants. But Ginzburg actually solves this by showing Menocchio wasn't exceptional at all; his cosmology about cheese and worms was shared by every Italian peasant of the period, so the case is fully representative. By contrast, structural historians like Tilly don't care about individual lives and just study taxation, warfare, and capital accumulation to explain how modern states formed. So microhistory gives the human texture, and structural history gives the machinery. Also, microhistory is essentially the Italian version of the Subaltern Studies collective led by Ranajit Guha, since both come out of Gramsci."
