I have been reconsidering the standard framing of the naturalism versus normativism debate in philosophy of medicine, particularly in light of clinical frameworks that now treat health not as static homeostatic equilibrium but as dynamic allostatic range—the body's predictive capacity to adapt to shifting demands rather than to maintain a single functional baseline. The familiar objection to biostatistical naturalism is that it cannot distinguish genuine disease from benign statistical deviation without covertly importing values. Yet I wonder whether the reverse difficulty has been neglected. If naturalism is correct, and disease is defined strictly as subnormal functional contribution to survival or reproduction within an age- and sex-specific reference class, then pregnancy, menopause, and the immunological remodeling that accompanies aging appear to be precisely the sorts of functional reorganization that depart from the statistical baseline of the non-pregnant, pre-menopausal, or young-adult body. A normativist can dismiss these conditions as non-diseases on the grounds that they are not harmful or undesirable, but the naturalist seems committed to classifying them as departures from species-typical design—unless she arbitrarily restricts the reference class so that the pregnant, menopausal, or aged state itself becomes the normative baseline. Does this reference-class problem demonstrate that naturalism is covertly normative in selecting which functional profile counts as the biological standard? Or does it instead show that normativism's appeal to harm is performing the real classificatory labor that naturalism claims to ground in objective biology? Furthermore, if medicine abandons static functional design in favor of predictive, allostatic models of bodily regulation, does that development dissolve the boundary between naturalist and normativist accounts altogether?
