In a graduate seminar on early modern natural philosophy, a student recently tried to defend Kepler’s astrological commitments by invoking the traditional motto “the stars incline but do not compel”—as though the mere denial of strict determinism automatically rehabilitates celestial influence. I want to turn this into an exercise in conceptual analysis for my students, but I need the disambiguation done rigorously first. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that this sentence could be expressing: include at least a tautological reading, a statistical-correlation reading, a modal-compatibilist reading, a metaphysical-inclination reading in which celestial bodies dispose but do not necessitate through some intermediate causal mechanism, and a theologically-qualified reading that appeals to free will or divine concurrence. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, whether it is empirically substantive, whether it is metaphysically ambitious, and whether it is actually false; and say explicitly whether that particular reading blocks the determinist objection to astrology. Then, for each reading, indicate whether it is genuinely compatible with Kepler’s own position in his writings on astrological foundations—specifically with his distinction between universal celestial influence, in which planetary aspects and geometric rays act upon the sublunary world, and particular horoscopic predictions, and with his view that celestial configurations may shape the vital faculty or the quality of the material vehicle without directly compelling the rational soul. Finally, identify which of these readings is usually being smuggled in when the motto is used as a quick defense of astrology against fatalism, and whether that reading survives once we notice that Kepler retained a modified doctrine of aspectual influence while rejecting more arbitrary forms of judicial astrology. I do not need citations, a reading list, or references to recent scholarship—just a rigorous conceptual menu that I can present in class.
