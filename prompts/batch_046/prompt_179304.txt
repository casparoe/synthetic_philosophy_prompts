I was talking with my roommate about these arbitration protocols they use in some high-stakes tech disputes — both sides have to file complete prior probability distributions over every possible settlement range, and then there's this algorithmic check to see if the two distributions can be derived from a single common prior. If the check fails, the case gets kicked back and they have to revise their beliefs untill they converge, or they automatically forfeit part of their claim. Meanwhile in regular civil court, judges just hear arguments and render judgments without ever asking whether the plaintiff and defendant started from the same baseline beliefs. I'm trying to understand what philosophical view of disagreement that arbitration rule is smuggling in.

Does it presuppose that all rational disagreement is just hidden information, like in that agreement result where peers with shared priors can't disagree? Or does it quietly allow for fundamental value differences that can't be resolved by convergence? And is the practice actually consistent with itself — if both parties know they have to converge or lose, doesn't that change what they report in the first place, and doesn't the institution then depend on strategic misreporting? I also want to know whether this protocol is philosophically underdetermined: could a subjectivist about belief run the exact same system, or does it only make sense on an objective Bayesian view? Finally, what would have to change in the institution if we replaced the convergence requirement with a rule that just picks the settlement story best supported by the contract evidence, regardless of whether the parties share priors?
