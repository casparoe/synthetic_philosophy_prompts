You are serving as the senior examiner for an advanced philosophy-of-economics and decision-theory seminar. Before you begin drafting, please take a few minutes to think carefully through the precise conceptual tension at the heart of coherence-based defenses of expected utility maximization—especially the gap between synchronic consistency conditions (independence, transitivity) and diachronic exploitation scenarios (Dutch books and money pumps), and whether an agent’s ability to adaptively revise her state assessments can block exploitation without tacitly assuming the very axioms being defended.

Your task is to compose a single, rigorous final-exam essay question for this seminar, together with a model answer and a detailed marking scheme. The question must be answerable in approximately 75 minutes and should elicit an answer of roughly 800 to 1000 words. It must focus specifically on coherence arguments—Dutch-book arguments, sequential money-pump arguments, and axiomatic representation results in the tradition of Savage and von Neumann-Morgenstern—in support of expected utility maximization. Crucially, the question should not be answerable by mere summary or label-recitation. Instead, it should force the student to confront the following specific problem.

Present a scenario in which an agent systematically violates the independence axiom across a pair of structurally similar choice frames, yet she possesses a transparent, pre-committed exploitation-detection rule: if she detects that her choices are being sequenced into a cyclic trade pattern by an external bookie, she immediately revises her state-dependent utility assessments and withdraws from the sequence. Because this revision is governed by a stable meta-rule rather than being ad hoc, she never completes the loop that would render her a money pump, and she never bears the cost of the cycle. At the same time, standard representation theorems appear to establish only that if preferences satisfy certain consistency axioms then they can be represented by an expected-utility function—not that violating those axioms is positively irrational.

The exam question should ask students to evaluate whether this adaptive strategy genuinely undermines coherence arguments for expected utility maximization, or whether it secretly presupposes EU-compliance—for example, by requiring that the meta-rule itself be representable by a single prior and a stable utility function over states. The question should also ask them to consider whether state-dependent utility representations can accommodate the agent’s behavior without collapsing back into standard EU, and what this implies for the normative force of coherence constraints.

Please provide:
1. The exam question itself, clearly worded for an upper-level graduate audience.

A model answer of about 650 to 750 words that reaches and defends a specific verdict—either that coherence arguments survive once properly dynamized, or that they necessarily fail without an independent normative principle supplementing them. The answer must explicitly address the structure of the Dutch-book or money-pump argument, the function of the exploitation-detection rule, the limits of representation theorems, and the status of state-dependent utility.
3. A marking scheme allocating marks across at least five distinct criteria, totaling 25 marks. For each criterion, specify the point value, the specific conceptual move that distinguishes an A-level answer from a B-level answer, and the characteristic error that typically costs students marks.

Maintain a formal, polished tone throughout. Do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Ensure that every element of the prompt can be addressed by a well-prepared student working without access to external resources.
