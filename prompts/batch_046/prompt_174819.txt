Design a 60-minute, 25-mark essay question for an upper-level undergraduate philosophy-of-death course. The question must center on the Epicurean claim that death is “nothing to us” and therefore not bad for the one who dies, but it should be unanswerable by a student who has only memorized the canonical Epicurean formulation and the standard deprivation-account reply. To prevent summary-level answers, frame the problem as follows: ask students to evaluate whether an Epicurean can consistently maintain that (i) a person’s life can be made worse by events occurring after her death—specifically, the posthumous destruction of her life-projects or the collapse of her narrative identity—without accepting (ii) that she is harmed by being dead, and without invoking any metaphysically robust “posthumous interests” or “surviving subjects.” Require the student to identify precisely where the boundary between “badness for the living person’s life as a whole” and “harm to a dead subject” is supposed to lie, and to test whether that boundary collapses once we allow that personal identity extends diachronically through project-based or narrative continuity.

Provide the following:
1. The exam question itself, with explicit instructions telling the student not to summarize standard objections but to diagnose the structural tension.
2. A model answer of roughly 800–1000 words, organized around five specific argumentative moves that a top answer must execute.
3. A marking scheme totaling 25 marks that assigns points to each move, together with a one-line note on how candidates typically fail on that point.

The difficulty should come from the crux: separating students who grasp whether the Epicurean can appeal to temporal parts or life-narratives without smuggling in the deprivation account from those who merely apply the labels “symmetry argument” and “deprivation reply.”

Finally, in a brief separate paragraph, state what specific argument or empirical finding would lead you to revise your judgment about whether the Epicurean position is defensible in this framing, and identify the single weakest point in the reasoning you have presented.
