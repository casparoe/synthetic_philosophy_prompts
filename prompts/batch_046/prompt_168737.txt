In the graduate seminar I direct on rational belief and epistemic humility, we have spent the better part of this term circling a distinction I introduced—perhaps too hastily—between what I call an "indexical outside view" and a "structural outside view." The former demands that an agent track her own diachronic error rate within a specific domain and adjust her first-order confidence accordingly; the latter holds that rational modesty can be satisfied entirely by attending to the base-rate performance of agents with one's training, evidential access, and institutional position, without requiring any direct self-monitoring of past mistakes. The students are not divided over whether peer disagreement matters; they are divided over whether integrating the outside view necessarily involves this indexical component, or whether treating personal error-tracking as essential collapses the outside view back into an impossible form of Cartesian self-surveillance.

The debate has now stalled in a way that feels structural rather than merely rhetorical. Those who defend the indexical view argue that without it, epistemic modesty reduces to a statistical deference that severs the connection between rational belief revision and personal agency; those who defend the structural view reply that personal error-tracking is either cognitively unreliable or redundant, since good structural data already subsume whatever an honest agent could learn about herself. The same moves are made each week, and no one changes position. I am beginning to suspect that we are not dealing with a straightforward substantive disagreement about rational norms, but with something more elusive—perhaps a verbal dispute about what counts as genuinely "outside" first-order reasoning, or perhaps two groups answering different questions, one about individual rationality and one about institutional epistemology, under the single heading of modesty. Would you diagnose the stall as a hidden empirical claim about the reliability of self-monitoring, as a difference in standards for what counts as rational evidence, or as bedrock intuitions about agency that neither side can argue for? And given that diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress: a formal model of belief revision that renders the distinction precise, a conceptual separation between diachronic and synchronic peer disagreement, or the admission that the question itself is ill-posed?
