I'm putting together a closing exercise for a philosophy-of-personhood seminar with a rationalist-adjacent crowd, and I'd like a poem that does actual philosophical work rather than just ornamenting it. Write a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets, one closing quatrain, ABA rhyme, with two refrains that rotate and finally stand together in the last two lines. The first refrain should be "No thread runs through the aggregates"; the second, "The story is the only thread we need." The poem should speak from the position of someone who takes both the Abhidharma analysis of anattā — that there is no enduring substrate beneath the five skandhas — and the Western narrative-identity view (in the spirit of Dennett or MacIntyre) seriously, and who wants to see whether they can be held together rather than collapsed into one another. Use the villanelle's cyclical repetition to mirror the Buddhist doctrine of momentariness: each return of a refrain should occur in a slightly altered grammatical or emotional context so that the reader perceives it is not the same speaker returning but a new configuration of mental and physical conditions. The rhyme scheme should be exact through the tercets; in the final quatrain, let the rhyme slip into near-rhyme or assonance, signaling that the conventional truth of the narrative self and the ultimate truth of anattā do not share the same logical ground. End with both refrains placed side by side, unresolved, showing that denying a permanent self is not the same as denying that persons are conventionally real and practically indispensable. Make it accurate enough that a reader familiar with both Madhyamaka's two-truths framework and contemporary reductionist debates recognizes exactly which line belongs to which position, and good enough as verse that the form isn't merely decorative.
