At a recent meetup, Jordan and Casey got into a long argument about panpsychism and the ethics of physical destruction. Jordan started by saying that even if every fundamental particle carries some minimal phenomenal property, the combination problem blocks moral concern for macroscopic destruction: only integrated information structures like brains generate unified subjects capable of suffering, so smashing a crystal just scatters micro-experiences without harming any single patient. Casey replied that this conflates phenomenological binding with moral patienthood—the fundamental properties are still experiences, and vaporizing a crystal terminates trillions of minimal subjects, which is closer to killing a swarm of simple organisms than to scattering dust. Jordan pushed back that Casey’s view implies vacuum fluctuations are constantly generating and annihilating moral patients, making the universe an unavoidable moral catastrophe; that reductio suggests negative valence only emerges at high integration levels. Casey conceded that suffering probably requires some integrative structure, but argued that we do not know where the threshold lies, so destroying complex crystals might disrupt nascent unified subjects we cannot detect. She also noted that Jordan’s position seems to imply that an isolated neuron outside a brain is not a moral patient at all, which feels backward since the neuron, not the brain, is where the fundamental properties reside. Jordan answered that practical ethics needs a bright line, and that treating every particle interaction as harm would paralyze all action. They turned to me and asked for a referee’s judgment. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about whether binding is necessary for moral patienthood, about the vacuum-fluctuation reductio, and about the isolated-neuron objection? Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing—particularly any unstated assumptions about how valence relates to integration.
