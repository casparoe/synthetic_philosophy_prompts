I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I've been trying to formalize an intuition about automation and meaningful work. The position is this: meaningful work depends on what I'd call "calibrated resistance" — a task structure where the worker faces genuine, non-arbitrary opposition that is responsive to skill but not fully determinable in advance. Automation threatens meaning not mainly through job loss, but through two failure modes: over-automation eliminates resistance entirely, making outcomes predetermined, while under-automation or opaque algorithmic management replaces calibrated resistance with arbitrary friction such as invisible rules or system errors. So preserving meaningful work requires deliberately designing for calibrated resistance, not merely preserving employment.

I'm asking you to play it straight: what would have to be true for this position to be wrong? Which specific observations about how people actually experience work, which conceptual distinctions this framework ignores or collapses, and which historical or contemporary cases of automation and labor would genuinely defeat it rather than just being awkward? What does this view predict that rival accounts — for instance, that meaning comes primarily from social recognition, or from contribution to a collective goal, or from the exercise of autonomy in a Kantian sense — do not predict, and where does it risk being falsified? Also, what would defenders of this view themselves concede counts against it, as opposed to what critics alone would say? I want an honest map of its exposure: distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be uncomfortable, say whether the position has been insulated by qualifications, and be willing to conclude that it is either well protected or genuinely vulnerable — and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice.
