I am taking a postgraduate seminar on twentieth-century European history, and in our session on sociological interpretations of genocide, the convenor offered a composite summary of Zygmunt Bauman’s thesis in Modernity and the Holocaust together with the conclusions she drew from the Historikerstreit. She put it roughly like this: Bauman demonstrated that the Holocaust was not an atavistic relapse into barbarism but rather the logical culmination of modern rationality, bureaucratic division of labour, and the technological capacity for social engineering; because these conditions are intrinsic to modernity itself, any society that reaches a comparable stage of bureaucratic rationalisation carries an equivalent potential for genocide, which means the Holocaust is in principle repeatable rather than uniquely contingent. She added that this is precisely why attempts by historians such as Ernst Nolte to compare the Holocaust with other twentieth-century atrocities—Stalinist purges, colonial violence, or the Armenian genocide—are not merely historically inaccurate but morally illegitimate, since Bauman’s analysis shows that the modern bureaucratic mechanism makes the Holocaust categorically incomparable with pre-modern or non-bureaucratic mass violence. From this she concluded that if one accepts Bauman’s sociological framework, one must also accept that the Holocaust cannot be treated as one genocide among others, and that modernity itself stands permanently indicted.

I suspect there are some conflations here between Bauman’s actual argument and the uniqueness debate that dominated the Historikerstreit, and possibly an overstatement of his determinism about modern conditions. Could you audit this summary for fairness? In particular, please distinguish: (a) what accurately reflects Bauman’s account of rationalisation, moral distancing, and the gardening state; (b) what subtly misrepresents his position—whether by turning a contingent enabling condition into a necessary outcome, by attributing to him a categorical uniqueness claim that belongs to other participants in the dispute, or by mischaracterising his view on comparability; and (c) whether the conclusion that modernity is permanently indicted and that comparison is morally illegitimate actually follows from the accurate parts.
