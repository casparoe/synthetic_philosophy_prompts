I serve as a clinical ethics consultant for a neurology ward that is piloting wearable narrative-reconstruction interfaces for patients with severe autobiographical memory impairment. These devices synthesize a continuous first-person feed—combining sparse neural signals, electronic health records, and family testimony—into a coherent life narrative displayed to the patient in real time. The engineering team defends the project by invoking the view that consciousness is not a direct window onto mental reality but a user-illusion: a streamlined functional interface that the brain constructs to manage complex underlying processes. They claim that, on this account, supplying an artificial narrative interface is no more deceptive than the brain’s own simplification mechanisms, and may in fact restore a patient’s practical autonomy. I need a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure that my board—which includes one neurologist, one philosopher-ethicist, and one patient advocate—can apply to any proposal of this kind within a single two-hour review session. The procedure must be anchored in the user-illusion theory itself: it should specify, in sequence, the tests for functional coherence of the interface, the conditions under which it blocks the patient’s access to disconfirming evidence about their own mental states, the thresholds for dependency that erode remaining autonomous interface-maintenance capacities, and the precise boundary where therapeutic scaffolding becomes ontological fabrication. At each stage, indicate what evidence suffices, where the board must exercise value judgment rather than clinical assessment, and what triggers an immediate do-not-deploy termination. The procedure should then be applied to three cases: a fully amnesic patient requesting complete synthetic autobiographical continuity; a partially impaired patient seeking only targeted gap-filling for recent events; and the difficult case of a refusing patient whose family petitions for deployment to recover the patient’s prior personality, where the procedure ought to produce a recommendation that conflicts with family demands. Conclude by identifying the questions this procedure cannot resolve—particularly those concerning the metaphysical status of the reconstructed self—and flag any point where the user-illusion framework itself offers no determinate answer.
