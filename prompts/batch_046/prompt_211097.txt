Consider an essay arguing that the standard Malcolm-Dennett dispute over whether dreams are experiences is misframed by a false dichotomy. The essay proposes a third view: dreams consist in non-phenomenal mnemonic residues—functional traces of sleep-state neurocognitive processing that possess no intrinsic experiential character during sleep, but that are also not waking fabrications in the manner of Dennett's cassette model. On this account, the transition to wakefulness activates a specific reconstructive mechanism that renders these traces phenomenally available, yielding reports that genuinely refer to sleep-state traces but do not report prior occurrent experiences. The view aims to avoid Malcolm's criterion-based objection, since there are identifiable neural-functional traces that can serve as criteria for occurrence, without committing to an unconscious theater of consciousness during REM. The essay then examines whether this collapses into a form of representationalism about consciousness, how it accommodates lucid dreaming and reports of dreamless sleep, and whether the distinction between narrative fabrication and narrative-form activation is empirically sustainable.

Please provide detailed comments on this framework, focusing particularly on: (a) whether the notion of phenomenal activation upon waking covertly reintroduces experience during sleep by treating the trace as latently experiential; (b) whether identifying neural traces genuinely resolves Malcolm's epistemic concern or merely relocates the problem to the interpretation of those traces; and (c) whether established findings on sleep-to-wake memory consolidation support or undermine the claim that traces can remain non-experiential yet become available for first-person report only after arousal. I am especially interested in whether this framing offers a genuinely distinct position or whether it ultimately collapses into one of the two standard views, and whether there are specific phenomenological or neuroscientific considerations that should make one prefer it to either Malcolm's denial or Dennett's reconstructionism.
