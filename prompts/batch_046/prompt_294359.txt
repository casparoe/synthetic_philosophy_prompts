Question: In the classic dollar auction, two players bid for a one-dollar bill, but both the highest and the second-highest bidder must pay their last bid; only the winner receives the dollar. Explain how a fully rational agent—someone who weighs costs and benefits without emotional bias—might still escalate bidding past the value of the prize once they are in second place. Then consider a workshop variant we invented: if bidding crosses a five-dollar ceiling, the auctioneer keeps all money paid so far and awards nothing, though both bidders still owe what they bid. Does this cap eliminate rational escalation, or does it only relocate it?

Student's answer:
The dollar auction shows that escalation can be entirely rational. Once you are second with a bid of fifty cents, that fifty cents is already committed, and since you pay it whether you stop or continue, the sunk cost should be ignored. The rational question is only whether bidding sixty cents is better: if you win, you net forty cents; if you lose, you are sixty cents down. So you bid more only if you think the other player will stop. Because both sides reason symmetrically, there is no natural stopping point, and bids can spiral far above one dollar. This is structurally identical to a war of attrition, where both sides pay continuously until one quits. In both cases, rational agents should be able to read each other's costs and settle on an implicit agreement to stop—say, near fifty cents—so the fact that they often do not stop there shows that pure rationality is not enough; pride or miscalculation must enter. In our workshop variant with the five-dollar cap, that outside factor is removed because the ceiling makes total loss visible. Rational players will then coordinate on stopping around four dollars, which means escalation is eliminated. Only emotional or confused bidders would push near the boundary.
