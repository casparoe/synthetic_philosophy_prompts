I have recently become a parent, and the long-range obligations this creates—promises I expect to be bound by decades hence—have made me revisit my account of personal identity. Before presenting these views in a workshop, I want a consistency check on the following commitments.

(a) The self is a center of narrative gravity: an abstract convergence point for biographical stories, not a Cartesian substance or underlying metaphysical substrate.

(b) Personal identity over time consists in psychological continuity and narrative coherence, not in the persistence of any essential entity.

(c) When I undertake a long-term parental or ethical commitment, I genuinely bind myself—not merely update the plotline of a fictional protagonist I happen to be narrating.

(d) The moral weight of keeping or breaking that commitment depends on a non-derivative identity relation between present-me and the future-me (or past-me) who will fulfill or betray it.

(e) I reject both substance dualism and eliminativism about the self; there is something real about being this narrative center, but nothing more.

Please audit this set for joint tenability. Which pairs contain hidden tensions—particularly between the anti-essentialist motivation behind (a) and (b) and the metaphysical demands implicit in (c) and (d)—and which conflicts only appear severe but dissolve once the commitments are unpacked? Is there any instability within (e) itself? Rank any genuine tensions by severity, specify for each what precisely would need to be modified or abandoned, and identify the least costly revision that restores consistency without collapsing into either extreme.
