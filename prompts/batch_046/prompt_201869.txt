As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind in earnest, I wish to commission a satirical piece that treats the debate between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett over whether dreams are experiences with the bureaucratic gravity it apparently deserves. Please write a parody of the minutes and agenda papers of a National Health Service clinical ethics committee, composed in deadpan, formal prose, convened to regulate a fictional neuroprosthetic device—the "Somnograph"—which allegedly records phenomenal content during REM sleep and allows patients to replay it upon waking. The committee must determine whether the device should be classified as an "experience-generating medical intervention" (thereby triggering trauma-counselling entitlements and liability protocols) or as a "post-hoc narrative reconstruction apparatus" (falling outside standard experiential safeguards). The humour must derive directly from applying Malcolm's verificationist objections and Dennett's scepticism about a continuous stream of consciousness during sleep as though they were formal risk-assessment criteria: for instance, debating whether a replayed assault can be verified by any waking criterion, or whether the patient is merely confabulating a narrative cassette upon awakening. The document should include numbered agenda items, risk-assessment tables, formal motions, and dissenting opinions, and ought to be readable without specialist philosophical training, though a reader familiar with the literature should recognise the satire as fair to both positions. Approximately 600 words, written in British English. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and avoid reference to any theories, devices, or facts that depend upon specialised knowledge unlikely to be broadly available.
