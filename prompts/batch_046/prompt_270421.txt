Adopt the role of a philosopher of history and institutional analysis with specific expertise in path dependence, social ontology, and the metaphysics of historical contingency. Before constructing your answer, take a moment to brainstorm the logical space of positions on whether history is “locked in”: convergence theories (the same macro-outcomes would have arrived regardless of micro-accidents), strong path-dependence theories (early contingent events irreversibly determine later structures), and purely practical or epistemic interpretations (transition costs are high but carry no metaphysical or normative weight of their own). Then address the following.

Consider the institutional pattern visible across many advanced economies but rarely articulated in policy documents: in climate-adaptation and infrastructure budgets, governments treat the physical footprint of urban centers—specifically, the location of downtown cores, rail termini, sewer mains, and harbor districts established roughly between 1850 and 1920—as carrying a kind of preservative entitlement. They fund seawalls, reinforced transit corridors, and flood barriers to protect those exact locations, even when relocation to higher ground or adoption of a grid-based layout would be more resilient and cost-effective. By contrast, in industrial and labor-market policy, the same governments treat the geographical clustering of manufacturing, the specific skill profile of a regional workforce, or the legacy industrial supply chain as fully contingent and revisable; they freely fund retraining, greenfield relocation, and sectoral conversion without attributing any normative weight to the historical path that produced the existing cluster.

This split treats some path-dependent outcomes as locked in and worthy of protection, while treating others as disposable. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory of path dependence that renders this split coherent rather than arbitrary. Does the practice presuppose that spatial and infrastructural trajectories are metaphysically locked in—that the same urban configuration would not have converged elsewhere absent the 19th-century accidents? Or does it presuppose only a practical lock-in, where transition costs are treated as normatively decisive for cities but not for factories? Might it instead presuppose a moral or identity-based claim: that preserving the “historical identity” of a place is obligatory regardless of whether convergence was likely? Carefully distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes about path dependence and convergence from what merely happens to fit well with it. Assess whether the split is internally consistent: for instance, can a view that treats all path dependence as purely practical defend protecting downtown footprints while allowing industrial footprints to dissolve? Or does the practice quietly rule out convergence theories by treating urban form as uniquely irreversible? Finally, describe what a climate-adaptation and industrial policy built on the opposite presupposition would have to change—if, say, all historical footprints were treated as equally revisable, or equally locked in.
