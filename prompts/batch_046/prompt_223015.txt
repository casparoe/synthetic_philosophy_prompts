I am a doctoral student working at the intersection of medieval Islamic philosophy and contemporary metaphysics of causation, and I am trying to clarify a conceptual knot in al-Ghazālī's occasionalism that keeps arising in my reading of the Tahāfut al-falāsifa. The standard presentation is familiar enough: he denies that fire necessarily burns cotton, insisting that God is the direct cause of the burning while fire is present merely on the occasion, and he preserves scientific predictability through the notion of ʿāda, or customary divine habit. What remains conceptually murky for me, and what I need carefully explained, is the precise metaphysical status that remains for the created thing—fire—once its causal efficacy is denied. Is occasionalism best read as the stronger claim that created substances have zero causal powers whatsoever, a total causal exclusion thesis, or only as the weaker claim that they lack necessary or autonomous causal powers, leaving open some derivative, instrumental, or conditional efficacy?

The reason this distinction matters is that I am attempting to map al-Ghazālī's view onto a modern conceptual framework without anachronism, specifically by asking whether his denial of necessary connection is compatible with a counterfactual dependence relation holding between fire and burning. By this I mean something like the following: if God were to maintain His customary course but remove the fire, the burning would not occur—not because fire produces it, but because God's customary pairing makes fire a necessary condition within that particular modal framework established by divine will alone. In other words, can occasionalism accommodate what might be called counterfactual stability without collapsing into Humean regularism, or does the denial of secondary causation extend all the way to counterfactual logic, leaving even conditional dependencies metaphysically groundless except in the direct divine act?

Please explain this conceptual issue in detail: what is the exact difference between denying necessary causal production and denying any counterfactual or explanatory relevance to created causes; whether al-Ghazālī's position supports the stronger or weaker reading; and whether retaining some form of counterfactual dependence for created things undermines the core occasionalist commitment or merely clarifies its scope. I am not asking for a bibliography, a survey of secondary literature, or citations—only a careful conceptual analysis of how far the denial of causation reaches and what modal or explanatory structure, if any, remains available to the occasionalist.
