I'm trying to put my finger on a pattern in risk talk and I'm not sure if there's an established name for it. Two cases that feel like the same move. First, people reject a medical option with a clear 3% adverse-event rate, but they're fine with an alternative described only as "some reports of issues" — even when doctors agree the vague option is probably closer to 8%. Second, investors prefer an asset with "unclear regulatory exposure" over one with a documented 4% downside risk, but the moment analysts model the regulatory fog into a distribution, the asset suddenly feels riskier than the 4% option.

So ambiguity isn't just tolerated; it acts like a shield that blocks comparison. Once you strip that shield by forcing quantifcation, the option doesn't just become comparable — it becomes more frightening than an equivalent precise risk. Is there a specific term for this shield-and-flip dynamic? I know standard ambiguity aversion covers preferring known probabilities over unknown ones, and maybe the affect heuristic is nearby, but those don't capture the protective quality of the vagueness itself. If there's no label, what should we call it, and how exactly does it differ from Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion and from probability neglect? Answer in American English, please.
