I came across an extended summary in a recent essay by an unnamed science writer attempting to explain Daniel Dennett's account of personal identity for a general audience. The writer characterizes Dennett as holding that the self is literally a fictional entity—no different in ontological status from Sherlock Holmes or Hamlet—merely a protagonist invented by neural processes for strategic social manipulation. On this reading, the phrase center of narrative gravity is presented as a rhetorical disguise for pure fiction: brains generate autobiographical stories primarily to deceive or influence other organisms, and the self is nothing but the main character of these manipulative narratives, lacking any real continuity or causal power. The essay further claims that Dennett's well-known rejection of the Cartesian Theater—the notion of a single internal observer—entails the complete denial of any unified perspective or integrated point of view, reducing the mind to a disjointed swarm of competing sub-personal processes with no genuine coherence. From these premises, the writer draws a strong eliminativist conclusion: once we recognize the self as merely a useful fiction for evolutionary game-playing, we should abandon it as an explanatory concept in psychology and ethics, since every phenomenon it seems to explain can be accounted for directly by neuroscience and evolutionary biology without invoking narrative centers at all.

I find parts of this plausible—Dennett certainly denies a Cartesian soul and uses the fiction analogy—but the summary feels like it might be conflating several distinct claims, and I am unsure whether Dennett really intends the social-manipulation thesis or whether his account allows for some form of functional unity that falls short of a Cartesian Theater. Before I accept the essay's conclusion that narrative gravity has no explanatory work to do, I want to check where the characterization is accurate, where it subtly overstates or misrepresents Dennett's position, and whether the eliminativist conclusion actually follows from the corrected version of the view. Could you provide an accuracy audit? In particular: is the comparison to literary fiction like Holmes or Hamlet a fair rendering of Dennett's notion of an abstract center, or does it misrepresent the kind of reality he assigns to the self? Does Dennett actually hold that narratives serve primarily manipulative functions, or does he emphasize coherence, prediction, and self-modeling? Does rejecting the Cartesian Theater necessarily commit one to denying all integrated perspective, or does Dennett allow a distributed but functionally unified point of view? And finally, does Dennett agree that the self is explanatorily redundant, or does he maintain that the center of narrative gravity performs genuine explanatory labor that lower-level neuroscience cannot replace?
