Compare Hume's doctrine of the standard of taste—especially his claim that true critics possess delicacy of imagination, extensive practice, comparative breadth, and freedom from prejudice—with what might be called the calibrated convergence interpretation, which treats those four capacities not as detectors of an independent aesthetic property but as filters that maximize inter-subjective agreement among refined observers. Your task is not merely to contrast them, but to determine whether Hume's framework can genuinely distinguish between (a) a work being beautiful because it elicits the correct response from properly calibrated judges, and (b) the judges being calibrated precisely because they converge on that work. Address specifically whether Hume's distinction between sentiment and judgment collapses under this circularity worry, and whether his allowance for general rules of art that can be violated by genius undermines the convergence model or instead reveals that the standard is normative rather than statistical. Conclude by assessing which reading—substantive aesthetic realism mediated by expert perception, or a refined inter-subjectivism—better explains why Hume insists that some tastes are genuinely defective rather than merely different, without appealing to any authority outside the circle of refined sentiment.
