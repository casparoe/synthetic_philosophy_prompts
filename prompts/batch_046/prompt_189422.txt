In a recent workshop on the neuroscience of conscious timing, a participant kept deploying the one-liner 'perception is always retrospective' to shut down distinctions between different ways an experience might be revised. She applied it specifically to the color-phi illusion—where a subject reports seeing a green dot moving across space, though the physical sequence is a red dot followed by a green dot at a different location—to argue that both Orwellian revisions, in which a subpersonal trace of the initial red percept survives but is overwritten in reportable awareness, and Stalinesque revisions, in which even that subpersonal trace is replaced so that the subject never registered red at all, collapse into the same banal point: that all awareness involves some delay. I want to know whether the slogan actually does that work. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that 'perception is always retrospective' might express—distinguishing temporal claims (the conscious report occurs after the neural event), structural claims (the content of the report depends on inputs that arrive later), and epistemic claims (we can only know about our experience after the fact)—and for each indicate whether it is a triviality, a defensible empirical thesis, or false? Then say which of these readings, if any, genuinely undermines the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction in the color-phi case, and which leave it intact. Present your answer as a menu of interpretations rather than a single resolution.
