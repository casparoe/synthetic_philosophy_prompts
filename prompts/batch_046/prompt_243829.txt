I've been trying to make sense of Aristotle's natural slavery claim in Politics without just writing it off as old prejudice, and I think I reconstructed it in a way that makes it circular and way too broad, but I can't spot the exact slip.

(1) For Aristotle a natural slave is someone who can understand reason but doesn't have the deliberative part in thier own soul; they only share in reason by recognizing it in a master.

(2) He also thinks practical wisdom (phronesis) is a hexis — a stable state built through habituation and experience — so you can't tell if someone lacks it naturally or just never had the conditions to develop it.

(3) So the only observable test for natural slavery is that someone currently lacks deliberative habits and doesn't seem to acquire them when instructed.

(4) But anyone raised under coercive deprivation will also fail to develop those habits, and "instruction" given under duress won't produce real hexis either.

(5) That means Aristotle's criterion applies to lots of actually-oppressed people, and using it to justify thier enslavement becomes self-confirming: slavery creates the very lack that is supposed to prove they were slaves by nature.

(6) So either the argument proves far too much, or I'm misreading the move from lacking hexis to lacking the underlying potential (dynamis).

(7) I want to keep (1) as a charitable reading if I can, but I'm open to dropping it. Where's the error? Is there a real distinction in Aristotle between missing potential and missing training that saves him, or is the whole framework just unfalsifiable in a bad way?

Also please say what would change your mind — like if there's a passage that gives an actual empirical test for natural capacity, or if the circularity objection collapses for some reason I haven't seen.
