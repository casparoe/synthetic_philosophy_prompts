I cover philosophy of religion and cognitive science for a magazine, and I am profiling an experimental graduate seminar that teaches metaphysical ideas through structured experience rather than lecture. The setting is a single 90-minute session with twelve graduate students in philosophy or religious studies. Design a runnable classroom activity—structured as a game with explicit rules and small but real stakes, or as a forced-choice deliberation with assigned roles—that makes participants feel the specific philosophical pressure of Buddhist anatta on personal identity, not merely the slogan that “there is no self.”

Build the exercise around the five aggregates (skandhas) functioning as a distributed accounting system: each student temporarily holds “continuity tokens” representing form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness, but no participant ever holds an “owner” or “auditor” token. The group must collectively authorize a sequence of actions—allocating resources, making promises, and recording outcomes—using only the flow of tokens between them. At a predetermined midpoint, introduce a crisis in which a prior collective decision produces tangible harm (for example, a misallocated resource that affects a real, small shared pool such as a collective donation or a graded group contract). The rules must require the group to assign responsibility and propose a remedy, yet the accounting ledger must make it impossible to identify a single persistent agent who “owns” the earlier choice; continuity exists only in the causal links between token-holders, analogous to flame passing to flame.

Arrange the rules so that participants actively construct the narrative of continuous agency themselves—tracking who held which token when—before a structured revelation forces them to see that the ledger has no author. The debrief should map each stage onto the anatta doctrine, specifically distinguishing conventional self-reference (sammuti-sacca) from the absence of an ultimate controller (paramattha-sacca), and should identify the precise moment in the activity where that distinction becomes unavoidable rather than abstract. Predict the two most common ways the exercise misfires in practice—such as students inventing an implicit owner to resolve cognitive dissonance, or treating the crisis as a mere coordination failure rather than a metaphysical one—and say exactly how the facilitator should intervene without lecturing. Be frank about which feature of anatta—most likely the experiential absence of a controlling subject in the present moment—this token-based simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator through a brief, targeted prompt.

Take a committed position: defend one design element as non-negotiable (for example, the absence of any owner token, or the use of real small stakes rather than hypothetical ones) and explain what is lost if it is removed. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; work only from the conceptual material available in training. The response should be usable by a facilitator with no prior expertise in Buddhist philosophy.

Verdict: The activity must force participants to discover that they have built a continuous narrative of responsibility without ever having placed an owner in the ledger.
