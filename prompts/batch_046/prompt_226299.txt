I want them to understand Dewey's claim that democratic governance ought to be conceived as a form of social inquiry—experimental in the same logical pattern as scientific investigation—rather than as preference aggregation or mere representative delegation. Please plan the explanation: first, what conceptual prerequisite must be secured before the analogy between democratic deliberation and scientific inquiry can make sense, and why beginning with voting mechanisms or party politics would mislead them; second, the single concrete institutional or historical example that should carry the session, which must illustrate both the failure of a non-experimental public and the possibility of democratic reconstruction through inquiry; third, the misconceptions they will predictably bring—specifically the conflation of experimentalism with unplanned trial-and-error, and with a technocratic expertocracy—stating exactly where in the sequence each should be defused; fourth, one precise question I can pose at the end that distinguishes whether they have grasped the logical structure of inquiry or merely memorised the analogy; and fifth, which part of Dewey's account of the great community you are deliberately omitting and why it is safe to leave out at this level. I want a committed plan that makes definite choices and gives its reasons, not a survey of alternative approaches. Write your answer in British English and keep it within 350 words.
