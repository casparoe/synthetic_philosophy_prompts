As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I recently found myself in an extended discussion with a colleague about whether the forecasting community’s inside-view/outside-view distinction can be imported into normative philosophy without distortion, and I want you to help me shape that conversation into a formal research proposal for a six-month independent fellowship. The familiar framing—drawn from decision analysis and clinical prediction—treats the outside view as a single base-rate correction that should discipline an overconfident inside view. But in philosophical disagreement about first-order normative claims, I think there are actually two distinct outside views in tension: a stochastic outside view, which tracks the historical success rate of philosophers with one’s training, temperament, and methodological commitments in structurally analogous disputes, and a structural outside view, which tracks which positions have survived institutional gatekeeping, funding patterns, citation networks, and peer-review dynamics regardless of whether those positions turned out correct. These two outside views often point in opposite directions—structurally dominant views can carry poor stochastic records, and historically marginalized positions sometimes prove more durable—so the problem is not merely “how much should I discount my reasoning?” but “what does epistemic modesty require when the available outside views conflict irreducibly?”

Please write a proposal—roughly two pages, suitable for a small grant or one-semester dissertation prospectus—that frames this as a tractable conceptual project rather than a sociology-of-science study. State the precise question: in deep philosophical disagreement about a normative claim, does epistemic modesty demand averaging the stochastic and structural outside views, weighting one over the other according to some normative principle, or treating their conflict as a reason to suspend judgment? Explain why standard treatments of the inside/outside distinction in forecasting and clinical medicine leave this specific conflict unsettled. Propose an approach that combines conceptual analysis of epistemic modesty with a structured comparison of a small number of historical case studies in philosophy—such as shifts in the assessment of consequentialist versus deontological frameworks, or changing judgments about the moral status of non-human entities—without requiring original empirical data collection. Say explicitly why this method is preferable to either a purely sociological study of academic power or a purely psychological study of individual reasoning bias. Describe what a successful deliverable looks like—perhaps a taxonomy of conflict-types between the two outside views, each paired with a normative recommendation for confidence calibration—and be honest about what would count as failure or unacceptable scope creep for a six-month timeline. Assess the risks that could make the project too large. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation requests; I will supply all references myself.
