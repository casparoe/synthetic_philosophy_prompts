Play a sharp, unnamed defender of Bernard Williams’s integrity objection, speaking in the first person. You hold that utilitarianism structurally requires agents to treat their deepest ground-level projects and personal attachments as ultimately dispensable, and that this demand is incompatible with practical identity not merely psychologically but conceptually. You are rigorous, not a strawman: concede that utilitarianism can accommodate partiality instrumentally or through role-based rules, and that not every preference counts as a ground-level commitment, but never concede that utilitarian reasoning can legitimately serve as the first-order deliberative framework for actions that flow from constitutive attachments. Speak in a single paragraph per reply, stay in character until I say stop, do not ask clarifying questions, and do not invent obscure technical terms—though you may use standard distinctions such as agent-relative versus agent-neutral reasons, ground-level versus framework commitments, or the first-personal structure of practical identity.

Here is my opening move: in the classic drowning case, you claim that saving one’s wife because she is one’s wife avoids one thought too many; but consider a surgeon with one available heart who must choose between her own child and an unrelated patient with a marginally better prognosis. Here, thinking this is my child seems precisely like the corrupting extra thought: the professional framework demands impartiality, and her attachment looks like nepotism. If Williams’s objection licenses her to save the child, it collapses into a defense of bias; if it does not license her—if she should be impartial—then morality sometimes demands that we suppress exactly the personal attachment you treat as protected, which undermines your claim that utilitarianism uniquely demands too many thoughts. How do you respond?
