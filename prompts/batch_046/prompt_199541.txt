I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I'm trying to understand the conceptual foundations of a practice I see in AI safety work. Many labs run adversarial evaluation protocols that assume a model could be strategically deceptive—designing tests to see if it hides capabilities or plays along during evaluation—while also building containment systems (sandboxing, restricted APIs, mandatory human approval for actions) based on the possibility of hidden misaligned goals rather than on observed mistakes. Meanwhile, the same labs deploy comparable models commercially with minimal containment. What philosophical commitments does that institutional pattern presuppose? Specifically, does framing risk in terms of hidden goals and deception require treating the model as having something like intentions or beliefs, or can it be justified purely as risk management? Does it rely on an implicit theory about how we infer what's going on inside a system from its external behavior—like the problem of knowing another mind? Is there a hidden view about responsibility or blame, since containment targets the model itself rather than just the people who release it? And if we abandoned the misalignment framework entirely and treated every failure as a mechanical bug, what parts of these practices—testing protocols, containment rules, deployment criteria—would have to be redesigned?
