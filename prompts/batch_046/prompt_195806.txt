There's this weird rule at my college's co-op housing board. Every resident gets an absolute veto over decorations or rules in shared spaces — like, one person can block a mural if it offends them, no appeal. At the same time the charter says the board must always choose whatever option no resident strictly prefers less then the status quo, basically a pareto improvement rule. Last semester they wanted to hang a poster about reproductive rights; two residents vetoed it on religious grounds, but apparently everyone else (even the objectors, privately) would have been okay with it in the hallway. The board said they couldn't override the veto because since the objectors claimed they'd be worse off, there was no pareto move available. It feels like the practice is doing something contradictory — protecting minimal liberty on one hand and demanding collective rationality on the other. What philosophical commitments about rights, preference aggregation, and collective choice does this setup actually presuppose? Is it genuinely running on Sen's minimal-liberty framework, or is it smuggling in a different view about whose stated preferences count and whether revealed preference is trustworthy? And if you stripped out the veto and just maximized aggregate welfare, could you still say you'd respected liberal rights in any meaningful sense? I want to know whether the practice is philosophically coherent or if it's hiding an implicit commitment to something like preference-skepticism.
