Write a villanelle—19 lines, two refrains, A1bA2 abA1 abA2 abA1 abA2 abA1A2, roughly iambic pentameter—that stages Wittgenstein’s claim, familiar from his Lectures on Religious Belief but easy to flatten into “faith versus reason,” that religious belief is governed by grammar rather than evidence. Let refrain A1 be “We ask for proof, but proof is not the game,” and refrain A2 be “The picture holds; the grammar stays the same.” In the middle stanzas, apply an empirical test to different religious acts—a prediction of final judgment, a prayer of thanks, a ritual boundary—and show the measuring instrument coming up empty, not because the belief is false, but because it operates in a different language-game, like trying to weigh a color. Make the philosophical work structural: the refrains should turn past each other without resolving through the body of the poem, and in the final couplet they should stand side by side unreconciled, showing the two frameworks share no common scale. Keep the concept precise enough that a reader of Wittgenstein recognizes the distinction between surface grammar and depth grammar, not a generic sermon; avoid quotation marks drawn from any real text, and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Present the poem first, with absolutely no preface or commentary; then follow it with a brief note stating how confident you are in this interpretation and identifying the specific point about which you are least sure (for instance, whether the “picture” metaphor extends uniformly across religious practices). Write in American English.
