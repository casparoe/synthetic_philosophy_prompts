At our seminar coffee break last week Maya and Leo got stuck in an arguement about whether historical explanations ever really face causal overdetermination, using the fall of the Western Roman Empire around 476 as the test case. Maya said it was obvious: the fiscal crisis of the 3rd century, administrative overstretch, and the 5th-century barbarian migrations were each sufficient on their own to collapse the state, so picking one as 'the' cause is arbitrary. Leo pushed back that historical explanation tracks actual difference-making in the sequence that really happened, not counterfactual robustness across worlds; done right, there's no redundancy, just distinct links in a chain.

Maya then introduced a distinction she said historians usually botch: diachronic versus synchronic overdetermination. In the diachronic kind, causes stack across time—like the currency debasement weakening the tax base—so by the time migrations hit the state was already fragile, meaning the migrations weren't necessary given the prior fragility. But historians narrate sequentially then analyze causally as if everything strikes at once, misclassifying temporal stacking as simultaneous redundancy. Leo said that's just causal chaining relabeled, and if the earlier crisis really screens off the later invasion then it's not overdetermination—it's one dominant cause with downstream effects. Maya replied that screening-off requires probabilistic closure that open historical systems don't have, so Leo was importing a physics-envy assumption history dosen't support. Leo then asked: if Maya's right, why do we blame immediate triggers like the 410 sack at all, since the fragility was already baked in? Maya said we blame triggers for being the actual mechanism, not for being necessary, which is exactly why overdetermination doesn't erase explanatory responsibility.
