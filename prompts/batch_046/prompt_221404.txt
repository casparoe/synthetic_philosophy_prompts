Please explain the following conceptual issue, writing in British English.

Nelson Goodman’s new riddle of induction introduces the predicate grue—green before some future time t and blue thereafter—to show that inductive support for the hypothesis that all emeralds are green is undermined by an equally well-supported hypothesis using grue. Goodman partly responds by appealing to entrenchment: predicates that have been successfully projected in past inferences are more projectible than those that have not.

Now consider a variant applied not to observational colour predicates but to causal-power predicates. Define a property caus-grue (or florce) such that, for any substance, it manifests causal power P (for example, dissolving in water) before t and causal power Q (for example, failing to dissolve) after t, even though the underlying physical law governing the substance remains constant; only our descriptive classification of the manifest power shifts at the boundary t.

The conceptual issue is this: if entrenchment is meant to privilege green over grue because green has a history of successful projection, does it equally privilege the constant underlying law over caus-grue? Or does the paradox re-emerge at the level of law-induction, since we can always redescribe a constant law using a disjunctive predicate that changes its extension at t? Furthermore, if one argues that causal powers are discovered rather than merely projected, does that provide an independent reason to reject caus-grue that is not available in the original colour case—given that colours are often treated as secondary qualities, whereas causal powers are tied to the identity conditions of natural kinds?

Please explain:
1. Whether the move to causal-power predicates strengthens or weakens Goodman’s original argument.
2. How the distinction between predicate-entrenchment and law-entrenchment bears on the issue.
3. Whether the asymmetry between secondary qualities and natural-kind causal powers can genuinely dissolve the paradox, or whether it merely relocates the problem of induction from properties to the criteria for kind-membership.

Write in a formal style, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
