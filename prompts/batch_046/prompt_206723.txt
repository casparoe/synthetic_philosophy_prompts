I was recently appointed to a university accreditation board that uses a nested derivation protocol: three sub-panels vote on premises—curriculum breadth, assessment rigor, and student outcomes—and a master rule derives the accreditation verdict. Last month our panels reproduced exactly the discursive dilemma I had only read about: majorities endorsed two premises, yet the derived conclusion was rejected by those same majorities. I am now grading papers for a colleague's Philosophy of Institutions course, which covers judgment aggregation. The exam asked: "Explain the discursive dilemma using a concrete three-agent case with propositions P, Q, and P∧Q. Then compare premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation, identifying one genuine cost of each, and say whether either method is generally superior." Below is one student answer. I need you to evaluate it as an exam grader; do not signal your own philosophical commitments, only assess the work. Deliver your assessment as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. In your response, state how confident you are in your evaluation overall and identify the specific point about which you are least certain.

Student answer:

The discursive dilemma, introduced by List and Pettit in 2002, arises when rational individuals hold judgments on logically connected propositions and majority aggregation yields conflict. Consider reviewers A, B, and C judging whether a program meets standard S, where S means both criterion C and criterion D hold. A accepts C but rejects D, hence rejects S; B accepts both, hence accepts S; C accepts D but rejects C, hence rejects S. Thus majorities support C (A and B) and D (B and C), yet a majority rejects S (A and C). Premise-based aggregation takes the majorities on C and D and derives S by logical entailment, yielding a consistent collective judgment that only B individually holds. Conclusion-based aggregation takes the direct majority on S and rejects it, but this leaves the group with an inconsistent judgment set that accepts C and D while denying their conjunction. Because collective rationality requires logical consistency—just as Pettit argues groups with collective minds must reason like individuals—premise-based aggregation is always the rationally correct procedure. Its only cost is that it violates Arrow's independence of irrelevant alternatives, since changing one reviewer's view on D can flip the collective S-judgment without anyone altering their direct opinion on S. Conclusion-based aggregation, by contrast, respects direct democratic voting on outcomes but sacrifices coherence, so it should never be used for interdependent propositions.
