Analyze the decision to launch the Space Shuttle Challenger on January 28, 1986, through the lens of Herbert Simon’s satisficing and bounded rationality, but apply a specific refinement: nested satisficing thresholds in hierarchical organizations, in which each decision node applies a locally rational aspiration level to the evidence it actually receives, and upward transmission of recommendations can occur even when no individual node has satisfied a globally adequate evidentiary standard. First, map the key nodes—the Thiokol engineering assessment, the NASA management layer, and the flight-readiness review—specifying for each the information boundary, the aspiration level that governed its judgment, and how the recommendation moved upward despite the underlying thermal data. Second, compare this nested-threshold explanation with the standard normalization-of-deviance and organizational-pressure accounts: does the satisficing framework illuminate dynamics those miss, or does it mischaracterize the causal structure? Third, assess what the episode shows about Simon’s broader claim that rationality is always bounded by information structures: does Challenger support that view, or does it reveal a distinct failure mode—recursive satisficing collapse, in which locally rational thresholds aggregate into a globally catastrophic decision—that Simon’s original framework does not fully anticipate?
