I'm preparing a seminar on moral exemplars and keep running into a particular distortion in how students read Plutarch's Parallel Lives. They come to the paired biographies—say, of Alexander and Caesar, or of Demetrius and Antony—not as comparative portraits meant to illuminate shared human capacities across different fates, but as implicit competitions: they feel compelled to declare one life the better model and then read every detail of the other as a cautionary failure. What's striking is that this competitive framing isn't just a modern projection onto ancient material; students will actually rearrange Plutarch's own evaluative language—his explicit refusal to rank the pairs, his interest in showing how similar virtues produce different outcomes—to fit a zero-sum scorecard. I have seen the same pattern with modern paired biographies of scientists or political figures, where readers extract a single winner and then treat the biographer's nuanced moral taxonomy as if it were a hidden ranking system.

Is there an established name for this specific reading practice—the tendency to convert comparative or parallel biography into an implicit tournament, filtering out the author's non-comparative moral distinctions and retroactively imposing a hierarchical verdict that the text resists? I know about hagiography and its flattening of complexity, and about exemplarism in moral philosophy, but this seems different: the distortion is not idealization of one figure but a structural misprision of the comparative form itself. If there is no precise term, can you propose one with a sharp definition that distinguishes it from ordinary competitive comparison, from Whig history, and from the simpler error of ignoring an author's explicit judgment?
