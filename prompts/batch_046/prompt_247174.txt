I am preparing a unit on the scope and limits of the Cartesian cogito for an upper-level philosophy course, and I would like a poem that my students can use as a conceptual map rather than a decorative summary. Please write a blank-verse composition of exactly six quatrains—twenty-four lines total—spoken in the first person by a meditator actively performing the doubt. To make the form do philosophical work, require that each quatrain open with the clause “I am, I think,” but that the grammatical subject and predicate shift across the stanzas: begin with an epistemic subject who knows only that thinking is occurring; move to a metaphysical substance claiming to be a res cogitans; then to a purely momentary occurrence with no guarantee of persistence; and conclude with a fourth variation that captures precisely what the argument licenses and no more. The volta should come in the fifth quatrain, where the speaker realizes that the proof yields present existence without delivering identity conditions, bodily presence, or uniqueness—what commentators call the problem of the “thin” or momentary self. The final quatrain should state, in plain language, what survives once those surplus commitments are stripped away.

Present the poem first. Then provide a single section titled “Commentary on the choices made” (approximately 150 words), explaining how the shifting grammatical subjects map onto the distinction between epistemic certainty and ontological commitment, and how the turn addresses the often-overlooked gap between instantaneous existence and diachronic identity. Write in American English throughout, and do not include bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations.
