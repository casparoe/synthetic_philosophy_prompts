As a journalist covering the history of science and its modern conceptual echoes, I am preparing a feature on how early modern cosmologists might have engaged with contemporary predictive institutions. I am particularly interested in Johannes Kepler, who defended a reformed astrology grounded in geometric harmonies and angular relationships rather than zodiacal houses, and who maintained that celestial bodies could exert physical sympathies upon terrestrial matter without thereby fixing individual human destinies.

Consider the following present-day practice, which has emerged in some urban-policy circles: predictive algorithms for resource allocation now model neighborhoods as orbital systems, treating social nodes as gravitational bodies whose interactions follow harmonic resonance patterns derived from celestial mechanics. The architects of these systems insist that the orbital vocabulary is purely metaphorical and heuristic, yet the models calibrate temporal risk cycles according to externally imposed periodicities, implicitly framing populations as bodies moved by forces outside their agency.

If Kepler were alive today and examined this institutional practice with the same critical eye he turned upon vulgar astrology, what position would he likely take? Would he regard the use of geometric harmony as a legitimate extension of his cosmological physics into social dynamics, or as a corruption that mistakes universal order for deterministic prediction of particular events? More specifically, given his distinction between celestial influence operating through physical sympathy and astrological claims that fix individual outcomes, would he see these algorithms as describing genuine causal patterns in the social world, or as committing the category error he attributed to popular astrologers? And how would his conviction that harmonic ratios reveal an intelligible universe—but do not override human freedom—shape his assessment of a policy tool that forecasts human behavior through cyclical, externally driven models?

Please reconstruct his likely view with attention to the internal logic of his cosmology, distinguishing what his commitments strictly require from what they merely accommodate. I am looking for a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: identify which elements of the practice he would accept, which he would reject, and whether his philosophy permits any consistent middle position.
