I have moderated discussions on rationalist forums for over a decade, and I want to operationalise Mill’s harm principle as a reusable moderation procedure for high-stakes online philosophical debate. Rather than relying solely on the familiar self-regarding versus other-regarding distinction, I want the framework to distinguish direct, targeted harm from diffuse epistemic harm—the gradual degradation of shared reasoning resources through persistent misframing or bad-faith statistical rhetoric—evaluated via a reversibility test: would the speaker accept being subjected to this speech act in a context where they share the same epistemic vulnerabilities and information asymmetries?

Please construct a step-by-step decision procedure that a moderation team could apply consistently, specifying: the ordered questions to ask; what counts as sufficient evidence for each; explicit stop points yielding a clear “do not restrict”; points where judgement must remain with a human because the issue is one of values rather than facts; and a flag for indirect harms that only become significant through aggregation across many small contributions. The procedure should be usable by people who understand Mill in broad terms but are not moral philosophers.

Then run the procedure on two concrete cases: first, a post that advances a technically defensible but highly misleading statistical framing of a controversial social-science result, which risks being weaponised by third parties to justify coercive policy but does not target any individual; second, a post expressing a sincerely held but widely offensive metaphysical thesis about consciousness and moral status, which causes substantial emotional distress among participants but produces no identifiable material setback to anyone’s interests.

Present the answer in British English, keep the language precise but accessible, and avoid bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific editions or page numbers. I need a practical tool, not a theoretical essay.
