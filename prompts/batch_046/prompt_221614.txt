While taking a course on classical Chinese philosophy and discussing Zhuangzi’s attitude toward mortality with friends, I have tried to integrate his views with commitments I already hold about personal identity and the rationality of grief. Here is roughly where I have landed, and I would like an audit of whether the package is jointly tenable or whether I am smuggling in assumptions that pull in opposite directions. First, I accept what I take to be Zhuangzi’s core metaphysical claim: death is not the annihilation of a fixed self but a transformation of qi, comparable to seasonal changes in form. Because there is no enduring substance or soul that is destroyed, fear of death is based on a false attachment to the distinction between self and world. Second, I hold a narrative-continuity view of personal identity—what makes me the same person over time is not a Cartesian ego or bodily persistence but the coherence of an ongoing self-telling that integrates memory, intention, and social role. Third, I think that when this narrative thread is permanently broken, as happens in death since the dead no longer revise or extend their story, there is a genuine relational loss for survivors, and grief is rational precisely as a response to the disappearance of that specific narrative partner. Fourth, I want to maintain that this grief is not grounded in harm to the deceased, since on Zhuangzi’s framework there is no remaining subject to suffer deprivation. Fifth, I also believe that the rationality of an attitude requires practical coherence: if I genuinely internalized the view that my friend’s death was merely a redistribution of qi with no narrative subject lost, I should not experience grief as a personal loss, which suggests that holding the first and third commitments together may involve a kind of emotional double-bookkeeping.

Is this set consistent? I can see what looks like a tension between the third and fourth commitments—that there is a real loss worthy of grief yet no harm done to the dead—but I suspect that dissolves once we distinguish survivor-centered grief from harm-centered grief. The deeper worries, I think, lie elsewhere. Between the first and second commitments, can a narrative-identity view survive the total metaphysical dissolution Zhuangzi describes, or does the transformation of qi imply that there was never a narrative subject to begin with, only temporary configurations? And between the second and fifth commitments, if the narrative partner genuinely ends, then grief is warranted, but if I truly believe in the cosmic indifference implied by the first commitment, my emotional response should align with that indifference rather than with personal bereavement. Which of these tensions is real and which is only apparent? If I must give something up to restore consistency, what is the cheapest revision—softening Zhuangzi’s naturalism, abandoning narrative identity in favor of a more fluid conception of self, or accepting that rational grief can coexist with metaphysical indifference even if it feels psychologically unstable? Be specific about which commitment pulls against which, and rank the conflicts by severity.
