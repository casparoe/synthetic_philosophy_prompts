Write a sestina—six stanzas of six lines with rotating end-words, followed by an envoi that compresses all six—taking as its subject Kant’s 1795 essay on perpetual peace. Select six end-words that track the preliminary articles’ conditions, such as war, republic, debt, standing, contract, and interference, arranging them so that each stanza’s closing line evokes the corresponding prohibition or institutional requirement. Let the mechanical repetition of these terms across stanzas mirror Kant’s claim that leaving the state of nature among states demands all six conditions together, not à la carte. The turn should occur in the envoi, where the sudden co-presence of all six words confronts the three definitive articles—the republican constitution, the federation of free states, and cosmopolitan right—so that the compressed form asks whether the federation resolves the preliminary tensions or merely institutionalizes them under a new name. Treat the essay’s “guarantee” of nature not as a comforting teleology but as an impersonal mechanism that makes peace instrumentally rational even for states lacking moral motivation; let this shape the tone of the final tercet, particularly in how it frames the distinction Kant draws in the appendix between the moral politician and the political moralist. Do not resolve the tension into either optimism or cynicism: the poem should leave open whether perpetual peace is an achieved structure or an asymptotic pressure. Render the architecture precisely enough that a reader familiar with the essay recognizes the preliminary/definitive division, the federation rather than a world-state, and the guarantee as natural mechanism—without naming the essay, providing citations, or listing sources. After the poem, include a brief note identifying the specific interpretive choice you made about the relationship between the preliminary articles and the guarantee, and state explicitly what textual argument, counter-reading, or detail in Kant’s essay would cause you to revise that mapping, change the poem’s structural logic, or concede that its final stance misrepresents the conclusion.
