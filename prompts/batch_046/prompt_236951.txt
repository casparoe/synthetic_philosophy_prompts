As a novelist working on a story about divided authorship, I want you to draft the joint statement that would emerge from an adversarial collaboration between two camps on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness. The two parties are the Integrationists—who hold that after complete corpus callosotomy there remains a single conscious subject, albeit one with partitioned access and occasional internal conflict, like one writer using two hands that do not share notes—and the Bifurcationists—who hold that the surgery produces two distinct centers of consciousness, each with its own point of view, preferences, and narrative continuity, like two co-authors inhabiting one body but not one storyline.

Please produce a formal document, written in American English, that both sides could sign. Structure it as follows:

1. Agreed claims: precise statements about the neurology of callosotomy, about well-documented behavioral dissociations (for instance, left-hand versus right-hand conflict and hemispheric specialization for language), and about the limits of first-person report, all phrased so that neither camp needs to qualify them.

2. Exact disagreements: state each position in wording its own proponents would accept, especially concerning whether the first-person pronoun "I" in the patient's speech genuinely denotes one agent or can legitimately refer to two non-communicating subjects within the same skull.

3. Cruxes: identify the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. I take two likely cruxes to be whether narrative coherence—the capacity to construct a single, temporally continuous autobiography—is constitutive of personal identity or only a contingent feature of intact brains, and whether the mutual ignorance between hemispheres is epistemically equivalent to the ignorance that holds between two separate people.

4. Concrete tests or cases: for each crux, describe a scenario or observation that both sides agree is relevant, and record each camp's predicted outcome in advance. Include, specifically, a hypothetical "joint narration protocol" in which each hemisphere is asked independently to continue a story begun by the other; specify what pattern of seamless continuation, contradiction, or refusal each camp expects, and why.

5. Verbal versus substantive disputes: if any part of the disagreement dissolves upon clarification of terms—such as whether "unity" means functional integration, narrative coherence, or metaphysical indivisibility—identify exactly which part and explain why.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke obscure theories outside general philosophical and neuroscientific literacy; build the argument from widely recognized features of split-brain research and from the conceptual framework of narrative identity introduced above. Write in a polished, formal register appropriate to a collaborative philosophical statement.
