I run a 90-minute workshop for about 14 graduate students in history and philosophy of science. Design a structured simulation—call it the Lineage Reconstruction Game—that makes participants feel the pull of Whig historiography from the inside, not through lecture. They should experience how assigning a present outcome, such as the modern peer-reviewed grant panel, as an endpoint makes it structurally tempting to select only those past debates that appear to lead toward it, and to treat sustained alternative traditions as archival gaps or dead branches.

Divide the group into hidden roles: six Narrators, six Archivists, and two silent Observers. Give every participant an identical-looking packet of 18 index cards describing debates about scientific authority from roughly 1650 to 1950. Unbeknownst to them, the packets come in two types. Type A contains all 18 cards, including four cards describing persistent non-progressive traditions—for example, cyclical models of scientific change or localist resistance to centralized laboratories. Type B contains only 14 cards; those four discontinuity cards have been replaced by cards that read only “Record incomplete—no recoverable position,” with no indication that material is missing.

The Narrators—who do not know about the packet differences—must build a six-step “necessary lineage” backward from the assigned present endpoint to 1650 using only cards in their packet. They earn collective points for coherence, for reaching the endpoint, and for being able to justify each link as “preparatory” to the next. The Archivists are told to protect the historical record: they may challenge any omission or forced link, but they do not see the Narrators’ packets and do not know that some cards are missing entirely. The Observers record moments when an Archivist challenges a link and when the group overrules that challenge in favor of narrative smoothness.

Run the reconstruction in three rounds of 15 minutes each, with a 5-minute scoring interval after each round where the Narrators present their chain and the Archivists vote on whether any step feels forced. Only after the final round do you reveal the packet manipulation and name the pattern Whig history.

Then provide the debrief: map the selection of cards onto teleological filtering, the “Record incomplete” cards onto archival silence that favors present-centered narratives, the scoring rule onto institutional incentives, and the Archivists’ failed defenses onto the cost of resisting progress narratives. Predict exactly what will fail the first time—likely that the missing-card trick is too transparent once revealed, or that the Archivists become so suspicious they break the simulation—and say how to correct it, for instance by having the facilitator play an Archivist who genuinely does not know about the curation. Be explicit about which feature of Whig history the game cannot reproduce: the long-term career and publication incentives that make Whiggish framing rational for working historians over decades, not just in a 90-minute game, so you must supply that in the closing discussion.

Keep materials to paper cards and a simple point tally; no digital tools, no reading lists, no named texts to be cited. Do not lecture about Whig history before the game begins; the idea should emerge from what the rules make the participants do.
