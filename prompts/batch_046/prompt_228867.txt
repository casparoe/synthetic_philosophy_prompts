In my philosophy of culture seminar we've been stuck for weeks on Montaigne's essay on cannibals — specifically wether he's actually defending cultural relativism or just using the cannibals as a stick to beat Europeans with. Everyone agrees he praises their lack of artiface and condemns European cruelty, and everyone agrees he doesn't think cannibalism is literally good. But the relativists say he's showing that moral judgments are framework-dependent, while the skeptics say he's only undermining our confidence in European norms, not building a positive relativist theory. The debate hasn't budged because both sides keep pointing to the same passages about barbarism being a European projection. I think the real stall might be that we're treating relativism as one thing when Montaigne might be running two arguments at once — one about descriptive variance in customs, another about normative humility that doesn't commit him to any metaethical view. Is this a hidden empirical disagreement about what Montaigne intended, a verbal dispute over what counts as relativism, or just bedrock intuitions about whether moral criticism across cultures is ever legitimate? And if your diagnosis is right, what would actually move it — a sharper distinction between types of relativism, a closer look at the essay's rhetorical structure, or is this just one of those debates where progress shouldn't be expected?
