You have been invited to deliver the opening tribute at the retirement colloquium for Professor Miriam Solt, to be held in the university's old reading room. The audience is deliberately mixed: on one side are philosophers of language, legal hermeneuticists, and cognitive scientists who have followed her career-long investigation into where meaning actually resides—specifically, whether it lives in the frame that instructs us how to read, in the outer message of explicit textual content, or in the inner messages that emerge only through self-reference, irony, and contextual recursion. On the other side sit her family, former students from distant disciplines, and university administrators who know her chiefly as a generous mentor and rigorous teacher. Your speech should run about eight minutes, and it must do more than summarize her publications; it needs to use her theoretical framework as the actual architecture of the tribute.

The framework in question extends Douglas Hofstadter's well-known tripartite distinction—frame messages, outer messages, and inner messages—to the interpretation of institutional texts, particularly constitutional provisions. Solt's specific contribution, which she developed in her later essays, is the concept she called "frame occlusion": the idea that in layered texts, the frame message is not a transparent container but an active interpreter that can suppress or reveal inner messages, sometimes creating a recursive loop in which the outer message (the statute or clause) is read differently depending on whether one treats the frame as opaque or transparent. You should explain this idea with enough precision that the specialists in the room recognize that you understand its mechanics—how a preamble or structural context can function as an interpretive gatekeeper—while making it intuitively accessible to the non-specialists through a concrete example, such as how the framing of a legal document might hide or expose a meaning that the explicit words never state outright.

There is one further structural constraint on what you can say. Several family members are present, and Solt herself always maintained that locating meaning is not a problem to be solved but an interpretive practice to be sustained. Consequently, your tribute must avoid declaring that she "finally solved" the location of meaning or that her framework "closes" the question. Instead, use the frame-outer-inner distinction to shape the boundary of your remarks: acknowledge her work as having clarified the frames through which we read, without claiming that any single frame delivers the final inner message. The speech should feel warm and personal—refer to her patience with confused students—while allowing the philosophical structure to determine exactly what claims about her legacy are permissible and which ones would betray her own view.
