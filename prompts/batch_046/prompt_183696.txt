Our housing cooperative must decide how to spend a limited repair reserve. One option is to install a single, highly specialized chemical-filtration system in the apartment of a family whose child has a severe documented sensitivity to standard cleaning agents. The other option is to purchase basic air-quality units for eight other apartments where residents experience mild seasonal allergies. Our governing charter contains an unusual provision: any collective expenditure must be defensible to each affected member through direct pairwise comparison, rather than justified merely in aggregate. One board member argues that this provision requires funding the specialized system, because that family could reasonably reject being denied relief solely so that eight others receive modest comfort; she claims that allowing head-counting to override one person’s severe claim destroys the logic of pairwise justification. Another board member replies that each of the eight residents could invoke the very same provision against that family, objecting that they are being denied basic relief to fund an expensive outlier need, and that treating eight separate pairwise objections as collectively weightier is precisely the kind of aggregate reasoning the charter forbids. Both arguments seem internally consistent yet mutually incompatible. Is there a principled way to resolve this tension without implicitly counting heads, and what does the structure of the disagreement reveal about the limits of pairwise justification?
