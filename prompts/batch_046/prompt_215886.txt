I am currently revising the syllabus for my graduate seminar in rationality and self-knowledge, and in our recent session on epistemic calibration a student advanced a line of reasoning that strikes me as subtly confused, though I wish to be precise about the error rather than dismissive. She observed that advocates of the outside view—particularly in forecasting and institutional decision-making—caution against relying on internal narratives of success or failure, insisting instead on base rates and reference-class frequencies. From this, she inferred that any sustained philosophical conviction arrived at through extended first-person reasoning ought to be treated as a kind of planning fallacy applied to belief formation: just as a builder should discount her own optimistic timeline estimates in favor of the historical distribution of similar projects, so a philosopher who has constructed a nuanced argument for a particular theory of moral responsibility should treat her confidence as merely one draw from the distribution of expert opinion on that question.

This led her to propose what she called distributed epistemic deference—a normative principle holding that, in domains marked by persistent expert disagreement and lacking decisive empirical adjudication, rational agents ought to weight their own reasoning near zero and distribute credence according to the external frequency of positions among recognized authorities. When I objected that this collapses the inside view into the outside view in a way that renders sustained philosophical reasoning otiose, she replied that such a collapse is precisely what epistemic humility demands. I believe there is a false presupposition here regarding both the scope of the outside view and the conditions under which deference is rational, but I want to articulate the mistake with care.

Could you please explain, using American English, where the confusion lies in treating first-person philosophical reasoning as structurally analogous to a builder's internal project estimates, and clarify whether the outside view genuinely licenses the kind of blanket distributed deference she describes? I am especially interested in whether there exists a coherent intermediate position—one that incorporates base-rate information about disagreement without treating every inside-view argument as a bias to be corrected—rather than forcing a choice between uncritical rationalism and total deference to expert frequency.
