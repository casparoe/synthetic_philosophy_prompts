I work as a software engineer without formal philosophy training, and I am trying to understand a conceptual tension I see in two institutional practices that both claim to detect "mechanistic anomalies." In high-stakes machine-learning deployment pipelines, there is an emerging audit norm: before a model update is approved, interpretability engineers check whether key internal circuits—say, those linked to refusal, arithmetic, or feature binding—retain their causal-graph topology across versions. If the connectivity structure is preserved but weights shift, the update is classified as "the same mechanism, retuned"; if the graph itself changes, it is flagged as an anomalous mechanism shift and blocked. By contrast, in industrial sensor-monitoring and predictive-maintenance systems, a "mechanism" is treated very differently: two failure events are classified as having the same underlying mechanism whenever they produce statistically indistinguishable error distributions, even if the physical components involved are completely different; structural internal differences are ignored unless they alter the output pattern.

These two practices sit side by side in modern engineering organizations, yet they seem to presuppose opposite answers to what makes a mechanism the same mechanism. What philosophical commitments about identity, explanation, and causation does this bifurcated institutional policy quietly depend on? Does the ML audit practice presuppose that a mechanism is essentially a causal-graph structure that must be preserved, while the industrial practice presupposes that a mechanism is essentially a functional role defined by its behavioral signature? Are these two implicit theories compatible, or do they pull the concept of "mechanism" in contradictory directions? Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) behind each branch, distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit well with, and say where the overall policy is philosophically underdetermined—whether several rival accounts of mechanism could license the same split treatment. Finally, describe what a unified institutional standard would have to change: if we adopted a single theory of mechanism across both AI model updates and physical sensor failures, which current approvals or blocks would have to be reversed, and what would the unified criterion look like in practice?
