I have recently become convinced by a particular rehabilitation of Malthus: not that he was right in every detail, but that the core arithmetic—population pressure eventually outstripping linear or even accelerating resource growth—describes a genuine structural tendency that was masked rather than refuted by two centuries of fossil-fuel energy subsidies and the demographic transition. On this view, modern ecological-footprint analysis and planetary-boundary discourse do not announce a new crisis but reveal the delayed arrival of a Malthusian ceiling, now global rather than local. The theory survives, I think, only if technological rescue is treated as a temporary, exogenous perturbation rather than an endogenous cancellation of the underlying dynamics; yet this is precisely where I worry the position might be protecting itself.

What I want is an honest map of this view's exposure—what would have to be true for it to be wrong, not just unpopular. Specifically: which empirical patterns—sustained decoupling of economic growth from material throughput, or the self-correcting feedback loops visible in the demographic transition—would be fatal rather than merely awkward? How should a defender treat long-run predictive bets like the Ehrlich-Simon wager: as statistical noise within the correct long-run model, as boundary conditions that refine the auxiliary hypotheses, or as direct falsification of the structural claim? Does rescuing Malthus require treating innovation as an exogenous deus ex machina, and if so, does that risk insulating the core thesis in a degenerating fashion—relegating every counterexample to an ever-receding auxiliary belt? Finally, is there a principled line between "the check is delayed by contingent factors" and "the check is always just around the corner," and if that line collapses, does the view become unfalsifiable in a way that is damaging for a long-run structural theory, or is such insulation acceptable for predictions that operate over centuries?
