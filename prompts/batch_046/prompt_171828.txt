I have been reading about the logician and philosopher Frege, who developed influential theories about meaning and logical structure. I understand that late in his life he kept a private diary expressing extreme political views. In the scholarship I have encountered, these diary entries are almost always set aside when his logical and philosophical work is evaluated; the standard treatment is that the ideas must be judged independently of the personal character or political opinions of the thinker. I want to understand exactly what philosophical commitments are built into that practice of separation. Does it presuppose that logical and mathematical truths are entirely autonomous from the psychology or moral character of the person who formulates them? Or does it presuppose something about the nature of justification — that an argument's validity does not depend on the biography of its author? I am particularly puzzled by a hypothetical case: if historians demonstrated that one of Frege's formal rules was not merely discovered by a person who held ugly views, but was actually constructed in such a way that its structure implicitly encoded those views, would the practice of separation still hold? Would scholars treat that rule as part of pure logic, or as a contaminated artifact? Please reconstruct the implicit theory — or theories — that make the separation practice coherent. Distinguish what that practice strictly requires from what merely fits well with it. Tell me whether the theory is consistent, and describe what would have to change in philosophical evaluation if we replaced it with a view that always binds an idea to the conditions and character of its origin.
