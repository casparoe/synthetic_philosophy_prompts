In effective altruism and rationalist forums, a familiar move is to frame the scale argument for insect sentience—vast populations multiplied by a low but non-negligible prior on conscious experience—as analogous to managing a portfolio of uncorrelated, low-probability, high-stakes bets, or to searching for a weak signal buried in overwhelming noise. Proponents of this framing sometimes invoke it to license conclusions about resource allocation: just as one wouldn't ignore a diversified long-shot portfolio, one shouldn't dismiss insect welfare. I want a rigorous audit of this analogy. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: what corresponds to the portfolio manager, the cost of entry, the payout structure, the correlation between positions, the risk-neutral versus risk-averse utility function, and the epistemic state of an agent who cannot identify which specific insects are sentient? Second, identify the fractures: financial payouts are fungible and linearly aggregable, but welfare may exhibit nonlinearities, threshold effects, or identity-dependent moral constraints; portfolio theory assumes independent outcomes, whereas insect suffering might be systematically correlated through shared ecological conditions; and the noise metaphor treats sentience as a binary hidden state rather than a graded capacity that scales with neural architecture. Third, address whether the analogy covertly imports contested assumptions—specifically, whether it presupposes that moral obligations aggregate via expected utility across individuals, and whether it illegitimately borrows the quantitative legitimacy of risk management to resolve what is partly a conceptual question about the boundaries of moral patienthood. Finally, report whether the normative conclusion—that the scale argument genuinely supports serious investment in insect welfare—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along.
