I am a novelist drafting an essay for a literary quarterly on whether immoral art can still be genuinely good art, and I should be grateful for your critical remarks on the argument sketched below. Rather than rehearsing the familiar biographical lists of disgraced painters and composers, I wish to shift the conceptual ground by treating an artwork as a persistent virtual structure: not a physical object but an ordered pattern of instructions capable of generating aesthetic experience in any suitably prepared recipient, rather like a virtual world that continues to run after its original programmer has logged off. From this ontological premise I derive that the creator's moral corruption is analogous not to a flaw embedded in the work itself, but to the private misconduct of the engineer who designed a bridge: it may demand justice, exclusion, or punishment directed at the person, yet it does not necessarily alter the structural integrity or the experiential value of what was built. I then consider the objection that certain moral defects — cruelty, for instance — are not external to the creator but are actually encoded into the work's representational patterns, so that appreciating the work requires endorsing or rehearsing that cruelty; I concede that in such cases the virtual structure itself is contaminated, but I maintain that this is an empirical feature of particular works rather than a metaphysical truth about art in general.

What I should particularly like you to address is whether this virtual-ontology framing genuinely advances the separation debate or merely dresses up an old intuition in unfamiliar vocabulary, whether the bridge-engineer analogy holds under closer scrutiny or collapses once we recognise that aesthetic experience is not merely structural but participatory, and whether I have overlooked a more fundamental disanalogy between digital persistence and aesthetic transmission — for instance, that artworks are not run by independent servers but are reconstructed anew in each recipient's consciousness. Please reply in British English, keep your observations precise and free of bibliographic recommendations, and conclude with a single-sentence summary of your overall assessment on its own final line.
