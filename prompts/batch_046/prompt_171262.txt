I want to compare intent alignment and value alignment not as a taxonomy exercise but as competing interpretations of the narrow alignment problem — that is, the task of ensuring an advanced system does what its designers meant rather than merely optimizing some misspecified proxy. The standard framing in alignment discourse treats these as nested: solve intent alignment first, then layer on values. I think that nesting breaks under reflexive pressure, and I want to see whether the distinction survives intact.

Here is the pressure. Designers' explicit intentions are unstable in a specific way: they are subject to the revealed-preference problem, but applied reflexively. A designer's stated goals are poor predictors of what she would endorse after sustained reflection, not through deception but through ordinary epistemic decay — her explicit model of her own preferences updates faster than any external system can track. So an intent-aligned system chases a target that its creators have already abandoned, while a value-aligned system must appeal to values that may never have been stably articulated in the first place.

Compare the two concepts under this condition. Which framing retains independent content if what we intend and what we value are not cleanly separable for agents whose self-models are themselves in flux? Does the narrow problem, strictly construed, still have work to do once we acknowledge that tracking designers' intentions may be equivalent to tracking a noisy, lagging indicator of their values? I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want a conceptual comparison that takes both positions seriously and tells me whether one collapses into the other, or whether there is a defensible boundary that survives this reflexive turn.
