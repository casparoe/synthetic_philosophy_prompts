I’m writing a feature on drug-policy frameworks for my science-and-ideas column. At a recent roundtable, two policy analysts—Ana and Ben—disagreed over whether Portugal’s decriminalization model is conceptually coherent when set beside cannabis-legalization regimes. Ana argued that Portugal’s framework is normatively contradictory: possession of all drugs, including heroin, was decriminalized and reframed as a public-health issue, yet supply remains fully criminalized and no legal markets exist, leaving users dependent on an unregulated black market. She said the state therefore sends a split message—treating consumption as a medical problem deserving compassion while implicitly treating the substances themselves as so dangerous that even regulated access is unthinkable. Ben replied that this separation is intentional and defensible: decriminalization targets the user, prohibition targets the supplier, and conflating the two erases the moral distinction between harming oneself and facilitating harm to others. He added that comparing Portugal with cannabis-legalizing jurisdictions is a category error, because cannabis carries a different risk profile, and extending legal supply to opioids or stimulants would amount to state endorsement of far more dangerous consumption. Ana countered that citizens do not experience policy in institutional silos; they perceive a unified normative signal about which behaviors and substances society accepts. Ben responded that if policy coherence required unified signaling across all domains, then the legal status of alcohol and tobacco would have to be reconciled with drug prohibition, a consistency no government pursues. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: who is right about each specific claim, what—if anything—both are missing, and who is more right overall about whether Portugal’s model embodies a genuine conceptual contradiction or a sustainable institutional separation.
