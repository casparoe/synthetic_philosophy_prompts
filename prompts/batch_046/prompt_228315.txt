I serve on the strategic review board of a consortium of mid-sized AI research labs, and every quarter we must evaluate proposed safety modifications that carry a measurable alignment tax — a quantifiable or estimated reduction in benchmark performance, inference efficiency, or development speed. The problem is that we have no reusable framework for weighing that tax against the safety gain, especially when competitive dynamics mean that unilateral adoption might cede market position without actually reducing risk. I was discussing this with colleagues after a particularly contentious meeting over a monitoring proposal, and we agreed we need something more rigorous than case-by-case argument. I want a step-by-step decision procedure — essentially a flowchart rendered in ordered questions — that my board can run in about ninety minutes per proposal. The procedure should treat the competitiveness constraint as a structured input rather than an automatic veto, and it should explicitly separate mechanistic steps from points where human judgment is unavoidable.

Please design the procedure around three inputs that the board must estimate before running it: (1) the magnitude and reversibility of the alignment tax, categorized as temporary and rollback-capable, structural but hidden, or structural and observable; (2) the category of risk being addressed, distinguished as catastrophic and irreversible, serious but bounded, or speculative and long-tail; and (3) the competitive landscape, assessed by whether adoption is externally observable, whether rival labs could replicate the safety feature within one quarter, and whether credible multi-party coordination is feasible within six months. For each step, specify the exact question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the procedure terminates with one of three recommendations — adopt unilaterally, defer pending coordination, or reject and document — versus where it stops and requires a signed value judgment from the board chair because the question is ethical rather than empirical. Include at least four mechanistic test conditions linking tax magnitude and reversibility to coordination feasibility, but flag which of these are judgment calls disguised as arithmetic. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals: (A) a real-time interpretability monitor that increases inference latency by twelve percent and might reveal deceptive alignment behaviors, adopted in a landscape where rivals are actively hiring for the same capability; (B) a training-data filter that reduces top-line benchmark scores by three percent but eliminates a specific, documented failure mode, in a landscape where the feature could be hidden from competitors; and (C) a capability-capping architecture that reduces effective performance by twenty percent but is believed to prevent autonomous replication, with no credible coordination mechanism available. Be sure to include at least one case where the procedure yields a recommendation the board is likely to reject — for example, advising unilateral adoption despite high competitive exposure, or advising deferral despite catastrophic risk — and identify precisely which step produces that tension and why the value judgment must override it.

Finally, I want the procedure to be honest about what it cannot do: specify the classes of proposals for which it will misfire, the information that is typically missing at review time, and the ways competitive dynamics are systematically underestimated by boards with commercial stakes. If the premise of operationalizing the competitiveness constraint into a ninety-minute checklist is itself confused — for instance, if the constraint makes any unilateral safety investment irrational regardless of procedure — say so plainly rather than constructing an elaborate framework that ignores the contradiction. I am not asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific authors; I need this to be usable by people who will run it without searching the literature.
