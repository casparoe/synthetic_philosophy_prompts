I work as an independent mediator who sits in on family meetings when end-of-life decisions have stalled, and I have no formal philosophy training—just a lot of time thinking about the old argument that death is not a harm to the dead person because the dead do not exist to suffer anything. I need a reusable, step-by-step decision procedure I can run in about fifteen minutes to answer one recurring question: if we withdraw treatment, allow a transition, or honor a directive, are we harming the patient? The procedure should be built directly on the principle that harm requires a co-present subject, but it must also handle what I have started calling “shadow harm”—the worry that a living person is harmed now by the future absence of goods, or that an unconscious body is harmed by being kept in a state that severs narrative continuity with the person who once inhabited it. It needs to work for patients who are conscious, deeply unconscious, brain-dead with maintained circulation, or in a persistent state with no awareness, and it needs to distinguish factual steps from value judgments. For each step, state the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the procedure stops with a definite answer versus where it hands the decision to me because the issue is one of values rather than the presence or absence of a harm-subject. Then apply the procedure to three cases: first, an elderly patient in deep dementia with no awareness, family divided over withdrawing a feeding tube; second, a brain-dead pregnant patient on sustained support, where the family asks whether discontinuing support to allow cardiac death harms her specifically; third, a conscious terminal patient who requests sedation into permanent unconsciousness until death, with a relative arguing this harms her “future self” by cutting off remaining experiences. In each case, show where the procedure lands, flag the step that feels most uncomfortable or counterintuitive, and say what the procedure explicitly cannot settle. Lead with the bottom line—the core principle translated into one operational sentence—then the procedure itself, then the three worked applications, with no preamble and no restating of the question at the end.
