I recently had an extended, rather heated argument with a close friend who serves as a senior compliance officer for a regional municipal authority. The authority is now receiving a growing volume of procurement proposals that embed algorithmic risk-scoring systems—predictive maintenance, resource allocation, emergency routing—and she is convinced that Kant’s universalizability tests could serve as a genuine decision procedure for her office, not merely as post-hoc philosophical ornamentation. I think she is underestimating how much interpretive labor the Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity require, particularly once institutional mediation replaces direct interpersonal action, but rather than continue the dispute in the abstract I want you to build the actual procedure she imagines.

Design a step-by-step audit protocol that her team could apply repeatedly to any proposed contract: specify the exact sequence of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each node, where the protocol terminates in a clear approval or rejection, and where it must suspend judgment and refer the case to a human ethics panel because the conflict is one of values rather than formal contradiction. In translating Kant’s framework into this institutional setting, you must resolve a specific ambiguity: the maxim of action should not be reduced to the private psychological intention of a procurement officer, but should be articulated as the systemic rule that the algorithm instantiates across the resident population. You must preserve the distinction between contradictions in conception and contradictions in will, and you must include a dedicated structural check for the Formula of Humanity—one that detects whether residents are being treated as mere means even when no individual agent intends degradation, precisely because algorithmic mediation obscures the interpersonal relation. Apply the completed procedure to two concrete proposals: first, a predictive water-pipe maintenance contract that harvests granular household usage data without individualized opt-in mechanisms; second, an emergency flood-alert routing contract that systematically excludes neighborhoods with lower digital connectivity, thereby directing protective resources away from them. In your applications, include at least one outcome that runs against common moral intuition—either the procedure approves a contract that seems plainly exploitative, or it blocks a contract that seems clearly beneficial—and explain whether that result should be read as a failure of operationalization or as a faithful, if uncomfortable, consequence of Kant’s original framework. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot do: identify the class of cases where universalizability yields an indeterminate result, and explain what kind of supplementary normative reasoning—without collapsing into consequentialist balancing—would be required to resolve them.
