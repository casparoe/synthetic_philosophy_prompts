In debates about Aristotle's account of exchange and wealth-getting, interpreters often analogize oikonomia to healthy metabolism with natural satiety limits and chrematistikê to cancerous growth that ignores bodily boundaries. They use this biological framing to argue that his attack on interest-bearing money is essentially a naturalistic fallacy: since coin is barren, making it breed is like forcing reproduction on stone, so the critique collapses if we reject nature as a moral norm. Audit this analogy: lay out the correspondences between metabolic process and economic practice, identify what Aristotle's standard of exchange value — need, or chreia — actually does in the argument and whether it behaves like a metabolic signal or like a conventional measure, expose the assumptions about commensurability and justice in exchange that the cancer metaphor quietly imports, and determine whether his reasoning about proportional equality survives once those biological imports are stripped away. Ask also whether a different analogy, perhaps drawn from measurement or tool-use rather than biology, would do less violence to his view. Conclude by saying whether the metaphor was carrying the conclusion that Aristotle's economics is pre-scientific moralism, or whether that conclusion depends on misreading the role of chreia in his account.
