I've been arguing with my roommate about federalism and subsidiarity—specificaly whether the EU's setup is a betrayal of the principle or its best real-world approximation, and whether the US, Germany, or Switzerland actually do it better. I've ended up with this somewhat contrarian position: subsidiarity shouldn't be read as a lower-is-always-closer-to-citizens rule, but as a functional principle about which governance level can internalize the spillovers of a decision without triggering larger coordination failures elsewhere. From that angle, US competitive federalism, German cooperative federalism, and the Swiss cantonal model all miss the target in different directions, but Europe's problem is structurally worse. The member-state tier sits in an awkward middle—too large to capture genuinely local knowledge about regional labor markets or housing policy, yet too small to handle cross-border energy grids or migration flows on its own—while the EU level is too remote for the local stuff and still too fragmented for the transnational stuff. So there's a missing middle that doesn't map onto any existing political boundary, and just shifting competences between Brussels and the capitals can't fix the misalignment because the gap is the absence of a functional intermediate layer, not the wrong assignment of powers.
