Consider the following line of reasoning about the ontology of species, which has come up in recent seminar discussions and which strikes me as suspicious because, if sound, it appears to dissolve biological taxonomy altogether.

(1) The Biological Species Concept offers the most defensible account of specieshood: two populations belong to the same species if and only if they are capable in principle of interbreeding and producing fertile offspring.

(2) Capability in principle is a modal condition; it abstracts from actual geographic isolation, temporal separation, or behavioral barriers, and asks what could occur under biologically suitable conditions.

(3) Contemporary reproductive technology and synthetic biology have shown that, with appropriate laboratory intervention, gametes from organisms in taxonomically distant groups—say, a domestic horse and Przewalski's horse, or even more distant mammalian lineages—can be fused or engineered to yield viable, fertile hybrids.

(4) If technological mediation counts among the suitable conditions that determine modal capacity in (2), then the interbreeding condition is satisfied across virtually all mammalian populations, and analogously across broad swaths of other kingdoms.

(5) By parity of reasoning, if artificial conditions establish modal capacity for sexually reproducing groups, then analogous interventions—engineered plasmid transfer, viral vectors, or synthetic compatibility—must do the same for asexual and microbial lineages; hence the condition applies, and is satisfied, across domains.

(6) Therefore, on the BSC, there is essentially one mammalian species, one bacterial domain-species, and so forth—taxonomy collapses into a handful of hyper-inclusive groups.

(7) But this result is absurd: it contradicts well-supported practice in distinguishing, for instance, Canis lupus from Canis latrans, or ecologically distinct strains within Escherichia coli.

(8) So the BSC must be false.

(9) Any replacement criterion—morphological, phylogenetic, or ecological—either inherits the same modal collapse if interpreted permissively, or fails to cover asexual organisms, ring species, and allopatric populations if interpreted restrictively.

(10) Hence no coherent criterion distinguishes species, and there are no species.

The argument seems to show that accepting any principled account of specieshood forces antirealism about species altogether. I am inclined to preserve the intuition that Canis lupus and Canis latrans are genuinely different species, and I am also inclined to think that technological possibility should not dissolve natural boundaries. Yet I am unsure where the weakest link lies. Is the error in (3) through (4), where laboratory intervention is illegitimately treated as revealing a natural modal capacity? Or is it in (9), where we assume that all alternatives suffer the same fate? Or is the argument actually valid, so that we must pay the price of abandoning either (1) or (7)? Please identify the premise or inference you think should be rejected, explain why it initially looked plausible, and spell out exactly what giving it up costs—particularly for how we treat reproductive isolation in ring species, in microbes, and in cases of human-mediated hybridization.
