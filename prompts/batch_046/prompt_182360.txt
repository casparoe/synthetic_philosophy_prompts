Consider a philosopher of collective responsibility who consistently applies a fourfold taxonomy—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—derived from the framework associated with Karl Jaspers, though she modifies its scope in precise ways. Her rulings on earlier cases are these. First, present citizens of a continuously existing democratic state bear political guilt for past state crimes only if they knowingly maintain institutional benefits without repudiation; mere citizenship is insufficient. Second, moral guilt is strictly agent-relative and non-transferable; it extinguishes with the death of the perpetrator and cannot be inherited by descendants or assigned to groups. Third, metaphysical guilt applies to anyone—regardless of citizenship—who had knowledge of systematic suffering, possessed capacity to resist or aid, and failed to do so; it is therefore not bounded by state membership. Fourth, when a state is legally dissolved without succession, its political guilt lapses entirely, though moral guilt remains with identifiable individuals, and metaphysical guilt may still attach to survivors who were present and aware. Fifth, a continuously existing corporate entity can bear an analog of political guilt if its governance structures and profit mechanisms remain substantially unchanged, but it cannot bear metaphysical guilt, which requires conscious moral agency.

Now consider the case of the Republic of Kharon, a sovereign state that existed from 1971 to 2003, implemented a systematic policy of forced linguistic and religious assimilation against the Tenu minority, and was dissolved by international treaty. Its territory was partitioned among three neighboring states with explicit denial of legal continuity; the Tenu population is now dispersed across these states as minorities. No individual state officials have been prosecuted, and the successor governments reject any institutional inheritance. Activists among the Tenu argue that the dispersed former citizens of Kharon—now citizens of the three successor states—bear metaphysical guilt for collective failure to resist assimilation, and that the successor states inherit Kharon's political guilt by virtue of territorial and demographic continuity despite the absence of legal succession.

Predict this philosopher's verdict: does any Jaspers-style guilt attach, and if so, which form applies to the dispersed citizens, to the successor states, or to neither? Identify the two most plausible interpretive rules that explain her prior pattern—such as a legal-continuity rule for political guilt versus a demographic-territorial-continuity rule, and an awareness-plus-omission rule versus a citizenship-boundary rule for metaphysical guilt—and state what each predicts about Kharon. Offer your best-guess verdict with reasoning, and specify one further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the leading competing rules.
