I am trying to articulate the principle that distinguishes a long-run predictive theory that is genuinely falsified from one that is merely premature or structurally rescued. Here are my judgments on several cases.

Thomas Malthus's claim that population tends to grow geometrically while subsistence grows arithmetically seemed decisively undermined by the twentieth-century agricultural revolution; nevertheless, I regard defenses that invoke a temporary technological respite as legitimate structural rescue rather than ad hoc evasion, because the underlying pressure dynamics remain intact.

By contrast, a 1960s demographic projection forecasting twenty billion people and global famine by the year 2000, saved only by appealing to unforeseen contraceptive adoption, strikes me as falsified: the rescue alters the boundary conditions so radically that the original mechanism no longer explains the outcome.

A nineteenth-century economic argument that industrialization would exhaust coal reserves by the mid-twentieth century was wrong in its timeline, yet I consider it premature rather than false, because extraction costs eventually rose and coal's dominance did plateau, suggesting the mechanism was sound but the temporal parameters were misestimated.

A hypothetical 1990 neuroscience model asserting that all long-term synaptic plasticity depends exclusively on a single molecular pathway was disproven by the discovery of parallel pathways; however, since blocking that specific pathway does severely impair learning, I judge the theory not rescued but domain-restricted—partially confirmed within a narrower boundary.

I also hold that a 1988 climate projection that used an overly high climate sensitivity parameter and consequently overpredicted warming should be treated as disconfirmed with respect to that parameter, even though the broader greenhouse mechanism remains intact. Conversely, a 1970s thesis that automation would eliminate the vast majority of jobs by 2020 seems to me not falsified but delayed: capital-labor substitution is proceeding, only far more slowly than predicted, which suggests parameter error rather than mechanism failure.

Yet I remain uncertain about a 1995 forecast that gene therapy would cure all monogenic disorders by 2010. The delivery mechanisms failed, but cures are now emerging decades late, so the structural claim may be correct despite catastrophic temporal and boundary-condition errors.

Is there a unified principle—perhaps one that separates mechanism failure, parameter misestimation, and boundary-condition misspecification—that accommodates all of these verdicts? If so, apply it to the gene-therapy case and predict whether a hypothetical 2020 theory claiming that quantum error correction will render all classical encryption obsolete by 2035, which is then delayed by unforeseen engineering bottlenecks but eventually succeeds in 2060, should be classified as premature, rescued, or falsified. And please indicate which of my judgments, especially the contrast between the coal-exhaustion and the demographic-projection cases, must be revised or abandoned to preserve consistency.
