I am preparing a long-form feature for an ideas journal on whether nineteenth-century critiques of mass publicity can still illuminate our algorithmic media environment, and my current focus is Søren Kierkegaard’s suspicion of the public as an abstract, leveling phantom together with his corresponding anxiety about the press as an engine that manufactures collective opinion without individual accountability. I want to extend this analysis to the architecture of algorithmically curated social feeds—systems in which engagement optimization, rather than editorial judgment, determines visibility, and in which users are simultaneously producers and consumers within an infinite scroll engineered to maximize continuous affective reaction. Specifically, please predict how Kierkegaard might have responded to this environment: would he treat algorithmic personalization as merely an intensification of the press’s leveling force—now dispersed into private, predictive loops that eliminate even the pretense of a shared public sphere—or would he see in it a qualitatively new form of abstraction, perhaps a quantified crowd that dissolves the single individual not by homogenizing opinion but by continuously reconstituting the self through data-driven anticipation? In your answer, address whether he would still maintain that the antidote lies in the category of the single individual standing in inwardness before God, or whether the intimacy, speed, and silence-destroying design of these feeds would compel him to rethink whether such inwardness remains structurally possible today. I am looking for a philosophically precise projection rather than a historical summary—one that draws on the conceptual mechanics of his critique of publicity and individual responsibility rather than on biographical speculation.
