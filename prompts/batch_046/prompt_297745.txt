As a retired biologist who has recently begun reading philosophy, I have been reflecting on origin essentialism — the idea that my origins are essential to my identity. My judgements about several cases are as follows, though I suspect they pull in different directions.

I think I could not have come from a different sperm, even with the same egg; and equally not from a different egg with the same sperm. Yet if my zygote had split to produce monozygotic twins, I do not believe either twin could have been the other — each is tied to its particular post-split trajectory despite their shared origin. A clone carrying my nuclear genome is not me, which suggests genetic identity is not sufficient. But if my embryo had been cryopreserved for decades and then gestated, the resulting person would still be me, so mere temporal delay does not break origin. Finally, if donor gametes had, by sheer chance, produced a genome identical to mine, I still think that person would not be me — causal-historical connection to my actual conception matters more than genetic match.

Is there a single, precise principle that captures all six verdicts? If not, which one should I drop, and what does the best revised principle predict for a chimaeric individual formed from two distinct zygotes? Please state the principle clearly, show which case motivates each clause, note its verdict on the chimaera case, and be honest about which of my intuitions it cannot accommodate.
