In several environmental regulatory agencies, impact panels must deliver separate majority judgments on three linked propositions: that a development threatens a specific ecosystem service (P), that no mitigation reduces that threat below a statutory threshold (Q), and therefore that approval should be denied (R). The charter requires the panel to be “internally coherent,” yet published reports often show majorities for both P and Q paired with a recommendation to approve (not-R), or the reverse, with the chair resolving the conflict by invoking an unstated “precautionary weight” rather than logical closure. What philosophical commitments about collective rationality, consistency, and the nature of group belief does this protocol presuppose? Does it implicitly treat the panel as a single reasoning agent caught in the discursive dilemma, or does it deny that logical relations bind collective judgments at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory—whether premise-based aggregation modified by epistemic risk-aversion, conclusion-based aggregation, belief-revision, or pluralist disagreement—distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it, and say what in the protocol would have to change if the agency adopted the opposite commitment about how groups ought to reason.
