I was arguing with a friend late into the evening about whether Darwin’s scattered remarks on morality and religion in The Descent of Man and in his private correspondence can be coherently extended to problems that only crystallized after his death. My friend maintained that Darwin was essentially a proto-evolutionary psychologist who would have embraced modern accounts of morality as an adaptive cluster of social heuristics—something in the neighborhood of moral-foundations theory—and that he would have treated religious belief as an obvious evolutionary byproduct of hyperactive agency detection. I countered that Darwin’s insistence on the gradual cultivation of sympathy and on social instincts as genuinely moral, rather than merely strategic, suggests he might resist reducing ethics to a modular toolkit, and that his careful agnosticism about ultimate causes might make him wary of both adaptationist and spandrel accounts of religion that claim too much explanatory reach.

So I want to pose this as a reconstructed thought experiment: imagine Darwin, retaining his full command of natural selection and his documented methodological reluctance to overreach, is confronted with three bodies of post-Darwinian work. First, the evolutionary debunking argument in ethics—the claim that demonstrating moral beliefs arise from selective pressures on ancestral cooperation thereby undermines their objective normative authority. Second, the modern dispute between multilevel-selection accounts of altruism and strictly gene-centered explanations, especially the question whether group-level selection is indispensable for explaining large-scale human moral norms. Third, contemporary empirical models that treat religious cognition either as a potential adaptation for social cohesion at scale or as a misfiring of evolved mind-reading capacities. How would Darwin likely adjudicate these? Would he regard the debunking argument as committing the genetic fallacy he implicitly resisted when he defended the moral value of social instincts despite their animal origins? Would his own ambiguous commitments about the level at which selection operates push him toward a group-selectionist picture of morality, or would he preserve an individual-level account modified only by social context? And with respect to religion, given his personal drift toward agnosticism and his deep suspicion of design arguments, would he find the adaptationist account more plausible than the byproduct account, or would he reject both for overstepping the boundaries of naturalistic explanation?

Please lay out your reasoning as a map of reconstructed commitments: identify which specific positions in Darwin’s published writings and notebooks support each response, distinguish where modern debates force him into stances he never articulated, and flag precisely where his methodological caution—his insistence that natural selection explains the origin but not necessarily the value or the ultimate purpose of a trait—would lead him to resist the stronger conclusions drawn by present-day evolutionary psychologists and philosophers of religion. I am especially interested in whether he would see modern evolutionary ethics as vindicating his naturalism about the moral sense or as betraying it.
