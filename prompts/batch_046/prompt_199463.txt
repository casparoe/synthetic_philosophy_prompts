I'm working through Hobbes's philosophy of science and have constructed an argument that seems to show his account of geometric demonstration undermines his own materialist mechanics, but I cannot locate the precise misstep. The argument runs as follows:

(1) Hobbes holds that geometry alone achieves true demonstration, because its objects—points, lines, and figures—are not discovered in nature but are produced by the geometer's own motion, so every geometric property follows necessarily from definitions that lie entirely within our control.

(2) If an object's properties depend entirely on human construction and stipulation, then the only possible errors about it are internal inconsistencies; there is no independent state of affairs that could make a geometric claim false.

(3) Therefore, geometric necessity for Hobbes is conditional in a strong sense: it is the necessity of consequence from our own arbitrary starting points, not necessity rooted in an independent reality.

(4) At the same time, Hobbes models mechanics—the science of bodies in motion—directly on geometry, treating physical motions as if they fall under the same demonstrative structure and claiming that mechanical conclusions are necessarily true of actual bodies.

(5) For mechanics to apply to actual bodies in this way, those bodies must be subsumable under the same kind of constructed definitions that make geometric demonstration possible.

(6) But actual bodies exist and move independently of any human definition; if they are forced into constructed geometric categories, either the categories misrepresent them or they are imposed arbitrarily.

(7) Consequently, Hobbes must either restrict mechanics to artificial constructions rather than real bodies, or admit that real bodies have properties independent of our definitions, which breaks the parallel with geometry and removes the ground for necessary demonstration in physics.

(8) Either outcome collapses his ambition to make the science of motion a demonstrative, necessary discipline about the material world.

The conclusion strikes me as too destructive—Hobbes clearly intended mechanics to be both demonstrative and genuinely about nature—yet each step feels defensible. I would most like to preserve (1) and (4), since they capture his core commitments. Which premise or inference should be rejected, why did it appear sound, and what does abandoning it cost for his broader philosophical system?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
