I am a PhD student in ethics writing my dissertation on the architecture of non-consequentialist moral constraints, and I am examining what I call the core paradox of deontology: if duties are genuinely categorical then they must remain binding even when following them produces catastrophically worse outcomes, yet if catastrophic outcomes can defeat a duty then the duty appears to collapse into nothing more than a high-threshold consequentialist rule with an unusual weighting scheme. I would like you to rank five proposed resolutions to this paradox by how well they preserve a genuinely agent-relative, non-optimizing deontic structure, ordering them from the most conceptually stable to the least convincing. The five are Ross's pluralist intuitionism with its prima facie duties; Kant's distinction between perfect and imperfect duties; Scanlon's contractualist account of wrongness; a threshold-deontological view that permits constraint-violation only once harm exceeds a specified intersubjective magnitude; and a position I have been developing, which I term structural preservationism, that analogizes deontic rules to grammatical rules—conditions that preserve the form of practical agency rather than maximize states of affairs. Present your ranking and reasoning entirely in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; for each position write a brief paragraph indicating whether it resolves the paradox or merely relocates it, with special attention to whether it can accommodate agent-relative permissions without importing a hidden utility calculus. No citations, bibliographies, or reading lists are needed; I care only about the internal logic of each view and how it handles the tension.
