I was discussing with a friend last night whether disjunctivism about perceptual experience can survive not the classic argument from illusion, but a kind of continuous degradation problem that we kept circling back to. The standard dialectic, as I understand it, turns on discrete pairs—veridical perception versus matching hallucination or illusion—where subjective indistinguishability is supposed to force a common mental factor. Disjunctivists like McDowell respond by denying that there is any shared mental kind across the two cases: in veridical perception, the object itself is a constituent of the mental state, whereas in hallucination there is only a seeming-to-see. My friend kept insisting that this binary framing misses something important about real perceptual failure, which is rarely all-or-nothing.

I want to write an essay exploring whether disjunctivism can accommodate graded sensory corruption—cases where an object remains present and causally active, but the signal is progressively degraded by noise, partial occlusion, or neural interference, so that the experience slides continuously from high-fidelity perception toward something that looks, phenomenologically, like a poor copy or a fragmented appearance. Rather than asking whether there is a shared mental state with a matching hallucination, the question becomes whether there is a continuous spectrum of mental-state kinds, each constituted differently depending on the degree and nature of the corruption, or whether disjunctivism forces us to draw an arbitrary line somewhere along the gradient. I am particularly interested in whether this framing reveals that disjunctivism implicitly relies on a threshold concept of perceptual success that it never explicitly defends, and whether that undermines its response to the argument from illusion more generally.

Could you comment on whether my framing of disjunctivism is accurate—particularly whether it genuinely treats perceptual success as all-or-nothing, or whether resources exist within the view to accommodate degrees of fidelity without collapsing into representationalism? I am also interested in whether the gradient-degradation scenario I have outlined poses a structurally different challenge from the traditional argument from illusion, or whether it merely complicates the same dialectic without changing its logical shape. Finally, do you think the distinction I am drawing between discrete subjective indistinguishability and what I am calling continuous epistemic erosion is philosophically robust, or does it rest on an unstated assumption about how mental constitution must work?
