I am convening an advanced philosophy-of-history workshop on causal overdetermination in macro-historical explanation, and I need you to compose the formal joint memorandum that two adversarial collaborators might sign after sustained good-faith engagement. The first camp, which I will call the counterfactual monists, maintains that when a historical outcome is genuinely overdetermined—such that two or more causal complexes are each sufficient—the correct historiographical response is to isolate the single counterfactual-dependent triggering cause and treat the remaining sufficient conditions as explanatory redundancy to be pruned by parsimony. The second camp, the conjunctive pluralists, maintains that in cases of ontological overdetermination each sufficient complex retains irreducible explanatory necessity, so that the proper narrative form is conjunctive: removing any sufficient complex alters the explanatory architecture and the evidential warrant of the account, even when the outcome itself remains unchanged.

Please draft the document in three parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: shared commitments regarding the distinction between ontological and explanatory overdetermination, the limits of counterfactual testing in historical inquiry, and the evidential problem I want you to treat as a shared framing condition—what I term the archival visibility asymmetry, namely that documentary preservation is causally selective, tracking some causal pathways far more thoroughly than others, and thereby introducing a systematic bias into our assessments of which redundant causes are genuinely available for counterfactual evaluation. Second, a disagreements table that formulates each camp’s core thesis in wording its own members would accept, phrased neutrally with respect to the opposing view; the disagreements should cover not only the normative status of explanatory parsimony but also whether the archival visibility asymmetry undermines counterfactual monism or merely complicates pluralist verification. Third, identify the precise cruxes—those subordinate questions such that settling them would move at least one party—including specifically whether explanatory economy should be indexed to counterfactual fragility of the outcome or to the completeness of causal mapping, and whether multi-causal narratives commit a fallacy of redundant causation or avoid a fallacy of explanatory omission. For each crux, present a concrete historical case or evidentiary pattern both sides agree is relevant—such as competing explanations for the adoption of a specific institutional reform or the outbreak of a well-documented conflict—together with each side’s explicit predictive judgment about what that case demonstrates, written in advance of any adjudication.

The memorandum should be written in the voice of a formal philosophical text suitable for circulation among historians and philosophers of science: precise, without polemic, and avoiding any request for bibliographies, citation lists, or references to literature that a reader without access to external databases could not evaluate. Do not quote any specific author verbatim; paraphrase any doctrinal positions you invoke. The goal is a constructive document that makes the residual disagreement exact rather than diagnosing why the debate has stalled, and that treats both camps as capable of signing every line except those explicitly marked as disputed.
