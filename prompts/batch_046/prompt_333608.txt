Please describe the most important implications of Amartya Sen's entitlement approach to famine—specifically the thesis that mass starvation typically arises from collapses in exchange entitlements rather than from aggregate food-availability declines—for how we should morally and politically classify three historical crises: the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish potato famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward in China. For each case, explain whether the catastrophe is better understood as a natural disaster or as a political crime, and identify precisely which conceptual mechanisms in Sen's framework (direct entitlements, trade-based entitlements, labor-market linkages, or state-mediated distribution) do the primary explanatory work. Then extend the analysis to a hypothetical but structurally detailed scenario: a wealthy contemporary nation with ample national food stocks abruptly abolishes all non-market distribution mechanisms—food banks, school meal programs, and price-controlled ration shops—in a region characterized by extreme wealth inequality, producing localized mass starvation despite no reduction in total agricultural output. Using only the entitlement framework, analyze where moral and causal responsibility should lie in this invented case, and identify the specific points at which the framework's explanatory power reaches its limits when applied to a crisis driven entirely by institutional withdrawal rather than by harvest failure or market shock.
