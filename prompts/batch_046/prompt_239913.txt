I am preparing a discussion for a philosophy of biology seminar focused on the ontology of function, and I want to test whether the standard etiological versus causal-role distinction holds up under synthetic and exaptive conditions. Please write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—Dr. Aris Thorne, who holds that biological functions are essentially selected-effect properties grounded in evolutionary history, and Dr. Mara Voss, who holds that they are systemic contributions defined by current causal roles within an organized system.

Rather than using canonical examples like the heart or the kidney, have them debate a single, detailed hypothetical: a synthetic microbial consortium originally engineered in a laboratory setting (without evolutionary co-adaptation among its members) to metabolize an industrial pollutant. After accidental release, it persists in wild wetlands where the pollutant is absent, and now its dominant activity is nitrogen fixation that supports local flora. Within individual strains, there remain transcriptionally active but metabolically inert enzyme clusters—vestigial to the original design—that are physically coupled to essential regulatory elements and thus cannot be lost without damaging core metabolism.

Thorne and Voss should directly engage with one another on three specific questions: whether the nitrogen-fixing activity counts as a genuine function of the consortium given its discontinuous evolutionary history; whether the inert clusters still possess a function despite making no current causal contribution; and whether the original design intention can serve as an analog to natural selection for fixing functional ascriptions. Voss should press Thorne on whether an etiological account can accommodate designed systems without either collapsing into intentionalism or arbitrarily excluding artificial selection histories, and Thorne should challenge Voss on whether a pure causal-role analysis can distinguish a genuine function from an accidental by-product or a spandrel without implicitly invoking historical selection criteria. The dialogue should contain real argumentative exchange—each speaker must respond to the previous point rather than deliver parallel monologues—and should maintain a formal, philosophically rigorous tone throughout. Do not include bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations to specific texts.
