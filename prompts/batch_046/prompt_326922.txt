I've been tracking a recurring architecture in European reproductive policy. Several states prohibit or severely restrict commercial surrogacy and compensated egg donation within their borders—typically grounding the ban in concerns about commodification, exploitation, or human dignity—yet they do not criminalize citizens who travel to jurisdictions where these markets are legal, enter contracts there, and return home. Once back, the state generally recognizes the results: it registers births, grants citizenship to the children, and issues parentage orders that validate the very arrangements it domestically forbids. The prohibition remains territorially intact and symbolically enforced, but the practice is not eliminated; it is geographically displaced, with the home state accommodating the foreign outcome rather than suppressing it. I have noticed an analogous structure in other bioethical domains, where a practice is banned locally but its extraterritorial products are fully incorporated into domestic legal and social life.

Is there an established concept—perhaps in legal philosophy, bioethics, or political theory—for this specific configuration: a territorial moral-legal prohibition paired with non-penalized extraterritorial access and subsequent domestic recognition of the prohibited act's fruits? How should it be distinguished from "reproductive tourism" (which describes the individual's movement rather than the state's posture), from ordinary regulatory arbitrage (which typically concerns corporate relocation rather than state-sanctioned moral outsourcing), and from straightforward hypocrisy? If no precise term exists, I would like one coined with an explicit definition, boundary conditions that separate it from cases where the state genuinely attempts global suppression (for example, by refusing to recognize foreign judgments or by extending criminal liability extraterritorially), and an analysis of whether the recognition of outcomes is an essential feature of the phenomenon or merely a contingent institutional choice. What are the edges of this concept—does it apply only when the state actively facilitates recognition, or also when it merely tolerates the results through legal inertia?
