Recently, after a surgical procedure that temporarily eliminated proprioceptive and tactile feedback while leaving consciousness fully intact, I found myself in a state structurally analogous to Avicenna's suspended figure: aware that I existed, yet without sensory access to my body or its position. This experience has made me return to the flying-man argument with new urgency, and to the interpretive deadlock that has surrounded it for centuries. One camp reads the thought experiment as establishing that the soul is an immaterial substance ontologically independent of the body; another camp reads it as demonstrating only that self-awareness is phenomenologically independent of sensory input, carrying no commitment to substance dualism. Despite extensive discussion, the dispute shows no sign of convergence, and I want to understand why.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about Avicenna's text or about historical fidelity, but about an unacknowledged ambiguity in the very concept of "independence" at work in the argument. One side treats independence as a modal-ontological claim—that the soul can exist or be individuated without the body—while the other treats it as an epistemic-phenomenological claim—that knowledge of one's own existence does not require bodily sensation. If this is right, the disagreement may be partly verbal, with both sides answering different questions under the same formulation. But I am unsure whether there is also a substantive disagreement hidden beneath—perhaps about what counts as an adequate explanation of minimal self-consciousness, or about whether modern analogues such as prolonged sensory deprivation or states of complete bodily paralysis could ever adjudicate the ontological reading.

I want a diagnosis rather than a survey of interpretations or a verdict on what Avicenna truly meant. Please examine what each camp actually says: do they differ over empirical facts, over standards of what an argument must establish, over which cases count as relevant data, or over starting intuitions that neither side can independently defend? Is there a hidden formal question—perhaps about the logic of arguments from conceivability to possibility—doing the work? Are the sides answering different questions under one name? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is genuinely hard?

In particular, assess whether my ambiguity hypothesis is correct, and specify—if the disagreement is real—what kind of conceptual distinction, argument, or piece of evidence could actually move it; or, if it is verbal or ill-posed, say so and explain why further debate should not be expected to resolve it. Please support the diagnosis by engaging concretely with the positions as described, and avoid bibliographic lists, appeals to scholarly authority, or requests for citation.
