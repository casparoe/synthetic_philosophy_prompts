I defend the view that Japanese-style indirectness and white lies can be morally required rather than merely permissible, on the grounds that they protect interactional sovereignty—the hearer's authority over the social framing in which information is received—while Dutch directness wrongly reduces communication to bare information transfer, thereby violating that autonomy. Directness advocates reply that politeness is at best instrumental and that any deception, however gentle, undermines epistemic agency. What would have to be true for this defense of indirectness to collapse? Map the exposure: which arguments, cases, or distinctions would be decisive (for instance, that framing-control is not a distinct autonomy interest, or that indirectness in medical or contractual contexts is clearly harmful), which merely awkward, and whether the view can be qualified into unfalsifiability if every harmful instance of indirectness is dismissed as misapplication. Say what a committed defender should concede as genuinely damaging, distinguish that from what they would reject, and judge whether insulation from counterexample is a strength or a weakness for an ethical theory of communication.
