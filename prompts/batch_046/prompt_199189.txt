Next Thursday I am presenting a working-paper defense at our department’s workshop on moral change, arguing that the collapse of practices such as dueling and chattel slavery was driven neither by the triumph of moral argument nor by shifting material interests, but by what I term ceremonial dislocation: the structural rupture of the institutional rituals—court protocols, mediation ceremonies, codes of dress and deference—that made an honor-based norm legible and performable within a given social choreography. I take Bernard Williams’s claim that hypertraditional societies regulate conduct through honor rather than through utility or right, and Kwame Anthony Appiah’s analysis of honor worlds in which norms persist because they confer status within a shared script, but I depart from both by contending that honor norms do not erode primarily through internal contradiction or economic obsolescence; rather, they dissolve when bureaucratic rationalization and the state’s monopolization of violence render the old choreography unstageable, leaving the practice not refuted but ceremonially unexecutable. The audience will be a mixed seminar of political philosophers familiar with Appiah’s recent work, intellectual historians skeptical of rationalist narratives of moral progress, two Marxist historians of the Atlantic economy who treat abolition as a superstructural effect of capitalist transformation, and several analytic moral philosophers who will want to know whether my account leaves any normative work for moral agency or argument. I need a prep sheet for the Q&A: list the eight or so objections they will actually raise, in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each camp uses—honor-world dynamics, base-and-superstructure, normative justification, empirical historiography—with a note on which objections carry real force, what I should concede up front to avoid seeming evasive, and the concise reply that has to land for the thesis to survive. For each reply, flag its weakest point. Include the specific question I am hoping nobody asks—likely the one that forces me to admit whether my account reduces Wilberforce-style moral campaigning to an epiphenomenon of administrative centralization, and if so, whether I have simply replaced one reduction with another. Order everything by likely damage, and do not ask me for bibliographies or reading lists; I need arguments I can defend from memory in the room.
