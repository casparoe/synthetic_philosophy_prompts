A friend of mine, Jordan, and another friend, Maya, have been arguing about Leibniz's claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been trying to read along on my own, and I would value a clear-eyed assessment of where each of them actually stands.

Jordan opened by saying the claim is plainly false: children suffer, diseases spread, and if an all-powerful and all-good being could have created any consistent world, the one we inhabit is obviously not the best result. Maya replied that Jordan is missing Leibniz's actual reasoning. She explained that God does not select from pre-existing worlds like items on a shelf; rather, God computes every logically possible combination of things and chooses the one in which the greatest overall good is achieved relative to its metaphysical cost. Specifically, she said Leibniz holds that God jointly maximizes two criteria: the richness or variety of phenomena that occur, and the simplicity of the laws that generate them. The suffering we observe, she insisted, is not a failure of design but the unavoidable shadow cast by the very simplicity that makes consciousness, physical order, and moral agency possible in the first place.

Jordan said he could grant that framework for the sake of argument, but then he believes it shows the claim is either misleading or empty. If "best" really means "the highest ratio of phenomenal variety to law-complexity," then it is an engineering or aesthetic judgment, not a moral one. To make his point concrete, he proposed a comparison: imagine a world W* that is identical to ours in every respect except that one specific innocent child avoids one minute of severe pain, but achieving that requires adding an extra constant to the law of gravitation. By Maya's criterion, our actual world is still "better," because its laws remain simpler. Jordan argued that if trading a child's minute of suffering for a cleaner equation counts as "best," then the word has lost its ordinary moral meaning, and Leibniz is not defending our world so much as redescribing it with favorable vocabulary.

Maya answered that Jordan is treating suffering and law-complexity as separate, exchangeable entries, which is precisely what Leibniz denies. She said the suffering in our world is not an independent item that God could erase while leaving everything else intact; rather, the same simple laws that produce stars, minds, and moral freedom also produce the conditions under which suffering arises. One cannot keep all the goods and delete the suffering without breaking the law that sustains both. So God is not weighing a child's pain against an elegant equation; God is comparing entire world-structures, and ours is the structure in which the greatest variety of being is sustained by the simplest possible order, shadows included.

Jordan then charged that if suffering is genuinely inseparable from the goods produced by these laws, then saying "this is the best world" collapses into "this is the world that exists, and its parts are internally connected." It becomes tautological: any world with consistent laws could be described as the optimal version of itself. Maya pushed back by saying there are infinitely many other consistent worlds with different balances—some with richer phenomena but messier laws, some with simpler laws but far less variety. God actually compares these structures, she insisted, so the claim is substantive: ours sits at the unique optimal point on the curve where simplicity and variety are jointly maximized.

Jordan's final move was to ask whether that "curve" is anything more than a metaphor. He said that without a precise, independent measure of either "variety" or "simplicity," the claim that ours sits exactly at the peak is unfalsifiable. Any feature of our world can be retroactively labeled part of the optimal balance, and any absence can be explained away as something that would have required excessive complexity. Therefore, he concluded, "the best of all possible worlds" is not a real conclusion but a framing device that makes the actual world immune to criticism.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move—Jordan's opening objection, Maya's first reply about joint maximization, Jordan's W* comparison, Maya's inseparability response, Jordan's tautology charge, Maya's comparison-with-other-worlds reply, and Jordan's unfalsifiability objection. For each move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing—particularly whether there is a principled way to separate the moral and the metaphysical dimensions of "best" without simply relabeling Jordan's moral concern as Maya's metaphysical harmony.
