Question: In Martin Shubik’s dollar auction, two bidders compete for a prize worth £1, with bids rising in fixed increments. Both the winner and the loser must pay their final bid. Explain the precise mechanism—rooted in the payoff structure rather than in psychological bias—that can lock rational agents into an escalating commitment exceeding the prize value. Distinguish this structural feature from the rules of an ordinary ascending auction, and discuss whether the resulting escalation is best understood as a failure of backward induction or as rational behaviour under incomplete information about an opponent’s breaking point.

Student answer: “In the dollar auction, both players pay their last bid regardless of outcome. Once I have bid 80p and my opponent bids 90p, I compare quitting—which forfeits 80p—with bidding £1: if I win I break even, and only if I lose do I forfeit £1. Because forfeiting 80p is worse than breaking even, continuing is rational. This demonstrates that rational agents violate the sunk-cost fallacy, since they clearly factor past expenditure into their present choice. The structural feature that distinguishes this from a normal auction is not the payment rule itself, but a psychological mechanism of loss aversion: both parties suffer a loss, which creates emotional pressure to continue. In an ordinary auction only the winner pays, so there is no emotional investment. Therefore, escalation is ex ante rational for anyone who correctly anticipates the opponent’s withdrawal point, and it represents a rational response under incomplete information rather than a failure of backward induction.”
