You are preparing a speaker for the Q&A after a seminar on reparative justice in climate finance. The audience is international-law scholars and climate-policy negotiators: they reason in terms of state responsibility and distributive formulas, not individual psychology; they regard moral rhetoric with suspicion unless it yields treaty mechanisms; and they know Jaspers’s well-known distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, but consider metaphysical guilt too vague to guide institutional design.

Thesis being defended: Jaspers’s framework should be extended with a category of “institutional inheritance guilt”—not moral blame for ancestors’ actions, but a structural obligation that arises when present fiscal and institutional advantages remain causally continuous with colonial extraction. The claim is that wealthy states are not criminally guilty for past extraction, yet they commit a form of metaphysical bad faith if, in climate-adaptation negotiations, they treat current fiscal capacity as entirely self-generated and therefore resist obligations proportional to that continuity. The speaker applies this specifically to funding mechanisms, arguing that the obligation is a negative duty not to obstruct reparative transfers, not an emotional demand for collective self-punishment.

Argument sketch for objections to target: the speaker distinguishes criminal guilt (individual, punishable), political guilt (state responsibility for policies), and this new form, which attaches neither to present persons nor to current policies but to an institution’s refusal to acknowledge its causal inheritance. The speaker concedes that decolonization broke formal legal ties, but insists that economic and infrastructural continuity—not historical association—grounds the obligation.

Prepare the speaker as a prep sheet: list the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by likelihood and damage. For each, provide (a) the objection phrased in their vocabulary (e.g., “corporate moral agency,” “action-guidingness,” “causal discontinuity after decolonization,” “slippery slope to universal liability”), (b) whether it carries real force, (c) what should be conceded up front, (d) the concise reply that must land within seminar time, and (e) the weakest point of that reply. Identify the single reply that must succeed for the proposal to survive, and flag the objection the speaker most hopes nobody asks—together with a brief contingency plan if it arises. Keep the deliverable a rehearsal document, not a rewritten defense.
