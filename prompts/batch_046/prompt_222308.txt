I have been studying Aristotle's distinction between oikonomikē, the management of a household oriented toward use, and chrematistikē, the art of acquiring wealth for its own sake. He argues that money was invented solely as a medium of exchange and is therefore naturally barren: it should not be made to reproduce itself through interest-bearing loans, since treating currency as productive violates its proper purpose. Because modern financial systems clearly demonstrate that capital can generate returns through investment and compound growth, it seems to me that Aristotle's ethical condemnation of usury depends on an empirical error about whether money can in fact multiply. If that biological premise is false, then does his entire moral objection to lending at interest collapse? Or is there a deeper normative claim—perhaps that exchange-value ought never be pursued as an end independent of use-value—that remains intact regardless of whether capital actually grows? I am also uncertain whether this principle condemns all commerce aimed at profit, such as retail markup, or applies only to specific practices like usury, and I wonder whether contemporary philosophers who invoke Aristotle against speculative finance are relying on the same discredited metaphor rather than on this normative distinction.
