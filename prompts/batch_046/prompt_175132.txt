As a retired experimentalist now working through philosophy of belief, I am trying to understand the conceptual structure of the James–Clifford debate without reducing it to a simple clash of temperaments. James permits belief in forced, live, and momentous options even when evidence is insufficient; Clifford condemns any belief that outruns the evidence. I want to know whether this disagreement is best understood as a dispute about the loss function governing belief-formation—Clifford treating false belief as carrying infinite epistemic cost (like an uncontrolled Type I error), James allowing a calibrated risk of false belief when practical stakes are high and evidence is necessarily incomplete—or whether applying decision-theoretic reasoning here commits a category mistake, since belief aims at truth rather than at optimizing action under uncertainty. Is the core issue the context-sensitivity of "sufficient evidence," the legitimacy of practical reasons in epistemic norms, or the very applicability of risk-management frameworks to doxastic states? Please explain the conceptual terrain clearly.
