I've recently begun consulting for a regional planning authority that is redesigning its climate-accounting framework, and I've become preoccupied with an asymmetry that seems philosophically loaded rather than merely bureaucratic. The authority operates under a conventional carbon-budget model: it taxes or caps direct emissions, allows regulated entities to purchase offsets representing verified reductions elsewhere, and mandates efficiency improvements within existing infrastructure. Yet within this architecture there is no metric, no tax credit, and no offset category for what might be called deliberate throughput reduction or negative growth. If a municipality elects to forego a planned industrial zone and thereby prevents an entire future stream of emissions, that prevention is institutionally invisible; it generates no carbon credit, carries no market value, and is not recognized as a climate intervention at all. By contrast, if the zone is built and subsequently retrofitted with carbon-capture technology, the retrofit registers as a measurable, creditable reduction. The design treats the absence of development as a null event rather than as a causal contribution to the climate target, even though the physical outcome—fewer greenhouse gases in the atmosphere—is identical.

What philosophical commitments about causation, value, and progress does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the framework assume that only productive actions—building, retrofitting, substituting—can stand in causal relations to climate outcomes, while omissions remain causally inert? Or is the exclusion of degrowth measures compatible with a view on which omissions can indeed cause outcomes, but are assigned zero or negative value because growth itself is treated as an intrinsic good? Furthermore, examine whether the practice is consistent with the same legal system's tort doctrine, which in some contexts treats failure to act—when a duty is present—as causally and morally equivalent to action. Distinguish what the carbon-accounting framework strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, identify which rival theories of causation or welfare it quietly rules out, and specify what would have to change—both conceptually in the accounting rules and institutionally in planning incentives—if the underlying theory were replaced by one that treated deliberate non-expansion as a first-class climate strategy on par with technological abatement.
