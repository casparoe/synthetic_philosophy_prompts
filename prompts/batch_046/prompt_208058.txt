As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I've recently been working on a data-integration project that connects sleep-clinic databases with both clinical-trial platforms and disability-insurance verification systems. In doing so, I encountered a standardized practice that strikes me as conceptually puzzling. Sleep clinics administer a post-awakening "dream experience log" that asks patients to rate sensory vividness, emotional intensity, and narrative coherence, but deliberately excludes any question about whether the reported events actually occurred or whether the patient was genuinely perceiving anything during sleep. These ratings are then aggregated by an algorithm into a "dream experience index" used to measure intervention outcomes. By contrast, when these same logs are submitted in workers' compensation or disability claims, they are treated as records of a mental occurrence that can cause real daytime impairment, yet courts consistently reject any inference from them about the external world and refuse to treat them as analogous to eyewitness testimony. The clinical framework seems to treat dreaming as a measurable experiential state, while the legal framework treats it as a causally efficacious mental event lacking representational authority.

What implicit theory of experience, consciousness, or mental-state attribution makes this bifurcated practice coherent? Does it presuppose a view closer to Norman Malcolm's claim that dreaming is not an experience occurring during sleep but a retrospective judgment constructed upon waking, or closer to Daniel Dennett's narrative-construction account? Or does it quietly rely on a verificationist or access-based condition for genuine experience—one that allows phenomenal measurement in clinical contexts but denies world-directed representational status in legal ones? I want to understand which rival theories of dreaming and mental representation this institutional arrangement rules out, whether its clinical and legal halves actually presuppose consistent commitments, and what would have to change in both the scoring protocol and evidentiary rules if we replaced the underlying view with a straightforward representationalist account that treats dream content as misrepresentations of internal physiological states rather than as a special category of experience.
