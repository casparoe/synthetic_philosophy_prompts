I'm presenting a paper defending institutional tranquilism—specifically, a negative-state criterion for distributive justice—to a philosophy-of-medicine workshop next Thursday. The thesis is that just allocations of health resources ought to be guided exclusively by the reduction of craving and suffering, and that incorporating positive flourishing, capability achievement, or eudaimonic goods into welfare metrics is not merely redundant but normatively illegitimate: the state lacks authority to define mental goods beyond the absence of bad ones. I argue that QALY frameworks already smuggle in positive-state assumptions through disability-weight adjustments, and that a properly neutral metric should weight only the intensity and duration of craving and suffering. The audience comprises six applied ethicists (mostly capability-theorists in the Sen/Nussbaum tradition), two welfare economists who rely on QALY and DALY metrics, one negative-utilitarian who accepts suffering-reduction as necessary but insufficient, and a clinical psychologist specialising in positive-psychology interventions. They are not specialists in Buddhist philosophy, but they are sceptical of any welfare theory that lacks a positive dimension. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the six or seven objections I will actually receive, ordered by how damaging they are, each phrased in the vocabulary they will use (capabilities, neutrality, paternalism, metric design). For each objection, provide a concise reply suitable for a two-minute response, and explicitly note the weakest point in that reply. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises—likely that my view commits me to defunding resilience-building or social-connection programmes that do not directly eliminate suffering. Finally, please state how confident you are in this preparation overall, and single out the one element of my defence strategy about which you are least certain.
