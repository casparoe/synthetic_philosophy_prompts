I'm taking a course on well-being ethics and it's breaking my brain a little. I'm trying to figure out if suffering and pleasure are even the same kind of thing, or if there's a hard line between them, and I want to know what principle actually fits my gut reactions. Here are my verdicts: if I have to choose between one person in chronic, agonizing physical pain and ten billion people living lives that are barely pleasant — like, just above neutral — I pick the ten-billion world, but it feels really close, almost like suffering belongs in a different ontological bucket. But if it's one person with mild social anxiety versus ten billion people experiencing transcendent joy, I pick the joy world with no hesitation, so it's not like suffering automatically trumps everything. I also feel that adding a small pleasure to someone who is already suffering doesn't reduce the badness of that suffering — giving a torture victim a nice meal doesn't make the torture less bad. Yet I wouldn't say they're totally incommensurable, because I'd definately trade my own small headache for going to an amazing concert, which means I can weigh them in ordinary life. Then there's this implant idea I'm stuck on: a device that eliminates all suffering completely but also caps every positive feeling at exactly a flat 5/10, no peaks ever. I think I'd want that for everyone, which suggests I'm treating suffering as lexically prior, but that clashes with my second verdict where extreme pleasure clearly outweighs mild suffering.

Is there one principle — maybe lexical threshold negative utilitarianism with some commensurability condition — that saves most of these? Or do I have to drop one? And whatever the principle is, what does it predict for a case I keep flip-flopping on: a world with zero suffering but everyone locked at that flat 5/10 contentment, versus a world with occasional intense grief but also occasional transcendent joy?
