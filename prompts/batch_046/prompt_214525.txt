I am a journalist who writes on science and ideas, currently preparing a feature on the frame problem in artificial intelligence. Rather than treating it as a historical logic puzzle about listing all non-effects of an action, I am approaching it as a live problem of attentional economy: in open-ended embodied environments, the difficulty is not computing relevance symbolically but allocating scarce representational resources to determine what in the world-state has actually changed and requires updating.

Modern neural architectures, particularly large-scale reasoning systems, appear to sidestep the classical formulation not by employing explicit frame axioms, but by compressing world-state relevance into high-dimensional embeddings that implicitly encode which relations remain stable. From this vantage, the frame problem becomes a question about whether relevance is genuinely represented or merely simulated through statistical correlation.

What tension does this expose between explicit symbolic framing and implicit statistical compression of context? What design criteria should govern autonomous agents that must act in unstructured environments without exhaustive enumeration of state variables?
