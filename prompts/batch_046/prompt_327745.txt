Here is my opening move: contemporary work in artificial intelligence treats moral judgments not as truth-apt propositions but as patterns to be predicted, encoding them as reward functions within large-scale predictive models trained on human choice data. Researchers then ask whether, by your own criterion of ontological commitment—that existence is tied to the values of bound variables in our best-regimented theories—such systems are genuinely committed to moral entities, or whether they merely exhibit behavioral regularities that we misread as normative. I contend that if naturalism extends science without boundary, these functional regularities should earn their ontological place alongside the theoretical posits of physics: they contribute to predictive and explanatory success within a total theory of human behavior, and parsimony alone should not exclude them. Do you regard this as a legitimate extension of your naturalism into ethics, or does it collapse the distinction between describing what people happen to value and prescribing what ought to be valued?
