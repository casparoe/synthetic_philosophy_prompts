I'm an independent welfare auditor for an aquaculture certification body. I review farm-level proposals—changes to slaughter protocols, stocking densities, feed withdrawal schedules, or environmental structures—to decide whether they should be approved as welfare improvements for farmed decapod crustaceans and teleost fish. I need a reusable decision procedure I can complete in about thirty minutes per proposal using only the farm's technical documentation and my own training, with no lab access and no literature search. Ground the procedure in the conceptual distinction among three dimensions that should be treated as independently variable: nociception (reflexive harm-avoidance signaling), affective suffering (negatively valenced conscious experience), and ecological welfare (species-typical functioning and life-quality over time). The procedure must not assume that reducing nociceptive markers guarantees reduced suffering, or that improved survival and growth indicate improved welfare. Provide ordered steps with explicit pass/fail conditions, state what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, identify exactly where scientific uncertainty about crustacean and fish sentience forces a hand-off to value judgment rather than factual determination, and specify what I should record when the procedure yields an answer I personally dislike. Then apply it to three cases: (1) replacing ice-slurry killing with electrical stunning for shrimp, where documentation shows lower reflexive stress responses but a longer interval to insensibility; (2) reducing salmon stocking density, which improves swimming behavior but requires additional tanks and therefore extra feed-withdrawal cycles during transport; and (3) adding shelter structures for farmed lobsters, which reduces aggression-related injuries but may concentrate waste and elevate bacterial infection risk. For each case, say whether the procedure returns approve, approve-only-if, defer-to-board, or reject, and flag the point where judgment rather than evidence decides the outcome. Finally, list the two or three classes of proposals for which the procedure is not designed and should not be used.
