For an upper-level ethics seminar of approximately 14 students, meeting for 90 minutes, design a runnable classroom simulation—call it “The Partial Cloak”—that makes participants feel the force of Glaucon’s challenge in Plato’s Republic Book II and the ring of Gyges: specifically, that justice is valued only instrumentally, for reputation and the avoidance of punishment, and holds no intrinsic worth. To avoid a generic retelling, inject this structural twist: rather than granting full invisibility, the game gives each player exactly one Attribution Failure Token. If played during a contribution round, the token hides that player’s specific defection from the public reputation ledger, but the aggregate damage (a reduced pool) remains visible to all as an unexplained statistical anomaly. The payoff architecture should be arranged in four-person subgroups with a common-pool multiplier: cooperation yields shared points convertible into a small real privilege (for example, selecting the next week’s discussion text), while defection yields a private bonus. Crucially, include a trust-erosion rule: if more than two tokens are used in a subgroup, the multiplier collapses permanently for all remaining rounds, so that rational self-interest pushes each player toward using the token before others do, yet collective overuse destroys the very system that makes cooperation profitable. Assign specific roles—Ledger-Keeper (tracks visible contributions), Pool-Guardian (announces aggregate outcomes without naming sources), and Silent Observer (records emotional reactions without speaking)—with rotation rules so that every student occupies each role once. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline (four 10-minute rounds with shifting transparency conditions, plus setup and transition), the exact point-allocation scheme, and instructions for the Silent Observer’s non-verbal reporting method. The debrief must map each round onto Glaucon’s argument: Round 1 establishes the social contract under full transparency; Round 2 introduces the token as the Gyges condition; Rounds 3-4 reveal whether participants withheld defection out of intrinsic moral commitment or out of strategic fear of the erosion threshold. The debrief should also predict two predictable failures—some students will refuse to use the token out of genuine moral reluctance (which actually confirms part of Glaucon’s point about conditioning), and others will game the erosion rule strategically rather than experiencing it as a moral collapse—and must frankly admit which feature of the challenge the simulation cannot reproduce: the complete absence of internal conscience, since real participants still experience guilt even when the token protects them from social detection. State clearly what participants should leave seeing: that the distinction between justice-for-its-own-sake and justice-for-consequences becomes experientially unstable once attribution is weakened, and that the ring does not need to make one literally invisible—only probabilistically unaccountable—to trigger the same rational calculus Glaucon describes. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific secondary texts; the prompt should be answerable entirely from knowledge of Plato’s Republic Book II and basic game-design logic.
