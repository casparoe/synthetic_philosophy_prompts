Consider five competing frameworks for defining disease and health: (i) strict biostatistical naturalism, which identifies disease with statistically abnormal reductions in functional efficiency within a reference class; (ii) the harmful dysfunction analysis, which requires both a failure of an evolved internal mechanism and a socially evaluated harm; (iii) pure welfare-based normativism, which treats disease as any bodily or mental state that tends to impair well-being or the achievement of vital goals; (iv) strong social constructionism, which regards disease categories chiefly as instruments of institutional power and social control rather than as natural kinds; and (v) adaptive-lag normativism, a hybrid view holding that health consists in the alignment between an organism’s evolved phenotypic plasticity and its present socio-ecological niche, with disease arising when rapid environmental change produces a harmful regulatory mismatch that is assessed through culturally mediated life-projects.

Rank these five frameworks—assigning ranks 1 (best) through 5 (worst)—separately on two criteria: (A) their capacity to distinguish genuine psychiatric disorders from socially disvalued but biologically normal variations in cognition and affect, without over-medicalizing them; and (B) their success in resolving the demarcation problem, specifically in providing principled boundaries between disease, normal aging, and non-pathological physiological states such as pregnancy. For each framework, state its rank on (A) and on (B), provide a one-sentence justification for each rank, and then compute an unweighted composite score (the sum of the two ranks, with lower values indicating better overall performance). Finally, indicate whether the composite ranking reveals a clearly dominant framework or whether the top positions depend on how one weights psychiatric validity against demarcation rigor.
