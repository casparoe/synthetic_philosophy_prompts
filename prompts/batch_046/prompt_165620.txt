I am attempting to determine what I actually think about phenomenal consciousness by testing my intuitions against a range of concrete cases, likely in the neighborhood of higher-order theories but with some modifications I have not fully worked out. I want to know whether there is a single precise principle—perhaps an anchored, direct-access version of a higher-order account, or something adjacent to it—that delivers all of my verdicts, and if not, which verdict I should abandon and why.

Case 1: A fully awake adult feels a sharp pain in her foot and can immediately report that she is in pain. The first-order nociceptive state is present, and she possesses a direct, non-inferential representation of being in that state. Verdict: she is phenomenally conscious of the pain.

Case 2: A frog has intact nociceptors and withdraws its leg from a hot surface, but its brain lacks any architecture capable of representing its own mental states. Verdict: there is nociception, but no phenomenal consciousness of pain.

Case 3: A human patient has intact primary sensory and affective cortices, but due to extensive damage to prefrontal and parietal monitoring networks, she orients reflexively to loud sounds and bright lights, yet never reports any experience, consistently answers in the negative when asked whether she is aware of anything, and shows no behavioral signs of meta-cognitive access. Verdict: despite intact first-order processing, there is no conscious experience.

Case 4: Consider a hypothetical system—call it an empty loop—that possesses no first-order sensory, affective, or cognitive states whatsoever. It contains only a recursive representational module that continuously generates states of the form I am currently experiencing S, for various values of S, with no causal connection to any underlying state. The system can report these representations. Verdict: it is not conscious of anything; the presence of higher-order representations without anchored first-order states is insufficient.

Case 5: A mathematician is deeply absorbed in a proof. A mild headache runs in the background: the first-order nociceptive signal is present, but her attention is fully occupied, and she has no representation of being in pain. Only when the proof is solved and her attention shifts does she notice it. Verdict: during absorption, she is not conscious of the headache.

Case 6: Through an unusual neural miswiring, a subject’s direct higher-order representation represents I am experiencing joy, while the underlying first-order affective state is actually fear. There is no inference involved; the misrepresentation is direct. Verdict: I am inclined to say she is phenomenally conscious of joy—the phenomenal quality is joy—even though the first-order state is fear. This feels importantly different from Case 4, because here there seems to be a genuine phenomenal experience, just misaligned with its base.

Case 7: A person with alexithymic tendencies has a first-order physiological arousal state. She never has a direct representation I feel anxious; instead, she notices her trembling hands, checks a heart-rate monitor, and infers that she must be anxious. Verdict: I think she is conscious of the arousal, but in a thinner, derivative way—perhaps she is conscious, yet this case feels importantly different from Case 1, and I am unsure whether the consciousness is of the arousal itself or only of the inferred label.

My tentative principle is therefore something like this: a mental state M is phenomenally conscious if and only if (i) an appropriate first-order state is present, (ii) there is a direct (non-inferential) higher-order representation that takes that state, or something in its immediate vicinity, as its object, and (iii) the phenomenal character is primarily fixed by the first-order state, with the higher-order representation serving mainly to make it conscious rather than to constitute its quality.

This handles Cases 1 through 5 well, but Case 6 threatens clause (iii) and perhaps clause (i), while Case 7 threatens clause (ii). Is there a repaired version—perhaps allowing a degree of misalignment, or allowing inferential access under specific conditions—that saves all seven without becoming ad hoc? Or should I overturn one of my verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, note its predictions for cases I have not listed—specifically, a being with intact first-order states and accurate higher-order representations of them, but where every such representation is produced not by internal monitoring circuits but injected directly by an external device—and finally say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
