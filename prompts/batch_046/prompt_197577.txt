I chair my university's International Social-Science Ethics and Funding Review Board, and we must vote within thirty days on a multi-site proposal that has split the committee. The study compares moral-reasoning patterns across WEIRD and non-WEIRD populations—specifically agrarian communities in Southeast Asia and pastoralist groups in East Africa—using a modified harm/care framework that the principal investigator has augmented with a locally derived "relational-obligation" axis drawn from preliminary ethnography. The protocol frames its empirical aim neutrally, but its discussion section argues that if ecological risk predicts systematic divergence on this axis, then universalist moral objectivity is undermined and institutional ethics policy should abandon cross-culturally invariant standards. Half the board treats this as legitimate scientific framing; half sees it as embedding a contested metaphysical conclusion—moral anti-objectivism—into the study design, which risks both analytic bias and epistemic extraction, since participants are told only that the research concerns "community decision-making" rather than being informed that their responses will help adjudicate a philosophical dispute about objectivity.

Please draft the board's formal decision memo for circulation before our vote. You should adopt the voice of a senior research ethicist with expertise in cross-cultural psychology and philosophy of science, and you should begin with a brief internal brainstorming of the three tensions that matter most: whether measuring cultural variation can be cleanly separated from licensing metaphysical conclusions; whether partial disclosure is ethically permissible when the true analytic purpose is philosophical rather than purely descriptive; and whether cross-cultural divergence necessarily undermines moral objectivity or might instead reflect contextual expression of shared underlying capacities. After that brainstorming, deliver the memo itself: it must take a definite position—approve, approve with explicit conditions and a concrete amendment timeline, or reject—with clear reasoning that includes an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. If you approve or conditionally approve, explain precisely why embedding a philosophical conclusion in empirical design does not vitiate informed consent or scientific neutrality in this case; if you reject or impose conditions, explain precisely why the value of the cross-cultural data does not justify proceeding despite those risks. Be explicit about how committed you are to your recommendation—state where your reasoning is strongest and identify the specific point at which it is most vulnerable to rebuttal—rather than laying out options and leaving the board to choose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the memo should stand on its own reasoning.
