Consider the position that the standard tacit-consent account of political obligation fails for a growing class of subjects—call them territorially unbound digital workers—who never reside continuously in any single jurisdiction, derive no direct territorial benefits from a particular state, and access governance and dispute-resolution services exclusively through remote, subscription-based digital platforms that they can exit without physical relocation. Taking this failure as a premise, describe the most important implications for theories of civil disobedience in the Rawlsian and Razian traditions. Specifically: (1) Does the absence of a meaningful territorial exit option destroy the fairness-based reciprocity that is usually said to ground a duty of obedience, and if so, does it also dissolve the corresponding conditions that make civil disobedience a morally serious act rather than mere non-compliance? (2) How does the spatial and institutional detachment of these subjects alter the communicative aim of public disobedience when the relevant audience is not a shared body of co-citizens but distributed administrators and other platform users? (3) What does this scenario suggest about the boundary between justified disobedience and simple contract termination, particularly when the rules being resisted are terms of service enforced through algorithmic rather than coercive means? Write without bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; assume familiarity with the general structure of social-contract arguments and with Rawls's conditions for justified disobedience, but do not treat the digital-worker framing as requiring empirical data you cannot access.
