I am preparing a graduate seminar on cross-cultural aesthetics and metaphysics. Restate the contemporary analytic debate about whether immoral art can be aesthetically good — particularly the thesis that we should separate the work from the artist, together with its principal objections — using exclusively the conceptual vocabulary of Abhidharma Buddhist philosophy: the five aggregates (rupa, vedana, samjna, samskara, vijnana), the dynamics of clinging (upadana), dependent origination (pratityasamutpada), and the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle in analytic assumptions about individual authorship, intentional states, or object-bound aesthetic properties unless they have direct analogues in this framework. Then provide a residue report: which distinctions central to the original debate — for instance, between the moral character of the maker and the evaluative properties of the made object, or between aesthetic appreciation and moral endorsement — cannot be expressed in Abhidharma terms? For each point of failure, indicate whether the absence reflects a genuine limitation in the Buddhist framework's resources for aesthetic judgment, or whether it reveals that the analytic debate depends on metaphysical commitments about agency and creation that are less universal than they appear.
