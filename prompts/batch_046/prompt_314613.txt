In my graduate seminar on just war theory, the debate over the moral equality of combatants has reached an impasse that feels conceptually stuck rather than empirically open. The traditionalists hold that uniformed soldiers on both sides possess an equal permission to kill, not because the causes are symmetrical, but because combatant status functions as a role-based moral filter that insulates individual agents from the ad bellum evaluation of the war they fight. The revisionists reject this insulation: they argue that an unjust combatant is analogous to an unjust aggressor, lacking the defensive justification that renders killing permissible, while the just combatant retains it. Both camps accept the same normative premises—killing requires justification, ignorance of war aims can excuse but does not license, and collective institutions generate special obligations—and both treat the same cases, such as the conscript in an aggressive war of conquest or the soldier who suspects but does not know that his cause is unjust, as data. Yet the arguments have circulated for years without convergence: the traditionalists charge that revisionism collapses jus in bello into jus ad bellum and ignores the epistemic opacity of the battlefield; the revisionists charge that traditionalism confers a magical moral exemption upon wearing a uniform.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily about self-defense, liability, or epistemic limits, but about a hidden metaphysical premise concerning the granularity of moral agency within collective actions—specifically, whether collective authorization operates as a distributive property that can be decomposed into individual permissions, or as a non-decomposable role-status that creates a deontic boundary. If that is correct, then the sides may not be answering a single shared question at all, but rather pursuing two distinct inquiries—one about the ethics of institutional roles, one about individual liability—under the single heading of moral equality.
