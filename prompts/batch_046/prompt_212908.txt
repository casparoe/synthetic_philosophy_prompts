You are an expert philosopher of science with specialized training in metaphysics, the philosophy of causation, and collective action. Before addressing the dispute, please take a moment to think carefully: begin with a brief internal outline of the core commitments of counterfactual-dependence theories (in the Lewisian tradition) and interventionist theories (associated with structural causal models), then proceed to a point-by-point adjudication.

At a workshop on collective decision-making, I heard two philosophers—Mira and Jonas—debate how causal theories apply to an institutional vote. The case was this: a five-person board must approve a merger. Four members already favor it. The chair, also in favor, casts the formal deciding vote according to procedural rules, and the motion passes. Mira and Jonas disagreed about whether the chair's vote was genuinely causal, and about which theoretical framework gets the answer right.

Mira opened with a counterfactual-dependence defense. She argued that if the chair had voted against the motion—while holding fixed the other members' votes and the institutional rules—the tie-breaking procedure would have yielded a different collective outcome. For Mira, this counterfactual dependency is sufficient for causation, and it correctly identifies the chair's vote as a cause of the decision.

Jonas replied that this is precisely the overgeneration problem interventionist accounts are meant to solve. On his view, one must specify a causal graph and ask whether an intervention on the chair's vote, holding fixed other direct causes according to the graph, changes the outcome. In this structure, the four members' preferences are direct parents of their individual votes, but the chair's vote is only one input to a tallying mechanism. If we intervene solely on the chair's vote—flipping it—while keeping the other four votes constant, the tally changes, but that only shows the chair's vote caused the tally, not that it caused the collective decision. The collective decision, Jonas insisted, is caused by the members' preferences together with the institutional rules, not by any single ballot.

Mira answered that Jonas applies the interventionist criterion at the wrong level. She granted that the members' preferences cause their individual votes, but she insisted the chair's vote remains a difference-maker for the collective outcome. She also raised a modeling objection: in real committees, members often defer to the chair's authority or rhetoric. So the correct structural graph should include edges from the chair's disposition to the other members' votes. Once those edges are included, an intervention on the chair's preference—not merely the vote—does alter the other members' behavior, allowing the interventionist to recover the chair's causal importance after all.

Jonas conceded that deference edges might belong in the graph, but he argued this exposes a circularity in Mira's position. To know which edges to include, one needs prior causal knowledge—knowledge that often comes from the counterfactual judgments the interventionist is supposed to replace. If we insert the deference edge only because we think the chair's authority matters counterfactually, then we have used counterfactual reasoning to build the model, then claimed the model eliminates counterfactual reasoning.

Mira responded that this charge conflates model construction with model testing. Background knowledge, statistical patterns, and institutional information build the graph; the interventionist criterion then applies mechanically. But she offered a concession: in collective cases, the variables are genuinely ambiguous. There may be no clear fact of the matter about whether a single "committee preference" exists independently of its members. Thus both theories face an ontological challenge about emergent collective causation.

Jonas then pressed a different point. He said counterfactualism's deeper flaw in collective settings is indiscriminateness. Because counterfactual dependence can be satisfied by countless situational features, the theory needs extra machinery—stability conditions, proportionality, or normal-world restrictions—to filter spurious causes. For instance, if the chair had worn a different tie and consequently felt less authoritative, other members might have dissented. That counterfactual dependency exists, yet we do not want to say the tie caused the decision. Counterfactualism therefore needs ad hoc filters, whereas interventionism makes spurious causation visible as a modeling error.

Mira replied that the tie example is unrepresentative. Serious counterfactualists already accept that not every counterfactual dependency is causal; they require that the dependency hold across relevant background conditions or that the cause be proportional to the effect. In the committee case, she argued, the chair's vote is proportional to the outcome in a way the tie is not: the vote is part of the institutional mechanism that produces the decision, while the tie is only distantly connected. So counterfactualism can distinguish them using proportionality, without abandoning difference-making.

Jonas closed by observing that proportionality is doing the work a structural model should do. Once you require that the cause be part of the mechanism, you have moved toward an interventionist or mechanistic view. The real debate, he suggested, is not whether difference-making across worlds can stand alone, but whether it must always be anchored in an explicit causal structure.

Please adjudicate this dispute point by point. Specifically: who is right about the application of counterfactual dependence to the chair's vote? Is Jonas's charge of circularity in graph construction fair, or does Mira's distinction between construction and testing hold? Does collective decision-making expose a unique weakness in one theory, or in both? Does proportionality genuinely save counterfactualism from spurious causes, or does it secretly concede the interventionist point? Finally, state who is more right overall, and identify what important consideration—perhaps regarding the ontology of collective agency, the normativity of institutional roles, or the distinction between causal and constitutive relevance—both parties are missing.
