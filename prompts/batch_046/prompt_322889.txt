In a graduate seminar on modal metaphysics, my peers and I have been working through the disagreement between Lewisian counterpart theory and the transworld-identity framework associated with philosophers like Kripke and Plantinga. I want to make sure I understand the conceptual architecture of the dispute, not merely the standard slogan that counterpart theorists treat other-worldly objects as resemblance-based surrogates while transworld-identity theorists keep the very same object across worlds. Here is the specific knot I am trying to untangle. Take a concrete artifact—the Eiffel Tower—and consider the counterfactual claim that it might have been constructed from steel rather than iron. For the counterpart theorist, there is no Eiffel Tower in the nearby possible world where the material differs; there is only a counterpart that resembles the actual tower in relevant respects but is composed of steel. This immediately raises a difficulty about what fixes those “relevant respects”: if resemblance is doing all the work, then it seems we must already know which properties are essential to being the Eiffel Tower in order to identify the correct counterpart, which threatens to make the theory circular or parasitic on a prior essentialist distinction that was supposed to be explained rather than assumed.

By contrast, the transworld-identity theorist can say that the very same tower exists in that world with a different material property, but then she owes an account of how an artifact can survive such a change without violating its persistence conditions—conditions that, for artifacts, are usually taken to depend on functional design or historical origin rather than on material constitution. So my question is twofold. First, is the disagreement here genuinely metaphysical, or is part of it a semantic dispute about how we evaluate counterfactual identity statements and whether phrases like “the Eiffel Tower made of steel” refer to a single cross-world object or to a family of world-bound replicas? Second, is there a conceptually stable hybrid position—say, one that assigns transworld identity to artifacts but counterpart relations to biological organisms—or does any such hybrid collapse once we press on whether persistence conditions for artifacts and organisms differ in kind rather than merely in degree? Please explain the conceptual issue, particularly how each side would diagnose the circularity worry on the one hand and the persistence-conditions worry on the other, and whether there is a principled reason to prefer one framework that does not already presuppose the answer. I am especially interested in whether counterpart theory can accommodate our intuitive counterfactual identity claims without smuggling transworld identity back in through the resemblance relation.
