I am trying to work out my actual commitments about fatalist reasoning by testing my intuitions against concrete scenarios, and I want to see whether they converge on a principle or fall apart.

Here are my verdicts.

In the classic Damascus scenario, an infallible seer tells you that you will die in Damascus tomorrow, so you flee toward Aleppo, but your horse throws a shoe, you are delayed, and you die in Damascus after all. My verdict: attempting to flee was not futile. The seer's knowledge does not cancel your capacity to choose; ending up in Damascus despite your flight does not prove you could not have avoided it.

In the medical conditional, a physician tells you that if you maintain your current habits, you will suffer a fatal cardiac event in Damascus, where you currently reside. You relocate and change your behavior, and you avoid the event. My verdict: this is not fatalism at all, but an ordinary causal prediction that you successfully disrupted.

In the logical deduction case, a philosopher demonstrates that because the proposition that you will be in Damascus tomorrow is now true, and the past is fixed, it is now necessary that you be there, hence you cannot avoid being there. My verdict: the argument sounds valid, yet I still regard trying to avoid Damascus as rational. This makes me think there is a hidden ambiguity in either the modal claim or the claim about ability, but I cannot locate it.

In the self-fulfilling prophecy case, a credible authority declares that anyone who enters Damascus will be subjected to a conditioning protocol that makes betrayal psychologically inevitable. You remain outside Damascus and preserve your loyalty. My verdict: avoiding Damascus is clearly rational, and there is no paradox.

In the deterministic simulation case, a perfect physics-based simulator predicts, with no error in its track record, that you will board flight 347 to Damascus next Tuesday. You form a firm intention to take flight 348 instead, but a booking error places you on 347. My verdict: I keep oscillating. On one reading, you genuinely could have taken 348, and the simulator merely tracked a correlation; on another reading, the perfect prediction reveals that your choice was never genuinely open. I cannot settle whether this case is like the classic Damascus scenario or like the medical conditional.

In the probabilistic warning case, a well-calibrated risk model assigns a 99 percent probability that you will be in Damascus next year given your career trajectory. You switch fields and stay away. My verdict: acting on the model is rational, with no fatalist tension whatsoever.

Is there a coherent principle that accommodates verdicts one through four and six, and that also resolves case five without contradiction? If so, state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by each case, and say what it predicts for a variant I have not listed: a case in which the predictor is fully accurate but its predictions are generated from a model that includes your own decision-process as an explicit variable, so that altering your decision updates the prediction. If no single principle saves all six verdicts, tell me which one must be abandoned and why.
