I am preparing a diagnostic handout for a graduate seminar in political philosophy on the conceptual architecture behind the phrase the will of the people. Rather than asking students to memorize standard classifications, I want them to navigate a decision tree that sorts positions by answers to four concrete diagnostic cases—cases they can evaluate without knowing any specific author, text, or theoretical label.

The tree should branch as follows. First case: a referendum passes with narrow majority support, but the winning coalition is held together solely by shared opposition to an elite and contains groups with contradictory positive interests. Does this express a unified will, or does negative constitution undermine the very claim to a popular will? Second case: a leader enacts a policy never submitted to vote and explicitly opposed by polling majorities, justifying it by appeal to structural resonance—the idea that the movement aligns with underlying social patterns despite contradictory explicit preferences. Is this legitimate populism or disguised paternalism? Third case: in an election with high abstention, do the non-voters represent tacit consent, hidden dissent that fractures the people, or a neutral absence that should be excluded from the calculus? Fourth case: a government learns that the policy its base demands will harm those supporters' long-term welfare. Does the will retain unconditional authority, or is populist legitimacy conditional on such alignment?

Please construct a taxonomy with at least eight leaves corresponding to combinations of answers to these four cases. Each leaf should carry a concise position name—use standard names where they genuinely apply, but invent precise ones where needed, such as aggregative proceduralism, negative identitarianism, or resonant vanguardism—and one sentence on its most awkward commitment, the consequence defenders rarely highlight. For unoccupied combinations, label them unnamed or unoccupied and diagnose whether the gap stems from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect; specifically, flag one combination as unstable, such as the view that combines negative constitution with unconditional authority and interprets silence as dissent, which appears to collapse into contradiction.

Also identify at least one pair of positions that this tree cannot cleanly separate—perhaps between a view that treats the will as a deliberative ideal to be discovered and one that treats popular homogeneity as an empirical precondition for democratic legitimacy—and specify what fifth diagnostic case would be required to split them. The instrument is for students who understand the conceptual terrain but have not read the specialized literature; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific works. Keep the framing formal and conceptual rather than empirical or historical.
