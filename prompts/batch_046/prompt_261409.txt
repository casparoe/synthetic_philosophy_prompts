Consider the following reconstructed argument drawn from Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz’s correspondence with Samuel Clarke (1715–1716). Leibniz maintains that if two bodies were perfectly identical in every intrinsic quality and in every relation they bear to other things, then there could be no sufficient reason for God—or for any rational principle of order—to place one in spatial region A and the other in spatial region B, since empty space contains no internal distinctions that could ground such a difference. From this he concludes that the scenario is impossible: where there is perfect qualitative and relational sameness, there is only one thing.

Now consider a modified version of Max Black’s well-known two-spheres case. Instead of discrete bodies suspended in an otherwise empty, symmetric universe, imagine a single continuous, homogeneous field that fills all space except for two localized topological defects—each being an identical knot-structure embedded in the field, with the region between and around them being perfectly uniform in all local properties. There are no distant stars, no boundaries, and no external reference frames. The only putative difference between the two knots is a bare relational fact: one is “to the left” of the other relative to an arbitrarily chosen coordinate axis. Yet because the field is homogeneous and unbounded, that axis seems to have no physical significance, and the relational fact appears to presuppose the very numerical distinction it is meant to explain.

Please help me understand Leibniz’s argument in relation to this modified case. Specifically: does his appeal to the principle of sufficient reason apply with equal force to these topological defects, or does the global continuity and connectivity of the underlying field provide a hidden basis for distinction—perhaps through differences in how each knot is linked to the overall topology of space—that avoids any appeal to absolute position? Furthermore, if we grant that the bare relational “left-of” fact is not a genuine property but a pseudo-distinction arising only from our descriptive framework, does this vindicate Leibniz’s conclusion that the two knots are numerically identical, or does it instead show that his argument relies on a tacit assumption about the discreteness of bodies that fails for continuous fields?
