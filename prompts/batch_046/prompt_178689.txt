In debates over antinatalism, Benatar's asymmetry is frequently introduced through the party-invitation analogy: extending an invitation benefits the guest if they enjoy it, but withholding one harms no one if there is no disappointed party, a comparison used to license the claim that non-procreation avoids suffering without committing a corresponding wrong of deprivation. More recently, some writers have extended this to a satellite-office analogy: founding a branch in an unstable region makes the founder liable for harms that occur there, while potential employees who are never hired have no standing to complain, which is meant to reinforce that procreation imposes risks without a countervailing obligation to create beneficiaries. Audit both analogies together: provide an explicit correspondence table for each mapping the potential child, the parent, the harm, the absence of deprivation, and liability; identify where the business analogy introduces features—corporate jurisdiction, contractual consent, territorial risk assessment—that the social analogy suppresses; determine whether either can carry the asymmetry without importing a contested metaphysics of potential persons; and say whether Benatar's conclusion survives once these disanalogies, especially around agency and obligation, are fully exposed.
