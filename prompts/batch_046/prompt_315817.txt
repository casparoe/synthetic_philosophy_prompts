I'm in a seminar on strange loops and self-reference and we keep coming back to this idea of an "inviolate level" — the level that describes the system but isn't inside it, like the rules of chess aren't chess pieces. My professor brought up Escher's Drawing Hands as the opposite case: no hand is the base, they're tangled. Anyway, I was talking with my friends about The Neverending Story — mostly the film version, though the book works too — and we noticed Bastian is reading a book literally called The Neverending Story, and eventually the Childlike Empress calls him by name, so he's inside the text he's holding. That feels like a strange loop: the reader becomes a character, the story contains its own reception. But here's what I'm stuck on. Is there actually an inviolate level in the film? At the begining Bastian is clearly outside, in an attic with a physical book. By the end he's named in the narrative and saves Fantastica. So did the hierarchy collapse, or was Bastian always inside a larger loop? I'd like a reading that takes both the story and Hofstadter's idea seriously — not just "meta-fiction is cool" — but asks whether the film dramatizes the exact moment when an observer crosses into the observed system without destroying the distinction. And where it resists: the book has physical pages Bastian touches, which might be a kind of ground level that Escher's drawing doesn't have. Does that matter, or is it just a prop? Also, I wonder if there's a difference between being named in the text and actually altering it — Bastian gives the Empress a name, which is an act from "outside" that changes the inside. Is that a violation of the inviolate level or proof that the levels were never seperate to begin with? Don't just summarize the plot; I want to know if the movie knows the difference between epistemic uncertainty about Bastian's location and an actual ontological collapse of levels.
