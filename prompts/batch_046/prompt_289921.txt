I'm commissioning a deadpan satirical feature for a science-and-ideas column I edit, and I need you to write it as both a professional philosopher of science—well-versed in spacetime substantivalism, relationism, and Newton's bucket argument—and as an essayist capable of sustained irony who never breaks character.

Before drafting, please do some structured brainstorming: list the concrete ontological differences actually at stake—whether space exists independently of material bodies, whether rotational motion is absolute or merely relative to other bodies, whether genuinely empty regions are metaphysically admissible, and how reference frames anchored to distant matter bear on the dispute—then map each point onto an absurd but precisely worded contractual clause.

Then write a parody residential lease agreement of approximately 600 words, rendered in the relentlessly bureaucratic voice of a fictional property-management firm called Continuum Estates. The document offers two selectable versions—Substantivalist and Relationist—that a prospective tenant must initial before occupying an apartment. Encode the philosophical positions into real-estate fine print: security-deposit rules treating absolute rotation as a tenant-caused liability, occupancy restrictions on vacuum regions, arbitration provisions invoking distant stellar bodies, and maintenance obligations for geometric structure. Include a brief Frequently Asked Questions sidebar in the same deadpan legal tone, addressing tenant concerns that mirror standard objections to each view—who authors the ontology, whether passing the inspection demonstrates competence at navigation, and whether a tenant can subsist without neighboring bodies.

The satire should land the actual conceptual commitments of the debate, especially as illuminated by Newton's bucket, without ever stating them as abstract philosophy. A specialist should recognize that each clause genuinely tracks one side of the argument, while a general reader should find the document legible as absurd bureaucratic fiction. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for further reading. Do not quote any historical text verbatim; if you reference Newton's bucket, paraphrase the thought experiment in contractual language and avoid quotation marks. The target is the debate itself, not any named living person—invented firm representatives are fine. Write for a reader without web access, relying only on widely known arguments in philosophy of physics.
