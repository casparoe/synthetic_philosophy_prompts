As a retired natural scientist who has recently begun studying philosophy, I have become preoccupied with a tension I perceive between Nozick's tracking conditions for knowledge and the principle of epistemic closure. By closure, if one knows p and knows that p entails q, one should know q; yet tracking demands that in the nearest possible worlds where q is false, one does not believe q. In cases where p's falsity is not the nearest route to q's falsity, the conditions appear to diverge. Could you explain this conceptual issue with precision? To ground the discussion, imagine a researcher who tracks—via a calibrated thermometer—that a sample is exactly at water's triple point (273.16 K), and therefore knows the sample is not at absolute zero; but suppose the nearest worlds in which the sample is at absolute zero are physically remote, while worlds in which the thermometer malfunctions are nearby. Does she genuinely fail to track the entailment? Please respond in four sections of roughly equal length: first, "The tracking conditions stated precisely"; second, "The closure principle and its intuitive force"; third, "The thermometer case and the divergence of conditions"; fourth, "Whether the tension undermines closure or tracking, and why".
