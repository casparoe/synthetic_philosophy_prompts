I'm sitting on the clinical ethics committee at St. Edmund's Hospital Trust, and we've got to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting. The board is being asked to adopt something called the Real-Time Allocation Justification Protocol — in A&E, clinicians would have to complete a standardised form for every triage call, documenting the moral calculus behind the decision, and there's a specific box where "personal or familial connection to the patient" has to be listed as a potential bias factor that needs explicit correction. The trust thinks this will reduce favouritism, but I'm worried it institutionalises exactly the kind of moral alienation that Williams talks about.

There's this idea — you probably know it — about having one thought too many: if you're saving your own kid from a crash, the reason should just be they're mine, not they're mine, and this also maximises welfare, therefore it's permissible. The protocol seems to demand that second layer in writing, even in split-second emergencies. Plus there's the integrity objection: asking doctors to treat their deepest commitments as variables to be neutralised might actually undermine the kind of agent we want in medicine. But my collegue argues that bias is bias, and without transparency we can't audit life-and-death decisions. We need a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a clear time frame, and the memo should take the strongest pro-protocol argument seriously rather than just waving it away. Oh, and please write it in British English; I was discussing this with my friends over coffee and they said it sounds like we're turning doctors into utilitarian robots, which might be unfair, but I think there's a genuinely philosophical point here about whether institutional rules can require people to stand outside their own ground projects. Could you draft the opinion? Make it formal enough to vote on, but keep the reasoning honest, and include what the conditions should actually look like if we go that route.
