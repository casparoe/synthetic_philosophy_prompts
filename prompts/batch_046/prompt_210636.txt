Read Orson Scott Card's Ender's Game through the lens of Douglas Hofstadter's Ant Fugue and the specific question of whether Aunt Hillary—the ant colony whose collective behavior prompts the debate—can be said to possess a mind in any non-metaphorical sense. The Formics in Card's novel are organized around a hive queen: a singular reproductive and cognitive node whose death collapses the swarm's coherence, and whose egg Ender eventually carries into exile. Does this structure vindicate or undermine the ant-colony analogy? I want criticism that moves in both directions. Does that difference dissolve the philosophical question, or does it merely relocate the problem of emergence? Where does the novel resist a purely functionalist reading—for instance, in the apparent grief or absence that accompanies the queen's death—and where does it reduce collective consciousness to biology? I want a reading faithful to both the philosophical problem and the literary object: what the framework of distributed mind illuminates about Card's world that a standard moral reading misses, and what the novel's specific design of hive consciousness adds to the philosophical debate about ants that abstract argument alone cannot supply.
