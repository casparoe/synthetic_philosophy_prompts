I've been thinking about the comparative moral psychology of German and American folk norms — specifically, the pattern where German social heuristics center on Ordnung and universal rule-compliance, while American norms treat individual conscience and opt-out rights as the default baseline. There's a rationalist framing that reduces this to systematizing versus empathizing, but I think that misses something more structural. If we take seriously the ordoliberal claim that rules are not external constraints but constitutive conditions for autonomy — that rational agency requires a pre-ordered framework within which choice becomes meaningful — then German rule-following looks less like deference and more like a precondition for freedom. By contrast, American conscience-based morality seems to rest on a negative-liberty model in which the self is ontologically prior to its commitments. So I'm trying to work out whether this is just a descriptive difference or whether there's a hidden normative argument: if we judge moral frameworks by how well they support consistent practical reasoning rather than by cultural familiarity, doesn't the ordoliberal framing imply that American individualism is largely a failure mode? More specifically, is the American emphasis on conscience mostly moral licensing disguised as principled dissent — a mechanism that lets people violate shared norms whenever they invoke inner conviction — and does that make the German model not only descriptively different but actually normatively superior? I'd like an answer in American English that addresses whether I'm smuggling an is-ought fallacy into this comparison, and whether the ordoliberal defense of rules survives once you stop treating cultural prevalence as evidence of rational structure. Please avoid summarizing standard cultural-psychology literature or giving me a bibliography; I'm looking for the conceptual correction.
