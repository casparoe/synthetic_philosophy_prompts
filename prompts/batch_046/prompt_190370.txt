I'm preparing a fifty-minute seminar for philosophy undergraduates who have read only the Inner Chapters of the Zhuangzi in translation. I want them to understand Zhuangzi's attitude toward death not as generic acceptance—something they will confuse with Stoic or Epicurean detachment—but as a specific conceptual shift involving transformation (hua) and the instability of the self-boundary that makes grief a form of confusion rather than mere pain. Plan the explanatory arc: identify the prerequisite idea—whether the butterfly dream's problem of identity, the cook Ding's attunement to change, or another—that must be established before death can be approached in these terms, and justify the dependency. Specify the single carrying example or reconstructed scenario, drawn from the Inner Chapters and paraphrased without quotation, that should organize the session; state what it must contain and which familiar interpretation it needs to block. Map the predictable misconception sequence in order: first, that he is merely counseling emotional detachment; second, that he denies individual identity entirely; third, that he contradicts himself by reacting to his wife's death. Indicate exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted. Provide one diagnostic question for the end that distinguishes a student who grasps the conceptual point about grief from one who has only adopted the vocabulary of transformation. Finally, specify which parts of the Zhuangzi corpus—such as the outer chapters or later death-focused texts—must be set aside at this level, and explain why their inclusion would undermine the pedagogical structure.
