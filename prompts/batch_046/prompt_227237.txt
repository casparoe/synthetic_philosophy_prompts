Design an in-class game for an undergraduate philosophy seminar on rational choice—about 18 students, one 50-minute session—that makes participants feel the pull of the independence axiom as a constraint on sequential resolution, then feel it collapse when they compare complete prospects ex ante. Do not use the standard Allais 2×2 survey format. Instead, split the class into two cohorts that never see each other’s payoff sheets: one cohort resolves a compound lottery in two real stages (they learn an intermediate outcome before choosing again), while the other cohort commits to both stages at once from an identical tree. Use a token economy—convertible at session’s end to a shared snack or small privilege—with payoffs structured so that the sequential cohort’s stage-two choices, taken together, violate the preferences expressed by the simultaneous cohort, staging the common-ratio effect as a live inter-group coordination failure rather than an individual inconsistency. Specify the exact tree structure, the token values, the information partitions between cohorts, and how you prevent cross-contamination. Include a debrief that maps each stage of play onto the formal distinction between ex-post branch evaluation and ex-ante compound-lottery comparison, explains why the independence axiom feels rational in the sequential condition but not in the simultaneous one, predicts what will go wrong the first time (e.g., cohort confusion, attempts to renegotiate rules mid-play, or misattribution of the conflict to risk aversion rather than to resolution structure), and is frank about which feature of the Allais paradox—specifically the certainty effect in the face of identical probability ratios—this activity can reproduce and which feature it cannot reproduce without direct facilitator framing. The conceptual tension should emerge from the rules and the payoff asymmetry between cohorts, not from anything you say after the game ends.
