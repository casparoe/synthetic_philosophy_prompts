Write an essay on whether Quine's naturalism and thesis of ontological relativity commit him to treating deep moral disagreement as structurally analogous to the underdetermination of scientific theory by evidence. Some interpreters hold that if behavioral dispositions underdetermine the assignment of moral meanings—much as they underdetermine the translation of theoretical terms—then cross-cultural moral criticism becomes as indeterminate as radical translation, leaving no rational basis for condemning practices that cohere well within their local webs of belief. Others contend that Quine's pragmatism allows moral norms to be revised in response to broader empirical and practical pressures without requiring moral realism, so that moral progress is assessable in terms of global coherence and predictive success rather than correspondence to special moral facts.

Weigh these positions carefully. In particular, consider the objection that if moral commitments are merely nodes in a revisable web of belief, then core human-rights norms could in principle be abandoned whenever they generate sufficient pragmatic friction with empirical science or social organization—an implication many find unacceptable. Reach and justify a definite conclusion: does Quine's framework ultimately collapse moral disagreement into incommensurability and undermine the rational force of cross-cultural moral criticism, or can it accommodate a robust, naturalistic account of moral progress? Defend your verdict with explicit reasoning about how far the analogy between theory choice and norm choice can be pushed.
