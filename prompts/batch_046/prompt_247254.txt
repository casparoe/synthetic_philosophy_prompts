As a software engineer without formal philosophy training, I have been trying to formalize my commitments about epistemic justification—partly because debugging complex systems forces me to think about how hypotheses are supported. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Justification is hierarchical: there are basic beliefs—roughly direct perceptual reports and elementary logical truths—that do not derive warrant from other beliefs, and every other justified belief ultimately traces back to these root nodes.

(2) No belief is justified in isolation, however. Even a basic belief must cohere with my broader system of beliefs and methodological constraints; otherwise I treat it as noise or a sensor error rather than knowledge.

(3) I reject the idea that justification ever reaches a legitimate terminus. It is always rational to ask why? one more time, and there is no privileged class of beliefs that are epistemically immune from further scrutiny.

(4) In practice, when a specific observational report conflicts with a well-entrenched theoretical framework, my default is to preserve the framework and reinterpret the report—unless the report comes from an unmediated, direct perceptual source, in which case the framework should yield.

Do these hang together? I want an audit, not a lecture: which pairs are in genuine tension and how severely, which only look like conflicts but dissolve on closer inspection, and what the least costly revision would be if I must weaken or drop one commitment. Please rank the tensions by severity, and be specific about whether my practical rule in (4) can survive my rejection of epistemic termini in (3).
