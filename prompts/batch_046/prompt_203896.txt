I'm writing a paper for my metaphysics seminar and I'm stuck on why the endurantism vs perdurantism debate never actually resolves. Like, since the 80s everyone runs through the same moves: endurantists say they don't need temporal parts to explain change — they claim an object is wholly present at every time it exists, and that temporary properties can be had at times without contradiction if you index them right. Perdurantists reply that this indexing trick is just temporal parts in disguize, and that without parts spread through time you can't genuinely have incompatible properties like being bent then straight. Then someone brings up the rotating disc argument or the ship of Theseus, and then everyone just digs in. But lately I've been wondering if the stall isn't about persistence at all but about whether 'object' is even a kind that can persist — there's this niche framing I came across that treats macroscopic entities not as substrates but as stable patterns of activity, and if that's right then asking whether the pattern is 'wholly present' or 'spread out' is like asking whether a melody is in the piano or in the air. Anyway, is the debate stalled because it's verbal — are 'endurance' and 'perdurance' just different descriptions of the same underlying dynamics — or is it substantive but hidden behind disagreements about what counts as a counterexmaple? Or are endurantists and perdurantists literally answering different questions, one about how we track things through time and one about ontology? I want a diagnosis using what each side actually commits to, not just a list of arguments. If it turns out they're talking past each other, what would progress even look like — a formal model of persistence, an empirical finding about cognition, or just dropping the substrate framing?
