I'm preparing a feature on eighteenth-century metaphysics for a science-and-ideas audience, and I want to be precise about Berkeley's master argument before I summarize it for readers. In the Three Dialogues, Philonous asks Hylas whether he can conceive of a tree in a garden existing with no one present to perceive it; Hylas admits he cannot, and Philonous takes this to show that the idea of unperceived matter is contradictory. The standard reading is that Berkeley is exposing a definitional tension: matter is defined as that which exists independently of mind, yet any attempt to conceive it makes it an object of mind. But I'm concerned that this reading conflicts with a distinction Berkeley draws elsewhere—between passive 'ideas,' which include all sensible qualities, and active 'notions,' through which we grasp spirits, relations, and God without reducing them to sensory images. If the master argument relies on the principle that conceiving an object necessarily involves perceiving it as an idea, then doesn't the argument presuppose that all mental content is idea-like? And if so, hasn't Berkeley restricted the argument's scope to sensory objects, leaving open the possibility that material substance could be reconceived—not as an idea, but as a kind of active principle grasped by notion—thereby escaping the contradiction? Or does Berkeley have a hidden premise that extends the 'no unperceived conception' principle even to notions, in which case wouldn't that undermine his own account of how we understand other minds?
