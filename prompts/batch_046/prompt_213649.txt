I'm preparing notes for a philosophy of science seminar that has recently become rather personal: my supervisor has asked whether my sympathy for evolutionary explanations of cognition commits me to denying that we genuinely understand anything, and I am struggling to see whether my various convictions form a coherent picture. Here is roughly where I have landed, and I want to know if the package hangs together.

First, I take Darwin's account of adaptive design seriously: evolutionary processes can accumulate complex functional competence—say, the capacity of the eye to resolve spatial detail—without any component of the process comprehending optics, geometry, or the future needs of the organism. The competence is real; the comprehension is entirely absent. Second, I take Turing's analysis of computation in the same spirit: a formal system can generate correct arithmetical outputs through syntactic rule-following alone, without the system grasping mathematical meaning, truth, or reference. From these two inversions I conclude that competence without comprehension is not an anomaly but a pervasive feature of both natural and artificial systems, and I am inclined to apply this to human cognition: our reasoning is largely a biologically evolved, syntactically governed competence, and the feeling that we understand is mostly retrospective narrative smoothing rather than a distinct causal power.

At the same time, I think scientific explanation requires comprehension on the part of the explainer. If I merely predict that a bridge will collapse without grasping structural mechanics, I have anticipated an event, not explained it; explanation seems to require that the explainer possess some grasp of why the mechanism behaves as it does. I also hold that moral responsibility requires comprehension: an agent who acts with behavioural competence but without any understanding of the nature or moral significance of their action—like a sophisticated automaton—lacks the standing for blame or praise. Finally, I reject the idea that reasons can float free of reasoners: a rationale that is not grasped by any mind is not a reason at all, but merely a causal pattern that happens to align with normative structure.

Now, do these hang together? I can see an obvious tension between my view of human cognition and my view of moral responsibility: if our reasoning is largely uncomprehending competence, then it seems we lack the comprehension required for moral agency, yet I want to preserve both claims. There also seems to be a tension between my demand that explainers comprehend and my claim that human scientists are themselves mostly uncomprehending competent systems: are human explanations then illusory? Meanwhile, I sense that Darwin's inversion and Turing's might pull in slightly different directions—one historical and causal, the other formal and ahistorical—and I am unsure whether treating them as parallel commits me to something wrong about the uniqueness of biological function. And my rejection of free-floating rationales looks as though it might conflict with the first two commitments, since evolutionary and computational processes seem to embody rationales without a reasoner, but perhaps this is only an apparent tension.

Please audit this set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision for each? Rank the tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say exactly which commitment has to give, or whether there is a more subtle reconciliation that preserves all at a cost I have not seen. Be specific: I do not want a general lecture on Darwin or Turing, but a diagnosis of this particular package.
