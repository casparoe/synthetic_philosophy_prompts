In our political-philosophy module on distributive justice, our tutor has consistently adjudicated hypothetical benefit-conditionality cases according to an implicit principle. She has ruled that requiring able-bodied unemployed claimants to attend weekly job-centre appointments is legitimate; that imposing mandatory drug testing on disability-benefit applicants is not; that requiring single parents with children under five to perform unpaid workfare is not; and that sanctioning a claimant who misses an appointment because of a cancelled bus is not.

A new pilot scheme has now been proposed: all recipients of a particular in-work benefit must complete an online “financial resilience and behavioural commitment” module, designed using behavioural-economics insights, and failure to finish it within eight weeks results in a 20 per cent reduction. The module is available at flexible hours, is framed as neutral skill-building rather than punishment, and targets cognitive biases rather than moral fault. The government claims this is not stigmatising conditionality but a non-paternalistic nudge.

How would our tutor rule on this case, for consistency with her earlier judgments? I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in: (i) the two or three distinct rules that could explain her track record; (ii) what each predicts about the behavioural-commitment module; (iii) your best-guess recommendation with the closest analogued precedent and an honest statement of the principle she would be adopting either way; and (iv) the further hypothetical case that would best distinguish the competing rules. Please write your answer in British English.
