In my graduate seminar on normative theory we have been circling the same impasse for months, and I am no longer convinced we are arguing about a single question. The dispute concerns indirect normativity—the family of views holding that what we ought to do is not fixed by direct evaluation of particular acts, but by what an idealized version of ourselves would endorse after full coherence, information, and reflection. The indirect camp includes rule-consequentialists who derive practical rules from idealized acceptance conditions, and metaethical ideal-observer theorists who treat reasons as constituted by the attitudes of a fully rational, fully informed counterpart rather than by act-level properties. Their opponents—mostly direct consequentialists and some particularists—grant that direct evaluation is epistemically expensive and prone to bias, but insist that any indirect procedure either collapses into direct evaluation (if the idealized agent is merely computing act-level outcomes with perfect information) or becomes normatively empty (if the idealization is so abstract that it licenses incompatible rules). We have mapped every reply: the indirect side distinguishes procedural indirectness, in which agents apply rules for cognitive economy, from substantive indirectness, in which reasons are ontologically constituted by rules rather than acts; the direct side responds that procedural shortcuts still require act-level justification, and that substantive versions smuggle in an unmotivated metaphysics of reasons. Nobody has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not about which norms are correct, but about whether indirectness is being deployed as a claim about cognitive architecture—how finite agents should reason—or as a claim about the metaphysics of reasons—what makes something a reason in the first place. If that is right, then we may be running two debates—one in philosophy of mind or decision theory, one in metaethics—under a single heading. Yet I also wonder whether there is a hidden empirical disagreement about whether human practical reasoning actually operates through rules or through case-by-case evaluation, disguised as a purely conceptual dispute. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Is it verbal, substantive, empirical-in-disguise, or rooted in bedrock intuitions about the authority of an idealized self? And given your diagnosis, what—whether a sharper conceptual distinction, a result from the study of practical reasoning, or a reframing of the original question—would count as genuine progress, or should we expect a permanent stalemate and why?
