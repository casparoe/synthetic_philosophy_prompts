I'm writing a novel whose protagonist survives a total, gradual replacement of her body with synthetic analogues and, later, a discontinuous restoration of her psychology into a new substrate with no causal chain linking the instances—only an informational pattern. I want the metaphysics of persistence in the book to be rigorous rather than decorative. So: what are the actual desiderata any theory of personal persistence through time must meet? List the phenomena it must explain (gradual change, fission, amnesia, discontinuous restoration), the distinctions it must preserve (survival versus replication, being present versus being extended, having status versus having a degree of it), the constraints from biology or physics it must respect, and whether some cluster of these demands is jointly impossible. I particularly want to know whether requiring that the subject be wholly present at each time conflicts with the intuition that she can survive the loss of any particular part, and which single requirement eliminates the largest number of standard views. Focus on the criteria and the cases that motivate them; I will apply them to the narrative logic myself, so I do not need a scorecard of named theories.
