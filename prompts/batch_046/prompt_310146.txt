The debate between the violinist-style bodily-autonomy defense of abortion and the future-like-ours argument has been at an impasse for decades: proponents of the first treat sustaining another life as supererogatory and not compellable, even when the dependent being has a full right to live, while proponents of the second treat killing a being with a valuable future as a harm comparable to killing an adult, regardless of bodily burden. My working theory is that the stall is not empirical—both sides largely agree on fetal development and consciousness thresholds—but structural: one side is asking whether negative rights against intrusion can be overridden by another's need, while the other is asking whether potential future goods generate non-waivable reasons that bodily autonomy cannot cancel. Is this a single disagreement being argued badly, or are these answers to different questions—about the limits of negative duty versus the moral weight of potentiality—forced into one framework under the heading "abortion"? Diagnose it concretely: is the conflict verbal, about which analogies count as data, about hidden bedrock intuitions regarding naturalness or potential, or about an unacknowledged formal distinction between rights that can be waived and reasons that cannot? And given your diagnosis, what would actually move it—a conceptual distinction that separates the two questions, a thought experiment that breaks the violinist analogy, or an argument that no unified answer should be expected?
