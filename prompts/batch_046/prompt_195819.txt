I am writing a dissertation chapter in philosophy of technology on privacy harms that do not depend on the disclosure of hidden facts. In both legal scholarship and applied ethics I keep noticing a recurring configuration that I struggle to classify, and I would like to know whether it already has a precise conceptual name or whether it occupies an under-theorized gap between familiar categories.

The pattern can be illustrated with two concrete cases. First, a city publishes only aggregated, anonymized mobility data. Residents gradually realize that the system can identify statistical anomalies—an unexpected concentration of travel to a particular area on a given evening. Although no individual is ever singled out, people begin to avoid that destination, not out of fear of direct identification, but because contributing to an anomalous cluster now feels like a kind of practical error; the statistical environment has reshaped their sense of which actions remain genuinely open. Second, an employer uses a predictive model trained on company-wide communication patterns to forecast collective labor organizing. No specific message is read by management, and no worker is individually flagged; instead, the firm restructures teams and relocates projects preemptively, so that the organizational conditions necessary for collective action never arise. Here the harm is not exposure but anticipatory constriction: the predictive apparatus does not reveal a secret, it collapses the space of plausible futures before agents can inhabit them.

Is there an established term for this particular harm? It is not exactly the chilling effect, since that typically presupposes a direct threat of sanction following expression. Nor does it map neatly onto standard autonomy-based privacy theories, which usually center on an individual's control over personal information rather than on the preemptive foreclosure of possibility spaces through statistical inference. It also differs from mere self-censorship under surveillance, because the mechanism is not the agent's belief that she is being watched, but her awareness that her behavior is being incorporated into a collective model that can make certain outcomes structurally unavailable. If the concept is not already established, how should one delineate its boundaries—distinguishing it, for instance, from the influence of algorithmic recommendation or from structural power more generally—and what conditions would prevent it from collapsing into a generic complaint about technological determinism?
