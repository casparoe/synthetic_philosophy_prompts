I'm covering a debate for a piece on free will and cognitive architecture. At a recent philosophy-of-science workshop, a cognitive scientist and a philosopher of mind argued over whether human agency escapes what philosophers call sphexishness—the rigid, script-bound behavior exemplified by the wasp Sphex ichneumoneus, which will repeat its burial ritual even if its prey is moved during inspection. Here is the exchange:

(1) The scientist argued that human cognition is sphexish at the implementation level: we run cached action scripts with only superficial parameter adjustments. The appearance of choice is just high-dimensional rigidity, so free will is an illusion produced by nested mechanisms.

(2) The philosopher replied that sphexishness is defined not merely by repetition but by context-insensitivity—the wasp fails to update its behavior when the environment changes in ways that should matter. Humans, by contrast, routinely abort or revise scripts in response to novel contextual cues, which means they lack the defining feature of sphexishness.

(3) The scientist countered that this only relocates the rigidity: the abort response is itself triggered automatically by conflict-monitoring or anomaly-detection signals. What looks like open-ended flexibility is just a second-order sphex mechanism—a sphex that monitors other sphexes. Without showing that the monitoring layer is not itself scripted, the philosopher hasn't escaped mechanism.

(4) The philosopher answered that this line collapses every form of flexibility into rigidity by definitional fiat. If any responsive mechanism is automatically sphexish, then a system that can suspend its own goals for reasons not predetermined in the original script is indistinguishable from a thermostat with a feedback loop. The scientist owes an independent criterion for when a mechanism counts as genuinely open-ended rather than merely nested-sphexish; otherwise the argument proves too much.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification. Say who is more right overall, identify what both sides are missing—for instance, whether there is an empirical test that could distinguish nested rigidity from reason-responsive flexibility—and note whether either party has insulated their position against counterevidence by definitional maneuvering.
