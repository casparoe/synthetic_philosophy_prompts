I'm working through readings for a course on personal identity and clinical ethics, and I've noticed that defenders of strong advance-directive authority keep returning to what they call the "Ulysses contract": the competent patient is like Ulysses tying himself to the mast, deliberately binding his future, temptation-beguiled self so that the crew—the physicians—can enforce the original plan even when the demented patient later begs to be untied. The analogy is deployed to license the conclusion that a present protest should be treated as non-authoritative noise, just as Ulysses's later screams were not reasons to release him. Before I accept that move, I want the mapping audited with real precision.

Give me the explicit correspondence table—Ulysses, the sirens, the ropes, the crew, the mast, the binding moment—mapped onto the prior competent self, the demented patient, the directive's legal force, the clinical team, the institutional framework, and the experiential state being resisted. Then show me exactly where the analogy fractures: does severe dementia leave the later self "still Ulysses but bewitched," or does it produce someone with different critical interests in a way the siren-song model obscures? How does the fact that physicians are not neutral sailors but interpreters with discretionary authority alter the moral geometry? What about the epistemic asymmetry—Ulysses knew precisely what temptation awaited, whereas the directive-writer cannot genuinely imagine the phenomenology of advanced dementia? And does the conclusion—that present protest carries no moral weight—actually survive once these disanalogies are on the table, or was it smuggled in by selecting this particular myth rather than, say, a narrative of genuine, identity-preserving change of heart?

Please separate what is settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and bioethicists who work on this—particularly regarding whether personal identity survives profound cognitive decline, how to weigh critical against experiential interests, and whether binding authority requires some form of present endorsement. Do not present a contested position on these matters as if it were the consensus view.
