I recently joined an interdisciplinary working group that pairs evolutionary biologists with computer scientists to advise on educational technology policy, and I have been asked to present on whether artificial intelligence poses a genuinely new kind of epistemic challenge or merely repeats an older pattern. In preparing, I have become preoccupied with what seems to me a strange parallel—though perhaps a misleading one—between two inversions of reasoning that I encountered in my reading. The first is Darwin's evolutionary account: the accumulation of exquisitely adapted biological competence, such as eyes that track light or wings that generate lift, through selection processes that realize functional design without any agent who comprehends the purpose being built. The second is Turing's argument in his 1950 essay that a computing system might learn and behave intelligently without possessing internal states that amount to understanding or conscious comprehension. Both cases appear to decouple competence from comprehension, yet I cannot tell whether the decoupling operates identically in each, or whether recognizing the parallel actually illuminates anything about how human institutions should evaluate learned skill.

Because my professional background is in institutional assessment rather than philosophy of mind or biology, I want to see whether this decoupling can be made sense of in the vocabulary I actually work with—competency-based accreditation, rubric-aligned performance indicators, proxy measures of skill, distributed expertise across curricular structures, and the distinction between observable learning outcomes and inferred cognitive representation. Please restate both Darwin's inversion and Turing's inversion within that framework, treating them not as biological or computational theories but as models of how competence can be certified without reference to an underlying comprehending subject. For each, indicate what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in assessment vocabulary. Then report on the residue: is there something essential about Darwin's or Turing's separation of performance from understanding that competency-based education cannot express because its vocabulary implicitly assumes that demonstrated skill must eventually trace back to an individual learner's mental representation? Or does the failure to translate merely expose that the original philosophical puzzles about design and intelligence were always less substantial than they seemed? I am especially interested in whether the parallel between evolutionary adaptation and machine learning survives this translation, or whether the language of accreditation collapses the two into an undifferentiated notion of performance without mind and thereby loses something philosophically important.
