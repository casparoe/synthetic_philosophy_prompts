Read Franz Kafka’s The Trial through the lens of Blaise Pascal’s argument in the Pensées that justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and that human societies tend to resolve this tension not by empowering justice but by legitimizing whatever coercive power is already in place—making the strong appear just. Focus on two specific moments: the parable of the doorkeeper told by the prison chaplain, and Josef K.’s execution at the novel’s end. Ask whether the Court’s authority operates through precisely this Pascalian mechanism—its coercive power is masked by procedural form, and K. gradually comes to treat his own guilt as legitimate despite never knowing the law he allegedly broke. Does Kafka dramatize the idea that the absence of transparent justice forces subjects to invent justifications for the force they suffer? Or does the novel resist that reading by depicting K.’s final acquiescence as hollow and terror-induced rather than genuinely legitimizing? Be careful not to reduce the fiction to an illustration of the philosophy: say where the text complicates Pascal, for instance by making the Court’s power seem metaphysical or irreducibly bureaucratic rather than merely social, or by showing K.’s lawyers and neighbors as active participants in the legitimization of coercion. Conclude by identifying what this particular pairing reveals about the relationship between procedural opacity and political legitimacy that an abstract argument about justice and force alone tends to miss.
