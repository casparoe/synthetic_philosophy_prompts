I am working through my own commitments on bounded rationality and satisficing, specifically on the boundary between rationally limited search and culpable negligence in institutional decision-making. I want to know whether my case-level judgments hang together into something defensible, or whether I am just rationalizing inconsistencies.

Here are my verdicts.

A hiring manager accepts the first job offer that clears a pre-set salary floor and commute-time ceiling, without comparing other offers. This strikes me as perfectly rational satisficing.

A physician, facing ambiguous symptoms that mostly match a common condition, prescribes the standard first-line treatment without ordering a quick, low-cost test that would rule out a rare but serious alternative. This strikes me as negligent, not satisficing.

A municipal planning board approves a water-treatment upgrade that satisfies current regulatory thresholds but declines to incorporate a recent, non-mandatory study recommending stricter limits, on the grounds that full integration would delay the project by eighteen months. This strikes me as rational.

A researcher stops data collection once p < 0.05 is reached, even though the pre-registered plan called for two hundred participants and the current sample is at one hundred twenty. This strikes me as irrational.

A disaster-relief coordinator distributes supplies to the nearest reachable neighborhoods using a simple distance heuristic, knowing that remote areas will be missed but that route optimization would take days. This strikes me as rational.

A software engineer deploys a safety classifier that catches ninety-five percent of harmful outputs using a lightweight keyword filter, rather than delaying release by six months to build a more nuanced model. This strikes me as rational.

A small-claims judge applies a standard damages formula rather than investigating the particular emotional harm suffered by the plaintiff, because her docket contains three hundred active files. This strikes me as rational.

The case I keep revisiting is a university admissions office that uses a rigid GPA and test-score cutoff for its first screening round, allowing human review only for applicants near the boundary. The office knows this will screen out some exceptional candidates from under-resourced schools, but argues that holistic review of all thirty thousand applications would be impossible. Sometimes this feels structurally identical to the hiring-manager case; sometimes it feels closer to the physician case, because the office is ignoring information—contextual factors about school quality—that is already available in the file and highly relevant to the decision.

Is there a coherent principle that captures these verdicts, or close? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me what it predicts about a case I have not listed—say, a pilot in an emergency who lands at the nearest airport despite weather reports suggesting it is risky, because calculating fuel-range alternatives takes too long. Also, please say honestly which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop, if any.

If no single principle captures them all without contrivance, I would prefer a small set of candidate principles, each with the verdict it saves and the one it sacrifices, together with an assessment of which candidate best fits the pattern.
