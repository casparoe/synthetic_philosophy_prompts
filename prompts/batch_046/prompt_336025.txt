I'm the senior referee for a quarterly journal in comparative practical ethics, and each issue I must evaluate three to four manuscripts that compare a specific institutional practice in a Western liberal-democratic setting with an apparently analogous practice in a non-Western society—recent examples include informed consent protocols versus communal decision-making in a Pacific Island community, solitary confinement versus ritual adolescent isolation, and market-based organ donation versus kinship-obligated body-part exchange. The editorial board wants a reusable referee procedure grounded in Montaigne's analysis in "Of Cannibals" and his related essays on custom and judgment—specifically his claim that Europeans condemn cannibal practices by norms they do not apply to themselves, his insistence that every practice possesses an internal coherence that must be reconstructed before evaluation, and his refusal both to condemn and to romanticize—while accommodating a modern complication Montaigne did not face: authors now routinely embed quantitative outcome data (psychological distress scores, mortality rates, economic efficiency metrics) and treat these as settling the normative comparison.

Please write a step-by-step referee procedure I can apply to any such manuscript in about forty-five minutes. The procedure should specify, in strict order: (1) preliminary structural checks that determine whether the comparison is valid at all; (2) the mirror-test questions that expose unexamined normative projection; (3) the internal-coherence reconstruction steps that must be completed before any evaluation is permitted; (4) how quantitative evidence may enter—what it can legitimately show, what it cannot, and the specific error of treating a metric optimized for one cultural context as universal; (5) decision nodes (approve, request revision, reject) with explicit criteria for each; and (6) the points where judgment is irreducibly personal and must be flagged as such rather than disguised as procedural outcome. Include a small set of concrete test questions or checks I can apply quickly to each manuscript, and flag explicitly what the procedure cannot do—what kinds of comparison it will systematically miss or mishandle.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (A) a manuscript arguing that ritual isolation of adolescents in a specific East African community should be banned because it produces PTSD scores comparable to Western solitary confinement; (B) a manuscript defending kinship-obligated body-part exchange by romanticizing it as pure gift while ignoring documented coercion and gender asymmetry; and (C) a manuscript that applies the procedure rigorously and still reaches a conclusion I will find morally repugnant—please state clearly why the procedure allows that outcome and what that reveals about its limits.

Finally, separate what is philosophically settled about Montaigne's position—such as his rejection of unreflective universalist condemnation and his use of the mirror technique—from what is genuinely contested among scholars—such as whether his relativism extends to practices causing severe harm, whether quantitative cross-cultural comparison is ever legitimate, and whether his framework can accommodate modern human-rights discourse. Do not present any contested interpretive claim, for example that Montaigne was a full-blown moral relativist or that he endorsed every practice he described, as if it were the scholarly consensus. Mark the boundary between settled interpretation and live dispute in each section of your response.
