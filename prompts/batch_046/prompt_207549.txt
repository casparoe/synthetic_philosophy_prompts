I'm drafting the final exam for an upper-level seminar on ethics and historical representation. Write one 90-minute essay question worth 20 marks, aimed at students who can summarize standard debates about artistic license but who need to be pushed past labels. Introduce a non-standard conceptual distinction for this purpose: call it the "counterfactual intimacy" rule for biopics, according to which inventing an unrecorded private scene is permissible if and only if (i) every attitude and line of dialogue attributed to the real figure is consistent with their documented practical reasoning and value commitments, and (ii) the invention does not alter the causal explanation of any public event in which they participated. Set against this, present a critic's appeal to "posthumous autonomy" — the claim that the dead retain an interest in not being treated as raw narrative material, irrespective of consistency or causal irrelevance, because being made into a character instrumentalizes a life that was once self-authored.

The question should ask students to do three things: (a) clarify the counterfactual intimacy rule and explain precisely why it is not reducible to ordinary factual accuracy; (b) assess whether posthumous autonomy is coherent given that the dead lack consciousness and legal standing, and if it is incoherent identify the legitimate concern (e.g., dignitary, epistemic, or relational) that it is trying to capture; and (c) apply both frameworks to the following case and reach a defended conclusion — a director invents a private quarrel between a documented resistance figure and their adolescent child about whether to flee an occupation; the quarrel never occurred, but every position attributed to the parent matches surviving letters, and the scene changes nothing in the public historical record. Ask what, if anything, we owe the real dead in dramatizing them, and require the student to justify their conclusion with a principle rather than a bare intuition.

Provide a model answer structured around five must-hit moves, with the marks assigned to each (totaling 20): (1) accurate distinction of the rule from factual accuracy, specifying the practical-reasoning consistency condition (4 marks); (2) coherent evaluation of posthumous autonomy, either defending it through a proxy-interest argument or dissolving it and substituting the real protected value (6 marks); (3) recognition that causal irrelevance to public events is insufficient to justify private fabrication (3 marks); (4) precise application to the invented parent-child quarrel, engaging the asymmetry of the relationship and the letter-consistency condition (4 marks); (5) a synthesized conclusion that articulates an underlying principle (for example, "narrative non-instrumentalization" rather than autonomy proper) and shows why it follows from the analysis (3 marks). For each move, include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail it. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the question must be answerable from conceptual reasoning alone. Specify the exam conditions clearly: upper-level seminar, 90 minutes, approximately 1,200 words.
