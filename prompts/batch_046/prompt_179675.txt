During a recent after-school session of my philosophy and cognitive science club, a debate emerged over an artificial sensory archive I have been assembling for a science fair project: the system catalogues every quantifiable biochemical interaction, neural firing pattern, and electromagnetic signature that occurs when a person tastes saffron, yet it possesses no mouth, no gustatory cortex, and no conscious awareness of any kind. One of my peers insisted that because the archive contains a complete physical description of the experience, it must in principle know what saffron tastes like, and that any remaining mystery is only an engineering limitation rather than a philosophical one; I resisted that conclusion, sensing that there is a real distinction between holding objective information about a phenomenon and actually undergoing the subjective experience of it, though I could not determine whether that distinction undermines a purely physical account of consciousness or merely reflects our present technological inadequacy.

I should also mention that my friend—who studies computer engineering—kept framing the issue as a problem of missing sensors rather than missing experience, which made me wonder whether our disagreement is genuinely about the nature of subjective awareness, about the limits of representation, or about some hidden embodied or causal condition that a database cannot replicate. For tomorrow's club meeting, I would like a conceptual analysis that addresses the philosophical structure of this archive example directly, without generic advice about artificial intelligence or science projects; specifically, I want to understand whether my intuition reveals an irreducible gap in physical explanation, or whether I am conflating practical ignorance with a deeper metaphysical boundary, and what kind of reasoning would best defend either position.
