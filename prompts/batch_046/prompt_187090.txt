I'm running a 90-minute graduate seminar in philosophy of mind with 14 students, and I want an in-class simulation that makes them feel the structural pressure behind Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and the pull of the multiple drafts model—not through lecture, but through rules that force them to live inside the architecture. The deliverable should be a complete, runnable activity with assigned roles, a timeline, explicit rules, and a debrief.

Design a structured game with the following roles: one "Theater Manager" who must maintain a continuous spoken narrative of what is happening "right now"; four "Draft Modules" (Visual, Affective, Proprioceptive, Narrative) that each receive fragments of an ambiguous shared scenario—something like a sudden temperature drop, a face that might be angry or merely tired, a sound that might be speech or mechanical noise, and a bodily sensation of tilting; one "Timekeeper" enforcing staggered reporting delays (30 seconds, 2 minutes, 4 minutes); and the remaining students as "Audience Critics" who track contradictions. Here is the niche twist that should drive the design: any Draft Module may play a "Silent Revision" card at any time, retroactively amending its earlier report, but this revision is delivered quietly to the Critics and to other modules—not announced to the Theater Manager. The Manager must keep speaking without knowing whether the ground has shifted, constructing what feels like a single unified movie playing in one place.

The rules should be arranged so that the Manager initially builds a coherent "present experience," then is undermined by silent revisions that make the past they described impossible—forcing either confabulation or an admission that their authoritative "now" was never in control. The Critics should have a structured scorecard for tracking when the Manager's account conflicts with revised drafts. The scenario fragments should be concrete enough to generate real disagreement but ambiguous enough to allow genuinely different interpretations.

Include a precise timeline (setup, rounds, resolution), the materials needed (cards, delay timers, scorecards), and the payoff structure—this need not involve money, but there should be some small social or competitive stakes (e.g., the Manager loses authority points for each unrevised contradiction they miss, or the Critics earn points for identifying silent revisions).

After the simulation (about 35 minutes), provide a 20-minute debrief that maps specific moments onto Dennett's distinction: the Manager's running account as a "draft," the silent revisions as competing drafts without a central audience, the confabulation as the Cartesian theater trying to maintain continuity where none exists. Be frank about what the physical simulation cannot reproduce—particularly the absence of any subjective "feel" in distributed processing, or the neural-level dynamics of "fame in the brain"—and tell me exactly what feature of the idea I must supply verbally because the activity can only stage its behavioral shadow.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run—whether the Manager will freeze out of frustration, the revisions will cascade too quickly, or students will interpret the game as being about deception rather than temporal distribution—and how to handle each failure without breaking the structural point.

The students should not be required to have read Dennett in advance; they should encounter the problem structurally first, learn the name of the view only in the debrief, and never be asked for citations, page references, bibliographies, or specific textual quotations.
