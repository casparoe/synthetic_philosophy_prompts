I'm helping design a manual for the ethics review board at a prison rehab program. We keep getting proposals to run contemplative courses for inmates — metta, breathing practices, a stripped-down body-scan thing — and right now we approve them mostly by vibe. I want a reusable decision procedre that a board member can actually run in about 15 minutes, with no philosophy background, that translates Buddhist ethical ideas (the precepts, cetana as the volitional quality behind an action, the idea that harm can live in the mental state something trains rather than just the behavior) together with a consequentialist audit (recidivism base rates, institutional risk, whether the benefit is real or placebo).

The checklist should go in order: what question to ask, what counts as a sufficent answer, where there's a hard stop for 'no', and where it hands off to a values call because facts can't settle it. It needs to handle two specific failure modes we actually worry about: (a) the 'cooling problem' — a practice that looks harmless but cultivates a detached equanimity that blunts moral responsiveness to others; (b) a practice with decent outcome data but whose instructor manual requires endorsing metaphysical claims like rebirth or karma mechanics, which violates our secular mandate.

Then apply it to two proposals:
1. An eight-week metta course taught by a secular volunteer, framed as 'emotional hygiene', no Buddhist language, but with anecdotal reports that inmates become less aggressive.
2. A 45-minute silent body-scan program with preliminary data showing 12% reduction in violent incidents, but security notes it correlates with dissociative episodes in our population, and the instructor's manual talks about 'purifying mental formations'.

Also flag exactly where the Buddhist axis (what mental state is being cultivated?) and the consequentialist axis (what happens in the data?) talk past each other — don't paper over it. Mark which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure. This is for people running maybe ten proposals a year, with legal liability if something backfires, so keep it practical, not academic.
