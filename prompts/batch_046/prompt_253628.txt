I write as a retired molecular biologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has become rather absorbed in moral philosophy—particularly in evolutionary debunking arguments in metaethics. Having read several accounts of how natural selection might shape our normative convictions, I have formed my own tentative verdicts on a range of cases, and I should be grateful if you could help me determine whether these judgments hang together according to some defensible principle, or whether I am compelled to abandon one or more of them.

My intuitions are as follows. First, the belief that it is seriously wrong to harm one's own siblings strikes me as clearly calibrated by kin-selection mechanisms, yet I do not regard this evolutionary origin as undermining its justification. Second, the conviction that reciprocal fairness is morally binding also seems adaptively rooted, and I treat it as equally justified. Third, the intense suspicion or hostility that people often direct towards strangers in large, anonymous societies seems to me an evolutionary misfire of mechanisms evolved for small-band cooperation; in this instance I believe the evolutionary genealogy does debunk the intuition. Fourth, I am inclined to maintain that moral properties are objective and mind-independent, despite the possibility that such a belief is itself an evolutionary byproduct of hyperactive agency detection; here, too, I resist debunking. Fifth, consider a norm—purely culturally transmitted, with no clear genetic basis—prohibiting the unauthorised use of another individual's digital avatar within a virtual economy. I am inclined to dismiss this norm as not genuinely binding, precisely because it lacks both the adaptive pedigree of the first two cases and the kind of robust reflective endorsement that might rescue the fourth.

Is there a single principle—perhaps one that distinguishes direct adaptive calibration from evolutionary spillover, or that weighs evolutionary origin against the results of reflective equilibrium—that can accommodate all five verdicts? Please state any such principle precisely, indicate which clause of it is motivated by which case above, predict its verdict on a further case (for example, a culturally novel intuition that we have stringent obligations to protect distant future generations, which lacks adaptive history but survives careful rational scrutiny), and be candid about which of my judgments, if any, it cannot accommodate. I should also be grateful if you could present your analysis in British English.
