You are a professional philosopher of mind with extensive experience teaching graduate seminars and writing examinations. Before drafting the deliverables, take a moment to think carefully and briefly brainstorm the core conceptual crux: what exactly makes the paradox of phenomenal judgment a problem specifically for epiphenomenalism rather than for other forms of physicalism, and why a merely correlational link between neural states and phenomenal judgments does not automatically dissolve the difficulty.

Then, for an upper-level philosophy of mind course, write a single essay examination question that a well-prepared student could answer in 90 minutes. The question should concern epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment, but it must not be answerable by merely summarizing the standard formulation—that if phenomenal properties lack causal efficacy, then our judgments about them are epistemically undermined. Instead, embed the following specific hypothetical architecture: suppose phenomenal states are causally inert, but the brain contains a parallel phenomenal-tracking subsystem that receives input from the same neural conditions that generate the phenomenal states, producing judgments that are reliably correlated with the presence or absence of qualia without being caused by the qualia themselves. The question should ask students to evaluate whether this architecture genuinely resolves the paradox or merely relocates it, and it should explicitly require discussion of (a) whether first-person epistemic authority is preserved or transformed, (b) whether the tracking subsystem introduces a skeptical problem about other minds, and (c) how the proposal relates to the familiar distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness.

After the question, provide:
1. A model answer of approximately 700 words that demonstrates the required analysis.
2. A marking scheme totaling 25 marks, divided into at least five distinct components that a top answer must contain. For each component, state the marks available, the specific conceptual move required, and a one-line note on how candidates typically fail on that point.

Write formally and precisely. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations. Do not quote specific texts; if you refer to particular philosophers or positions, paraphrase without quotation marks. Make sure the scenario and the evaluation criteria are described in enough detail that the answer can be assessed without reference to external literature.
