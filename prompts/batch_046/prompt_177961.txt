Write an essay on whether Berkeley's master argument is actually self-undermining given his own rejection of representationalism. The argument claims you can't conceive of something existing unperceived because any such conception is itself a percieved idea in the mind. But here's the catch: for that gap between idea and object to really do work, you seem to need a representationalist picture where ideas stand in for external things — which is exactly the view Berkeley spends the rest of his philosophy attacking. Some philosophers say this makes the master argument circular or at least secretly dependent on the enemy's framework. Others think Berkeley can escape by saying the contradiction isn't representational but logical: it's not that the idea fails to copy the object, but that "unperceived object" is literally unthinkable because thinking it makes it percieved. Weigh both sides carefully and reach a conclusion: is the argument salvageable without smuggling in representationalism, or does Berkeley have to bite the bullet and accept that his best weapon borrows from the view he's trying to destroy? Keep it focused — no need for a reading list or citations, just work through the logic and say where you land.
