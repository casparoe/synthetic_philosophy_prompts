I've been working through the ethical implications of panpsychism with a small reading group — mostly rationalist-leaning people with a philosophy-of-physics hobby — and a particular analogy keeps surfacing in our discussions. Someone will say that if consciousness is genuinely fundamental, then the suffering of quarks, quantum fields, or whatever the base ontology turns out to be is like background radiation: omnipresent, unavoidable, and at such low intensity per unit that our moral instruments don't register it, so we should treat it as noise rather than signal. The analogy is being used to licence a specific conclusion — that panpsychism doesn't actually expand our obligations, because any suffering at the fundamental level is automatically sub-threshold and therefore ethically inert, leaving macroscopic agents with exactly the duties they had before.

I'd like you to audit this properly. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the radiation source, the Geiger counter, the shielding, the regulatory safe-exposure limit, and the human-scale tissue damage? Then the places the mapping breaks: suffering isn't physically measurable like ionisation, there's no agreed dose-response curve for micro-experience, and ethical obligations don't behave like safety regulations that apply only above a certain intensity. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended moral relaxation from those that don't, and tell me whether the relaxed conclusion survives once they're on the table — or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole time, especially since if consciousness is fundamental there may be no "shielding" principle that protects macroscopic agents but not the particles themselves.

One further request: if my framing rests on a confused premise — for instance, if the background-radiation model already smuggles in physicalist assumptions that a serious panpsychist would reject, or if auditing an analogy this loose can't be done rigorously without first fixing a specific theory of consciousness — say so plainly instead of complying politely.
