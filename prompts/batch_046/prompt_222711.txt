Consider the following argument, which I constructed for a seminar on Sartrean ethics and now find suspicious because its conclusion makes authenticity structurally impossible. (1) Radical freedom entails that for any conscious state, I could have interpreted or chosen it differently; no external cause fully determines my consciousness. (2) Bad faith is the refusal to acknowledge this freedom—treating oneself as a fixed essence, or as causally determined by circumstances, or as compelled by social roles. (3) Sartre insists that bad faith is not a passive mistake but an actively maintained posture: one freely chooses to deceive oneself. (4) Therefore, being in bad faith is simultaneously an exercise of radical freedom (the choice to deny freedom) and a denial of that freedom. (5) Authenticity, by contrast, requires lucid recognition and acceptance of one's radical freedom. (6) If escaping bad faith requires a free choice to embrace freedom, then that very choice can be performed in bad faith—for instance, adopting "authenticity" as a fashionable role, or claiming one was compelled into self-awareness by therapy or crisis. (7) Consequently, no state of consciousness is guaranteed authentic, because every recognition of freedom can be undermined by the same freedom that produces it. (8) So Sartre's view proves that authenticity is unattainable, which contradicts his claim that lucid self-awareness is available, however difficult.

This strikes me as suspicious: each premise sounds Sartrean, yet the conclusion is absurd. Where does the reasoning fail? Is there an equivocation between the freedom that chooses a posture and the freedom that is recognized as content, or does the error lie elsewhere? If I must keep the claim that bad faith is freely chosen (step 3), which revision is least damaging—denying that every escape can be undermined, or distinguishing levels of reflection?
