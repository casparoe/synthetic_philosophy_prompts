I'm working through an idea for my ethics seminar about tranquilism — the view that only the absense of craving and suffering has intrinsic value, while pleasure, achievement, or love don't actually add anything on their own. My study partner threw this chain at me over coffee and now I'm stuck trying to find the exit.

Here is the argument:
(1) Tranquilism holds that the only intrinsic good is the absence of craving (taṇhā) and suffering; success or joy have zero intrinsic weight.
(2) Compassionate action requires representing another person's suffering in your own mind — some kind of empathic uptake.
(3) So whenever you act to help someone, you necessarily import suffering into your own mental state.
(4) Even if you succeed, all you've done is move the other person closer to zero (absence of suffering), which removes a negative but doesn't generate positive value.
(5) So compassionate action always introduces suffering into you and never produces intrinsic value.
(6) By parallel reasoning, any intentional project involves anticipatory craving, another negative with no offsetting good.
(7) Therefore the rational tranquilist should avoid all action, including moral action, and just sit in isolated non-desire.
(8) But that seems to collapse the view into moral indifference — you shouldn't save a drowning child because you'd feel their panic and disturb your tranquility, and even if you save them there's no value gained.

I'd really rather not drop (1), and (2) feels psychologically hard to deny. Is there a hidden equivocation on "suffering" between phenomenological pain and representational awareness? Does (4) misapply the value structure by treating reduction-to-zero as worthless? Or is the argument actually valid and I just have to bite the bullet on (7)? Tell me which step to reject and what I'm paying for it. Keep your reply under 300 words, one paragraph if you can swing it, two max — I just need to see where the slip is.
