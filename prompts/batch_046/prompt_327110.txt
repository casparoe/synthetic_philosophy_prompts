Write a joke about whether maximal moral goodness is desirable, centered on the “moral saint” problem. The joke should turn on actual philosophical content—specifically, Susan Wolf’s concern that a life devoted entirely to moral improvement crowds out the non-moral goods (personal projects, aesthetic experience, ordinary attachments, even bad taste in movies) that make human life valuable—rather than on generic “philosophers are weird” stereotypes. Inject this niche twist: the protagonist is a “second-order moral saint” who recognizes that first-order moral perfection requires leaving room for imperfection, and therefore treats the deliberate cultivation of an unremarkable, joyless hobby—competitive stamp collecting pursued with no genuine interest—as a direct moral obligation. The punchline should hinge on the self-undermining structure of her position: her attempt to optimize her own character by manufacturing non-moral goods ends up indistinguishable from ordinary vanity or self-absorption, so that the logic collapses the distinction between the saint and the ordinary person she is meant to serve. The joke should be intelligible to someone familiar with the debate over whether moral saints must be unpleasant, and with the distinction between saints motivated by love and those motivated by rational duty, but it should not depend on knowledge of any specific scholar other than Wolf. After delivering the joke, include a brief analytical note that separates what is settled in Wolf’s original argument (that moral perfection is neither necessary nor obviously attractive as a life ideal) from what is genuinely contested or speculative—particularly whether preserving non-moral goods can be coherently moralized as an object-level duty without generating this reflexive collapse, and whether a “loving” saint could avoid the paradox differently from a “rational” one. Do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view, and do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or requests for further sources.
