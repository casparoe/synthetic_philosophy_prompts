Right, I've been mucking about with philosophy since I retired from the lab — mostly epistemology and decison theory — and I've jottted down where I stand on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Here is roughly where I've landed. First, I think the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are really the same beast at bottom — both come from applying standard probabilistic reasoning to belief sets that are just too big. Second, I reject any fixed Lockean threshold; there's no magical 0.95 line where credence suddenly turns into full-blown belief. Third, even so, I think a working scientist ought to be able to "accept" a well-supported model at about 0.90 credence and use it to design experiments, without having to pretend she knows it's literally true. Fourth, I also think it's perfectly rational to hold 150 separate scientific beliefs, each at 0.98 credence, and simultaneously reckon it's virtually certain that at least two or three of them are wrong. Fifth, I've started calling this my "local coherence budget" — the idea that a belief set can contain scattered errors without being irrational, provided they don't cluster in one area and wreck your practical reasoning. Sixth, but in the lottery case I still want to say it's irrational to believe of every single ticket that it will lose, even though each individual belief is well-supported by the odds. Seventh, I think the preface writer is in a different boat altogether, because her low confidence in the whole book is a meta-judgement about her own fallibility as a filter, not a direct claim that chapter three is false. Eighth, I don't think we need knowledge-first epistemology or any other fancy machinery to sort either one out — just a cleaner separation between credence, acceptance, and meta-credence.
