Write a short story about Mara, a junior archivist at the Bureau of Comparative Harm, whose job is to place every documented atrocity on a single 1-to-10 scale for the International Reparations Ledger. She usually defends the system — she thinks ranking evils is distasteful but unavoidable if you're going to allocate recognition and resources fairly. Then she recieves a dossier on the 'Calibrated Collapse' of 1983, a regime that didn't just kill people but engineered its violence so that survivors were constantly forced to rank each other's suffering to recieve food or safety; the harm was inseparable from comparison itself. Mara refuses to assign it a number, arguing that filing it alongside other events would replicate the regime's logic. Her supervisor calls her sentimental and says justice requires measurement. The story should take a definite stance: is Mara right that some evils must stay outside the scale, or is she ducking the hard moral work comparison demands? Use British English. After the story, include a brief author's note explaining exactly where you took liberties with the philosophical debate on comparing atrocities and which contested position — that comparison is always reductive, or that refusal to compare is itself an obscenity — your story leans toward. Don't sit on the fence.
