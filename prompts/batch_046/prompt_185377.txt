For decades, interpreters of Tolstoy's essay What Is Art? have stalled on whether his definition of art as the transmission of feeling is descriptive—specifying what successful art does—or normative—ruling out anything not born of sincerely felt emotion. Both sides invoke the same discussion of infection (zarazhenie), agree on Tolstoy's rejection of modernist formalism, and agree that his criteria exclude much contemporary gallery work; yet neither camp has moved, and the exchange feels circular. My working hypothesis is that the real blockage is not aesthetics versus ethics, but a hidden disagreement about whether artistic sincerity requires first-person phenomenal access or can be satisfied by functional transmission alone—so that a procedurally generated sound pattern that reliably induces grief in an audience might still fail to be art if no human ever experienced that grief in producing it. Is that the right diagnosis? Or is the impasse verbal—turning on the ambiguity of feeling—or substantive, resting on incompatible ontologies of artistic intention, or on bedrock intuitions that evaluation and description cannot be separated? Give me a structural diagnosis of what each side actually needs to be true, not a survey of Tolstoy scholarship, and tell me honestly whether anything—an empirical study of audience response, a conceptual distinction between transmission and expression, or a revised account of the infection metaphor—could actually resolve it, or whether no progress should be expected and why.
