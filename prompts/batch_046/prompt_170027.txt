I have been trying to extend Einstein's hole argument into a broader metaphysical thesis about material objects, and I am concerned that the reasoning proves far more than I intended. Here is the argument:

(1) In the hole argument, applying a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside a region but not inside produces two metric fields that agree everywhere except inside the hole, yet correspond to the same boundary conditions. If spacetime points possess intrinsic identity—haecceities—this yields physically distinct but empirically indistinguishable histories, violating determinism.

(2) To preserve determinism, we must reject the view that points have intrinsic identities; only the relational structure among them is physically real.

(3) But if points lack intrinsic identity, then any particular assignment of properties to specific points is arbitrary up to permutation. The "same point" across two models is merely the occupant of an analogous structural role.

(4) Now consider a macroscopic object—say, a wooden table—composed of these featureless points arranged in a certain structural relation. Since the points themselves have no identity, the table just is that structural arrangement.

(5) Over time, the table's constituent points are constantly replaced through thermal motion, decay, and interaction, so persistence through time can only be persistence of structure, not of point-constituents.

(6) By the same reasoning, if the table is destroyed and a perfect replica is built elsewhere with an entirely different set of points arranged in the identical structural pattern, there is no metaphysically relevant difference between the original table and the replica: both are the same structural arrangement instantiated by featureless points.

(7) Therefore, destroying an object and rebuilding its replica is not destruction followed by creation, but continuous existence of one and the same object. More radically, there is no genuine distinction between an original and its copy, or between survival and replacement.

(8) But this collapses all distinctions of persistence, ownership, and responsibility that depend on the identity of particular material things, so the argument proves too much.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would strongly prefer to keep (2)—the denial of point-haecceities—if possible, because the hole argument itself seems compelling. But I also want to avoid (7).
