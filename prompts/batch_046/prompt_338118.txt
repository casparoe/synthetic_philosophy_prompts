I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has become absorbed in philosophy of mind, and I have been working through Douglas Hofstadter's proposal that the self is a strange loop — a self-referential symbol, 'I', that emerges when a representational system directs its capacities back upon itself. In a short essay I am drafting, I argue that this account remains incomplete unless we distinguish what I call 'thin' and 'thick' strange loops. A thin loop is purely syntactic: the symbol 'I' denotes the very system that produces it, structurally analogous to a Gödel sentence that asserts its own unprovability within a formal system. A thick loop, by contrast, involves embodied causal feedback — the system not only symbolizes itself but continuously modifies its own dynamics through that symbolization, comparable to a recursive observer in second-order cybernetics. To render this concrete, I draw on non-linear dynamics: I propose that the self is best understood not as a static symbol but as a recursively stabilized attractor in a predictive, error-correcting architecture — an autopoietic strange loop that maintains its identity through continuous self-production rather than through logical self-reference alone.

I would welcome your critical comments on this synthesis. Specifically, I wonder whether the thin/thick distinction genuinely clarifies Hofstadter's framework or merely imports terminology from physics and control theory without philosophical profit. Does modeling the self as a dynamical attractor risk reducing phenomenological presence to a mechanistic process, and can Hofstadter's symbolic account be preserved without that reduction? I am also uncertain whether appealing to recursive observation is compatible with Hofstadter's insistence that the loop is fundamentally logical and symbolic rather than causal-mechanical. Where do you see the weakest point in this argument, and what conceptual refinement or counter-example would you propose to test it?
