I am currently drafting a dissertation chapter on moral recognition in platform labor relations, and a recent seminar on Hegelian social philosophy has left me uncertain about the foundations of my argument. The visiting lecturer summarized the master-slave dialectic from the Phenomenology of Spirit roughly as follows: the encounter begins as a life-and-death struggle for pure prestige; because the master risks his biological existence while the slave, paralyzed by the fear of death, submits, the master alone demonstrates the absolute freedom required for genuine self-consciousness, while the slave remains confined to thinghood through his labor upon external nature. Over time, however, the slave's transformative work on objects yields a practical mastery and independent self-certainty that the idle master cannot achieve, which is why the dialectic contains an implicit historical law—every hierarchical order eventually collapses into revolutionary equality as slaves collectively realize their true status. The lecturer concluded by suggesting that Hegelian recognition is essentially a reciprocal contractual grant of autonomous status, so that modern labor contracts which explicitly recognize worker agency already represent a resolution of the dialectic.

I am inclined to use this framework, but I worry that several moves have been compressed. Could you audit this summary for me? Please separate what is textually accurate, what is a common but misleading simplification, and what is a straightforward inversion or overstatement—especially regarding whether the master actually achieves self-consciousness, whether the slave's development entails a historical prediction of revolution, and whether recognition can be equated with contractual reciprocity. Finally, does the lecturer's conclusion about employment contracts survive once the summary is corrected?
