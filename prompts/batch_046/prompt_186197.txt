I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving active laboratory work, has taken up the philosophical study of consciousness with some seriousness. I currently serve on the advisory ethics board of the Meridian Neuroscience Institute, and I ask you to draft the formal decision memo I will circulate before our board meeting next Thursday. The board must rule on a proposed clinical assessment protocol—developed by a research group led by Dr. Elena Voss—for evaluating patients diagnosed with disorders of consciousness. The protocol rests on a position its authors term phenomenal fictionalism: first-person reports of qualitative states, such as a patient's claim that she feels warmth or experiences anxiety, are not direct descriptions of irreducible phenomenal properties, but rather functional narratives that coordinate behavior without corresponding to any real inner qualitative substrate. On this basis, the protocol proposes to eliminate all subjective-report instruments from diagnosis, relying exclusively on neuroimaging and behavioral markers. The proponents argue that this removes confabulation and improves inter-rater reliability; they further maintain that retaining subjective measures implicitly endorses a naive realism about qualia that their framework rejects. Opponents—clinicians and patient advocates—counter that excising subjective report does not merely refine measurement but conceptually eliminates the very phenomenon under study, since phenomenal consciousness, even if illusory in the sense of being systematically mischaracterized by introspection, remains the clinical target. They also argue that if phenomenal properties are genuinely non-existent in the strong eliminativist sense, then the ethical rationale for invasive neural monitoring shifts substantially, as there is no subjective subject whose welfare must be weighed. Your memo should recommend whether the board approve the protocol outright, reject it, or approve it with conditions—perhaps requiring that subjective reports be retained but interpreted through the fictionalist framework rather than discarded. The reasoning must treat the illusionist distinction between denying that phenomenal properties exist at all and denying that we enjoy transparent, direct access to them; it must present the strongest argument for the losing position with intellectual honesty; and it should be articulated in terms the research team could directly respond to, addressing their reliability and confabulation claims specifically rather than relying solely on abstract philosophical objection. Write in a formal, polished register suitable for circulation to board members with scientific training but limited philosophical background, include a clear actionable bottom line, and specify a concrete timeline for implementation or required revisions.
