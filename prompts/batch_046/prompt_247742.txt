After a lengthy argument with a friend about what Descartes actually proves in the second Meditation, I want to check whether I have missed a hidden premise in his reasoning. He claims that the cogito, rendered as I think, therefore I am, functions as a token-reflexive speech act that necessarily binds every instance of its assertion to the very same speaker, and that this linguistic fact directly entails a metaphysical conclusion: namely, that there is a single, diachronically continuous thinking substance that underlies all of my mental episodes. He further argues that without this underlying Cartesian substrate, the indexical I in successive assertions would fail to co-refer, leaving us with a scattered sequence of momentary minds rather than one continuous epistemic agent capable of carrying out methodical doubt. I objected that the certainty of the cogito extends only to the existence of a present thought, and that linguistic co-reference of an indexical does not by itself establish the metaphysical persistence of a substance; yet he insisted that if the self were reducible to a series of discrete psychological states—along the lines of contemporary reductionist accounts—then there would be no stable referent to anchor the first-person pronoun across time, and Descartes's project of systematic doubt would collapse for want of a continuous doubter. Given all this, I am trying to determine whether the cogito's token-reflexive structure genuinely commits Descartes to a persisting substantial ego, or whether my friend is conflating the pragmatic conditions of indexical reference with the metaphysical requirements of personal identity.
