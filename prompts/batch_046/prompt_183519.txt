You are an expert in political philosophy and comparative constitutional theory. Before you begin, spend a moment brainstorming the key philosophical distinctions that matter here—sovereignty, autonomy, democratic legitimacy, consent, and whether authority can be divided without contradiction—so that your analysis is precise rather than generic.

Consider the following institutional pattern across several federal or quasi-federal systems. In the European Union, the principle of subsidiarity is formally inscribed: the Union should act only where objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved by member states acting alone. In practice, however, EU framework directives and regulations typically bind member states to specific outcomes and deadlines while leaving them only the “form and methods” of implementation. In Switzerland, the federal constitution reserves all powers not explicitly assigned to the Confederation to the cantons; yet federal law increasingly takes the form of framework legislation that establishes uniform national standards, leaving cantons mainly with execution and adaptation, and federal fiscal transfers come with conditions that steer cantonal priorities. In the United States, the constitutional structure reserves powers not delegated to the federal government to the states, but federal grants and conditional funding allow the national government to induce state compliance with policy objectives without formally overriding state legislatures. In Germany, the Basic Law distributes legislative competences among the federation, concurrent fields, and the Länder; in concurrent areas, federal law prevails, and the federation frequently enacts framework laws that the Länder must fill in administratively.

In each system, there is a persistent structural gap: the constitutional text proclaims lower-level democratic autonomy—whether through subsidiarity, reserved powers, or cantonal sovereignty—while the operational reality is that the center defines the substantive policy and the periphery largely administers or adapts it. What philosophical commitments about authority, democratic self-rule, and the nature of consent does this arrangement presuppose? Does it require a conceptual distinction between “decision-making authority” and “implementation discretion”? Does it assume that democratic legitimacy can be bifurcated—so that citizens consent to central goal-setting but preserve local identity through administrative choice? Or does it quietly rest on a layered, non-unitary conception of sovereignty that treats formal jurisdiction and functional influence as independent variables?

Reconstruct the implicit theory of authority and democratic legitimacy that renders this gap coherent rather than merely hypocritical. Be careful to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely happens to fit it well.
