After a long and somewhat circular argument with a close friend about how we ought to design governance mechanisms for powerful predictive systems, I keep returning to one institutional norm that seems to be gaining traction in alignment discourse: the principle that such a system should not be optimized for the preferences its users actually express, but rather for their coherent extrapolated volition — that is, for the preferences they would hold if they were fully informed, had resolved all internal inconsistencies, and had reflected over an extended period. My friend insisted this was simply a more rigorous form of respect for autonomy, yet I found myself unable to articulate exactly what philosophical machinery must be in place for that claim to hold together.

What philosophical commitments does this practice actually presuppose? Must one be a realist about values — holding that there is a fact of the matter about what someone truly wants, independent of their present mental states — or can a sophisticated constructivist account license the same design rule without metaphysical baggage? Does the practice presuppose a particular theory of personal identity, specifically that there exists a stable true self separable from current biases and cognitive distortions, and if so, is it consistent with views that treat identity as a narrative or relational achievement rather than an underlying essence? Furthermore, the practice seems to treat coherence as a normative filter: inconsistencies in preference are not merely logical curiosities but errors to be corrected prior to optimization. Does that commit us to a rationalist conception of agency in which irrational preferences are not genuinely ours? I also wonder whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined: could a contractualist or deliberative-democratic framework justify the same institutional rule without endorsing any metaphysics of true preferences, or does the appeal to extrapolation necessarily smuggle in a form of paternalism that democratic legitimacy cannot accommodate?

There is one specific wrinkle my friend and I never resolved, and it concerns what we might call the extrapolation distance problem. If we extrapolate a user’s preferences far enough — correcting not only for ignorance but for deep-seated values the user currently endorses yet that seem incoherent with their broader commitments — at what point are we no longer serving that person’s autonomy but instead constructing a preference profile for a counterfactual agent only nominally connected to them? Does the institutional practice implicitly draw a boundary here, and if it does, what theory of continuity — psychological, narrative, or normative — justifies placing the boundary exactly there rather than closer to or farther from the actual person?
