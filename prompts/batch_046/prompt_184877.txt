There's this youth rehab scheme running in parts of East Asia — and copied in a few other places — where teenagers who've done minor offences spend six months in a residential programme. Every morning they do formal bowing rituals, recite classical ethical texts from memory, and get scored on how precisely they perform the gestures. There's no bit that asks them to explain what the texts mean or examine thier own motives; the idea is that if the body is trained right, the mind will follow. But the programme brochure says the whole point is to awaken the innate moral sense every child is born with.

That feels like it's trying to have it both ways. What view of human nature does that combo actually presuppose? Is it Mencius's sprouts-of-virtue story, where goodness is innate but needs the right conditions to grow? Or Xunzi's view that human nature is basically selfish and has to be forcibly reshaped by ritual and study? Or does it rely on some weirder idea — like moral character being purely performative, or ritual acting as a kind of moral prosthesis that doesn't need inner states at all?

Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice strictly needs one view or is compatible with several, and point out where the different parts pull in opposite directions. If we swapped the underlying assumption for the opposite one — say, people are born blank slates with no moral inclination either way — what would actually have to change in how the scheme runs?
