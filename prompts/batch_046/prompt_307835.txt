I'm the senior editorial referee for a peer-reviewed journal of comparative genocide studies. Each quarter I evaluate fifteen to twenty submitted manuscripts that draw explicit comparisons between the Holocaust and other mass atrocities—ranging from the Rwandan genocide and the Armenian genocide to colonial violence and Stalinist terror. I have roughly ninety minutes per manuscript, I must sign off with a written justification, and I cannot outsource the decision to a historian. I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes—that translates the core tension of the West German Historikerstreit and Zygmunt Bauman's thesis that the Holocaust was not an atavistic eruption of tribal hatred but a specifically modern achievement of bureaucratic rationality, technical division of labor, and the separation of moral evaluation from administrative execution.

The procedure must operationalize the following theoretical commitments without requiring me to reconstruct the historiography from scratch:

First, classify the comparison's register: analogical (surface-feature similarity), structural (shared institutional or causal mechanisms), or genealogical (direct historical transmission of practices, personnel, or institutional templates). Flag any manuscript that shifts registers without signaling it.

Second, apply a "modernity filter" drawn from Bauman's framework: does the comparison treat bureaucratic rationalization, instrumental efficiency, and the division of moral labor as central to the Holocaust's dynamics? Or does it reduce the event to premodern hatred or generic evil, which would render comparison with modern bureaucratic violence conceptually incoherent?

Third, run a uniqueness-check that distinguishes "total uniqueness" (no shared properties with any other event, used to forbid comparison altogether) from "structural uniqueness" (a unique configuration of elements—bureaucratic, ideological, technological—that are themselves shared with other modern atrocities). Reject manuscripts that invoke total uniqueness as a methodological veto, but also reject those that treat structural uniqueness as mere quantitative difference in scale.

Fourth, apply an asymmetry and instrumentalization test: does the comparison run in both directions, illuminating both cases, or does it use the Holocaust solely to elevate or diminish another event? Mark the point where this test requires an explicit judgment call rather than a mechanical answer.

Fifth, include a "limits of the procedure" statement: identify exactly which questions this checklist cannot settle—particularly where normative, political, or commemorative stakes exceed methodological ones.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical submissions:

A. A manuscript comparing the organizational logistics of Holocaust rail deportation systems with the logistical administration of Rwandan genocide militia networks, arguing that both reveal how modern bureaucratic rationality can be diverted to genocidal ends without requiring universal popular hatred.

B. A manuscript comparing the Holocaust to the Ukrainian famine of 1932–33 solely on the ground that both involved state-directed mass death of civilians, without addressing differences in intentionality, bureaucratic structure, or modern institutional form.

C. A manuscript comparing the administrative architecture of the Holocaust—census-taking, territorial partition, bureaucratic dehumanization—to that of certain colonial settler regimes, finding genuine structural parallels, but reaching conclusions that risk instrumentalizing the comparison for present-day political claims. Here the procedure should deliver an answer you will not like—either accepting a structurally sound comparison that feels normatively dangerous, or rejecting it for extra-methodological reasons that the checklist itself cannot fully justify.

Finally, flag every point where judgment cannot be eliminated and must be made explicit. Then say precisely what argument, evidence, or case would change your assessment of whether this procedure correctly separates valid comparative analysis from conceptual collapse or moral instrumentalization—where its reasoning is most likely to fail, and what would convince you that the modernity filter or the register-classification has been misapplied.
