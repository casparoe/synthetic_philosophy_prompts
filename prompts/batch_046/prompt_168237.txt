So I was running a grad seminar last week and two of my students, Maya and Jonas, got into it over whether germs, climate shifts, or volcanoes can count as "historical actors." Their back-and-forth was sharp but I think they kept talking past each other, and I'd like you to map exactly where each is right and wrong.

Maya kicked things off by saying that calling a virus or a climate event an "actor" is basically a sloppy metaphor. She argued that the concept is meant to pick out beings with practical reason and the ability to choose otherwise, and if you drop that requirement you're not broadening agency—you're just diluting it until it means "anything that causes change." She mentioned the Black Death and said sure, Yersinia pestis reshaped labor markets, but describing the bacterium as an "actor" with "interests" makes it sound like there was a strategy behind the mortality, which ends up hiding the social structures that actually determined who died and who survived.

Jonas pushed back hard. He said reserving "actor" for humans is just leftover human exceptionalism, and that evolutionary biology already talks about viral "strategies" and climate "forcing" without anyone thinking we've sneaked minds into the picture. He claimed that treating microbes as agents with their own evolutionary timelines actually helps resist environmental determinism, because it frames human societies as interacting with other dynamic, responsive systems rather than being passive victims of weather patterns.

Maya wasn't buying it. She said there's a real distinction between functional explanation and agential explanation, and she introduced this idea she calls "causal salience versus narrative agency"—you can grant that a volcanic eruption in 1816 had massive causal salience without writing it into the historical narrative as if it had intentions or stakes. She accused Jonas of conflating determinism with mono-causality; the real peril, she said, isn't saying non-humans cause things, it's framing them as protagonists so that human responsability gets diffused into "the climate did it."

Jonas said she was hiding behind a tecnicality. He pointed out that if we ban all agential language for non-humans, then we can't talk about symbiosis, co-evolution, or epidemiological feedback loops without reducing everything to dead mechanism. He asked whether she really thinks the gut microbiome is just a passive environment, or whether it has its own kind of distributed agency that actively shapes host behavior and decision-making.

Maya replied that distributed agency is still agency, and she wasn't denying interaction—she was denying that interaction equals historical acting. She warned that once you start writing history from the volcano's point of view, you slide into a kind of environmental determinism where social conflicts get explained away by aerosol loads and temperature curves, and human choices disappear from the story.

Then Jonas asked a pretty sharp question: if agency really requires practical reason, then are infants or severely disabled adults excluded? And if not, then her boundary is already porous, so why shouldn't we extend it to complex adaptive systems that exhibit goal-directed behavior?

Maya said that was a reductio that missed the point—those humans still belong to the moral community and can be represented by others, whereas volcanoes can't be wronged or held accountable. So importing agential language for them isn't just imprecise; it's a catagory error with real historiographical consequences, because it changes what kinds of explanations we find satisfying.

Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who's right at each step—not just who wins overall? Also say what, if anything, both parties are missing. And please, no need for citations or reading lists; I just want the conceptual map, even if it's a little messy.
