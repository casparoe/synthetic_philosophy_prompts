A philosophy blogger I follow recently summarized the three classic responses to the epistemic regress problem in a post about whether justification is even possible. According to the blogger, foundationalists halt the regress by identifying basic beliefs that are justified without being supported by further reasons; these beliefs can still be false, so the system is structurally stable even if not guaranteed to be true. Coherentists, in this account, reject linear justification altogether and replace it with a web of mutual support: beliefs are justified when they cohere tightly with one another and the system is free of internal contradiction, with no belief anchored outside the web. Infinitists are presented as accepting linear justification but denying that it ever terminates: for every belief there must be a further reason behind it, stretching backward in an infinite, non-repeating chain, and a belief counts as justified only if this actual infinite chain exists.

The blogger then offers three critical claims. First, that infinitists are therefore committed to the psychological impossibility of human justification, since no mind can actually traverse or hold an infinite chain of reasons. Second, that coherentists are not solving the regress but merely disguising circular reasoning with complexity, because every belief still ultimately depends on others within the same closed system. Third, that foundationalists avoid both difficulties only by arbitrarily declaring certain beliefs immune to challenge, with no non-circular criterion for selecting which ones qualify. From these premises, the blogger concludes that all three positions fail and that the regress problem demonstrates that the very concept of epistemic justification is incoherent.
