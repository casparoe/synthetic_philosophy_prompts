Many secondary-school history curricula in the United States and in several post-Soviet states organize their Cold War origins units around a single evaluative question—“which side started it?”—and grade student essays partly on how clearly they assign primary blame to one state actor. The textbooks that serve this model present diplomatic cables, economic statistics, and military deployments as evidence for or against a single agent’s intentional initiation, while treating structural explanations—security dilemmas, misperception loops, institutional momentum—as background context rather than competing causal accounts. What philosophical theory of historical explanation and collective moral responsibility does this pedagogical practice presuppose? Must it assume an intentionalist, agent-centered model of international causation, or could a structural realist license the same grading rubric? Does the practice implicitly commit to a view on which collective entities like states can bear blame analogously to individuals, and if so, what theory of group agency does that require? Reconstruct the implicit framework, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits well with, and describe what a curriculum built on emergent-system causation would have to change—not only in the content it teaches, but in how student answers are evaluated.
