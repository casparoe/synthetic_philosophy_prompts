Here's an argument my study partner cooked up and now I can't sleep because the conclusion feels like a hostage situation. (1) Bell's theorem only bites if the hidden variables are statisticaly independant of the measurement settings — that's the "freedom of choise" assumption. (2) If we drop that, we can keep locality; the correlations are just pre-arranged by a common cause that also fixed the settings. That's superdeterminism. (3) But superdeterminism doesn't just apply to particle detectors. If my choise of setting A and your choise of setting B can both be predetermined to match some cosmic λ, then so can any pair of human decisions. (4) So either we reject measurement independence everywhere — which kills free will — or we keep it everywhere, which forces us to reject locality even for macroscopic objects. (5) Nobody actually thinks my picking tea over coffee is spookily linked to your picking socks over sandals. (6) So we must be implicitly assuming measurement independence holds for ordinary life. (7) But then we're applying a double standard: we allow the assumption for humans but deny it for particles to save locality, or deny it for particles but then have to deny free will. (8) The only escape is that Bell's derivation smuggles in something that works in physics labs but collapses outside them — maybe an equivocation between statistical independence and causal independence. Where exactly does the slide happen? I'd rather keep (5) than (8), but I want to know the price tag.
