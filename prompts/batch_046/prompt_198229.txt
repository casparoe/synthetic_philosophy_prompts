Question: In the literature on color-phi people distinguish Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions. Explain the core difference, then say whether either view actually forces us to accept that conscious awareness is always running ~200ms behind the world.

Student answer:
Sure. The Orwellian story gets its name from 1984-style record editing. Your brain genuinely registers red at spot A then green at spot B in real time, but then it retroactively rewrites the trace of the first experience, replacing it with a smooth-motion experience that includes some blended middle color. So there's an honest first draft that gets corrected. The Stalinesque story, named after Stalin's show trials, denies any honest first draft. The brain waits until it has all the data, then manufactures the whole sequence—motion, intermediate shade, and all—presenting it with backdated timestamps so it feels live. So Orwellian is editing after publication; Stalinesque is publishing a fabricated transcript.

People usually discuss this with the phi illusion, where two colored dots flash in quick succession. Both explanations agree there's a fixed procesing latency of about 200-300ms, so yes, both commit us to saying awareness is always delayed behind physical reality by that window. But I actually think Stalinesque preserves immediate awareness, since on that view the experience is genuinely present right when it's staged, whereas Orwellian splits consciousness into a raw first draft and a corrected version, which sounds like a Cartesian theater with an audience watching both. So if the worry is delay, Stalinesque is the better option, even though both accept the same lag.
