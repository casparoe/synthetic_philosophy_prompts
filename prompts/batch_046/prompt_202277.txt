I am proposing an activity for our school philosophy club—fourteen students, mostly sixteen or seventeen, one fifty-five-minute session after classes—to make us feel, rather than just discuss, the tension between Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness, specifically around whether small white lies are morally wrong. I want the exercise to avoid cultural tourism; instead, please construct it around a synthetic framework I will call the Tri-Account Protocol, in which every utterance must be priced in at least two of three invisible ledgers—Truth, Harmony, and Autonomy—so that participants discover for themselves whether a stable middle ground exists.

Please design a structured simulation with assigned roles: some participants operating under “bespreekbaar” norms that treat open disagreement as respect, others under “kuuki” norms that treat implicit repair as moral labor, and neutral arbitrators who must render binding judgments without appealing to either culture. Give us a concrete, shared decision task—whether a peer’s sincerely held but clearly erroneous view should be corrected in public—plus rules, materials, and a penalty scheme that uses no real money but creates genuine social stakes (for example, being required to defend the opposing position during the debrief). The incentives should be arranged so that directness damages collective reasoning, indirectness erodes trust, and arbitrators face an incommensurable choice, all before anyone names the paradox.

Include a timeline that lets the conflict emerge organically, then a debrief that maps each moment onto the philosophical question: where could a step be resisted, and what does resistance cost in terms of the three accounts? Be explicit about which feature of the problem—perhaps the absence of a culture-neutral arbitration point, or the impossibility of optimizing all three ledgers simultaneously—the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. Also tell me what will predictably fail the first time we run it and how to correct for it. No lecture should occur up front; participants should learn the Tri-Account Protocol’s name only after they have lived inside it, and please do not ask us to provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—this must work entirely from the rules and roles you set out.
