here's a rough sketch for an essay i'm trying to write and i'd like your take on whether it's actually coherent or just sounds smart. the working title is "Jaspers, inherited culpability, and the carbon-debt analogy" — probably too cute, but whatever.

the core idea: Jaspers splits guilt into criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. political guilt sticks to citizens qua members of a state, but it feels weird to say "Germany in 2024 is politically guilty for 1941" if the state has been rebuilt, the citizens replaced, etc. so people either drop the claim or water it down to "liability" or "responsibility for repair."

i want to push that this watering-down misses something, but not in the usual "collective soul" direction. instead i'm treating intergenerational national guilt like ecological debt — not that the present generation "feels bad" in a reactive-attitude way, but that they occupy a structural position of unearned benefit derived from past wrong, and that this creates an obligation that behaves like debt rather than like blame. but i'm worried this just collapses moral agency into causation: carbon doesn't choose, nations (sort of) do, but over time they don't.

then there's the identity problem. asking "is the nation guilty" assumes the nation persists like a person. but a nation is more like the ship of Theseus sustained by legal narratives and institutional continuity. so maybe the right question isn't "is Germany guilty" but "under which identity conditions does the predicate apply?" and if we pick loose conditions, guilt transfers easily but becomes trivial; if we pick strict ones, it never transfers and we lose the intuition that something is owed.

the part i'm least sure about is metaphysical guilt. Jaspers describes it as solidarity with human failure in general, not tied to specific acts. i think this might be the only kind that genuinely survives across generations, but if so it's not specifically national — it's just "being human after atrocity." so the essay might end up arguing that specifically national intergenerational guilt is either structural debt (not real guilt) or metaphysical solidarity (not specifically national). that's a deflationary conclusion, but maybe that's okay.

questions for you: does the carbon-debt anology do real philosophical work here, or is it just a metaphor that hides the lack of an agent? does the ship-of-Theseus move actually help, or does it sidestep Jaspers' point that political guilt attaches to the state-structure regardless of personnel? and am i misreading Jaspers on whether metaphysical guilt can be collectively inherited, or is it strictly contemporaneous? also, is the whole thing just avoiding the obvious answer that nations can bear liability without guilt, and dressing that up?

feel free to be harsh on the structure — i haven't written the actual draft yet, just these notes, and there might be a hidden commitment to methodological individualism i'm not seeing. typos in my notes are mine, not yours.
