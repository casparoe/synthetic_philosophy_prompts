I am preparing questions for a podcast interview with the author of a recent monograph—let us call her Dr. Voss—who argues that what she terms the paradox of deontology dissolves once we stop treating moral law as a regulative constraint on pre-existing agents and instead understand it as a constitutive framework for rational agency itself, comparable to the rules that make chess chess rather than merely limiting how one moves pieces across a board. Her central claim is that deontological duties do not compete with welfare outcomes in a calculus; rather, to violate them is not merely to do wrong but to perform an action that, like an illegal chess move, falls outside the very practice it purports to belong to. I want my questions to press precisely where the literature presses—on the problem of catastrophic scenarios in which rigid adherence to duty appears to destroy all conditions for rational agency, leaving nothing but a formal consistency that has emptied itself of content—while anticipating the standard deflections and following up on them. Specifically, I need a sequence that begins by asking how her constitutive reading handles the classic case in which telling the truth to a murderer at the door seems to annihilate the rational being the moral law was meant to honor, then moves to the reply that such hypotheticals are empirically remote, and finally reaches a question about what kind of conceptual or practical development could genuinely count against her view rather than being absorbed as a further refinement of it.

Please construct for me a polished sequence of interview questions that tracks this arc without allowing her to retreat into the claim that extreme counterexamples are merely "research programs, not objections." The questions should turn on the actual mechanics of her position: the relationship between universalizability and self-annihilation, whether moral psychology can survive a framework that renders catastrophic outcomes conceptually external to agency, and whether a community that systematically treated moral rules as merely regulative could, in principle, demonstrate greater rational autonomy than one bound by her constitutive account. I am not looking for generic biography or for requests that she cite specific texts; I want pointed, substantive follow-ups that force the defense to do real philosophical work in real time. End with one question about what evidence—conceptual, historical, or phenomenological—would cause her to abandon or substantially revise the constitutive thesis, phrased so that she cannot answer it by redescribing the challenge as part of the view's internal development.
