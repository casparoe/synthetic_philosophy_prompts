I'm drafting a scene for my novel where a philosopher gets a little drunk at a dinner party and starts arguing about whether you really owe more to your neighbors than to strangers half a world away.

Theres this pattern I keep runing into in real essays that I'm trying to capture, but I don't have the word for it -- if there even is one. A writer will say nationality is just an accident of birth, morally no different from being left-handed, so it can't justify special obligations to compatriots. Then, same essay, they'll defend closed borders or local welfare states by saying people naturally care more about those they share a culture with, and you can't build solidarity without that shared feeling. So they deny nationality has any moral weight, but then use the psychological fact of national attachment -- which is partly created and kept alive by those very borders and welfare states -- to justify keeping the structures in place. I've seen the same loop with immigration debates and with tax policy. It's not just hypocrasy; it feels like a specific kind of feedback loop where you reject a bias as a normative reason, then invoke that same bias as a practical constraint that only exists because of the institutions you're defending.

Does this have an established name in the nationalism versus cosmopolitanism literature? If not, can you coin one and draw its boundaries -- how is it different from ordinary pragmatic compromise or from adaptive preference, and does it apply only to nationality or to any case where someone denies X has intrinsic value but defends X-preserving institutions on pragmatic grounds? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
