I'm auditing a graduate seminar on literary translation theory, and we've spent several sessions on Douglas Hofstadter's 1997 study Le Ton beau de Marot, which takes as its central case the challenge of rendering Clément Marot's brief poem Ma mignonne into English without sacrificing rhyme, meter, or tonal warmth. The instructor proposed a framing idea that I have not encountered in standard textbooks—something she called structural parasitism—where a formal constraint such as rhyme or meter does not merely decorate meaning but actively governs which semantic options are available, almost as if the form were an organism selecting its content. I am trying to determine whether this framing is genuinely illuminating, and more importantly, what it implies for how we should evaluate translations that are not self-conscious literary experiments but ordinary acts of cross-linguistic communication.

Please describe the most important implications of treating formal poetic constraints as structurally parasitic rather than as superficial ornaments, especially for the assessment of whether a translation has succeeded. If rhyme and meter restrict lexical choice so severely that they determine which metaphors, emotional registers, or logical relationships can be expressed, does preserving the form amount to preserving a hidden layer of semantic content, or does it instead risk substituting the conceptual landscape available in the target language for that of the original? Furthermore, what does this tension suggest about the translation of authoritative texts—legal preambles, liturgical recitations, or constitutional language—that depend on rhythmic parallelism or sonic repetition for their performative force, when those formal patterns have no direct equivalent in the language into which they must be rendered?
