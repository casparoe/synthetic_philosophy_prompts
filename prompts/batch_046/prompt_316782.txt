Design a 75-minute seminar activity for 14 philosophy undergraduates that makes them feel the pressure of Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument without naming it until the debrief. Split the group into panels of three or four and give each panel a printed case file describing a morally weighty act—say, a large anonymous donation or a calculated betrayal—along with a deck of “origin cards.” The rules: the panel must decide whether the agent is truly morally responsible (in the sense that genuinely justifies praise or blame) by tracing the causal origin of the act. They begin with the agent’s choice; to maintain responsibility for that choice they must claim responsibility for the character or reasoning behind it, then draw a card for the origin of that character, and continue. Include a “Self-Reform” card a panel may play to claim the agent previously reshaped their own nature, but playing it forces them to apply the same tracing to the origins of that reform effort—its motivation, capacity, and circumstances. The deck contains only cards pointing to heredity, environment, prior conditions, or social context; there is no “uncaused cause” card. Panels earn a point only if they can declare a stopping point that is genuinely self-originated; otherwise they accumulate “unassigned causation.” The incentive structure should push them to want to stop at rational agency or willpower, only to discover that every proposed stopping point can be pushed back one step further. After all panels have reached an impasse or conceded, the facilitator reveals the argument.

In the debrief, map each round of play onto Strawson’s sequence—choice, character, self-formation, conditions of self-formation, and ultimately heredity and environment. Identify the predictable failure point: a panel will try to halt the regress by appealing to “they could have done otherwise,” and the facilitator should press them on the origin of that very capacity. Be explicit about what the card game cannot reproduce—the metaphysical claim that true responsibility requires being causa sui, which is impossible—and state that the facilitator must supply this directly. Also be frank that the activity offers no legitimate compatibilist stopping point by design, so it cannot show where a compatibilist reply might succeed; the facilitator should note this limitation rather than pretend the game has refuted it. The deliverable should require only printed cards and case files, with no outside reading.
