I teach an upper-level philosophy of action course and I am writing the final exam. I want one essay question on the absent-minded driver problem that cannot be answered by reciting standard summaries of Davidson on basic action or Anscombe on conditions of intentionality. Embed the following constructed case and theoretical framing in the prompt itself: a driver sets out with an explicit, occurrent intention to drive to his office, but his habitual home-route diverges from the office route only at a late junction. At that junction he takes the home turn without conscious awareness, continues for several minutes, then notices a familiar landmark, realizes the error, and immediately corrects course. Crucially, upon reflection he insists he was "going home all along" despite having formed no explicit intention to do so and having actively resisted that idea before leaving. Ask examinees to evaluate whether this modified case supports a "branch-sensitive" account of action-individuation—according to which the very same initial bodily movement counts as the action of going-to-the-office up to the junction and as the distinct action of going-home after it, with no single continuous intention governing the whole sequence—or whether it instead shows that intentional action can be sustained by a purely dispositional or sub-personal intention that operates without any occurrent representational state. Require them to address three specific issues: (1) whether the driver's post-hoc report can be treated as authoritative evidence of an earlier intention; (2) whether a two-layer model of habitual motor control plus supervisory planning can consistently individuate actions across the branch point without collapsing into behaviorism; and (3) what the case implies for the principle that an action is the kind of thing it is in virtue of the agent's reasons rather than in virtue of its causal etiology. The question should be answerable in about 90 minutes and should require conceptual analysis rather than historical exposition. Provide a model answer of approximately 650 words in continuous prose, and provide a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totaling 20 marks: (i) clear reconstruction of the branch-point problem (4 marks); (ii) evaluation of the authority of post-hoc reports (4 marks); (iii) assessment of the two-layer model and its risk of behaviorism (4 marks); (iv) engagement with the reasons-vs-causation principle (4 marks); and (v) a coherent verdict on whether dispositional intention or branch-sensitive individuation (or neither) best handles the case (4 marks). For each point, include a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks—e.g., treating the problem as merely about "unconscious intentions" without addressing individuation, or conflating causal explanation with rationalizing explanation. Separate an A answer (which evaluates the coherence of branch-sensitive individuation and identifies a specific tension in the two-layer model, such as the problem of attributing contradictory reasons to a single continuous agent) from a B answer (which describes the case accurately and mentions the right distinctions but fails to press the individuation issue). Do not ask examinees to provide citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts; the assessment must depend entirely on argument and conceptual clarity.
