I have been thinking on my own about a framework that divides minds into four kinds: those shaped only by natural selection, those shaped by reward and punishment, those able to imagine and test possibilities internally before acting, and those that use language and culture to inherit ideas. I think the framework overcounts. The fourth kind is not a genuinely distinct cognitive architecture, but merely the third kind operating with cultural tools. Language and tradition amplify and distribute internal-testing capacity without creating a new kind of mind; the real boundary lies between minds that can run mental simulations and minds that cannot. If I am right, then capacities usually called Gregorian should always reduce to enhanced Popperian ones, and any being that lacks mental simulation should be incapable of genuine cultural learning regardless of its communicative abilities. What would count against this position? I want the arguments and observations that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive they would be: evidence of language-using beings that clearly lack mental simulation, cases in which cultural transmission operates without internal hypothesis-testing, or formal results showing that cultural inheritance requires a representational architecture separate from simulation. I also want to know what my view predicts that the standard four-part framework does not, whether I have insulated it by allowing that culture modifies the third kind without being a separate kind, and whether that insulation renders the view unfalsifiable in a damaging way. Please include an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything concrete, and tell me specifically which argument or finding would change your mind about it, as well as where my reasoning is most likely to fail.
