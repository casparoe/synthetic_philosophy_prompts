Describe the following argumentative pattern I keep encountering in discussions of historical moral change, and tell me whether philosophy of history has a recognized name for it, or whether existing labels miss something essential. Give your analysis in prose, then summarize your verdict in a structured table.

The pattern works like this. A speaker explains long-term improvements in political or legal institutions—say, the gradual abolition of torture, the expansion of suffrage, or the development of international humanitarian norms—not by appealing to divine providence, historical laws, or an inevitable endpoint, but by citing local, adaptive mechanisms: courts resolving particular disputes case by case, social movements copying tactics that succeeded elsewhere, norms spreading through proximity and imitation. The speaker insists this is not teleological; there is no arc bending toward justice, no final cause, only piecemeal problem-solving with no predetermined direction.

Yet in the same breath, the speaker treats the present as farther along than the past, describes earlier societies as lagging behind or being on the wrong side of history, and assigns present agents an obligation to continue the trajectory. These judgments presuppose a normative direction that the mechanistic account explicitly denies. I have seen this in defenses of incremental constitutional development that reject originalism as backward, and in narratives that present human rights as contingent political achievements while simultaneously treating them as inevitable progress. Is there an established concept that captures precisely this tension—between a non-directional causal story about institutional change and a directional normative story about where history should go? If there is, explain how it applies and where it falls short; if there is not, coin a precise term, define it, and provide two boundary cases that separate it from Whig history, from naive progressivism, and from ordinary inconsistency.

Please do not provide a bibliography or reading list. I want the conceptual carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
