For an advanced philosophy elective I am taking as a high school senior, I am constructing a comparative analysis of Derek Parfit's hitchhiker dilemma, specifically the tension between causal decision theory and the broader puzzle of rational irrationality. Rather than asking for a general summary, I would like you to rank the following five interpretive stances from the most philosophically defensible to the least defensible in resolving whether it can genuinely be rational to cultivate an irrational disposition, and for each provide a brief explanation of its decisive strength or weakness. The stances are: first, the canonical causal-decision-theoretic defense that treats the present disposition to pay as causally efficacious for being rescued; second, the evidential-decision-theoretic objection that one's current reasoning reveals information about one's agent-type and thereby undermines the very rescue prospects it seeks to secure; third, a modified evolutionary account in which the driver can read not merely your conscious intentions but your deep evolutionary history of reciprocal altruism, so that cultivating a genuinely uncooperative disposition requires altering your biological baseline rather than merely changing your mind; fourth, the austere instrumentalist claim that rationality is always about maximizing expected utility and that any self-modification toward unreliability is therefore definitionally irrational, regardless of whether it happens to secure rescue; and fifth, a hypothetical scenario I have developed in which the hitchhiker is a distributed artificial agent whose "payment" is enforced not by moral obligation but by cryptographic consensus across nodes, so that local irrationality—corrupting one node to withhold payment—simultaneously fractures the agent's functional identity. Please treat all five seriously, but indicate especially where the fifth case either illuminates or distorts Parfit's original concern about the coherence of being rationally irrational.
