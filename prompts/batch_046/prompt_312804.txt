Design a 90-minute seminar activity for ten postgraduate philosophy students that makes them feel the strategic force of program equilibrium rather than merely grasp it conceptually. Each student should write a short “decision programme” on an index card—an explicit conditional rule that reads another student’s card and then outputs either Cooperate or Defect—before all cards are shuffled and paired at random. Structure three rounds: first with hidden source code (Nash-style reasoning), second with full transparency before pairing (program-equilibrium-style mutual inspection), and third with a hidden “observer” card that can read every programme but cannot itself be read in return. The rules and pairing protocol must be arranged so that participants discover the shift in reasoning themselves: defensive isolation in round one, self-referential cooperation in round two, and the collapse of mutual transparency in round three. Provide the exact card-template wording, the shuffling and pairing mechanics, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps each round onto the formal distinction between Nash equilibrium and program equilibrium, identifies what will predictably go wrong during facilitation and how to manage it, and includes an honest admission of which feature of the formal model—unbounded computational inspection of arbitrarily complex source code—cannot be reproduced with paper cards, together with how you will supply that missing element verbally. The answer should be written in British English.
