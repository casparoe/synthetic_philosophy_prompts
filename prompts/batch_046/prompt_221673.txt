My friend and I just finished a long argument about how consciousness should be assessed in clinical settings, and I want to pin down the philosophical stakes precisely. Consider a neurointensive-care unit that institutes a formal protocol: it determines whether an unresponsive patient is conscious solely by computing an integrated-information metric from intracranial neural recordings, and it systematically discounts all behavioral evidence—including responses facilitated by brain-computer interfaces. What view of consciousness, and of the epistemic access we have to other minds, does that institutional practice presuppose? Does it implicitly commit to the claim that phenomenal consciousness is identical to integrated information, or can it consistently treat the metric as a merely reliable proxy while remaining agnostic about the hard problem? Moreover, is the protocol philosophically consistent with the same unit's practice of administering analgesia based on behavioral and physiological pain indicators rather than on that same information-theoretic measure? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysical and epistemological theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, indicate where it is underdetermined between competing accounts—such as global-workspace or higher-order-thought theories—and specify exactly which elements of the protocol would need to be revised if one replaced its underlying commitment with one of those alternatives.
