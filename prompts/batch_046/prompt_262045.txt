I work as a protocol referee for a consortium of independent research groups that periodically face a collective resource decision: whether to contribute computing and personnel to a shared pool or retain them locally. The groups are rational, know one another are rational, and operate under fully transparent payoff structures, but they vary in how much they can observe about each other’s internal decision architectures—what I have come to call differences in epistemic transparency rather than simple payoff symmetry. I want to turn the principle of superrationality—the idea that agents who genuinely share a common reasoning structure should be able to converge on cooperation not through bargaining but through recognizing they will mirror each other’s decision—into a reusable audit procedure I can run before each round. The procedure must be usable by referees with no background in formal game theory, so it should take the form of an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes: at each stage, what observation or answer is required to proceed, where the answer forces a stop with a recommendation of non-cooperation or arbitration, and where the question is irreducibly one of values rather than structural facts and must be flagged for human judgment. Crucially, it must operationalize a distinction I have found groups constantly blur: first-order symmetry, meaning identical payoff matrices, versus second-order symmetry, meaning identical reasoning about each other’s reasoning capacities, because in my experience parties invoke superrational cooperation whenever payoffs align, even when their decision architectures are opaque or heterogeneous to one another.

Please construct this procedure, including a small set of concrete test questions a referee can pose to the parties or observe in documentation, and specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (a) two groups with identical payoffs and full transparency into each other’s reasoning algorithms; (b) two groups with identical payoffs but where one group knows the other employs a different heuristic for reasoning about reciprocity; and (c) a scenario where payoffs are slightly asymmetric—one group loses substantially more by cooperating—yet both parties claim they share a “superrational commitment to open science” that should override the asymmetry. Case (c) should be the one in which the procedure yields a recommendation the parties will find unwelcome. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle—for example, whether it can adjudicate disagreements about whether two reasoning architectures are genuinely isomorphic, whether it scales beyond two parties, or whether it applies when agents can alter their architectures between rounds—and mark those boundaries as hard limits rather than temporary gaps. Keep everything free of citations, bibliographies, or references to specific named theorems; the deliverable should be a practical instrument, not a literature review.
