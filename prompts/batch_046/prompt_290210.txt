Since retiring from experimental physiology I have begun reading philosophy of decision theory, and a fellow amateur recently summarised Herbert Simon's idea of satisficing for me. I should be grateful for a point-by-point audit of its fairness.

He said that, because human cognition is bounded, we do not maximise utility but halt our search once an option exceeds a fixed aspiration level; that this is essentially optimisation conducted under an information budget, not a genuinely different decision rule; that Simon introduced it chiefly to explain bureaucratic behaviour in large organisations; and that only agents with explicit intentional states can satisfice, so the idea cannot extend to thermostats or to simple organisms. From this he concluded that satisficing poses no real challenge to rational-choice theory, but merely notes practical computational limits.

Please examine these claims in turn: which accurately capture Simon's view, which subtly distort it—particularly regarding whether aspiration levels adapt, whether satisficing is structurally distinct from constrained maximisation, and whether Simon confined the idea to bureaucracy or to intentional minds—and whether the final inference actually follows. I am also curious whether the analogy I have drawn, between Simon's aspiration-level adjustment and the shifting set-points of physiological homeostasis, gives any reason to apply satisficing to non-cognitive systems, or whether Simon's own writings on organisations and artificial computation already permit such an extension.
