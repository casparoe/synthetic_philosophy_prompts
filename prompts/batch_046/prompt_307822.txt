Before I evaluate any particular account of human death—brain-death, circulatory-death, higher-brain, or integrated-function—I want the full desiderata specification: what a philosophical theory of human death must actually deliver to count as adequate, not merely legally convenient.

Structure your answer as a requirements list, with weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing views.

For each desideratum, provide three elements:
1. The phenomenon or boundary case it must handle correctly (e.g., anencephalic infants who never possessed consciousness; locked-in patients with intact brainstem arousal but absent cortical integration; brain-dead bodies sustained with technological support that successfully gestate a fetus to term; decapitated non-human organisms that persist for days; metabolically arrested but structurally intact brains in suspended animation).
2. The conceptual distinction the theory must preserve rather than collapse (e.g., death of the organism versus death of the person; irreversible cessation versus currently undetectable recovery; biological integration versus technological substitution; legal declaration versus metaphysical fact).
3. The biological or medical result with which the theory must remain consistent (e.g., that consciousness requires thalamocortical integration coupled with brainstem arousal systems; that brain-dead bodies can maintain temperature, immune response, and fetal gestation via extracorporeal support; that multicellular organisms exist without centralized nervous systems).

Then classify each desideratum as either non-negotiable or camp-specific preference, with brief reasoning. Identify any subset you believe is jointly unsatisfiable given current biology—for instance, whether preserving the organism/person distinction forces the conclusion that a brain-dead pregnant body remains a living organism, which in turn conflicts with treating brain death as the death of the human being itself.

Finally, address one niche constraint that standard debates usually neglect: the theory must account for a case (observed or carefully hypothesized) in which a human body has lost all brain function, receives no mechanical ventilation or cardiac support, yet retains spinal-reflex-mediated homeostatic loops—localized temperature regulation, wound contraction, isolated immune signaling—that persist for hours or days. Must such a body be classified as dead, alive, or as breaking the binary? Explain why this case tests the underlying concept of “integrated vital function” rather than merely testing technological dependency.

If you wish, score brain-death, circulatory-death, higher-brain, and integrated-function theories against the list in no more than a brief paragraph or table, but do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Keep the focus on what the theory has to get right.
