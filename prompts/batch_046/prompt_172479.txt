I was arguing with my friend about whether Athenian democracy had any coherent philosophy behind it, not just politics, and I keep circling back to three institutional moments that feel like they share a hidden logic. There's the Mytilene debate, where the assembly votes to execute every male citizen of the rebel city, then literally sends a second trireme the next morning to cancel the order after Cleon's speech makes them feel guilty. There's the Sicilian Expedition decision, where thousands vote to invade Sicily despite clear warnings that the fleet is underprepared, partly because Alcibiades frames it as a test of Athenian courage and honor. And then there's Socrates' trial, where 501 jurors vote death after he refuses to propose a counter-penalty and basically lectures them about their own ignorance. These aren't just random failures; they're the same practice operating in war, foreign policy, and criminal justice.

I'm trying to figure out what philosophical commitments this institutional design actually presupposes, if any. Does it require the view that emotional arousal in a crowd is a kind of distributed wisdom rather than a bias to be filtered out? That moral responsibility for collective violence is so fully diluted across thousands of voters that reversing the Mytilene decision doesn't require anyone to admit personal error? And does the trial of Socrates show an implicit epistemology - that democratic knowledge is produced through competitive public speech in front of the assembly, which makes private philosophical questioning of that authority a capital crime?

I'm also stuck on an inconsistency: if the assembly's immediate emotional verdict is authoritative, why build in reversal within 24 hours? If collective judgment is trustworthy only when heated, why trust it at all for long-term strategy like Sicily? And is there a consistent theory of blame that explains punishing generals for losing battles while also treating the assembly's own war decisions as beyond individual reproach?

Please separate what's pretty well settled about these Athenian procedures from what's genuinely contested among people who study ancient political philosophy, and don't present one interpretation as if it's the consensus. Write in American English, please - and I'm especially curious whether there's one implicit theory that covers all three, or if the practice is just philosophically incoherent and needs different explanations for each case. Also, what would this practice have to change if it replaced its emotional-deliberation model with something closer to modern expert-panel decision making?
