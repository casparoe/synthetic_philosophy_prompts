Compare moral presentism and conceptual presentism in the practice of historiography. Moral presentism judges historical actors by contemporary ethical standards; conceptual presentism interprets past beliefs and practices through the categorical frameworks of the present. Then consider a less-discussed variant—call it question-structure presentism—in which a historian avoids explicit moral judgment and resists anachronistic concepts, yet still frames her inquiry around problems, distinctions, and explanatory demands that are only visible within current disciplinary boundaries. How does this structural form differ from the other two? And does its presence mean that the aspiration to escape the standards of one's own time is not merely difficult but conceptually unstable?
