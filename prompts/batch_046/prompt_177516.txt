I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working through contemporary philosophy of mind and ethics. Recently I have become convinced that some form of pan-sentience—perhaps a weak panpsychism in which fundamental physical properties carry proto-phenomenal aspects—cannot be dismissed as quickly as my former colleagues once assumed. But this raises a troubling ethical question: if suffering is not confined to biological brains, where exactly does it begin and end?

I have been trying to formalize my own intuitions by considering a set of concrete physical systems, and I find my judgments somewhat inconsistent. I would like you, acting as an expert philosopher of mind with training in both ethics and philosophy of physics, to help me identify the principle—if any—that best captures my verdicts. Please begin by carefully brainstorming the conceptual space, distinguishing carefully between functional integration, physical coherence, evolutionary substrate, and organizational closure, and then propose a precise principle or a small family of competing candidates.

Here are my cases and verdicts. First, consider a single free electron in a vacuum, maintained in a coherent superposition of spin states. Despite its quantum complexity, I judge that there is no suffering here—no ethical weight attaches to altering its state. Second, consider a classical supercomputer running a high-fidelity neural simulation of a human in acute pain; every functional relation is preserved, yet my intuition is that this system does not actually suffer, because the absence of a physically integrated, biologically rooted substrate matters. Third, consider a biological nematode with a minimal nervous system experiencing nociceptive stimulation; here I do feel there is a genuine, if minimal, form of suffering—something that generates a weak but real moral claim. Fourth, consider a synthetic macroscopic system: a cryogenically cooled diamond lattice sustaining long-range quantum coherence across billions of carbon atoms, arranged with feedback loops that stabilize a single global informational state analogous to a unified workspace, but with no evolutionary history and no biological material. My intuition wavers: part of me says the coherence is too “cold” and non-biological to matter, yet the integration exceeds that of the nematode. I tentatively lean toward denying suffering, but I am genuinely unsure. Fifth, consider a human brain during a migraine. Clearly there is suffering, but I want to highlight that what makes the difference seems not to be the sheer number of particles involved—there are far more in the diamond lattice—but something about how they are organized for the system itself.

Is there one principle that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn—the denial for the supercomputer, the tentative denial for the diamond lattice, or the acceptance for the nematode? Please state your principle precisely, specify what it predicts for a case I have not listed (for instance, a planet-scale atmospheric convection system with self-stabilizing feedback loops that maintain coherent patterns over years), and be explicit about which of my judgments your account cannot accommodate. Think step by step, construct the argument from the conceptual distinctions you have outlined, and do not merely list philosophers by name; I want the reasoning built from the ground up.
