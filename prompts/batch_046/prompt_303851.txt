Here's an argument my study partner built off Avicenna's flying man and now I can't see the exit. (1) The suspended man knows he exists with no senses and no body-image, so self-awareness is purely reflexive - the soul is present to itself without any mediating represention. (2) If there's no mediation, there's no representational content that could misrepresent, so this knowledge is incorrigible. (3) Now take an occurent mental state, like thinking you're jealous. You can be aware of that in the same direct, first-person way, without observing your behavour. (4) So by parity, awareness of all your current mental contents is also direct and incorrigible. (5) That kills self-deception and unconscious motives entirely - you'd always correctly register what you're thinking. (6) But that's clearly false; people misread their feelings constantly. (7) So either Avicenna's point fails, or we have to deny psychoanalysis exists. I'd rather save (1) and (2) if possible, but (4) feels like the sleight of hand. Is the mistake treating intentional states like bare existence, or is there a hidden modal slide between (2) and (3)? Which step do I reject and what does it cost me?
