Here is the student's answer:

Galen Strawson's Basic Argument aims to show that moral responsibility in the basic desert sense—being genuinely deserving of blame or praise—is impossible. The argument begins with the idea that if you are responsible for what you do, you must be responsible for the mental states that produce your actions. But to be responsible for those mental states, you must have caused them through prior mental states for which you are also responsible. This produces a vicious regress: either it extends infinitely backward, or it stops at mental states—such as those shaped by genetics and environment—for which you are not responsible. So no one can be the ultimate source of their actions.

Strawson considers two escapes. First, determinism holds that all events are caused by prior events according to natural law. But this only deepens the problem, since your mental states are then fixed by factors outside your control. Second, indeterminism introduces randomness, perhaps via quantum fluctuations in neural processes. Yet randomness is not agency; an uncaused mental event is not something you do. Hence neither determinism nor indeterminism lets you be causa sui.

Strawson illustrates the point with a thought experiment involving a neuroscientist who programs a victim's brain to produce a specific criminal decision. Because every mental state leading to the act is externally caused, the victim is clearly not responsible. The Basic Argument generalizes this: even absent a scientist, our mental states are always products of prior causes outside our control.

Against this, compatibilists like Frankfurt propose that we can be responsible if we identify with our desires through second-order volitions. Strawson rejects this because identification is itself just another mental state in the causal chain. Moreover, Frankfurt's model requires an infinite hierarchy of volitions, and without a highest-order volition that is self-caused, the regress never finds a foundation. So hierarchical identification cannot establish basic desert responsibility.

It follows that since basic responsibility is impossible, moral practices of blame and punishment are entirely unjustified and should be replaced by purely instrumental social regulation.
