Two members of a political-philosophy workshop argued about whether the current U.S. government can meaningfully apologize for slavery and Jim Crow. Maya said yes: the state is an institution with diachronic continuity, not a collection of individuals, so it can acknowledge wrongs done in its name without attributing personal guilt to present citizens. The apology transforms the normative relationship because it recognizes that the institution—which still exercises authority over the affected communities—committed the wrong, and because the state still holds the accumulated advantages of that wrong. Leo said this confuses legal continuity with moral agency. A corporation apologizes for defects, but a state claiming collective culpability implicates citizens who never consented to the wrong; if I inherit my grandfather's stolen painting, I return it but do not apologize for his theft unless I endorse it. The state should provide material redress on grounds of distributive justice, but framing it as apology smuggles in false collective guilt.

Maya replied that Leo misses the distinction between guilt and institutional responsibility. Without the apologetic dimension, reparations become charity rather than justice, because they fail to address the specific moral injury of being wronged by this particular agent. Leo countered that this treats apology as an irreplaceable ritual, when accurate historical record, memorialization, and structural reform can address the injury without a performative utterance that risks being hollow. He added a further concern: given demographic and cultural transformation over generations, the "wronged community" addressed by the apology may no longer exist as the same collective entity, so the claim that apology restores "their" standing involves a questionable assumption about diachronic group identity.

Please go through the exchange point by point, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then say who is more right overall and identify what both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether an institutional apology requires the wronged party to maintain strict diachronic identity, and whether material redress and symbolic acknowledgment can be separated in the way Leo suggests.
