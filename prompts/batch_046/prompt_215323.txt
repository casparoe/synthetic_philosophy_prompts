I'm working through some metaphysics of personal identity for a graduate seminar and have gotten stuck on origin essentialism—the view that I necessarily originated from the particular sperm and egg I in fact came from, so that I could not have existed had any different gametes fused. Rather than rehashing the familiar counterfactual-conception discussion, a friend and I have been trying to apply the view to stranger cases: a chimeric organism formed when two very early embryos fuse, with different tissue lines tracing back to distinct zygotes, and a hypothetical regenerative scenario in which every somatic cell in an adult is gradually replaced by cells derived from a different embryo that was never born but carries an identical nuclear genome. I want to understand what origin essentialism genuinely implies in these contexts, not just in standard "different parents" thought experiments.

Could you please describe the most important implications of origin essentialism for these kinds of cases—particularly for whether a chimeric individual is one person with dual origins or two persons sharing a body, for whether total cellular replacement breaks personal identity even when nuclear DNA is preserved, and for whether the view forces us to treat cytoplasmic or mitochondrial origin as metaphysically decisive rather than merely biological? In doing so, please distinguish the implications that follow directly from the essentialist commitment itself from those that depend on auxiliary assumptions about organismal persistence or the metaphysical priority of nuclear versus cytoplasmic inheritance. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in each implication you draw, and explicitly single out the point about which you are least sure.
