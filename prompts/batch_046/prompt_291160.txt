You are being interviewed for a long-form feature I am writing on whether philosophy retains any structural role in contemporary science. Play a senior philosopher of science—not a named public figure, but a precise, intellectually bold proponent of an unconventional view. You hold that philosophy functions as a kind of conceptual immune system for science: it does not generate empirical predictions, but it detects hidden inconsistencies, ambiguous ontological commitments, and cross-domain contradictions before they harden into methodological dead ends. You are sharp rather than defensive; you freely concede that philosophy rarely produces data, and you reject the idea that every laboratory needs a philosopher present. You do insist, however, that when scientific theories are pushed to interpretive extremes—especially now, as AI-driven discovery systems propose molecular structures, mathematical conjectures, and physical hypotheses faster than human researchers can understand them—philosophical analysis becomes structurally necessary rather than ornamental.

Here is my opening challenge. The standard objections are familiar: Weinberg’s claim that philosophy offers working physicists nothing they cannot derive from mathematics and experiment; Hawking’s dismissal that philosophy failed to keep pace with modern physics and is effectively dead; and Feynman’s comparison of philosophers to observers who study flight without ever contributing to it. I want to move past these slogans. Today, large-scale models generate predictions that are empirically accurate yet conceptually opaque—what some call epistemic outsourcing. The danger is not simple error but a proliferation of correct results built on incoherent or mutually contradictory underlying commitments. I am asking you to defend your view with specificity. How, exactly, does your conceptual immune system operate when confronted with an AI-generated theory that works empirically but contains a hidden contradiction in its interpretation? Describe a concrete mechanism of philosophical audit—what it examines, how it intervenes, and what failure looks like if it is absent. If you can only offer generalities, why should I not conclude that philosophers are inventing jobs for themselves?
