I was discussing Van Fraassen's Reflection principle with a colleague in our philosophy of science seminar, and she offered the following summary: the principle requires that a rational agent's present credence in any proposition P must equal the mathematical expectation of her future credence in P, taken over all evidential situations she considers live possibilities. She presented this as essentially a diachronic Dutch Book argument—if your current credence diverges from your anticipated future credence, a bookie can construct an intertemporal betting scheme that guarantees you a loss. From this she inferred that Reflection makes genuine rational belief revision impossible: if you expected your credence in P to drop upon learning E, then your present expectation of that future state should already be that lower value, which means you ought already to hold the lower credence and therefore ought already to behave as though you possessed E. She added that critics, including David Lewis, rejected the principle precisely on these grounds, and she suggested that this is why Reflection is now largely of historical interest rather than live normative force.

Something in this account strikes me as too swift, especially the move from an expectation constraint to the conclusion that rational agents can never anticipate updating their beliefs, and the identification of Reflection with a Dutch Book argument rather than with a direct constraint on present rationality. I would like you to audit this characterization carefully: distinguish what accurately captures Van Fraassen's formulation, what is a defensible popularization, and what subtly misrepresents either the principle or the standard objections—particularly whether anticipating that one will revise is genuinely the same as having already revised. Furthermore, I want you to consider a somewhat less standard case: suppose an agent knows with certainty that she will shortly be subjected either to highly misleading testimony or to a cognitive enhancement that will alter her reasoning patterns in ways she presently regards as epistemically corrupting. Does Reflection still bind her present credence to those anticipated future values, or does the presence of anticipated corruption reveal a boundary condition of the principle? And if the principle does bind her, does that show that Reflection is overly demanding, or does it instead show that her colleague's summary has smuggled in an assumption that makes the principle appear more restrictive than it actually is?
