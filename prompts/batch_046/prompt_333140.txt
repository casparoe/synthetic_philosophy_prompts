In contemporary debates about transitional justice—particularly arguments over whether present-day democratic states should offer institutional reparations for colonial extraction or chattel slavery—interlocutors frequently invoke the maxim that "guilt is not inherited; only individuals can be guilty, never a collective." I recently encountered this deployed as a conversation-stopper against the claim that a modern state might bear what Karl Jaspers called "political guilt" for actions taken by its institutional predecessors, with the speaker treating the slogan as if it entailed a logical contradiction in attributing any form of responsibility to a collective body across generational change. I am convinced the phrase is performing ambiguous philosophical work, but I find myself unable to separate its possible meanings with sufficient precision.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses hiding in that one sentence? I am especially interested in four possible readings: a metaphysical claim about the conditions for moral agency (that only beings with present intentional mental states can properly bear guilt); a legal claim about criminal liability requiring a personal actus reus; Jaspers' own framework distinguishing criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, together with the question of which of these can be ascribed to collective entities that persist through structural continuity despite changing membership; and a pragmatic or strategic claim that attributing guilt to groups inevitably dissolves individual accountability. For each reading, say whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or actually false. Then tell me which interpretation the speaker in that reparations debate was most likely invoking to block the argument, whether that particular reading actually succeeds in undermining institutional responsibility, and how Jaspers' apparent willingness to allow that a nation might carry political liability—even in the absence of individual moral guilt—bears on the assessment.
