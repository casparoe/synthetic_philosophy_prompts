I am a retired experimental physicist who, in recent years, has turned my attention to the philosophy of science, particularly to the epistemic standing of theoretical frameworks in fundamental physics that appear to transcend direct empirical access. In working through this literature, I have been tracking the judgments of a particular philosopher of science—an author of recent essays on non-empirical confirmation—across a sequence of cases. His verdicts, as I understand them, are as follows.

First, a quantum-gravity proposal achieves full internal mathematical consistency and removes divergences present in its predecessor theory, yet produces no observationally distinguishable signatures at energies accessible to present or foreseeable instrumentation. His judgment: the proposal earns only a limited, derivative form of epistemic credit—credit for resolving an internal theoretical difficulty—but does not acquire the stronger warrant that attaches to predictive success.

Second, a multiverse cosmology accounts for the observed value of the cosmological constant through anthropic selection within a vast landscape of vacuum states, where neither the landscape itself nor the selection mechanism is subject to independent observational verification. His judgment: the account may serve as a consistency check, but it does not amount to empirical confirmation; the model should be held in epistemic suspension rather than affirmed.

Third, string theory possesses a unique ten-dimensional formulation that necessarily incorporates gravitation. Proponents cite this formal uniqueness as supporting evidence, despite the theory's failure to yield a unique four-dimensional vacuum matching observed physics. His judgment: uniqueness of formal structure supplies a distinct species of non-empirical support—structural coherence—but this support is defeasible and is undermined to the extent that the theory cannot recover the observed low-energy world.

Fourth, a cosmological model predicts spatial flatness and a specific spectral index for primordial fluctuations, predictions subsequently confirmed by cosmic microwave-background observations, even though the model also entails an unobservable multiverse sector. His judgment: the empirically confirmed predictive component confers support upon the model taken as a whole, including its unobservable entailments, but only on the condition that the predictive success is genuinely inseparable from the multiverse component rather than reproducible by an empirically equivalent alternative lacking that ontology.

Fifth, two competing theories share every observable prediction but differ in ontology: one invokes a single universe with finely tuned initial conditions, the other invokes a multiverse with randomly distributed conditions plus observer selection. The multiverse version is preferred solely on the ground that it eliminates the appearance of fine-tuning. His judgment: such a preference is methodologically permissible only if the avoidance of fine-tuning is explicitly treated as an explanatory virtue; if it is instead presented as empirical evidence, the preference collapses into an illegitimate metaphysical commitment.

These five judgments appear jointly consistent with more than one underlying rule. One rule might privilege predictive uniqueness and empirical risk; another might privilege internal problem-resolution and structural coherence; a third might draw a sharp boundary between empirical warrant and metaphysical or explanatory virtue, allowing the latter only when explicitly labeled.

Now I wish to apply this framework to a new case. The case involves a recently proposed framework—let us call it the modular-cell theory of quantum gravity—which postulates that spacetime consists of discrete modular cells whose collective dynamics reproduce general relativity in the continuum limit. The theory generates no deviation from classical gravitation at currently accessible energies. However, it resolves the black-hole information paradox by distributing information across cell-boundary relations in a manner that is, in principle, unobservable from any single asymptotic region. Moreover, the lattice combinatorics of the cells yield exactly one consistent value for the cosmological constant, and this derived value coincides with the observed value. The theory's advocates maintain that this coincidence, together with the resolution of the information paradox and the complete absence of free parameters, constitutes strong confirmation despite the lack of direct empirical signatures.

Given the pattern of verdicts above, what should the philosopher say about the modular-cell theory? Specifically, identify the competing rules that could consistently generate his earlier judgments, show how each rule produces a different verdict on this new case, and state which rule best coheres with the full sequence from the first to the fifth judgment. In addition, specify a further hypothetical case—one not yet considered—that would decisively distinguish the remaining viable interpretations. I am less interested in a single answer than in the rigorous mapping of principle to verdict.
