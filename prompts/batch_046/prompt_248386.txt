You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral theory and metaethics. Before answering, please think carefully and do some brief conceptual brainstorming: consider how moral principles are refined, how exceptions function in practical reasoning, and what separates one kind of context-sensitivity from another.

I just finished a long, draining argument with a close friend about whether moral life can ever be governed by rules. During it, I noticed a recurring argumentative rhythm that I do not have a name for, and I want to know whether philosophy already has one.

Here is the pattern in concrete form. One person begins with a flat moral claim: honesty is good. The other does not deny this; instead, they present a situation in which honesty causes gratuitous harm, and the first speaker revises the claim: honesty is good except when it causes gratuitous harm. Then the second speaker produces a case in which gratuitous harm is present, yet honesty is still bad—not because of the harm, but because the manner is contemptuous. So the first speaker adds another layer: except when it causes gratuitous harm delivered with contempt. But then the second speaker finds a case in which contempt is present, yet honesty is good—say, the contempt is directed at an injustice, and the honesty functions as moral resistance. Each round leaves the original feature intact, but wraps it in a deeper nest of conditions that themselves depend on further moral features. The principle never collapses; it simply becomes a long, branching chain of unless-clauses, each clause referring to values that are themselves unstable across cases. The attacker never says the feature is irrelevant; they just keep showing that any proposed boundary can be crossed from the inside.

Is there an established concept that names exactly this dynamic—not moral particularism in general, not holism about reasons, not ordinary defeasible reasoning with ceteris paribus qualifications, but the specific iterative nesting of context-sensitive exceptions that never kills the principle yet never lets it stand alone? How is it different from standard counterexample-driven refinement, from value pluralism, and from the plain claim that moral reasons are context-dependent? If no established term covers it precisely, please coin one, define it sharply, and mark its edges with clear boundary cases: what should count as an instance, and what nearby patterns should be excluded? I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list; I only need the conceptual carving done precisely.
