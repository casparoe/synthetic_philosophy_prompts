Hey, I'm a retired chemist who started reading philosophy last winter and now I can't stop thinking about these arguments. Anyway, I was at a philosophy meetup in my town and two regulars got into it over wether Germans or Americans have the healthier folk morality about rules and order. Let's call them Klaus and Mike, though those aren't their real names.

Klaus said in Germany people wait at the red pedestrian light at 2am with zero cars in sight, and that's not stupidity — it's a moral practice. The rule isn't just a traffic tool; following it expresses that you're part of a shared order that doesn't depend on your personal risk calculation. He said American morality gets this backwards: you treat every rule as a suggestion that your private conscience gets to veto, which sounds like freedom but actually just licenses the powerful to ignore norms they don't like.

Mike said that's romantic nonsense. He said the German view confuses morality with habit, and that real moral agency requires judging whether the rule actually applies here. He pointed out that Americans cross empty streets precisely because they recognize the rule's purpose (safety) is already satisfied, so following it anyway is just performative obedience — like saluting a flag that's not there. He said if you make rule-following itself a virtue, you end up with bureaucrats who hide behind protocol instead of helping people.

Klaus shot back that Mike is smuggling in an outcome-based ethic without admitting it. He said the "purpose" of a rule isn't always visible to the individual, and that treating every case as special is exactly how corruption starts — not dramatic corruption, just the slow erosion where everyone makes "reasonable" exceptions until only suckers follow the rules.

Mike answered that Klaus is describing a slippery slope fallacy dressed up as sociology. He said democratic societies fix bad rules by changing them collectively, not by demanding individual martyrdom to outdated protocols. He added that there's a specifically American folk idea — maybe from frontier culture — that being too rule-bound makes you a "sheep," and that maintaining your own judgment is itself a moral duty, not just selfishness.

Then Klaus said something I hadn't heard before: he called it normative residue. Even if Mike is right that the rule is outdated, the person who breaks it still owes an account — not just "it was safe," but a justification addressed to the community. Without that residue, he said, rules become pure coordination devices with no moral weight, and then you've lost the distinction between law and morality altogether.

They turned to me and asked who had the better of it. I honestly don't know. Can you go through their exchange step by step and say who's right at each point? Don't just pick a winner — tell me what each is missing, and whether there's a way to have both the collective order and the individual conscience without them eating each other. Also, I'm curious: if you take away Klaus's normative-residue idea, does his whole argument collapse, or is there another pillar holding it up?
