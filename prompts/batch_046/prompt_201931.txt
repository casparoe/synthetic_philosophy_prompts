I am giving the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on moral exemplarity and biography, where half the audience are classicists steeped in Plutarch's Parallel Lives and the other half are moral philosophers with little background in ancient biography. The prevailing mood in the room is that hagiography corrupts moral learning by substituting uncritical admiration for judgment. I want a five-minute address that does not deny Plutarch reshapes political failures and amplifies private virtue, but instead argues that this reshaping is itself the pedagogical mechanism: his parallel pairings train the reader in comparative moral attention, forcing an active correction for exaggeration rather than passive absorption, so that the suppression of context becomes an exercise in ethical discrimination rather than propaganda. The speech should present this as a real, contestable interpretive claim recognizable to the classicists, yet explain clearly to the philosophers why a deliberately distorted biography can still function as a critical tool for moral thinking. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific editions, and do not ask the audience to defer to scholarly authority. Write entirely in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one might write a formal letter. Within that same prose, please indicate how confident you are in the interpretation you advance and single out the specific claim about which you are least certain.
