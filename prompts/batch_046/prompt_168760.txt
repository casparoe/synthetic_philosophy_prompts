I am preparing a long-form essay on whether collective mental states—say, a legislature’s shared judgment that a particular fiscal policy is equitable—can genuinely drive macro-level outcomes, or whether they are always causally preempted by the underlying neural and physical events. In discussing this with a philosopher of mind, I was led through a version of the causal exclusion argument that seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the precise misstep.

The reasoning runs as follows. (1) For any physical event P, there is a sufficient physical cause C that is complete at the microphysical level. (2) If a mental or collective representational state M also causes P, then either M is identical to some part of C, or P is overdetermined by two independent sufficient causes. (3) Systematic overdetermination is implausible in the mental-to-physical domain, so M must be reducible to or identical with elements of C. (4) But if M is identical to those elements, then the representational content of M—its being about justice, risk, or obligation—contributes no causal work beyond what the bare physical states already accomplish. (5) Apply this now to collective intentional states: a parliamentary body’s belief that redistribution is warranted appears to cause the enactment of a tax law, yet every vote, every vocalization, and every neural firing in every member already has a complete physical explanation. (6) By (2) through (4), that collective belief is either identical to the aggregate physical events or causally redundant. (7) If it is redundant, then representational properties at every scale—individual belief, collective judgment, institutional intention—are causally inert, leaving only fundamental physics with genuine efficacy. (8) That conclusion is intolerable, not only for psychology and sociology but for any macrohistorical or policy analysis that treats shared beliefs as genuine drivers of events. (9) Therefore the exclusion argument must contain an error. (10) Yet if the error lies in rejecting (1) or (2), we seem to license widespread overdetermination as a routine feature of causation.

I would very much prefer to preserve the causal relevance of collective representational states, but I want to understand exactly which premise is least defensible and what philosophical price one pays for abandoning it—whether that means embracing non-reductive physicalism, allowing downward causation, or accepting that overdetermination is not as damaging as it initially appears. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
