As a retired physicist who has only recently begun working through the philosophy of probability and belief, I have become preoccupied with the Sleeping Beauty problem and the broader literature on self-locating belief. The familiar dispute between halfers and thirders is well mapped, but I am less interested in rehearsing it than in asking which remaining open questions, unresolved formal issues, and underexplored implications actually merit sustained intellectual effort, and which are largely decorative. Specifically, among the live problems—such as the formal treatment of centered-world updating rules and whether they require a revision to standard Bayesian conditioning; the analogy between Beauty-style self-location and statistical inference problems in repeated scientific measurements or evolutionary sampling; the potential relevance of indexical reasoning to cosmological multiverse hypotheses and to interpretive issues in quantum mechanics; and the question of whether self-locating credences have any genuine normative force in practical decision-making—which two or three should command the attention of a researcher or small working group with, say, three years and limited resources? Rank them, but do not merely list them: defend the ordering with explicit criteria—philosophical importance, tractability given current formal tools, and how many other questions depend on resolving this one. Be explicit about the trade-offs, for instance between a problem that is highly important but currently intractable and one that is narrower but genuinely solvable. Furthermore, I want an honest assessment of which alleged implications of this debate are overrated: does resolving the halfer-thirder dispute genuinely change how we ought to reason about anthropic arguments in science, or is that connection largely speculative? Are there domain-specific analogues—perhaps in the replication crisis, in climate-model ensemble interpretation, or in biological taxonomy—that philosophers have neglected because they seem too applied, but which in fact illuminate the core formal issue? The deliverable should be a defended research agenda, not a survey: what deserves priority, what can be safely deprioritized, and what governing criteria justify the division. If you invoke specific frameworks—such as compartmentalized updating, centered-world semantics, or non-ergodic decision rules—define them briefly in your own terms rather than appealing to named authorities.
