I am drafting an essay on whether Douglas Hofstadter's strange-loop conception of the self—where the symbol "I" is a self-referential pattern that loops back through hierarchical levels of representation—can be made adequate without supplementing it with a distinction I want to call horizontal versus vertical recursion. On my framing, vertical recursion is the familiar hierarchical self-reference (a representation representing its own representational machinery), while horizontal recursion is the distributed, temporally extended loop through which a self-model is continuously revised by social interaction, memory consolidation, and predictive error across contexts. I argue that Hofstadter's account, if read as purely vertical, collapses into a synchronic snapshot that cannot explain diachronic identity or intersubjective correction; adding horizontal recursion saves the stability of the self but risks importing externalist commitments that sit uneasily with the brain-bound, pattern-internalist flavor of the original view. Please comment on the draft's coherence: (1) Is the vertical/horizontal distinction genuinely novel, or does it merely rename existing distinctions in extended cognition or narrative-identity theory? (2) Does horizontal recursion solve the diachronic-stability problem, or does it simply relocate the homunculus objection from inside the skull to the social environment? (3) Where is the weakest conceptual link—at the boundary between the two recursion types, or in the claim that the strange-loop model lacks them? I am particularly interested in whether this framing successfully avoids the charge that strange loops are just disguised homunculi, or whether it inadvertently revives them at the collective level.
