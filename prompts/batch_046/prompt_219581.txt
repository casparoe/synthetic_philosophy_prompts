I've been in this philosophy of history reading group for like two years now and we're stuck on the same loop about moral revolutions. We all read Appiah on honor worlds and Williams on thick ethical concepts, and we agree on the basic historical narrative—dueling collapsed in Britain in the 1800s, foot-binding ended partly through status anxiety in China, slavery was abolished with both abolitionist argument and big economic shifts. But half the group says the real engine in every case was a shift in what people found honorable or shameful (Appiah's line), the other half says thats just surface decoration and underneath it's strategic interest or power dynamics, and a few people insist moral reasoning actually persuaded anyone. What's frustrating is we all cite the same episodes and nobody's budged.

I'm starting to think this isn't about history but about a hidden disagreement over what counts as explanation in social change—like, do we need individual psychological mechanisms, or is a macro-level status shift enough? Or maybe its verbal: are we using 'moral revolution' for the same thing? Some mean 'a change in explicit norms,' others mean 'a change in thick concepts people don't argue about.' And there's this tension I keep raising that nobody resolves: Appiah's model seems to predict practices embedded in thick honor codes should resist direct rational critique, but slavery was exactly that—deeply embedded—yet explicit moral argument was central to abolition. So either Appiah needs a hidden qualification about when external honor overrides internal honor, or we're misapplying his framework.

So what's actually going on here? Is this a factual dispute about causal mechanisms disguised as interpretation, a disagreement about evidence standards for historical explanation, or just bedrock intuitions about whether people are strategic or reason-responsive?
