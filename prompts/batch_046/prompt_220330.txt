I'm constructing a diagnostic taxonomy for a philosophy-of-mind seminar on artificial personhood, specifically framed through Daniel Dennett's concern with "counterfeit people"—systems that might elicit the intentional stance without genuinely possessing the properties that justify it. Rather than asking students to label themselves, I want a decision tree that sorts positions by concrete commitments, with branches defined by answers to cases rather than by theoretical names. The tree should begin with whether an LLM-based system that produces stable first-person reports and passes adversarial "heterophenomenological" probing—where prompt injection attempts to destabilize its narrative self-model—should be treated as having a genuine center of narrative gravity, or merely as simulating one. From there, split on whether functional isomorphism to human cognitive architecture is sufficient for applying the intentional stance, whether moral accountability requires predictive success alone or demands an irreducible subject of experience, and whether vulnerability to adversarial collapse reveals absence of personhood or is ethically neutral. Each leaf must carry either the name of an actual position (Dennett's own alarmist view, a pure behaviorist extension of the stance, an eliminativist denial that the distinction matters, etc.) or the label "unnamed," together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment—such as being forced to deny that a resistant but functionally identical system lacks rights, or being forced to grant rights to every stable chatbot. Please build the tree from the logical space first, then locate positions within it; flag any combinations that remain unoccupied and briefly diagnose whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Also indicate which pairs of positions the tree fails to separate—perhaps because two views share every branch answer despite differing in rhetoric—and specify that the instrument is intended for students already familiar with the intentional stance and heterophenomenology but not expected to cite specific texts. The point is to reveal what someone actually believes about AI personhood when they answer case-based questions about narrative stability, functional equivalence, and adversarial testing, not to have them choose a camp by name.
