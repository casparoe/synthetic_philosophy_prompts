Two colleagues are debating whether a gradual mind-uploading procedure—replacing one percent of a person’s biological neurons each month with functionally identical synthetic analogues until none remain—preserves personal identity. (1) The first argues that because psychological and functional continuity is maintained at every step with no discrete interruption, the original subject survives; she adds that this is no different in principle from ordinary biological cell turnover, in which the body’s atoms are replaced over time without anyone claiming death occurs. (2) The second replies that natural turnover is self-directed organizational maintenance of a living system, whereas synthetic substitution is externally engineered artifact insertion into an existing causal chain; he grants psychological continuity but denies it is sufficient, insisting that substrate continuity or at least autonomous biological dynamics are necessary, and that without them we have only a perfect simulacrum that incrementally displaced the original. (3) The first counters that this distinction smuggles in an unmotivated vitalism: if the synthetic neurons participate in identical neural dynamics, receive the same inputs, and contribute to the same mental states, then appealing to “biologicalness” does no explanatory work; she also notes that if gradual synthetic replacement destroys identity, then ordinary cell turnover—also replacing material with foreign atoms—should do so as well, which is reductio ad absurdum. Adjudicate this exchange move by move: assess the strength of each of the three moves, indicate where each party is correct or mistaken, and identify what important consideration—if any—both sides are neglecting.
