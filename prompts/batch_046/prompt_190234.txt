I am a high school junior serving as the student representative on my school’s Campus Grounds and Sustainability Committee, and next Thursday we must vote on a proposal to convert our eastern two-acre manicured lawn into an unmanaged wild meadow over a three-year period. The proponents emphasize carbon sequestration and native pollinator recovery, but in my environmental ethics elective we have been discussing whether altering habitat genuinely reduces wild animal suffering or merely redistributes it across different populations and life-stages. I need a formal memo—something polished enough to circulate to the faculty chair and the administration before the meeting—that recommends one concrete course of action: approve the conversion outright, reject it with an alternative proposal, or approve it with binding conditions and a defined review timeline. The memo should treat the biodiversity advocates’ position seriously rather than dismissing it, but it must also engage the strongest counter-argument: that suppressing wild biomass through mowing and landscaping actually minimizes aggregate suffering by preventing the high reproductive turnover, predation stress, and starvation events that unmanaged ecosystems tend to generate.

To keep the reasoning usable by committee members without philosophical training, please organize the memo around a practical framework I have been sketching out, which I am calling a welfare-weighted biomass assessment. This framework weighs potential suffering not only by population size but also by the neural complexity and expected lifespan of the affected taxa, and it distinguishes carefully between suffering caused by predation, by starvation and exposure, and by human-mediated displacement or maintenance activity. Apply it explicitly to the lawn-to-meadow case by comparing the current regime—low rodent and invertebrate biomass, occasional deer grazing, minimal predation pressure, regular human presence—against the projected wild state, which should include increased rodent and invertebrate populations, greater avian and mammalian predation, reduced stress from machinery and chemicals, but also higher rates of juvenile mortality and winter starvation. The memo should state clearly where the framework forces unavoidable judgment calls—especially in comparing subjective welfare across species with very different nervous systems—and should present a worked comparison that reaches an answer the biodiversity advocates will not like. Finally, it should specify exactly what the committee can do in practice: for example, a phased conversion with annual population and welfare audits, a kill-switch clause that halts the project if rodent biomass exceeds a predetermined threshold within eighteen months, or a rejection paired with an alternative carbon-offset plan funded by the maintenance budget. Please avoid bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific texts I would need to look up; I need reasoning the committee can evaluate on its own merits.
