Write a concise philosophical short story—roughly 400 words—set in a near-future administrative archive. In this world, cohort labels such as "Gen Z" or "Millennial" are not tied to birth years but are assigned each year by a bureaucratic algorithm that clusters citizens according to adaptive patterns to emerging communication technologies. A sociologist protagonist discovers archival proof that these clusters lack any stable psychological or biological substrate: they dissolve entirely when the algorithm's weighting parameters are adjusted. Nevertheless, members display robust collective intentionality, shared normative frameworks, and mutual recognition that survive reassignment. The narrative should center on a single scene—perhaps a tribunal or preservation hearing—where the protagonist must decide whether to expose the algorithmic origin (undermining the categories' claim to being natural kinds) or defend their institutional reality (arguing that sustained collective practice and recognition confer ontological weight). The conceptual tension should emerge through dialogue and concrete institutional detail rather than explicit philosophical citation. Treat the algorithm as an ordinary bureaucratic instrument, not a dystopian mechanism, and avoid well-known science-fiction tropes.
