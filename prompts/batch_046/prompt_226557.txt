Frontier AI laboratories routinely publish capability benchmarks alongside release notes but treat any degradation in those metrics caused by safety fine-tuning—the so-called alignment tax—as an engineering failure to be eliminated rather than a legitimate cost of responsible development. Research funding and publication prestige flow to alignment techniques that preserve or enhance capabilities, such as preference optimization that improves instruction-following scores or constitutional methods framed as reducing over-refusal without sacrificing reasoning, while approaches that would substantially reduce capabilities for safety reasons are typically abandoned or never pursued, despite the same institutions acknowledging in governance documents that competitive markets penalize labs which unilaterally reduce performance. What philosophical commitments about the relationship between power and restraint, between market competition and moral obligation, or about the ontology of value itself, does this institutional pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it assume that safety and capability are orthogonal dimensions that can be separately optimized toward a Pareto frontier, that competitive dynamics generate a special exemption from ordinary moral trade-offs, or that alignment possesses only instrumental value? Assess whether the practice is internally coherent, given that these labs simultaneously invoke precautionary reasoning when arguing for external regulation. Finally, if we replaced its implicit framework with one that treated sustained capability reduction as an acceptable, even structurally desirable, feature of robust alignment, what concrete changes would be required in how laboratories benchmark, publish results, and allocate internal research resources?
