For an assignment in my advanced ethics course, I have been examining my own intuitions about whether we should honor, contextualize, or reject the commemoration of historical figures whose legacies combine major public achievements with grave moral failures. I want to know whether my judgments follow a coherent principle, or whether they collapse into inconsistency once pressed, and I am especially interested in how the so-called "man of his time" defence applies differently across cases.

Here are my concrete verdicts.

First, Thomas Jefferson. He authored foundational texts of liberal equality and served as President, yet he held hundreds of people in slavery and fathered children with an enslaved woman he kept in bondage. My judgment is that we should not tear down every monument or rename every institution, because his political achievements remain historically significant; but we should not present him as an unqualified moral exemplar, and commemorative contexts should include explicit acknowledgment of his slaveholding. The wrong is too deep for pure celebration, yet too structurally entangled with his public role for total erasure.

Second, Winston Churchill. He led Britain in its resistance to fascism during the Second World War, yet he held virulently racist and imperialist views, and cabinet decisions under his leadership contributed to the catastrophic Bengal famine. Here I think the response should be sharply differentiated: honoring his specific wartime leadership in appropriate historical contexts is defensible, but naming schools or public parks after him as a general role model is not, because that implies endorsement of the whole person rather than recognition of a specific achievement. His imperialism and the famine are inexcusable, yet they feel somewhat less central to the reason for his commemoration than Jefferson's slaveholding is to his.

Third, Christopher Columbus. His 1492 voyage initiated sustained European colonization of the Americas, accompanied by enslavement, mutilation, and catastrophic population decline among indigenous peoples. I believe celebrating him with a national holiday or erecting uncontextualized monuments is clearly wrong; at the same time, treating him as an inexplicable monster unique in his era ignores that other European powers pursued similar projects. The "man of his time" defence fails here, I think, because contemporaries such as Bartolomé de las Casas documented and condemned the brutalities, showing that dissenting moral knowledge was available within his own institutional and religious world.

Fourth, the comparison that unsettles me most is between Jefferson and Columbus. Both had access to dissent—abolitionist arguments circulated in Jefferson's intellectual circle, and critics within Columbus's own expeditions and Church condemned the enslavement of indigenous people. Both persisted in massive, intentional harm for economic and political gain. Yet I feel more inclined to preserve Jefferson's commemorative presence in modified form than to preserve Columbus's. I wonder whether the distinction lies in the nature of the harm: Jefferson's was sustained through domestic intimacy and institutional continuity over generations, whereas Columbus's was initiatory, explicit, and violently extractive. I am not sure this distinction holds moral weight, but it drives my intuition.

Finally, there is the case I keep going back and forth on: the nineteenth-century American surgeon J. Marion Sims, who developed foundational gynecological techniques through repeated experimental surgeries on enslaved Black women without anesthesia, even after anesthesia was available. His research saved countless subsequent lives, and he was not unique among his contemporaries in using enslaved subjects. Some argue his statue should remain with rigorous contextualization; others say it must be removed because there is no separable achievement worth honoring when the method was direct, non-consensual bodily violation of specific, named victims. I cannot settle whether my principle should distinguish between harms that are instrumental to a public good (Sims's research) and harms that are incidental to it (Churchill's famine policies), or whether that distinction collapses under scrutiny.

So my question is this: is there a single principle—perhaps about the availability of dissenting voices in one's context, the separability of a specific achievement from the harm used to produce or maintain it, or the symbolic intent of commemorative practice—that captures all five verdicts? If so, what does it predict for Sims? And which of my judgments, especially the asymmetry between Jefferson and Columbus and the tentative distinction between instrumental and incidental harm, must I abandon for the sake of coherence?
