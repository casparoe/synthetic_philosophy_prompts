Student answer:
A free-floating rationale is a normative consideration that holds independently of any actual or potential agent's mental states. Unlike motivating reasons, which necessarily involve some psychological push, free-floating rationales exist out there in the structure of the world. For instance, the fact that a certain ecosystem has intrinsic value might be a reason to preserve it even if no conscious being ever recognizes that value, which shows reasons are not essentially relational.

The motivational-void objection says that without a reasoner to be moved, calling something a reason is just attaching a fancy label to a brute fact. i think this misses the point. Normative reasons are not psychological pushes; they are considerations that count in favor of actions or attitudes. Scanlon's view is that to be a reason is just to be a consideration that supports a particular response, and this can be true even in the absence of anyone who actually responds. So the relational view, which says reasons require a subject, is too restrictive.

Still, one might save the relational view by appealing to potential reasoners: a reason only needs a possible agent, not an actual one. But this just pushes the problem back. If the agent is merely possible, then the reason's existence depends on a counterfactual mind, which is still a kind of relational dependence. Therefore, the relational view fails.

However, i think there's a deeper problem. If rationales truly float free with no connection to any mind, actual or possible, then they seem indistinguishable from mere causal patterns. The fact that suffering is bad is only a reason if there is some standpoint from which it matters. So free-floating rationales either smuggle in a potential reasoner or collapse into descriptive facts. Despite this tension, i defend the existence of free-floating normative reasons because well-being is objectively valuable, following the idea that reasons are ultimately about what is good for people regardless of whether those people exist.
