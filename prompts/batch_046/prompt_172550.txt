I have recently joined an institutional review board that evaluates synthetic cognitive architectures for research ethics clearance, and I am expected to classify each submitted system according to Daniel Dennett’s taxonomy of minds—Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian—before monthly hearings. The board members lack technical backgrounds, I have roughly thirty minutes per case, and I cannot run evolutionary histories or full behavioral batteries, so I need a reusable decision procedure translated from the conceptual taxonomy into an ordered practical checklist. The protocol should specify which observable capacities and failure modes distinguish the four kinds, include explicit tests to separate Gregorian tool-use and cultural mediation from mere Skinnerian conditioning with artifacts, and distinguish Popperian internal hypothesis-testing from stochastic output variation. It must handle hybrid cases where a system exhibits traits across categories, clearly flag every step that remains an unavoidable judgment call rather than a mechanical determination, and state its own limits—particularly that it does not adjudicate consciousness or moral status. Apply the procedure to three submissions: a large language model fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback; a classical reinforcement-learning agent equipped with an external writable memory bank; and a hypothetical system that revises its own objective function in response to linguistic instruction. For at least one case, the procedure should yield a classification that conflicts with my intuitive assessment, and you should explain why I should still follow the protocol’s output. Finally, specify what the checklist cannot determine, who should sign off on the judgment-call steps, and how I should document ambiguity when the board demands a single label.
