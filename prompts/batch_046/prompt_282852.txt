I'm giving the closing remarks at the retirement symposium for Dr. Elena Vasquez, a theoretical physicist who spent thirty-five years constructing what she herself called "the scaffold" for a quantum interpretation that was conclusively undermined by experiments published last year. The room will be split: about half are her former students and collaborators who understand the technical failure in precise detail, and about half are family, university administrators, and friends who do not. I want the speech to take Dennett's notion of the self as a center of narrative gravity as genuine structural architecture, not as a decorative epigraph—meaning the address should avoid any language that treats her career as a failed excavation of nature's "true" underlying form, and should instead frame her contribution as the temporary gravitational center around which an entire research program, with its own internal norms, collaborative rituals, and conceptual coherence, orbited for a generation.

To keep this from being a standard eulogy-to-career, I want to inject a somewhat niche framing: treat "the scaffold" itself—not the physical theory it supported—as a deliberately engineered fictional object, never intended to map reality in a correspondence sense, but constructed to make certain kinds of reasoning, error-correction, and collective inquiry possible. This should mirror, analogically, how the narrative self operates: not a substance discovered, but a center constructed so that other processes can organize around it. The speech needs to honor her sincerely without lying about the scientific outcome, and it must be fully intelligible to the non-physicists without patronizing the experts—no jargon without translation, no false triumphalism, and no retreat into vague inspiration. Write it in a tone that sounds like a longtime reader of rationalist forums attempting a genuinely philosophical retirement address rather than a conventional toast: formal, conceptually rigorous, but spoken aloud to a mixed room that includes people who loved her and people who merely respected her work.
