I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I am trying to make sense of a tension in an internal compensation system I have been building. We run a development platform where contributors earn non-transferable credits—call them work-units—only through verified, peer-reviewed code contributions. Each unit is technically redeemable for one hour of server compute, and the design intent was that the credits should function as accounting tokens: signs that point back to labor actually performed, rather than assets valuable in themselves. Over the past quarter, however, a secondary behavior has emerged. Contributors hoard large balances and display them prominently in profiles; they barter units informally for goods and services entirely outside the platform—conference tickets, freelance contracts, even apartment sublets—at exchange rates that fluctuate independently of compute costs; and some refuse to redeem units even when they genuinely need server time, treating the accumulated balance as a form of prestige or stored wealth. One senior engineer argues that this is exactly what successful money does, and therefore the system works: people value the units, so they are real value. Another engineer insists that the moment the units become desired for their own sake—accumulated, traded, and detached from the specific labor that generated them—they cease to be tracking devices and become fictional wealth, no different from paper that promises nothing. I am caught between them, and I want to know whether my unease points to a genuine conceptual problem about the relationship between a sign and the value it is supposed to represent, or whether I am simply projecting an aesthetic dislike of speculation onto an ordinary economic mechanism. If my framing is confused—if there is no coherent philosophical issue here, or if I am misapplying distinctions that do not belong in this context—please say so plainly rather than forcing an answer to match my intuition.
