I am writing a multi-volume novel cycle centered on a protagonist who makes binding intertemporal commitments—contracts, vows, investments—that are revisited in later volumes as her information and tastes evolve. To preserve psychological consistency without flattening her into a static utility function, I need a reusable practitioner’s procedure I can apply to any sequence of her choices across chapters: a word-flowchart that determines whether her preference shifts expose her to a diachronic money pump, distinguishes rational belief-updating from exploitable reversal, and marks precisely where the judgment belongs to me as author rather than to the theory.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist. For each step, specify the question to ask of the preference sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether the node terminates in vulnerable to pump, rationally updated, or hand off to authorial values. Ground it in the actual logic of diachronic Dutch Books and intertemporal preference consistency—include Hammond-style consistency conditions where they matter—but do not require me to compute probabilities or know formal economics. The procedure must work when outcomes are narrative goods (reputation, loyalty, artistic integrity) rather than monetary payoffs, and it must explicitly flag cases where non-transitive or spectrum-style better-than relations among those goods cause the flowchart to misfire.

Apply the procedure to three sequences. Case A: in volume one she sells a family heirloom; in volume three she buys it back at triple cost out of nostalgia; in volume five she sells it again. Case B: she refuses a risky collaboration in volume two, then accepts an identical offer in volume four after learning the partner was honest all along. Case C: she adheres to a vow made in ignorance despite knowing in volume six that keeping it will destroy her, and she refuses any compensation that would undo the vow—here the procedure likely returns vulnerable or inconsistent, yet I want the explicit option to override it on narrative grounds. Include, for each case, the step at which the procedure stops and what it says.

Keep the response to no more than 650 words, including the worked cases. State clearly what the procedure cannot settle.
