I have recently been asked to help revise the ethical charter of a professional association, and the exercise has made me reflect on whether institutional norms actually shift because people are argued into new beliefs, or because the social conditions that made the old norms valuable disappear. This has led me to revisit the debate about moral revolutions—particularly Bernard Williams's claim that dueling faded not through moral persuasion but through the dissolution of the honor culture that required it, and Kwame Anthony Appiah's counter-insistence that moral argument can be causally effective precisely when it reconfigures the economy of honor.

Please state your credence, as a number between 0 and 1, in the following compound claim: that for both the decline of European dueling by the late nineteenth century and the abolition of Atlantic chattel slavery in the same broad era, the dominant causal mechanism was a transformation in the social economy of honor and reputation, rather than either direct moral persuasion or purely material interest. Decompose this into three sub-credences: (i) that Williams's honor-dissolution account is more accurate than a moral-persuasion account for dueling specifically; (ii) that the abolition of slavery followed a structurally analogous honor-reputation mechanism rather than being driven primarily by economic calculation and geopolitical pressure; and (iii) that "direct moral persuasion" can be isolated as a coherent, independent causal variable in macro-historical normative change at all. Assign each a rough probability with a brief justification, show how they combine—whether multiplicatively, as a weighted average, or otherwise—to reach your overall figure, and identify the term whose revision would shift your total the most. Finally, specify what kind of historical or sociological finding—if it existed and were credible—would most likely move that weakest term.
