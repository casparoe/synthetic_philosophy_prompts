The problem concerns what I will call algorithmic inheritance: in economies where a significant share of productive labor is performed by AI systems owned by a small class of citizens, the returns do not flow as wages but as capital dividends that are pooled into a universal basic asset. The constitutional question is whether the rules governing ownership of these systems should be chosen behind a veil of ignorance that includes not only contemporaneous citizens but also representatives of future generations who will live with the locked-in technological infrastructure.

Assume Rawls is alive today and serving as an advisor to a constitutional convention drafting the basic structure for such an economy. Predict how he would apply the veil of ignorance and the difference principle to this case. Specifically, address two points. First, would he permit representatives of future generations in the original position, or would he maintain that the parties must be contemporaneous and that obligations to the future are handled separately through just savings? Second, under the difference principle, how would he evaluate computational advantage—the inequality that arises when some citizens own or design the AI labor systems—if that inequality produces a higher baseline for the least advantaged than any equal distribution of ownership could achieve?

Write your answer as a reasoned conceptual reconstruction predicting Rawls's view, not as a generic summary of his theory. Treat seriously the strongest counter-argument: that extending the veil across generations collapses the distinction between justice and benevolence, and that allowing computational advantage violates the fair equality of opportunity he insists upon.
