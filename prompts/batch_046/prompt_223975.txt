Two philosophers I know, Jordan and Sam, got into an extended argument over dinner about what makes promises binding. I was present but did not take sides, and I genuinely could not referee it. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

Jordan began by claiming that promises operate as exercises of a normative power: when someone sincerely says “I promise,” they do not merely report an intention but generate a claim-right in the hearer. This change is irreducible to pre-existing reasons. Even for trivial promises—say, agreeing to meet for coffee that neither party particularly needs—the speaker acquires a real, if weak, obligation that did not exist before the utterance. Sam pushed back with the immoral-content objection: if someone promises to harm an innocent person, that promise creates no obligation whatsoever. This shows, Sam said, that the binding force comes entirely from the underlying moral reasons, not from any sui generis normative change produced by the act of promising itself. Jordan conceded that immoral promises fail to bind, but argued that this only proves the normative power is conditional, not that it is illusory. She compared it to consent: consent can be invalidated by coercion or ignorance, yet no one denies that consent is a genuine normative power.

Sam then proposed an alternative framework. Promises, he said, are essentially coordination devices that stabilize expectations and create reliance interests. The “obligation” to keep a promise is just the instrumental reason not to frustrate reasonable reliance and not to undermine a useful social practice. Jordan replied that this account misses what she called normative residue: when someone breaks a trivial promise with negligible reliance and no practical harm—say, failing to show up for coffee after a casual “I’ll be there”—the wrong seems specifically directed at the promisee, not merely a general inefficiency or disappointment to the world. To capture this, Jordan introduced the idea of promissory deferral: in promising, the speaker temporarily transfers part of their practical authority over that domain to the promisee. The promisee thereby acquires a conditional, domain-limited veto over the speaker’s reasons in that area. Sam objected that autonomy is not divisible like property. If practical authority could genuinely be transferred, then a promisee could demand fulfillment even after catastrophic changes in circumstances—say, insisting on the coffee meeting immediately after the speaker has suffered a severe bereavement—unless Jordan smuggles in conditions that collapse the view back into Sam’s reliance-based account. Jordan answered that the deferral is indeed conditional on the normative point of the promise not being frustrated, but insisted that this is a feature rather than a bug: it explains why promises bind more strongly than mere intentions yet can be overridden, without reducing them to tools for managing expectations.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of Jordan’s and Sam’s main responses, rate whether it is clearly mistaken, mostly right but incomplete, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly whether Jordan’s “promissory deferral” adds anything genuinely explanatory that Sam’s reliance-based view cannot capture, or whether it is, as Sam suspects, largely a metaphorical redescription of conditional reliance. Be precise about whether there are plausible cases where promises seem to bind despite negligible reliance, and whether those cases genuinely support Jordan’s normative-power account or can be explained within Sam’s framework. Finally, say whether either position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
