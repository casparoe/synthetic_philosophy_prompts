So I'm writing a feature for a science magazine about why "life" is still a mess of a concept, and I keep coming back to a chat I had with a biologist friend. She's got these pretty sharp verdicts on the borderlines. Viruses? Not alive — no autonomus metabolism, total dependence on host machinery. Prions? She puts them in a weird "quasi-alive" bucket because they replicate and evolve without nucleic acids, but she won't call them fully living. Fire is out, even though it eats and spreads, because there's no informational continuity, nothing like a genome being passed along. Mitochondria and chloroplasts she counts as alive in a qualified way — they have their own DNA, they were free-living bacteria once — but she denies that to synthetic vesicles that just happen to carry enzymes. Computer viruses are out too, not because they're digital, but because there's no physical substrate undergoing selection.

Then there's this new synthetic protocell — I'm just calling it the X-7 vesicle, since the actual lab name is still under embargo — engineered with around four hundred synthetic genes in a lipid membrane. It divides, scavanges lipids from the environment, keeps homeostasis, but it can't build its own membrane precursors and has no evolutionary ancestry; it's designed top-down. Based on her other calls, do you think she'll say it's alive? I'm not really after a yes/no; I want to know what rule she's actually running — metabolism, information, evolutionary origin, or something messier? And what are the other rules that could fit her earlier verdicts just as well? If there's a rival principle that flips the answer for X-7, what's the one extra case — maybe a dormant tardigrade, a self-replicating RNA in a tube, or a language model trained on biological data — that would best tell the rules apart?
