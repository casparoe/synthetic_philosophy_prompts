I am taking an advanced metaphysics course, and last week a visiting lecturer offered what she called a streamlined overview of the debate over dispositions, finks, and masks. Her summary ran roughly as follows. On the standard simple conditional analysis, an object is disposed to manifest M in conditions C just in case, were C to obtain, M would occur. Martin’s fink cases show that an object can possess the relevant categorical base—say, an electrical charge—yet have that very disposition removed the instant the manifestation conditions are satisfied, as when a live wire is connected to a mechanism that cuts the current upon contact. Johnston’s mask cases are, she said, the structural inverse: an object may lack the categorical base for a disposition, yet acquire that disposition precisely when the conditions for its manifestation obtain, as with a substance that becomes toxic only once an activating enzyme is present in the bloodstream. Taken together, she argued, finks and masks do not merely pose local counterexamples to the conditional analysis but reveal a deeper instability: because any categorical property can in principle be either finked away or masked into existence by an external relational factor, there is no stable intrinsic ground that distinguishes genuinely dispositional properties from merely categorical ones. She then extended this reasoning to institutional capacities—claiming, for instance, that a university’s authority to confer degrees can be finked by an accreditation body and masked by emergency statutes—so that collective dispositions are never intrinsic but always relationally constituted. From this she concluded that the categorical–dispositional distinction collapses altogether, leaving only a continuum of context-sensitive relational profiles.

Something about this feels too swift, particularly the slide from counterexamples to a full-blown collapse of the ontological distinction, and I am unsure whether my suspicion is well founded. Would a defender of dispositional realism accept her characterisation of finks and masks? Is the extension to institutional capacities a legitimate application of the same logic, or does it risk a category mistake? And does the argument actually establish that there is no intrinsic ground for dispositions, or does it conflate the failure of one particular analysis with the failure of the ontology itself? I should be grateful for a careful separation of what is accurate in her summary, what counts as a standard simplification, what is subtly misrepresented—especially regarding the relationship between finks and masks—and whether her final conclusion survives once these corrections are made.
