I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have spent considerable time thinking about personal identity, partly after encountering Douglas Hofstadter's suggestion that people we love deeply can persist as living patterns within our own minds and brains. The following commitments represent where my own thinking has settled. I want to know whether they form a coherent package, or whether I am helping myself to premises that pull in incompatible directions.

(1) I take identity to be a matter of pattern continuity rather than physical continuity: if every neuron in my brain were gradually replaced by functionally identical synthetic equivalents that preserved my thoughts and memories, the resulting person would still be me.

(2) I also believe that long-term emotional and cognitive entanglement allows mental patterns to migrate between people. After decades of intimacy, parts of my partner's patterns genuinely operate inside my own brain, and if she died, those fragments would continue as living components of my mental life.

(3) From this I conclude that the self is not strictly confined to one skull; it can overlap and be distributed across multiple brains, like a network with shared nodes.

(4) Nevertheless, I hold that if a perfect technological copy of my mind were created in a separate body, that copy would not be me. It would be a distinct person with my memories.

(5) Similarly, I believe that if I entered a teleporter that destroyed my original body and reconstructed an identical pattern on Mars, I would not survive. The reconstructed individual would be a replacement, and I would be dead.

(6) Despite my view that the self can be distributed, I maintain that moral responsibility is never distributed in this way. If I commit a wrong act, the blame is fully and exclusively mine, even if parts of the mental patterns that led to the decision originated in my partner's long influence on me over the years.

Are these commitments jointly tenable? I am especially concerned about whether (2) and (3) can be reconciled with (4) and (5)—if patterns can migrate naturally through intimacy, why can't they be duplicated technologically without creating me?—and about whether (3) undermines (6). But I also suspect that at least one of my worries is only apparent.

Please provide an audit rather than a lecture: identify the tensions in order of severity, specify what each conflict costs (which commitment must be modified or abandoned to restore consistency), and distinguish between points that are relatively settled in the literature and points that remain genuinely contested. Do not present a disputed position as if it were the consensus, and do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
