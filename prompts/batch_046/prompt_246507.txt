I have constructed an argument about self-referential higher-order defeat that seems to show rational agents can never coherently revise their own reasoning. (1) If I rationally determine, using my reasoning in domain D, that my D-reasoning is unreliable with error rate r, then I ought to reduce confidence in its outputs. (2) That reduction applies to the determination itself, since it too was produced by D-reasoning. (3) Hence accommodating the evidence either leaves me with first-order D-beliefs that I simultaneously distrust—epistemic akrasia—or forces me to restore confidence in those beliefs by undermining the unreliability claim. (4) Epistemic akrasia violates coherence: one should not hold P while judging one's own support for P inadequate. (5) So, if coherence is binding, I must dismiss the self-critical evidence and remain uncorrected. (6) But this implies rational agents are permanently immune to self-correction, which is absurd. I am inclined to defend (4) and (5), but I cannot see which step fails. Identify the flawed step, explain why it initially looks acceptable, and state what abandoning it costs.
