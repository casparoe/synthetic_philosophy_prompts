Compare Malcolm's argument that dreams are not experiences—grounded in the absence of any independent criterion for being wrong about a dream's content—with Dennett's opposing account that dream reports are best understood as narrative constructions formed upon awakening, possibly without any corresponding experiences occurring during sleep. Rather than surveying both positions generally, evaluate them specifically against the constraint introduced by lucid dreaming, in which subjects report performing deliberate, voluntary cognitive acts—such as directed attention, symbolic manipulation, or sequential reasoning—while asleep, and in which post-awakening reports of such agency are not plausibly dismissed as pure confabulation. Does Malcolm's criterion-based objection apply with equal force to these reported acts of intra-dream agency, or does the presence of voluntary control supply an independent check that undermines his claim? Conversely, can Dennett's constructionist framework accommodate genuine agency inside the dream state without collapsing into the view that the agency itself was fabricated retrospectively, and without implicitly relying on the very first-person authority his account seeks to eliminate? Do not lay out both options neutrally: take a definite position on whether this constraint forces a substantive revision of one framework over the other, specify which feature of experience—if any—remains defensible once the comparison is complete, and defend your choice by identifying the precise point at which the losing framework fails to account for the phenomenon.
