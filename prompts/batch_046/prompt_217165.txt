Having retired from a career in evolutionary ecology and only recently begun auditing philosophy-of-science seminars in earnest, I am assembling a collection of conceptual prompts that reward reasoning over retrieval. For this entry, please compose a deadpan parody—approximately 500 words—of an NSF grant proposal’s “Broader Impacts” narrative. The fictional applicant is an evolutionary biologist who proposes to determine whether Gould and Lewontin’s 1979 critique of adaptationism—the spandrels argument, with its San Marco architectural analogy and its charge of Panglossian reasoning—is itself best understood not as an adaptive theoretical advance but as a spandrel of 1970s Harvard institutional ecology: a non-functional byproduct of departmental politics, citation-network topology, and the selective environment of peer review. The proposal must systematically classify every component of the spandrels critique—developmental constraint, the rejection of atomized trait analysis, the emphasis on historical contingency and architectural necessity—as either an adaptation to academic survival or a non-adaptive architectural leftover. Maintain strict bureaucratic optimism throughout; the applicant must never break character to acknowledge that applying adaptationism reflexively to its own critics collapses the very distinction being defended, yet the circularity should remain legible to a philosophically attentive reader. The satire should depend on accurate familiarity with the actual content of the spandrels critique rather than treating it as a generic synonym for accidental utility. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the exercise should be performable from conceptual understanding alone. I want the piece to read like a genuine document that has accidentally wandered into reductio ad absurdum, and I want it to leave the reader with the real question of whether an adaptationist framework can ever be turned back on its own genealogy without dissolving.
