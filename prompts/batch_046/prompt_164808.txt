After 35 years running a marine ecology lab I’ve only just started reading philosophy of science, and I keep running into this analogy: people say causation in nature is like flipping a switch on a circuit board — you change the setting and you see the effect, so the cause is whatever makes a difference when you wiggle it. The philosophers who favour this call it interventionist, and they set it against the counterfactual lot who talk about what would have happened if the cause hadnt occured. I want to know whether the switch picture actually carries the argument they think it does. Can you lay out exactly what maps onto what — the switch, the current, the circuit board, the engineer — for causation in something like a food web or a climate feed-back loop? Where does the mapping break down? Does it quietly import that causes must be local, controllable and separate from the system, and if so does that bias us against evolutionary drift or emergent properties? And if the switch analogy is shaky, is there a better one from my old field — maybe a trophic cascade or a regime shift in an ecosystem — that captures the interventionist intuition without smuggling in all that baggage?
