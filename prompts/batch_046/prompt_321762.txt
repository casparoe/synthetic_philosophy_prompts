Consider the judgments of a scholar of medieval Islamic philosophy—call her the commentator—on modifications of Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment and related scenarios about embodied self-awareness. In each case she is asked whether the subject achieves non-inferential knowledge of her own existence, and separately whether she can know she possesses a body.

Her verdicts so far:
- The classic suspended man, deprived of all sensation and suspended in empty space: she says he knows he exists but does not know he has a body.
- The suspended man who retains false memories of a corporeal life—tactile and visual recollections that do not match his actual suspended state: she says he still knows he exists, but his belief in a present body is not knowledge.
- The suspended man with intact interoceptive signals—heartbeat, breath, visceral position—though all exteroception is blocked: she says he knows both that he exists and that he is embodied, since interoception is not external sensation.
- A distributed cognitive architecture in which no single node is the locus of awareness, yet each node generates reports using the first-person singular: she denies that any subject knows “I am,” and denies embodied knowledge.
- A synthetic agent activated moments ago, running a perfect simulation of human sensory life with no biological history and no actual body: she denies self-knowledge, though she grants the system has an accurate internal body-model.

Next, consider a new case: a suspended biological human, awake and conscious, who receives an artificial proprioceptive feed describing a body with a non-orientable spatial structure—limbs that loop back through themselves in ways that violate three-dimensional topology—delivered with perfect internal consistency and no signal conflict. The subject has never experienced contradiction, has no memory of any other body, and all other sensation is absent.

Predict the commentator’s verdict: does this subject know she exists, and does she know she has a body? Identify the implicit principle that best unifies her earlier rulings—whether it concerns phenomenal unity, causal-biological anchoring, or the internal coherence of the self-model—and say what each of these three principles predicts about this new case. Give your best guess at her actual verdict and the reason for preferring that reading. Finally, specify one further case—different in kind from the ones listed—that would most cleanly distinguish the principle you favor from its strongest rival.
