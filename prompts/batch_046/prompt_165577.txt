For an advanced philosophy-of-mind seminar of approximately 14 students meeting for 75 minutes, design a structured in-class simulation that makes participants experience the specific conceptual tension in the "Ant Fugue" problem—whether a distributed ant colony lacking any central controller or shared phenomenal field can nonetheless constitute a single mind. Do not open with a lecture on collective intentionality, emergentism, or Hofstadter; the pressure should arise entirely from the rules, roles, and payoffs.

Organize the session in three 15-minute phases separated by brief resets. In Phase 1, students act as neurons governed only by local connectivity rules with no global representation; in Phase 2, they become ant-like agents communicating solely through a constrained "pheromone" protocol (for example, colored cards placed face-down that only adjacent players may inspect). In both phases the group must reach a collective decision about a hidden resource allocation without speaking or writing. Phase 3 introduces a forced-choice scenario in which the collective output must treat a "phantom integration signal"—a piece of information that no individual has received and that the communication protocol does not permit to be transmitted—as if it were binding on the group. Embed this signal in a non-monetary payoff structure: if the group behaves coherently in accordance with the phantom signal, all members gain access to a shared reward (for instance, reserved seating and a distributed refreshment); if they split, ignore the signal, or appoint an explicit spokesperson, each individual suffers a minor personal penalty (such as remaining in an uncomfortable chair for the rest of the session).

Assign roles so that two students serve as hidden "observer-integrators" permitted only to record whether the collective behavior exhibits coherence that outstrips any individual's knowledge; reveal these roles only in the debrief. Provide explicit rules governing silence, adjacency, card-passing, and the conditions under which the phantom signal counts as satisfied. Include a minute-by-minute timeline and facilitator instructions.

After the simulation, provide a debrief that maps Phase 1 onto substrate-level aggregation, Phase 2 onto distributed but non-unified cognition, and Phase 3 onto the critical leap from collective behavior to integrated information. Identify what will predictably go wrong—most likely that participants either smuggle in a central controller by inventing a spokesperson, or dismiss the phantom signal as noise and collapse the problem back into Phase 2—and explain how to correct both failures without giving away the conceptual point prematurely.

Finally, state frankly which feature of the ant-colony-mind hypothesis the simulation cannot reproduce—specifically, the total absence of any first-person phenomenal unity or subjective point of view at the collective level—and explain how the facilitator must supply this limitation verbally in the closing discussion rather than expecting it to emerge from the mechanics. The entire design should be runnable without any external reading, citation, or web access.
