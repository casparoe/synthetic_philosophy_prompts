I'm defending a two-stage commitment model of voluntary action in a twenty-minute talk followed by thirty minutes of Q&A at a joint law-and-neuroscience workshop. My thesis is that Libet-style readiness-potential studies are routinely misread as showing that a deterministic brain event "decides" before conscious awareness. I argue that the readiness potential is better interpreted as a competitive bias signal within a distributed preparatory network, not as an executive decision, and that conscious agency functions by selecting among competing preparatory states rather than initiating action ex nihilo. I then apply this to criminal culpability: I claim that forensic assessment should turn not on whether neural preparation preceded subjective awareness, but on whether the agent had "frame-access"—the registered capacity to weigh competing preparatory trajectories—at the relevant choice point. The audience is about fifteen people: three cognitive neuroscientists who run voluntary-action EEG and fMRI labs and know the post-2008 replication and predictive-coding literature; two analytic philosophers of action who are committed compatibilists and regard popular Libet interpretations as conceptually sloppy; one criminal-law scholar who wants to import neuroscience into mens rea doctrine; and graduate students in philosophy of mind and law. Give me a prep sheet ordered by how damaging each objection is and by who will raise it first. For each objection, phrase it exactly as that subgroup would put it; then specify what I should concede, what I should hold, a reply deliverable in about ninety seconds, and a frank note on where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks—most likely from the legal scholar about institutional implementation, or from the compatibilists about whether my view collapses into determinism—and tell me how to survive it without derailing the session. Finally, identify the structurally weakest point in my own position—the part I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss—and flag which concession, if pressed, would actually undermine the forensic application.
