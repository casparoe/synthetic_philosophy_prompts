A professional licensing board for mental-health practitioners publishes a concise code of conduct listing categorical prohibitions—confidentiality breaches, exploitation of clients, falsification of credentials—framed as exceptionless duties. At the same time, its adjudication manual directs hearing panels to avoid invoking any listed rule as a sufficient reason for sanction. Instead, panels must construct what the manual terms a situational coherence narrative, weighing the particular emotional economy of the therapeutic relationship, the practitioner’s idiosyncratic history of supervision, and the institutional pressures specific to that practice setting. The board’s published disciplinary rationales never state that a specific rule was violated; they always conclude that the particular moral texture of this interaction made the practitioner’s conduct inexcusable. Meanwhile, the same board requires trainees to memorize the categorical code and to pass an examination that tests recognition of rule violations in standardized vignettes. What philosophical commitments about moral reasoning does this bifurcated structure presuppose? Does the examination regime presuppose moral generalism—the view that correct judgment depends on subsuming cases under general principles—while the adjudication protocol presupposes particularism—the view that moral reasons are irreducibly context-dependent and that no principle determines the right answer across cases? Or can a sophisticated generalist accommodate both levels, treating the code as a pedagogical heuristic and the narrative assessment as the application of highly context-sensitive principles? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and tell me what would have to change in examination design and in the writing of rationales if the board adopted a consistently particularist or consistently generalist framework.
