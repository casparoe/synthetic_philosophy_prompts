I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on Philosophy of Mind and Early Modern Thought, and in our recent unit on non-human consciousness a classmate presented what she called the standard Cartesian position on animal minds. She summarized it this way: Descartes held that animals are completely lacking in any inner mental life because they do not possess an immaterial rational soul; he treated the absence of human-like language not as mere empirical evidence but as a decisive metaphysical proof that no thinking substance could be present in them; therefore, he regarded all animal behavior—including apparent cries of pain—as purely mechanistic reflex comparable to clockwork, which makes moral concern for animal welfare conceptually incoherent within his framework. She added that this view is essentially an early form of behaviorism, except that Descartes explained reflexes through hydraulic nerves and pineal mechanics rather than conditioning.

The summary sounds authoritative, but I recall our instructor emphasizing distinctions in Descartes’ later correspondence—particularly with More and with Princess Elisabeth—between sensory cognition and intellectual judgment, and warning against reading the beast-machine thesis as a denial of every form of interiority rather than specifically of rational thought. I want to know whether this classroom summary is fair to Descartes. Please audit it carefully: identify which parts accurately reflect his metaphysics and arguments (especially regarding language, the soul, and mechanism), which parts are standard pedagogical simplifications, and where it subtly misrepresents him—such as by conflating the absence of a rational soul with the absence of all conscious sensation, or by deriving an explicit ethical stance on vivisection from premises that do not entail it. I am also curious whether comparing his position to modern behaviorism is genuinely illuminating or anachronistic, and whether the claim that animal suffering is conceptually incoherent for Descartes survives closer scrutiny of how he treated sensory perception in the Passions of the Soul.
