I'm a doctoral student in comparative literature working on postwar German moral philosophy, and I'm preparing a seminar on how narrative fiction negotiates inherited culpability. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher with deep familiarity with Karl Jaspers's typology of guilt and with twentieth-century German literature. Before you begin, please take a moment to outline Jaspers's four categories—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt—and briefly note why he insists that moral and metaphysical guilt are strictly non-heritable across generations.

Then read Bernhard Schlink's novel The Reader (and, where relevant, its cinematic adaptation) through this framework. I want to avoid reducing the novel to an allegory, so please attend to where it resists the philosophical reading as well as where it illuminates it.

First, Hanna's illiteracy is revealed as the hidden cause of her failure to defend herself at trial and of her acceptance of a disproportionately harsh sentence. Does the narrative treat her inability to read and write as an epistemic deficit that mitigates her moral comprehension, or does it risk conflating literacy with ethical agency in a way that Jaspers—who separates criminal responsibility from inner moral awareness—would reject? Second, Michael Berg's second-generation shame: he sends Hanna audiotapes but withholds the knowledge that could have altered her conviction. Is his shame best understood as Jaspersian political guilt shared by citizens of a perpetrator state, as an illegitimate inheritance of moral guilt that Jaspers denies can pass to children, or as a form of metaphysical solidarity with victims that the novel misattributes to personal culpability? Third, the climax turns on Hanna's prison suicide and Michael's later delivery of her savings to the survivor's daughter. Where does the work complicate philosophy—perhaps by suggesting that narrative empathy generates a guilt that Jaspers's schema cannot classify?

Please think carefully at each layer, keep both the philosophical distinctions and the literary details in view, and conclude by saying what The Reader adds to the debate about collective and transgenerational guilt that conceptual argument alone cannot provide. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; my students will have no external access during discussion.
