I'm taking a course that brings together predictive-processing accounts of cognition with classical Buddhist phenomenology, and I'm stuck on a tension between anattā and the structure of contemplative practice. On the predictive-processing view, the self is not a Cartesian ego but a hierarchical model continuously reconstructed to minimize prediction error; this seems compatible with impermanence, since the model is never identical across moments. Yet when I read descriptions of mindfulness—particularly in Abhidharma-influenced traditions—the practitioner is said to observe mental states arising and passing without identifying with them. My confusion is this: if there is genuinely no enduring witness, not even a minimal observer that persists across these observations, then in what sense is it the same practitioner who reports having seen impermanence? It seems I must either smuggle in a hidden essentialist substrate—some constant awareness that owns the experiences—or accept that every moment involves a literally different cognitive agent, which would make long-term moral cultivation incoherent, since there is no continuous subject to whom virtue or wisdom accrues.

So my question is whether this dilemma is genuine or rests on a misinterpretation. Does the Buddhist denial of self actually entail the absence of any functional continuity that could support narrative development, or is the predictive-processing model of a reconstructed self-model precisely the kind of conventional, dependently originated identity that anattā allows? I want an answer that addresses the false presupposition directly—particularly the idea that sustained practice requires a metaphysically enduring observer—rather than giving a general survey of Buddhist metaphysics. I'm especially interested in whether reconciling anattā with embodied cognition forces us to abandon the ordinary notion that personal growth happens to someone over time, or whether it allows us to keep that language as a conventional designation without ontological commitment.
