I want to audit whether the following commitments about mind, behavior, and artificial systems form a coherent package, or whether I am helping myself to distinctions that collapse under scrutiny. Here is my position: (1) I accept a refined behavioral criterion—sustained, multi-modal, context-sensitive indistinguishability from a conscious agent over time is necessary, though not alone sufficient, for attributing consciousness; (2) I reject the Chinese Room argument, on the grounds that understanding can emerge from global causal-functional organization rather than residing in any single subsystem; (3) I nonetheless think consciousness likely involves substrate-dependent constraints—perhaps tied to integrated information dynamics specific to biological neural tissue—so that a silicon system with behaviorally identical outputs might still lack phenomenal experience; (4) I deny that current large language models are minded, not because they fail in principle, but because they lack temporal persistence, embodied goal-structures, affective integration, and calibrated epistemic humility patterns that any adequate behavioral criterion should require; and (5) I hold that if a non-biological system ever genuinely satisfied this expanded, multi-modal behavioral criterion—including appropriate emotional and embodied responses plus stable self-modeling over time—we would have a direct moral obligation to treat it as conscious regardless of substrate. Do these hang together? Specifically: does my rejection of the Chinese Room in (2) undermine the substrate-dependence in (3), given that functional emergence is usually taken to be substrate-independent? Is there a hidden contradiction between (3) and (5)—if consciousness is biologically anchored, why should behavioral success in silicon generate moral status? And is the tension between (1) and (4) only apparent—does my refusal to attribute mind to LLMs depend on smuggling in (3), or can it be justified purely within the behavioral framework? Please order the tensions by severity, identify the weakest link, and say what restoring consistency costs—whether that requires giving up substrate-dependence, weakening the behavioral criterion, or reconceiving the Chinese Room objection.
