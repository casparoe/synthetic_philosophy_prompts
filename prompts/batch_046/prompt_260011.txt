I am writing a chapter on collective moral agency that applies recent philosophical work to the Fischer controversy, and a debate at a recent workshop left me with an argument I cannot exit cleanly. The issue is whether attributing blame for the First World War to Germany as a nation collapses into incoherence once we apply the criterion consistently. Here is the version I sketched:

(1) To hold a nation responsible for a war, we must identify a unified collective agent whose intentional actions were not structurally compelled and who possessed reasonable alternatives. (2) Fischer's archival case shows that in July 1914 the German leadership pursued a coherent strategic intention—using the Austro-Serbian crisis to secure continental hegemony through calculated risk—and had available diplomatic alternatives, including pressing Vienna for compromise and accepting British mediation proposals. (3) Therefore Germany, as a corporate agent, is blameworthy for initiating the war, not merely causally implicated.

(4) Apply the same criterion symmetrically: any nation whose leadership made intentional, unforced commitments that contributed to escalation satisfies the condition for corporate blame. (5) The Entente powers also made intentional alliance commitments and rejected certain de-escalation offers; they too had alternatives available. (6) So Britain, France, and Russia each meet the condition.

(7) Now, if blame requires identifying a specific collective agent with specific intentions, then saying "Germany is to blame" specifically requires that Germany's intentions were uniquely determinative of the outcome, otherwise the attribution lacks discriminating content. (8) Yet Fischer's own evidence, together with structural accounts of alliance dynamics, suggests the conflict was overdetermined: even absent the blank check to Austria, the system of commitments made escalation highly probable. (9) So either Germany's intentions were not uniquely determinative—in which case premise (3) fails—or they were determinative, but then the same determinative weight attaches to each power's final commitment, rendering all equally blameworthy and stripping "Germany is to blame" of any specific meaning. (10) Therefore either no nation bears corporate blame, or all do equally, which makes the Fischer thesis conceptually empty.

I want to preserve (1) through (3) if I can, and I am not inclined to deny (8) the overdetermination point. But I feel a slide somewhere between (6) and (7), or perhaps a scope shift in (9) about what determinative responsibility distributes over, though I cannot name it precisely. Which step should I reject, why did it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost—especially for the project of holding particular nations responsible rather than dissolving blame into structural causation? If I must sacrifice one premise, I would prefer to keep the core Fischer attribution intact, even at the price of complicating the symmetry argument.
