I spent the better part of last evening in a long argument with a close friend about whether Max Black’s symmetric-universe scenario—two macroscopic spheres, identical in every qualitative respect, suspended in an otherwise empty space—genuinely refutes Leibniz’s principle of the identity of indiscernibles. I defended a qualified Leibnizian position: the spheres are not indiscernible, because they stand in an irreflexive relational property to one another, namely being separated by a specific spatial distance, which amounts to weak discernibility rather than intrinsic discernibility. My friend then pressed a modified case: suppose spatial relations are not fundamental but emergent from symmetric quantum-entanglement correlations, so that in this universe the correlation matrix linking the two spheres is perfectly undirected and self-same, leaving no irreflexive relation that can distinguish one sphere from the other without also distinguishing it from itself. I want a rigorous map of what would actually count against my qualified position, not merely complicate it.

Specifically, I am asking which observations, formal results, or metaphysical commitments would be decisive rather than merely awkward; whether there are symmetric configurations involving not macroscopic objects but fundamental quantum structures where even weak discernibility collapses; and what defenders of the principle themselves would concede as genuinely damaging rather than what critics alone claim. I also want an honest assessment of whether the principle has been insulated—through restrictions to intrinsic properties, to actual worlds, or to non-emergent relations—into something unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that insulation is legitimate theoretical refinement or a fatal weakness for a metaphysical principle of this kind. What predictions, if any, does my view make that its rivals do not, and what would I personally have to accept if those predictions failed?
