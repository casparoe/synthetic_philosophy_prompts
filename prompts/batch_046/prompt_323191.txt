Consider the institutional architecture of major professional sports leagues: they treat player contracts as nearly absolute entitlements—prohibiting rival teams from poaching signed athletes without compensation—while simultaneously enforcing salary caps and revenue-sharing rules that prevent star players from receiving the full market value generated by the audiences they attract. This design seems to combine a strong protection of voluntary contractual holdings with an explicit pattern-based redistribution of economic surplus. Reconstruct the implicit theory of justice in holdings that this combination presupposes. Is it consistent with a Nozickian entitlement view, or does it quietly rely on a background principle—such as fair equality of competitive opportunity, or collective ownership of the spectacle—that the Wilt Chamberlain argument is meant to reject? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, explain whether the argument from voluntary exchange actually applies when payments flow through broadcast and sponsorship markets rather than direct spectator choice, and describe what institutional features would have to disappear or be added if the league adopted a purely entitlement-based system with no salary caps.
