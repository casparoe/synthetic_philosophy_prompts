I am preparing a discussion paper for an advanced seminar on the philosophy of emotion, and I find the familiar tripartite division—cognitive or judgmental theories, perceptual theories in the neo-Jamesian tradition, and pure feeling or bodily-state theories—unsatisfyingly abstract when removed from lived historical contexts. Rather than asking for definitions, I want to test how each framework performs when applied to a well-known episode in which emotional display, emotional absence, and emotional judgment were all structurally central: the Jerusalem trial of Adolf Eichmann in 1961. I take the salient facts to be common knowledge—the survivor testimony describing deportations, Eichmann’s own bureaucratic self-presentation and noted emotional flatness, the judges’ visible restraint, and the intense emotional reactions of observers—without requiring obscure archival detail.

Analyse this episode through each of the three accounts. For the judgmental view, treat the survivors’ grief and outrage as evaluative judgments about moral wrong; for the perceptual view, treat them as direct perceptions of injustice or danger; for the feeling view, treat them as internally registered bodily states with representational content only derivatively. Then apply the same frameworks to Eichmann’s emotional nullity and to the court’s disciplined suppression of visible reaction: was Eichmann lacking the relevant moral judgments, failing to perceive the moral salience of his actions, or simply not experiencing the corresponding bodily feelings? Was the judges’ restraint a suppression of judgment, of perceptual orientation, or of affective display? Crucially, do not force the theories to speak where they are silent; say precisely which features of the episode each account illuminates and which it misdescribes or leaves unexplained. Finally, consider what the case shows about whether emotional response is necessarily tied to moral cognition: if Eichmann could execute bureaucratic reasoning without the emotional correlates that the survivors displayed, does this support one account over the others, or does it reveal a limit common to all three?
