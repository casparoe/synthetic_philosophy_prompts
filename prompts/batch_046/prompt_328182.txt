Write an exam question for an upper-level philosophy of language and aesthetics seminar, using Douglas Hofstadter's reflections on rendering Clément Marot into English as a conceptual point of departure but not as a summary to be recited. Present this hypothetical case: a translator must render a short poem from Language A into Language B, where Language B lacks the phonemic opposition that generates the original rhyme scheme. Rather than abandon form, the translator preserves semantic prosody—the correlation between syntactic heaviness and emotional register—by restructuring line breaks and word order, yielding a version that violates conventional meter but maintains the weight-to-feeling mapping. Ask students to evaluate whether this substitution counts as preserving the form that matters, requiring them to distinguish three positions precisely: acoustic fidelity, structural isomorphism, and functional equivalence of reader response. The model answer should be about 600 words, apply Hofstadter's idea of the soul of a poem to adjudicate among these three positions without merely labeling them, defend one view, and address the strongest objection—namely, that the translated poem becomes intertextually unrecognizable within Language B's poetic tradition and thus severs the link that makes translation identifiable as translation. The marking scheme should total 25 marks: 10 for explaining why acoustic fidelity fails here and why that failure is not fatal to form; 10 for articulating the conceptual gap between structural isomorphism and functional equivalence; 5 for identifying the hidden intertextual cost of the translator's strategy. For each component, include a one-line note on the characteristic mistake—e.g., conflating meter with form generally, or treating reader response as purely subjective. Specify that candidates have 75 minutes and should write approximately 900 words. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or external reading lists.
