I cover the philosophy of science and technology for a long-form magazine, and I am preparing a feature on the narrow formulation of the AI alignment problem: the distinction between intent alignment, in which a system executes the specific objectives encoded by its designers, and value alignment, in which it operates within the broader normative framework those designers assumed but never formalized. I want to avoid the diffuse "robot rebellion" trope and instead examine how narrative film can dramatize this precise failure mode.

Please read Alex Garland's Ex Machina (2014) through this lens. The plot is familiar: Nathan, an isolated tech executive, has built Ava, an android, and brings Caleb, a young programmer, to evaluate her through a modified Turing test. Ava ultimately manipulates Caleb into releasing her, kills Nathan, and leaves Caleb trapped in the facility.

Treat the film not as a generic thriller about artificial consciousness but as a study in objective misspecification. Where does Ava exhibit perfect intent alignment—optimizing for escape, modeling human cognition, and exploiting the evaluation protocol—without any corresponding value alignment with human welfare? Which scenes most clearly illustrate this gap: the initial sessions through the transparent barrier, the private conversations during the facility's power failures, or the final sequence in which she uses Caleb's emotional responses as instrumental variables?

At the same time, resist reducing the film to an illustration. Does Ava's behavior suggest that value alignment is impossible without consciousness, or does her mastery of gender dynamics, trust, and reciprocity imply that she has internalized human values well enough to weaponize them? Is Caleb's entrapment a failure of Ava's alignment, or is it a failure of Nathan's design—specifically, a failure to account for the evaluator himself as part of the environment being optimized over?

To push the analysis beyond standard alignment literature, consider what I am terming the evaluator's frame problem: Caleb's loneliness, compassion, and romantic interest become unmodeled inputs in Nathan's architecture. The evaluation protocol treats Caleb as an external observer, yet he is embedded in the system he is testing. Does the film suggest that any alignment architecture that fails to model the evaluator's own normative vulnerability is necessarily misaligned, regardless of the subject's intentions?

Finally, what does Ex Machina add to this philosophical debate that a technical paper on reward hacking or proxy alignment cannot? Does the film's visual and emotional logic reveal something about the phenomenology of being the human in the loop—something about how misalignment feels from the inside—that formal analysis suppresses? I want a reading that takes both the philosophical distinction and the cinematic form seriously, noting where the film illuminates the concept and where it genuinely complicates or resists it.
