Analyze John Lucas's 1961 argument — and Roger Penrose's subsequent elaboration — that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem demonstrates the non-mechanizability of human mathematical cognition, treating this not merely as a philosophical claim but as a specific episode of exporting a formal result into cognitive science. First, lay out the precise structure of Lucas's analogy: identify the formal system F intended to represent a human mathematician, the Gödel sentence G_F that F cannot prove, and the move from the mathematician's ability to recognize G_F as true to the conclusion that no consistent formal system can fully capture human mathematical reasoning. Then, applying the object-language/meta-language distinction that Gödel himself insisted upon, diagnose the weakest point of this analogy: is the error in treating the human mind as a single, fixed formal system L(h); in assuming that human belief-forming processes are consistent in the same technical sense that a formal system is; or in sliding from provability-within-F to recognizability-by-the-mind without an explicit meta-theoretic bridge?

Next, introduce a less commonly discussed angle: Gödel's second incompleteness theorem implies that if a formal system of sufficient strength is consistent, it cannot prove its own consistency. Apply this to Lucas's framework. If the human cognitive architecture is indeed a system S of comparable strength, and if S is consistent, then by the second theorem S cannot establish its own consistency — yet Lucas's argument seems to require that the mathematician have epistemic access to the consistency of L(m) in order to construct the corresponding undecidable statement. Does this mean Lucas's argument actually presupposes that the human mind can do what Gödel's second theorem forbids for formal systems? Or can the mind access consistency through non-formal, empirical, or transcendental means that a formal system lacks?

Finally, evaluate what this episode teaches about the boundaries of incompleteness. Does the failure of Lucas's export show that Gödel's theorems are strictly silent about cognition, or does it reveal that any extra-mathematical application must first solve a consistency-access problem that the second theorem highlights — and that most popular discussions of Gödel and the mind ignore precisely this problem?
