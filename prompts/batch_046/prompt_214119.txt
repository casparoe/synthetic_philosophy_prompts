I'm taking a seminar on cognitive architecture and analogical reasoning, and I've become convinced by a specific reading of Hofstadter's Copycat project: that conceptual slippage in the alphabet-string microdomain—where notions like "successor," "group," or "left-right" shift their precise interpretation depending on analogical pressure—is not an engineering imperfection to be minimized but the structurally necessary mechanism that makes analogy possible at all. On this view, fixed symbolic representations are not merely insufficient; they are actively misleading, because the ambiguity in the problem space can only be resolved by allowing concepts to slide probabilistically among competing codelets, rather than by selecting a single correct representation in advance. What would genuinely count against this position? I want a clear map of its exposure: which formal results, empirical findings, or scaling failures would be decisive—for instance, a demonstration that a fixed-representation architecture can reproduce Copycat's analogy space without comparable variability—versus which would merely be awkward, such as human analogical errors that don't match Copycat's probabilistic patterns. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated against refutation, for example by interpreting every failure to scale to open-ended domains as evidence of "insufficient fluidity" rather than as a flaw in the fluidity model itself. Please distinguish what defenders of this position would themselves concede as damaging evidence from what only critics cite, and tell me whether this insulation, if present, is a genuine problem for a claim of this kind or just the ordinary protection of a research program.
