I'm not a philosopher or a social scientist by training—I've spent the last decade working in logistics and local governance projects in parts of West Africa and Southeast Asia, and I've been trying to think through something that keeps bothering me. I'm going to present my view to a small working group next month: about fifteen people, mostly development economists and governance consultants who build anti-corruption metrics, plus a few anthropologists who study patronage networks, and two former diplomats who now advise NGOs on transparency policy. None of them are philosophers; they care about whether interventions work and whether they're ethically defensible.

My position, which I've sketched out in about twenty pages of notes, is this. We usually call corruption whatever involves personal exchange that bypasses formal rules. But I've started to think the real distinction isn't between formal and informal—it's between exchanges that are mutually visible and acknowledged within the community that practices them, and exchanges that are deliberately hidden from that same community because they violate its own shared expectations. In other words, what Western frameworks treat as corruption—gift-giving to officials, clientelist distribution—is often fully transparent and reciprocal in its local context. The problem isn't the exchange; it's the framing that treats anonymous contract-based transactions as the universal moral baseline, and then labels everything else as deviation. I want to argue that universal anti-corruption standards should distinguish named reciprocity—where obligations are tracked and socially enforced—from concealed extraction, where someone takes without the community's knowledge or consent. And I want to push the stronger claim that imposing Western transparency audits on named reciprocity can actually destroy the trust structures that make local cooperation possible, which means the audit itself becomes a kind of corruption—an external imposition that severs social bonds without replacing them.

The audience will be skeptical. The economists will think I'm romanticizing patronage; the anthropologists will worry I'm sneaking in normative judgment; the NGO advisers will say I'm giving cover to kleptocrats. I need a prep sheet: the objections they'll actually raise, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each group puts it, with what I should concede immediately, what I should hold firmly, the weakest link in my own reasoning that I should defend rather than hide, and the one question I'm praying nobody asks—probably something about how my view applies to cases where local elites use reciprocity to exclude outsiders or to mask coercion. I should be able to reply in about two minutes per objection, with a clear note on where my reply is soft.

Please don't ask me for citations, bibliographies, or specific studies I haven't mentioned—I'm working from observation and reasoning, not from a literature review. And please don't treat me as an expert; I'm an amateur trying to get this right.
