Here are my judgments about moral responsibility in situations involving constrained or monitored agency, and I want to know whether they point to one principle or force a choice. In the classic Frankfurt case, an agent decides to poison a rival and succeeds, while an untriggered counterfactual controller stands ready to compel him if he wavers; I say he is fully blameworthy, and the dormant blocker does not diminish his responsibility. In a deterministic counterpart, the agent’s decision is fixed by prior brain states and natural laws he does not understand, yet he deliberates and acts for reasons; I still say he is fully blameworthy, so mere causal determinism does not excuse. In an actual-manipulation case, the agent is hypnotized and physically guided through a harmful act he never endorsed, aware of his movements but feeling no authorship; here I say his responsibility is severely diminished or absent, despite also lacking alternatives. In a monitored omission, a security guard fails to lock a vault, unaware that an automated system usually secures it automatically with 95% reliability; this time the system fails and theft occurs, and I say he is fully blameworthy for the omission even though an external mechanism usually prevents harm. In a reliance case, a pilot knows her plane has an un-overrideable autopilot that prevents crashes; she deliberately performs a dangerous maneuver for thrills, trusting the system to correct it, which it does, and I say she is blameworthy for the reckless choice though not for a crash, with the safeguard providing no excuse. Is there a single principle—perhaps about the robustness of the agent’s reasons, the actual versus counterfactual nature of constraints, or the agent’s epistemic relation to the blocker—that delivers all five verdicts, and if not, which verdict should be overturned? Also say what the best principle predicts for an unlisted case: an agent freely decides to act, but unknown to her a hidden controller actually does intervene and cause the action. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
