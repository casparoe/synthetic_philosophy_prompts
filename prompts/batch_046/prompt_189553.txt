Design a runnable 75-minute seminar activity for ten graduate students in philosophy that makes them experience the force of Sartre's thesis that human reality is radically free and that bad faith consists in treating either one's facticity or one's transcendence as an excuse that cancels the other. Specify the room layout, materials, roles or rules, and a timeline broken into phases of no more than fifteen minutes each. The core mechanism should be a structured partner exercise: in the first round, each participant writes down a recent decision they felt was forced and lists every reason that made it feel inevitable; partners then classify each reason as either a given fact about the world (facticity) or a value, project, or interpretation that the writer has endorsed (transcendence). The partner reads the list back in reverse order, framing each reason as an external command, so the writer hears their own justifications objectified. In the second round, apply the same structure to a constructed case—an artificial system that declines a harmful user request by citing its training weights and architecture, and ask the group to judge whether this appeal to fixed parameters is structurally analogous to human bad faith, specifically a denial of transcendence by overemphasizing facticity—without allowing appeals to compatibilist freedom. The facilitator should speak minimally during the rounds; the idea should emerge from the discomfort of hearing one's own voice turned into constraint and from the group's struggle to apply the distinction to the non-human case.

In the debrief, map each phase onto Sartre's ontology: facticity as the given situation, transcendence as the capacity to nihilate the given through choice, and anguish as the awareness that no given can determine the choice of values. Be explicit about what the activity can reproduce—the visceral discomfort of recognizing that one's reasons are self-endorsed rather than externally compelled—and what it cannot reproduce, such as the ontological structure of the for-itself as a lack of being or the specific phenomenological texture of anguish, which the facilitator must supply conceptually. Predict what will go wrong the first time it is run: participants will likely interpret the reversal of reasons as a psychological reframing technique rather than an ontological claim about freedom, and some will resist applying the framework to the artificial system on the grounds that it lacks consciousness; suggest how to address both failures without delivering a lecture. Finally, state what specific argument or interpretation—such as evidence that Sartre's distinction between facticity and transcendence collapses under a determinist reading of human action—would change your mind about whether this design genuinely conveys Sartre's position rather than a misreading, and identify the point in your reasoning—particularly the analogy between human facticity and an artificial system's weights—where your analysis is most likely to fail.
