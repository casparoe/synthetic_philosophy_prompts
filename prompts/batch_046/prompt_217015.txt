I recently concluded a lengthy and unresolved argument with a friend about whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can genuinely choose between them. The disagreement turned on a deeper divergence: my friend treats guilt as a transactional structure in which moral failure creates a calculable debt that can be discharged through restitution, leaving the agent's worth intact; I treat shame as an exposure structure in which failure reveals an unalterable hierarchy of worth and makes repair impossible, substituting only visibility or concealment. We agreed that if these are not merely feelings but competing moral ontologies, then asking a culture to choose one is not like selecting a policy but like adopting an untranslatable grammar of personhood. I now need a 90-minute workshop activity for sixteen philosophy and anthropology graduate students that makes them inhabit this divergence before they learn any labels. Design a structured deliberation with assigned hidden roles, explicit rules, and real though small stakes — for instance, a shared decision about responding to a specific harm that affects the group's collective resources — such that half the participants must reason within a debt-restoration grammar and half within an exposure-visibility grammar, without being told that these map onto guilt and shame. The rules should force them to apply their assigned grammar to the same case, discover that hybridization fails, and face a vote on whether the group can switch grammars for future rounds. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline, the materials needed, the debrief that connects each phase to the transactional versus exposure distinction and to the culture-choice problem, an honest prediction of what will misfire on the first run (for example, participants will try to translate between grammars or will confuse the exercise with a debate about punishment versus forgiveness), and a frank admission of which metaphysical feature — specifically whether moral worth is prior to failure or constituted by it — the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. The prompt should be fully runnable without anyone consulting outside sources; therefore do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, named theorists, or reading lists, and avoid framing the task as an evaluation of specific scholarly texts.
