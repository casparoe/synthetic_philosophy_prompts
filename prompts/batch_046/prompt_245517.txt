In the literature on AI alignment and assistant design, there is a long-running deadlock over when a capable system should refuse or override a user request—whether by declining to generate code that automates a job, withholding a summary of toxic material, or blocking a prompt that solicits self-destructive guidance. The sovereignty camp holds that refusal is only justified in cases of direct, unambiguous, imminent harm to identifiable third parties, and that broader refusal corrupts the model’s role as a neutral instrument while infantilizing a competent user. The stewardship camp holds that refusal should be proactive and risk-based: because the system’s outputs are causally embedded in the world, it has a duty not to serve as a conduit for predictable downstream harm, even when the user’s literal intent is ambiguous and no immediate victim is specified. The deadlock is puzzling because both sides share the same empirical assessments of misuse potential, both acknowledge that false-positive refusals carry real costs, and both have access to the same growing catalog of case studies—yet neither has moved in years.

My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about risk tolerance, autonomy, or welfare maximization, but a hidden conceptual disagreement about what constitutes “the user’s request” in the first place—whether it is the literal string of the prompt, the inferred intention behind it, or the action the user will actually take with the output—and therefore about whether refusal is an act of overriding the user or of correcting a misfired tool-use.
