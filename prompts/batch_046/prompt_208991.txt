I am currently weighing whether to undergo a complete corpus callosotomy for intractable epilepsy, and the prospect has made the philosophical literature on divided consciousness suddenly urgent for me rather than abstract. In my reading, I encountered a summary—presented in a popular neuroscience feature whose author I no longer recall—that attempts to translate the classic split-brain findings into philosophical terms. The summary states that after full commissurotomy the brain supports two numerically distinct conscious subjects, each with its own beliefs, intentions, and experiential stream. It asserts that linguistic self-report is confined entirely to the left hemisphere, rendering the right hemisphere a sophisticated but non-conscious behavioral automaton that lacks any inner life. It describes the left-hemisphere interpreter module as generating post-hoc narratives exclusively for actions initiated by the right hemisphere, which, according to the summary, demonstrates that the right hemisphere possesses neither introspective access to its own motives nor any representational content that could be shared across hemispheres. The summary then draws a philosophical conclusion: empirical neuroscience has dissolved the traditional problem of the unity of consciousness by showing that there is no single unified subject of experience, only two co-located minds with no mental overlap, so debates about phenomenal integration versus joint accessibility are empirically moot. Something about this feels too tidy, and I worry it conflates behavioral dissociation with the absence of consciousness, but I am not expert enough to locate the specific errors. Is this summary fair to the experimental record and to the philosophical positions at stake? Please distinguish what is accurate or a defensible simplification, what is a subtle misrepresentation of the neuroscience, and what defenders of hemispheric unity—or of a more nuanced dual-mind view—would object to. In particular, I would like to know whether the summary correctly applies the distinction between phenomenal unity and joint accessibility, or whether it collapses them into a single claim that the data do not actually support.
