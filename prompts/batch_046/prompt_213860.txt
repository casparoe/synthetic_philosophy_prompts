Student answer (paraphrased from their handwritten page):

Dennett uses the coffee switch to show that people can't reliably tell Chase from Sanborn once they've switched, and they can't even recall what the old taste was like. This shows that the supposed intrinsic feel of coffee doesn't do any real explanatory work, because if it existed it should let you discriminate the two brands even after years of drinking the new one. Dennett also argues that because qualia are ineffable, neuroscience can't study them, so they're epistemically isolated from everything that produces knowledge. That means they're incoherent—not just hidden, but actually contradictory, since anything that exists has to be able to cause effects. So Dennett is basically an eliminativist: he thinks there are no qualitative experiences at all, just judgments about them. The reply that I still have a feeling right now misses the point because Dennett isn't denying present feelings, he's denying there's a special non-physical property called a quale. So the reply actually concedes his argument. Overall, Dennett proves that the standard four-property view is incoherent, but he doesn't prove that experience itself is unreal—just that we should talk about it in functional terms instead.
