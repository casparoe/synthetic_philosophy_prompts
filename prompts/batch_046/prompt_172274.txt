I am presenting a 30-minute colloquium paper next Tuesday defending the calibration-output thesis: indirect normativity is not mediated first-order normativity but architectural normativity that applies exclusively to the calibration procedures of practical reasoning. On this view, an idealized deliberator—stripped of cognitive bias and supplied with full information—does not deliver direct prescriptions for particular actions but outputs a second-order calibration profile that specifies which local revision rules are legitimate for a non-ideal agent. I distinguish calibration norms, which constrain the architecture of reasoning and govern how weights are updated, from execution norms, which constrain particular actions, and I defend the claim that idealization can access only the former because any attempt to derive particular actions from the idealized state necessarily reintroduces the very contextual biases the idealization was meant to eliminate. My argument proceeds in three steps: first, that idealization removes first-order motivational noise while preserving structural commitments; second, that structural commitments cannot determine particular actions without smuggling in local context; and therefore, third, that the legitimate output of ideal deliberation is a meta-procedure for local adjustment rather than a direct normative verdict.

The audience is a philosophy department colloquium of roughly eighteen people: three practical-reason specialists who work on ideal-observer theories and will defend direct-output accounts; two Kantians who will worry that any indirect move evacuates autonomy and collapses into heteronomy; one virtue ethicist who will ask whether this reduces practical wisdom to procedural rule-following; and several metaethics graduate students likely to press whether this is just constructivism relabeled.
