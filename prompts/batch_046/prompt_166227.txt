Consider the claim that, holding per-capita lifetime well-being constant at a mildly positive level and assuming only voluntary, non-coercive measures, there is an all-things-considered moral reason for a society to expand its population toward the long-run ecological carrying capacity, even if this compresses the per-generation resource share and accelerates depletion within sustainable bounds. Give me your credence that this claim is true—expressed as a number between 0 and 1—then decompose the reasoning: assign rough probabilities to (a) whether total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, or a critical-level view supplies the correct axiological framework for comparing populations of different sizes; (b) whether the non-identity problem undermines the coherence of saying that a larger population is strictly “better” rather than merely “not worse” for the people who exist; (c) whether ecological limits operate as a deontological side-constraint that rules out maximization altogether, or only as a consequentialist parameter that lowers the optimal population without eliminating the growth reason; and (d) whether a factor we might call relational scarcity—the hypothesis that well-being depends partly on the density of non-instrumental social bonds, and that these bonds dilute in a non-linear fashion as population scales—should independently push the optimal population downward even when material welfare per person is held fixed. For each component, state roughly how much it contributes to or subtracts from your final number, show how they combine, and identify the single sub-question that, if you revised your view on it, would move your overall credence the most.
