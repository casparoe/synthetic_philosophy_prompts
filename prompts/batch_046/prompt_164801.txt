I am working through my own judgments about the moral permissibility of group-targeted selection mechanisms in education, employment, and governance, and I want to see whether they cohere around a single principle or whether I must abandon one. Here are my verdicts.

First, I take race-conscious holistic admissions at American universities—where race is one factor among many in an individualized review aimed at educational diversity and remedying societal discrimination—to be permissible, provided they avoid rigid point allocations.

Second, I take Norway's statutory boardroom gender quota, which mandates that at least forty percent of board members of listed public companies be women and can result in the disqualification of otherwise qualified male candidates, to be impermissible.

Third, I take India's constitutional reservation system, which sets hard percentage quotas for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes in public universities and government posts, to be permissible, even when qualified general-category candidates are thereby excluded.

Fourth, I take legacy preference in university admissions—giving weight to applicants with alumni parents—to be impermissible.

Fifth, I take a hypothetical policy in which a philosophy department sets aside thirty percent of its tenure-track lines specifically for scholars trained in non-Western philosophical traditions, with the explicit aim of correcting a disciplinary epistemic monoculture, to be permissible, even if this means passing over a stronger candidate in the Western analytic tradition.

Sixth, I take a policy that automatically reserves board seats for individuals within a particular disability category regardless of whether the pool of qualified candidates in that category is sufficient to fill them without lowering competence thresholds, to be impermissible.

These six judgments feel right to me, but I am aware that they pull in different directions. The second and third both involve hard numerical quotas that can displace qualified individuals, yet I reject one and accept the other. The first and fifth both invoke diversity or corrective aims, but one operates within a flexible holistic framework and the other through a rigid disciplinary allocation. The fourth and sixth both involve selection criteria that seem arbitrary or insufficiently tied to merit, yet my reasoning for rejecting them may differ in ways I have not fully articulated.

Is there one principle that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which verdict is the outlier and why? Please state the principle precisely—specifying the conditions under which group-targeted selection is justified, the role of historical or structural injustice, the relevance of competence thresholds, and the distinction between flexible and rigid mechanisms—explain which case motivates each clause, predict its verdict on at least one further case I have not mentioned (for instance, a class-based affirmative action scheme that uses income but explicitly excludes race, or a quota requiring gender parity on editorial boards of academic journals), and be explicit about which of my six judgments the principle must overturn. Please also say whether the tension between my second and third judgments dissolves on closer inspection or requires giving up one of them. Write your response in American English.
