As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am trying to settle my own thinking on a problem that sits at the intersection of Darwin and Turing: when should functional competence be taken as evidence of underlying comprehension, and when does it signal comprehension’s absence? I have formed stable judgments about particular cases, but they seem to pull in opposite directions, and I want to know whether they can be unified.

Here are my verdicts.

In the evolutionary domain, natural selection’s production of the vertebrate eye—a system with optical precision that far exceeds any understanding possessed by its parts—strikes me as pure competence without comprehension: there is design-like success, but no designer and no mental grasp of optics. I reach the same verdict about a bacterial quorum-sensing network that optimizes collective metabolism without any cell comprehending the group dynamics.

In the artificial domain, a large language model that explains quantum entanglement in fluent, accurate prose, yet was trained only on statistical text patterns and has never physically interacted with quantum systems, strikes me as displaying sophisticated competence that mimics comprehension; I do not think it actually understands entanglement, even when its outputs are correct. By contrast, a hypothetical machine that not only explains entanglement but consistently revises its explanations in response to novel experimental anomalies—tracking the actual physics rather than linguistic correlations—strikes me as having crossed into genuine comprehension, even if its architecture is entirely non-biological.

The human cases are what create the tension. A split-brain patient whose left hemisphere invents a fluent but false narrative to explain an action initiated by the right hemisphere shows, I think, linguistic competence without comprehension of the true cause: the explanation is articulate but unmoored. Yet a chess grandmaster who plays brilliant moves by intuition and cannot articulate the strategic principles behind them still seems to me to possess genuine comprehension of chess—the understanding is present but tacit, not absent.

So my pattern is this: I deny comprehension in evolutionary and bacterial success despite complex functional design; I deny it in the language model despite articulate, correct reasoning, apparently because its reasoning is not causally tied to the reality it describes; but I affirm it in the grandmaster despite an absence of explicit, articulable reasoning. Is there one principle that delivers all four verdicts—evolutionary eye, quorum sensing, tacit grandmaster intuition, and the two artificial cases—or must I overturn one?

Please take a definite position: state the principle precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, say which of my verdicts it forces me to drop if any, and defend why that cost is acceptable. Do not merely survey options; commit to the account you think best.

Also tell me what your principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a robotic system that learns through direct physical interaction with quantum devices, developing predictive behavioral models that it cannot verbalize, but that guide its actions with the same adaptive precision as the bacterial network. Does it have comprehension, mere competence, or both?

Finally, be explicit about where your answer leans on a contested interpretation of Darwin’s or Turing’s inversions, and note any point where you think my framing risks conflating functional success with mental presence.
