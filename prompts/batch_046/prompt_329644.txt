My roommate and I got in a huge arguement after I told her I'm trying to rethink moral choices since my dad got sick and I had to choose between staying with him and keeping the job that pays for his care. I said maybe Buddhist ethics is basically consequentialist - karma is cause and effect, so you just maximize good results. She said thats totally wrong, cetana (intention) is the actual moral factor, and the Vinaya precepts are absolute, like never killing regardless of outcome. Then I brought up those bodhisattva upaya stories where an enlightened person kills one robber to save hundreds of merchants - thats weighing outcomes, right? She said thats only for beings with perfect knowledge of karma, and for everyone else the rules are non-negotiable, so its not consequentialism. I countered that if the rules depend on knowing outcomes, the underlying theory is still outcome-based with an epistemic filter. She came back saying no, because even that enlightened killer still generates negative karma for themselves personally - they accept the harm to their own mental stream out of compassion, not because the net result justifies it. So the system tracks individual mental states, not aggregate outcomes. I feel like she's right about something but I'm missing the part where Abhidharma distinguishes kusala roots from just "good results." Can you adjudicate point by point? Is she right that upaya doesn't prove consequentialism? Is she right that the personal karma-cost makes it non-consequentialist? And what are we both missing about how intention and mental continuum actually work in this framework?
