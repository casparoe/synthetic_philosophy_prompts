I work with predictive-processing and active-inference models, and I want to test whether the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem survives honest translation. Take the core debate—that performing the morally right act for self-interested, prudential, or otherwise inappropriate motives is deficient even when the behavior is correct—and restate it in the vocabulary of precision-weighted prediction errors, prior beliefs about value, and the distinction between epistemic (belief-updating) and pragmatic (action-selecting) inference. For each of the three main stances—the internalist who requires moral belief itself to be the direct generator of action, the externalist who accepts any reliable causal pathway from belief to behavior, and the hybrid who demands alignment between predicted reward signals and moral evaluation—specify what maps cleanly onto active-inference mechanisms, what must be distorted to fit (for instance, importing a normative “ought” that has no analog in prediction-error minimization), and what has no expression in this vocabulary at all. Pay particular attention to whether “moral worth” can be reconstructed without smuggling in a teleological prior that the framework does not supply. Conclude by judging whether the untranslatable residue reveals a genuine gap in predictive processing’s account of motivated action, or whether the original problem was trading on an ambiguity in the word “reason” that separates cleanly into causal history versus precision weighting once the translation is made rigorous.
