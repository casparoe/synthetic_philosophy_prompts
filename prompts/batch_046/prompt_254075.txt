I work in software engineering, not philosophy, but I keep noticing a parallel between how we classify system failures and how international law treats mass violence. There is a specific legal category of genocide—shaped by Raphael Lemkin's work and codified in international treaty law—that requires proof of intent to destroy a protected group, in whole or in part. Governments and courts apply this label selectively: mass killings that lack the precise intent element are classified as ethnic cleansing, war crimes, or crimes against humanity, which activates different legal obligations and political responses. Meanwhile activists often insist that the moral reality is continuous—targeted group destruction comes in degrees—while the law treats it as a binary threshold that either triggers a severe response regime or does not.

What philosophical commitments about moral categories, group identity, and the link between description and obligation does this selective labeling practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the binary framework assume that genocide is a qualitatively distinct wrong rather than an extreme point on a spectrum? Does it presuppose that intent is the morally decisive feature, rather than scale, pattern, or foreseeable consequence? Or is the practice mainly driven by evidentiary and enforcement concerns, leaving the underlying moral ontology philosophically underdetermined?

Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and say whether rival theories—say, one that treats targeted destruction as a matter of degree and distributes obligations across a graded scale—could operate within the same institutional structures. Finally, if we replaced the implicit view with such a graded commitment, what concrete changes would be required in how institutions name atrocities and assign responsive duties?
