My study-buddy in decision theory keeps citing Good's theorem — that free information can't hurt a rational agent who updates right — to defend her takes on whether you should learn stuff. Her verdicts so far:

- free genetic screening for an untreatable condition? yes, take it, knowledge is power even with no cure.
- market study for a product you've already contracted to build regardless? skip it, hands are tied.
- proof your partner cheated ten years ago but you're staying together? don't open that box, it'll just poison things.
- extra chess analysis of lines you already rejected? sure, run it, free computation can't hurt.
- a jurror seeing a witness's private diary that doesn't change legal facts but might bias them? exclude it, information that corrupts reasoning isn't real information.

Now there's a new case: a tenured physicist can open one envelope that contains two pages printed together — page one is the exact counterfactual career she would have had if she picked philosphy over physics, page two is whether her current theory is correct. She can't open just page two; it's the same sheet. The career page won't change her job, but might cause regret. The theory page could redirect her research. Should she open it?

I'm less interested in yes/no than in the rule she's actually running. Is she applying Good's theorem straight — rational agents filter noise and only use what's action-relevant? Or is she running a 'no-corruption' rule where bundled emotional harm taints the whole package? Or maybe a pre-commitment rule about not revisiting foreclosed paths? Which of her earlier cases best supports each, and what's the one further case that would actually split them?
