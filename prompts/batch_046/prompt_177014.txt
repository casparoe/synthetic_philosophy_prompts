Examination question: Consider the case of The Ascending Line. In 1952 the sculptor Elena Voss carved a single statue from one block of Carrara marble. In 1988 museum conservators discovered internal salt crystallization and, over three years, replaced approximately sixty percent of the marble molecule by molecule with marble from the same quarry, preserving the external form exactly; the extracted original marble was ground into aggregate and incorporated into the foundation of a new museum wing. In 2020 the artist Marcus Chen acquired records of that foundation, extracted some of the aggregate, and re-carved a new statue with identical dimensions and surface texture, titling it The Descending Line. The museum claims it still possesses The Ascending Line; Chen claims his work is the original statue restored, while the museum's object is a replica. Discuss whether the museum's 1988–2020 object is identical to Voss's 1952 object, whether Chen's 2020 object is identical to either, and what the correct metaphysical analysis is. In your answer, address the statue-and-matter problem, the relevance of artistic intention, and at least one argument involving modal properties or persistence conditions.

Student answer: The museum's object is not identical to the 1952 original, because the original marble was destroyed and replaced, which is the classic statue and clay problem. The statue is made of marble, but the marble is not the statue. As David Wiggins argued in his constitution theory, an object can be constituted by matter without being identical to that matter, because the statue has different modal properties: it could not survive being melted into a block, whereas the marble could. Therefore, the 1952 statue and the marble coincided but were distinct. When the marble was replaced, the original statue was destroyed, because its constituent matter was destroyed. The museum's object is therefore a new statue constituted by new marble.

However, Chen's 2020 object is not the original either. Although it contains some of the original marble, it lacks the correct artistic origin. The original was created by Voss with a specific intention; Chen's object has a different origin and different artistic properties, so it is a different statue.

Some philosophers, like the perdurantist, might say the statue is a four-dimensional worm extending through time. Since the museum's object shares temporal parts with the 1952 object—the first forty percent of marble—they are actually the same four-dimensional object, just with parts replaced, like a ship with replaced planks. Therefore the museum is right. But this fails because perdurantism requires that all temporal parts belong to the same worm, and here the replaced parts are not continuous with Chen's object, so the museum's statue is a different worm from Chen's. Thus Chen is wrong, but so is the museum: its object is a replica, not the original.
