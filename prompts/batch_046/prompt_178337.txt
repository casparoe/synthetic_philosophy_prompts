I was talking with my roommate last night about Émilie du Châtelet — specifically that essay she wrote on happiness — and I realised I need to open tomorrow's interdiscplinary workshop with something that actually uses her idea instead of just name-dropping her.

The audience is split: about half are historians of science who know she argued that study and the rejection of illusion are prerequisites for happiness, not just accessories; the other half are grad students in physics and literature who have never heard of her. I want a four-minute opening that does real structural work. First, explain her view in plain terms: happiness isn't passive pleasure but an active state you maintain by refusing false consolations, especially the ones that come from ignorance about how nature actually works. Second, push this somewhat niche reading that her position is structurally closer to Spinoza's intellectual love of God than to Epicurean ataraxia, because for her understanding Newtonian mechanics literally frees the mind from the fear and superstition that make people miserable. Make that comparison shape what I can say — for instance, that doing hard physics and philosophy together isn't an indulgence but a kind of mental hygiene. Don't decorate with biography; build the remarks around the argument. And please, no bibliography or 'for further reading' — half the room will zone out, and I don't want to pretend this is a lecture.
