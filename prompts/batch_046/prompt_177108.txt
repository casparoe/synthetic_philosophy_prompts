I serve on the acquisitions and display ethics committee of a mid-sized museum of modern and contemporary art, and we are trying to resolve a new proposal in light of our past rulings. Our record is as follows. First, we denied a loan request to exhibit a technically flawless eighteenth-century studio copy of a lost Vermeer, executed in identical pigments by a documented pupil using period methods; experts agreed it was visually and materially indistinguishable from an authentic Vermeer without chemical analysis, but we ruled that it lacks the specific causal chain linking the object to Vermeer’s own intentional act. Second, we approved the display of a severely damaged Mondrian canvas in which approximately thirty percent of the surface had been reconstructed by conservators using modern acrylic, on the condition that the reconstructions remain visibly distinguishable in texture; we held that the surviving substrate retains the core intentional structure of the original work. Third, we rejected a proposal to include a recently discovered, expertly executed forgery of a Cézanne still life in a dedicated “truth and deception” gallery wing, even with prominent labeling and comparative analysis; our reasoning was that its presence within the museum’s authoritative space confers an undeserved ontological parity upon it. Fourth, we approved substituting a fragile, light-sensitive watercolor in our permanent collection with a pigment-matched facsimile for the public viewing gallery, arguing that the viewer’s aesthetic encounter is preserved and the original is protected from deterioration. Fifth, we rejected an AI-generated image produced by training a model exclusively on the complete oeuvre of a living painter and rendering a new composition in that painter’s characteristic palette and compositional logic; we found that although no single element was copied, there was no unified act of artistic decision binding the image to a person.

The new case: we have been offered an “archival reconstruction” of a destroyed sculptural installation. The original artist left exhaustive written instructions, material specifications, and sketches, but never realized the work. A contemporary fabricator has now executed the installation exactly according to those specifications, resolving ambiguities in the sketches solely by reference to the artist’s other documented works from the same period, using only historically appropriate materials and techniques. The result corresponds precisely to art-historical descriptions of the lost piece, but the fabricator made no independent aesthetic choices; every decision was derived from the artist’s archive. There is no direct visual copying of a surviving original, since the original was destroyed without photographic record.

We need to decide whether to include this reconstruction in our upcoming retrospective of the original artist’s work. If we want to be consistent with our own precedents, what should our verdict be? Which of our prior rulings is the closest analog, and where exactly does the analogy hold or break? More importantly, our precedents seem compatible with more than one underlying principle—perhaps one centered on causal provenance and the artist’s hand, one on the preservation of intentional structure regardless of executant, and one on the integrity of the curatorial narrative and the risk of ontological confusion. For each of these candidate principles, say what it predicts about this reconstruction, identify which prior ruling it explains best and which it struggles with, and then state which principle we would effectively be adopting if we approve, and which if we reject. Finally, what further case—one that does not involve digital media, AI, or photographic reproduction—would best distinguish these principles from one another?
