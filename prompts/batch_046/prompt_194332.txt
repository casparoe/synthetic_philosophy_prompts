Next Thursday I'm presenting at our department's bioethics colloquium — audience is mostly analytic moral philosophers, one disability-studies scholar who writes on genetic determinism, and a grad student in reproductive law who thinks every bioethics paper is secretly a policy brief. I'm kinda nervous because I'm defending the claim that liberal eugenics, the usual state-neutrality-plus-parental-choice setup, doesnt actually give parents any reason to select at all unless you pair it with something like Savulescu's procreative beneficence. But I want to push beneficence in a weird direction: instead of selecting for traits like health or intelligence, parents should aim at what I'm calling "modal option-preservation" — picking genetic profiles that keep the widest range of future life-plans open regardless of how social institutions change. So a child could become a musician or a mathematician or live in a low-tech commune without their genome being a mismatch.

The disability-studies person will probably say I'm just dressing up ableism in modal logic; the law student will worry this collapses into state-mandated optimization; and the philosophers will ask whether "option-space" is just perfectionism with extra steps. I need the objections ordered by how hard they'll hit and how likely they are, phrased the way each of them would actually phrase them in the room, with notes on what to concede versus what to hold. I especially need help with the weak spot I know I'm hiding — I haven't solved the non-identity problem for option-preservation, since selecting for flexibility changes who comes into existence — and I want to defend that honestly rather than hope they miss it. There's also the objection I'm praying nobody asks: if social institutions are what actually constrain life-plans, isn't genetic selection just a distraction from structural reform? Give me reply strategies that dont require citing papers I haven't read, since I can't pull up sources during the Q&A and I dont want to bluff a reference.
