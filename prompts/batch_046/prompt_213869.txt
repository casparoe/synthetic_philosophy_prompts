I've been stuck in this grad seminar on American political development where we keep circling back to American exceptionalism, and honestly it's getting frustrating. One group treats it like a straight-up historical thesis you can test against other countries — like, did the US actually skip a feudal stage, or is its welfare state genuinely smaller if you control for federalism and race — and they keep pulling out comparative stats on union density and healthcare outcomes. Another group says that's totally missing the point, because exceptionalism isn't a factual claim at all but a normative ideology that naturalizes settler violence and empire, so asking "is it true" is like asking if a justification is true. Then there's this smaller contingent that says exceptionalism works best as a comparative framing device — not true or false, just useful for highlighting deviation — and they think both other sides are treating a methodological lens like a proposition. We've been at this for months with zero movement: the empiricists say the critics are just changing the subject, the critics say the empiricists are treating ideology as data, and the framing people say everyone else is reifying something that only makes sense as a heuristic. I keep wondering if the stall isn't about politics but about what kind of thing exceptionalism actually is — is it an exemption claim (the US sits outside normal historical laws), an intensification claim (it's an extreme version of them), or a normative alibi that disguises particular violence as universal destiny? And if it's somehow all three at once, are we actually disagreeing about standards of evidence, about which national cases count as valid comparators, or about whether you're even allowed to ask "is it true" of a normative framework? Is there a hidden formal question in here — maybe about whether comparative history needs counterfactual baselines to mean anything — that's doing all the real work? And given whatever diagnosis you have, is there a distinction or change of question that could actually move us, or is the whole debate just badly formed and we should stop expecting progress?
