I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" is really a parable about normative justification or just a sentimental shock-piece. Read it through the lens of indirect normativity and idealized deliberation: take seriously the view that correct norms are those an ideally rational, fully informed agent would endorse, rather than rules applied directly to particular cases. Do the citizens who stay represent direct rule-following without idealized reflection, and do the ones who walk away represent the disruptive output of such reflection? Focus on the moment the child is revealed and the departure scene. Give a committed reading: does the story vindicate indirect normativity by showing that direct cost-benefit reasoning fails to capture the normative force of leaving, or does it expose a flaw—that idealized deliberation can demand actions (abandoning one's entire community) that seem disproportionate and therefore undermine the authority of the ideal? Don't reduce the story to an illustration; say specifically where the narrative resists the philosophical framework and what that resistance teaches us about idealized norms that abstract argument alone misses.
