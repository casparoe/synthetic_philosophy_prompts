This question has become personally relevant because I am currently preparing a short philosophical essay for an exhibition committee that must decide whether to display reconstructed works by a recently disgraced creator, and I want to move beyond the familiar separationist debate—whether immoral artists can produce good art—by focusing on a narrower, less discussed problem that I am calling “editorial reconstruction”.

Here are my notes for the essay. I argue that when a work is posthumously remastered, restored, or assembled by third parties (producers, restorers, archivists), the artefact encountered by the audience is not identical to the original creation but a composite entity involving the judgement and labour of living collaborators. Consequently, the standard question—“does the artist’s immorality taint the work?”—fragments into three: does the stain attach to the original artefact, to the reconstructed version, or to the institutional framing that presents them together? I apply this to three cases: (1) a painting restored with substantial aesthetic judgement by conservators after the painter’s death and subsequent moral disgrace; (2) an album assembled from unfinished recordings by producers who made significant compositional choices; (3) a film re-edited by a studio following the director’s disgrace, altering narrative emphasis. I tentatively suggest that separation is easier to defend for the original artefact but much harder for the reconstructed version, since the latter involves the moral agency of living collaborators who may be complicit in presenting the disgraced creator’s vision.

I would be grateful for your comments on the following: first, whether my distinction between the original artefact and the reconstructed composite is philosophically coherent, or whether it collapses into a standard institutional theory of art; second, whether the three cases genuinely differ in ways that matter for moral evaluation, or whether I am inventing distinctions without a difference; third, whether my tentative conclusion—that separation becomes harder for reconstructed works—follows from the premises, or whether it illegitimately assumes that collaboration equals moral endorsement; and fourth, whether the essay as sketched is interesting enough to warrant development, or if the framing is too niche to be meaningful without extensive background on curatorial practice.
