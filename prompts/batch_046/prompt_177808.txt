I'm preparing a ninety-minute session for an interdisciplinary philosophy reading group—graduate students in literature and cognitive science—who have worked through Locke's account of personal identity and Parfit's reductionism but have never studied Buddhist philosophy. I want them to grasp anattā not as a nihilistic denial of the person, but as the denial of a permanent, unitary, controlling substrate (svabhāva) within the flow of the five skandhas, while explicitly disentangling it from the Western bundle-theory framing they are likely to impose. Plan the explanatory arc: the precise order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced so that anattā reads as a specific metaphysical claim about dependently originated processes rather than a mystical slogan or a psychological self-help mantra; indicate where the distinction between conventional designation and ultimate analysis must enter relative to the skandhas, and why that ordering prevents the audience from treating the aggregates as hidden parts of a missing whole.

For the carrying example, I want something that makes continuity without ownership visceral rather than abstract—specifically, a multi-day collaborative installation in which rotating contributors alter a shared physical structure without retaining individual authorship, so I need to know exactly what properties this example must possess and what it must lack to avoid collapsing back into either a hidden-owner view or a mereological sum. Map the predictable misconceptions in the sequence they will arise: the nihilism misreading ("so no one is here at all"), the aggregation fallacy (treating rūpa, vedanā, and the rest as fragments of an absent substrate), the narrative-ownership error (assuming that without a self, autobiographical coherence becomes impossible), and the conflation with Humean bundle theory; specify at which point in the arc each should be intercepted and how. Include one diagnostic question I can pose at the end to distinguish whether they have understood anattā as a claim about processual continuity or have merely replaced one substance with a "bundle-substance," and state explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level—whether Abhidharma taxonomy, Nāgārjuna's emptiness, or the mechanics of rebirth—and why leaving it out protects rather than weakens the core idea.
