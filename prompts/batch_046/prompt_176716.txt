Here are my gut reactions to cases of epistemic injustice, and I'm trying to see if there's a pattern or if I'm just being incohearent. Case 1: a patient describing chronic pain is dismissed by doctors because she is a woman and they think she's exagerating -- clear injustice. Case 2: a junior researcher from a small university proposes a weird but correct theory, and senior people ignore it because of institutional prestige -- also injustice, but feels different, more like unfairness in evaluation. Case 3: a community has no word for climate grief, so their experience isn't taken seriously by policymakers -- hermeneutical injustice, I think, and it feels structurally different from case 1. Case 4: an AI chatbot is trained mostly on Western texts and consistently misinterprets a user's culturally specific metaphor, leading the user to be seen as irrational -- I feel like this is also epistemic injustice, but I'm not sure if the harm is done by the AI or by the people who deploy it. Case 5: a person with a strong regional accent is repeatedly asked to repeat themselves in a meeting, not because people can't understand but because they associate the accent with lower competence -- injustice. Case 6: someone is told their trauma narrative doesn't fit the standard clinical categories and is therefore denied treatment; but here the categories themselves seem flawed, not just the listener. I think case 1 and 5 are testimonial, case 3 and 6 hermeneutical, and case 4 is something new -- maybe 'infrastructural'? But is there one principle behind all of these? Or should I separate them into kinds and accept that there's no unified account? And what about a case I haven't mentioned: a group develops a new concept for a mental state through social media, and mainstream psychiatry refuses to recognize it for ten years -- is that injustice, or just slow science? Please tell me what principle, if any, captures my verdicts, which ones I have to drop to keep it simple, and what it predicts for that last case.
