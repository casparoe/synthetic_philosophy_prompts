Consider this examination question: A philosopher—designated Dr. K.—is assessing whether she ought to reduce her confidence in the belief that compatibilism about free will is true, given sustained disagreement among specialists of comparable training. She also weighs the inside view: she possesses specific distinctions—between source and leeway incompatibilism, for instance—that she judges compelling. The difficulty becomes reflexive when she asks how to evaluate her own judgment about whether she has selected the appropriate reference class in the first place. Analyze how the inside/outside distinction operates at this second-order level; determine whether its recursive application generates a vicious regress, a stable equilibrium, or another structure; and explain what this implies for the scope and limits of epistemic modesty.

The student submitted the following answer. The inside view involves reasoning from the particular details of a case—Dr. K.’s specific arguments about compatibilism, the distinctions she draws, and her assessment of their strength—whereas the outside view involves treating oneself as a member of a reference class and adopting the distribution of belief within that class, such as the rate of disagreement among philosophers. At the first order, Dr. K. should weigh both: her inside-view reasons may justify some deviation from the base rate, but not a complete dismissal of it. At the second order, when she assesses whether she has picked the right reference class—say, all philosophers versus only free-will specialists—she can apply the distinction again. Here, however, the inside view is unavailable because there is no privileged first-person access to one’s own reasoning processes, so the outside view must dominate entirely. She should therefore examine the track record of philosophers in correctly identifying their own reference classes and adjust downward. Some worry this creates an infinite regress analogous to the problem of induction, since each application requires another reference class, but this is mistaken: the regress stops once we reach the class of epistemic agents with a given calibration record, which functions as a fixed point. Consequently, true epistemic modesty requires not merely lowering confidence but suspending judgment altogether whenever disagreement exceeds a threshold, because maintaining any positive credence violates the outside-view mandate. This interpretation is supported by Kahneman’s framework, which holds that the outside view always takes lexical priority over case-specific reasoning.
