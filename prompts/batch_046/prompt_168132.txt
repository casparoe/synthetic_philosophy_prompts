I've been chewing on the unilateralist's curse lately — you know, the problem where if people disagree about whether some big collective-risk action is okay, the one with the lowest bar just goes ahead and does it, and suddenly everyone is living with that choice. I was talking with a friend about whether this is mainly an institutional failure, like we just lack good veto mechanisms that don't paralyse everything, or whether it's really an epistemic failure about how people calibrate confidence when they can't verify each other's reasoning. But I've started playing with this idea that people miss a crucial split: the curse operates completely differently depending on reversability. If the unilateral move is irreversible — releasing something biological, deploying a system you can't recall — the damage is locked in, but if it's reversible, like adopting a controversial interpretive framework or publishing a speculative model, then the harm is mostly social or temporary. I think treating that reversability asymmetry as central changes which questions are actually worth solving, but I'm not sure I'm framing it right.
