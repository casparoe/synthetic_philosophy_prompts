I've been thinking on my own, without formal training in economics or philosophy, about what universal basic income actually changes at a conceptual level rather than at a budgetary one. Most public discussion treats it as a welfare redesign—how much, who pays, whether people stop working. I'm less interested in those forecasts than in three tensions I've noticed but can't resolve.

First, there's the problem of where "desert" goes. If income is unconditional, need and contribution are separated at the point of distribution. But the tax system that funds it still distinguishes earners from non-earners in a way that looks like a relocated desert claim. Is the philosophical work to be done here about whether unconditional cash eliminates desert-based reasoning altogether, or about whether it just hides it in the fiscal machinery?

Second, I'm struck by what might happen to informal reciprocity. In my own life and neighborhood, small obligations—watching a child, sharing tools, checking on someone sick—seem partly sustained by the fact that everyone is somewhat vulnerable and somewhat needed. If basic survival needs are guaranteed by the state regardless of social embeddedness, does that erode the moral economy of mutual aid, or does it free people to participate more genuinely?

Third, there's a structural asymmetry I keep returning to: unconditional income gives every worker an exit option from bad employment, which sounds like autonomy. But collective mechanisms—unions, workplace democracy, sectoral bargaining—depend on people being somewhat stuck and therefore having to organize voice rather than leave. Is the net effect on human autonomy positive, and is that question answerable without already knowing whether basic income weakens collective bargaining?

I'm trying to figure out where to direct my own thinking over the next few years. Among these lines of inquiry—and any others you think I'm neglecting—which open questions matter most? Which implications actually change whether basic income is conceptually coherent, and which are overrated distractions? Please defend a prioritization: for each question, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, whether it is tractable for someone without advanced quantitative training, and what kind of reasoning (rather than fresh data) would shift your view on its importance. I want a defended agenda, not a survey, and I want to know which two or three problems deserve sustained attention and which can be set aside without losing the core of the issue.
