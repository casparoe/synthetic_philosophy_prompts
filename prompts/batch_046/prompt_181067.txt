Exam question: Distinguish illusionism about phenomenal consciousness from eliminativism about mental states. Then outline the illusionist explanation of why phenomenal properties seem to exist despite not existing, and assess the objection that illusionism is self-undermining because the very belief that consciousness is illusory must itself be phenomenally conscious.

Student answer:

Illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, as developed by Keith Frankish and in certain respects anticipated by Daniel Dennett, denies that phenomenal consciousness—qualia, subjective feel, or what-it-is-likeness—actually exists. Instead, our cognitive systems produce a systematic misrepresentation: we represent ourselves as instantiating intrinsic qualitative properties, yet in reality there are only access-conscious processes and neural computations. This makes illusionism a restricted variant of eliminativism. Like Churchland-style eliminativism about propositional attitudes, it denies the existence of a class of mental entities, but unlike full-blown eliminativism it preserves beliefs, perceptions, and other cognitive states as genuinely real.

The illusionist account of seeming relies on an introspective calibration error. Introspection does not reveal hidden phenomenal properties; rather, it applies qualitative concepts to internal states that entirely lack them. This is analogous to a thermometer that reports heat because of a calibration flaw, even when no heat is present. Because the misrepresentation is structurally embedded in self-monitoring mechanisms, first-person subjects cannot correct it from within.

A prominent objection holds that illusionism is self-refuting. If the illusionist's belief that consciousness is illusory is a mental state, then it must either be phenomenally conscious or not. If it lacks phenomenal consciousness entirely, the illusionist lacks any experiential basis for claiming that consciousness is absent; if it possesses phenomenal consciousness, then phenomenal consciousness exists and the thesis collapses. Illusionists typically reply by conceding that the belief carries a representational phenomenal character—an intentional feel—while insisting that this is not an intrinsic quale. Yet this concession appears to reintroduce exactly the properties the theory aims to eliminate.

Although illusionism offers a parsimonious physicalist ontology, it struggles to explain why introspection should systematically misrepresent the mind as possessing phenomenal properties rather than misrepresenting it in some other fashion. Without an independent mechanistic account, the explanation risks being ad hoc.
