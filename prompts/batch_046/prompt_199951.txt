I'm working on a healthcare platform and we have to impliment advance directive storage. Got me thinking about the philosophy behind these things. So like, legally you can sign a paper saying no life support if you're permanently unconscious, and doctors have to follow it even if your family says you changed your mind, or even if you wake up with bad dementia and seem happy. What view of who you are is that practice assuming? Like is the version that signed the paper the real you, and the later version just a corrupted copy? Or could you explain it some other way?

Also we let people cancel contracts or change their religion whenever they want, but an advance directive isn't treated like a contract you can break. Is the law secretly saying the earlier you has special authoroty over the later you, in a way that doesn't apply to other big choices? And here's the niche thing: what if the directive tells doctors to consult an AI trained on your past preferences to make the call? Does that change the philosophical commitments, or is it the same idea just automated? I don't need citations or reading lists - just trying to figure out what theory of self or authority is baked into this legal practice, and whether it's consistent with how we treat people changing their minds about everything else.
