The prompt presented students with a hypothetical case—referred to in the exam as the "Nocturnal Cartographer"—in which an interpreter with no prior linguistic or psychological knowledge of a speaker must make sense of an individual who consistently applies directional terms in a manner inverted relative to the physical environment (for instance, pointing south while uttering the word conventionally mapped to "north"), yet who does so coherently and with logical consistency across her broader spatial reasoning. The exam question asked students to explain, using Donald Davidson's framework of radical interpretation, whether the interpreter should preserve the conventional meanings of the directional terms and thereby attribute widespread false belief, or instead reinterpret the meanings so that the speaker's beliefs come out largely true, and to discuss how this decision relates to Davidson's claims about the interdependence of belief and meaning.

The student responded as follows. First, she correctly noted that radical interpretation begins without any assumed knowledge of the speaker's language or mental states, so the interpreter must rely on the principle of charity to maximize agreement and rationality between herself and the speaker. Applying this to the Cartographer, she argued that Davidson requires us to reinterpret the directional vocabulary rather than attribute systematic error, because charity demands that we make the speaker's beliefs come out true whenever possible. She further observed that this illustrates the interdependence of belief and meaning: since we cannot determine what the speaker means without knowing what she believes, and vice versa, we must adjust both together until we reach an equilibrium of mostly true, mostly rational attributions. She also acknowledged that Davidson permits local, isolated mistakes—such as a single misidentified landmark—since charity is only a heuristic guideline rather than an absolute constraint, and it allows for occasional irrationality without collapsing the entire interpretive scheme. Finally, she concluded that Davidson's position is essentially equivalent to W.V. Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, claiming that just as Quine holds that multiple translation manuals can fit all behavioral evidence equally well, Davidson's framework allows that either the "inverted-meaning" interpretation or the "systematically-false-belief" interpretation would be empirically indistinguishable and therefore equally correct, leaving no objective fact about whether the Cartographer is linguistically deviant or merely mistaken.
