I recently spent the better part of an evening arguing with a close friend about whether Buridan’s ass reveals a genuine structural limit on rational choice or merely dramatizes an artificial symmetry that real agents never actually face. Our disagreement kept returning to a single pressure point: if every descriptive feature that could justify selecting one option over another is genuinely absent, is paralysis the rational outcome, or does the will possess some non-rational power to break ties? Rather than resolving this by reading further commentary, I want a seminar group to live inside the problem. For an upper-level metaphysics or philosophy-of-action seminar of about 14 students, meeting for 75 minutes with no technology beyond printed role cards and a whiteboard, design a structured classroom activity or game that makes participants feel the force of choice without preference before anyone names the paradox.

The exercise should center on a collective decision protocol—rather than a solitary donkey—so that the symmetry is social and visible. Divide the group into small committees of three or four, and present them with an identical, indivisible symbolic resource that must be allocated between two claimants whose profiles are structurally mirror images: same stated need, same merit score, same temporal priority, same relational distance to the committee. The rules should initially prohibit randomization devices, explicit fiat, appeals to hidden differences, and majority voting, and the payoff scheme should use symbolic points (not real money) that reward unanimous agreement but penalize deadlock with a shared loss. Structure the timeline—perhaps 20 minutes of deliberation under strict rules, 15 minutes in which committees may propose a resolution mechanism, and 10 minutes of cross-committee comparison—so that participants themselves discover a coordination stall precisely at the point of perfect indifference, and then discover that any mechanism they invent to break the deadlock (a coin flip, a turn-taking convention, an arbitrary designation) introduces an asymmetry that was not present in the original choice set. Arrange the roles and incentives so that the conceptual point emerges from the dynamics of the exercise itself, not from anything the facilitator explains beforehand.

After describing the rules and timeline, provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto parts of the philosophical idea—the symmetry of reasons, the experience of rational inertia, the external resolution mechanism, and the cost of importing an arbitrary breaker—while being frank about which feature of Buridan’s original scenario the classroom simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator’s explanation. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time you run it—whether a committee cheats the symmetry, whether the deadlock never actually occurs, or whether participants resolve the tension too quickly by smuggling in an unstated preference—and how to handle that failure without undermining the point. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in your predictions about participant behavior and the conceptual mappings you propose, and clearly single out the specific claim about which you are least sure, whether it concerns the likelihood of a genuine deadlock, the effectiveness of a particular constraint, or the fidelity of the analogy to Buridan’s conception of will and intellect.
