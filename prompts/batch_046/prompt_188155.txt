I'm drafting my philosophical profile for a graduate seminar on meaning and value, and I'd like a consistency audit before I present it to the group. Here is roughly where I've landed. First, I accept the absurdist framing: human life lacks any cosmic or transcendent purpose, and our deepest projects are, from what Thomas Nagel calls an external point of view, ultimately groundless. Second, I reject pure subjectivism—meaning is not just whatever someone happens to feel satisfied with—while also rejecting strong metaphysical objectivism that posits meaning-facts wholly independent of human practices. Instead I defend a practice-relative hybrid: within a form of life or domain of activity—science, art, care, sustained political struggle—value judgments can be genuinely correct or incorrect, and a life gains meaning through sustained engagement with projects that are valuable in that domain-specific way, even though the form of life itself has no external justification. Third, I think subjective investment is necessary but not sufficient: one must actually be drawn into the practice, not merely perform it, which is why I think something like Wolf's structure of fitting fulfillment captures part of the truth, though I want to relocate it from individual hobbies to participation in broader practices rather than private passions. Fourth, I hold that narrative coherence matters—not a single overarching life-plan, but local integration of one's commitments over time, so that one's activities are intelligible from the inside. Fifth, I think one can fully embrace the groundlessness described in my first commitment without undermining the others, because recognizing contingency from outside is compatible with recognizing internal authority from within. Sixth, I think Camus's image of Sisyphus is instructive but usually misread: the heroism is not in the bare act of rebellion against the absurd, but in the possibility that pushing the boulder might be embedded in a practice with internal standards—physical mastery, ritual repetition, or something similar—so that the activity has weight independent of the cosmic pointlessness; if it were purely arbitrary repetition with no internal structure, it would be pathetic rather than exemplary.

Please go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on closer inspection, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging. For each tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs—whether it requires abandoning the practice-relative hybrid, weakening the narrative-coherence requirement, or giving up on the idea that Sisyphus can be redeemed without self-deception. Then tell me which single revision, if I had to make one, would clean up the most problems at once. In doing this, please separate what is settled in contemporary philosophy of life from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic well, and do not present a contested reading—say, of Wolf's conditions or of Camus's intent—as if it were the consensus view.
