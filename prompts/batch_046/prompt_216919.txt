I am a doctoral student in the history of science consulting for a municipal museum that is building an interactive exhibit about a local bridge collapse in the 1920s. Archivists recently found that the failure traced back to a misfiled purchase order: a clerk accidentally approved a batch of rivets that did not meet specification, and these were installed without inspection. The design team wants a touchscreen feature where visitors can 'remove' that purchase order and see an alternate timeline unfold—the bridge holds, a rail spur is built, and the town avoids the economic decline that followed the disaster. Some board members say this is an excellent way to show how large outcomes can hinge on apparently trivial, unpredictable events; others say it wrongly suggests that the engineers bore no responsibility, and that it replaces real historical explanation with a spectacle of randomness. I am unsure whether to endorse the feature. Something about treating one clerical mistake as the decisive fork feels conceptually questionable to me—perhaps it confuses the condition that triggered the collapse with the deeper reasons the system was vulnerable—yet suppressing the contingency altogether seems to misrepresent how the disaster actually came about. From the standpoint of how we should represent historical causation to the public, is there a defensible middle position? Or does the interactive format inevitably commit us to a metaphysics of history—one that either exaggerates human agency or dissolves it into noise—that the museum should avoid?
