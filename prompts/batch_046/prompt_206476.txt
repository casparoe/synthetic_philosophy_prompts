As someone who has read rationalist forums on decision theory and preference architecture for years, I write in my capacity as a member of the Clinical Allocation Ethics Panel at our university hospital. We must produce a formal decision memo on Allocation Protocol 7-B, which governs the distribution of a single available dose of an experimental immunotherapy between two patients who are clinically identical across every measured parameter—prognosis, comorbidity profile, quality-of-life projection, and, unusually, scores on the hospital's validated preference-elicitation instrument. Because no medically relevant distinction exists, the protocol proposes selection via a quantum-random bit generator seeded by atmospheric noise, on the explicit grounds that constructing any human reason for preference would fabricate a distinction where none genuinely exists—essentially a clinical Buridan's ass scenario. A substantial minority on the panel contends that pure stochastic allocation abdicates the institution's duty of reasoned deliberation; they advocate instead for a lexical tie-breaker based on chronological admission order, accepting that the reason is arbitrary but insisting that arbitrariness must be owned by a decision-maker rather than outsourced to chance. Draft the panel's memo recommending approval of the random-allocation protocol, rejection in favour of lexical ordering, or conditional approval—presenting the reasoning with intellectual honesty, including a full and fair account of the strongest consideration favouring the position you reject. Within the memo, please state how confident you are in the recommendation and identify precisely the point about which you are least certain. Do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations; the memo must stand on its own reasoning.
