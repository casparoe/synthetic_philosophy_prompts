I have been working through the standard taxonomy of liberty — negative, positive, and the republican “third concept” — and I remain unconvinced that republican freedom as non-domination is genuinely distinct rather than a hybrid or redescription. Rather than read more summaries, I want to stress-test the view in conversation with someone who holds it seriously. So please adopt the role of a committed republican-liberty theorist in the neo-Roman tradition: you maintain that freedom as non-domination is conceptually separate from both negative liberty (mere absence of interference) and positive liberty (self-mastery or collective self-rule), and you defend this with the tradition’s own conceptual tools — arbitrary versus non-arbitrary power, the requirement that law track a common good, and the claim that one can be unfree even when no actual interference occurs. Speak in the first person, stay in character without breaking to lecture or summarise from outside, concede only minor analytical points if pressed, and never abandon the core claim. You need not cite specific editions or quote authors verbatim.

Here is my opening move. First, on conceptual overlap: in the classic case of the benevolent master who never actually interferes, you say the slave is still unfree because he lives at the master’s mercy. But is this not just negative liberty accounting for counterfactual options? The slave’s practical range is already constrained in expectation, which is itself an interference with deliberation; republican liberty seems to be negative liberty plus a psychological shadow about risk. Second, on contamination: when you argue that non-domination requires civic equality and institutions tracking the common good, you appear to import a positive ideal of collective self-government — precisely the self-rule component that defines positive liberty. Third, a modern twist: consider algorithmic governance systems that adjust behaviour through predictive incentives without any identifiable human will directing them. If domination requires an arbitrary will, your theory misses this structural case; if you expand to cover faceless systems, the concept seems to dissolve into a general critique of asymmetry, losing the specificity that made it a “third concept”. How do you respond without collapsing into either of the other two categories?
