I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mind and metaphysics, currently auditing a graduate seminar on modal epistemology. Rather than working theory-first, I've been trying to calibrate my own intuitive judgments about whether conceivability genuinely licenses possibility, going case by case and tracking where my confidence is high, where it frays, and where two structurally similar descriptions yield opposite verdicts. I keep finding patterns that don't sit cleanly with the standard Cartesian or Chalmers-style binaries, and I'm wondering whether there's a unified principle hiding behind my gut reactions, or whether I'm just rationalizing inconsistencies with ad hoc distinctions like domain-specific modal viscosity or an epistemic friction coefficient that I haven't formalized yet.

Here are my verdicts, ordered by firmness. First, a world physically identical to ours except for a minute shift in one fundamental constant, leaving all macroscopic regularities intact: I can form a stable mental model of it without resistance, and I treat it as genuinely conceivable, hence possible. Second, the familiar zombie scenario—behaviorally and physically identical humans lacking any inner experience: I can formulate the description fluently, yet there's a distinctive pull against treating it as a fully realized mental representation; still, my inclination is that it is conceivable and therefore possible, though my confidence is noticeably lower than in the constant-shift case. Third, a society running for centuries on a purely symbolic currency with neither material anchor nor institutional trust mechanism: I initially thought I could conceive it, but on reflection I realize I'm only manipulating abstract symbols; I cannot actually model the social dynamics, so I judge that it is not genuinely conceived, and I withhold the possibility judgment. Fourth, an object that is uniformly red and uniformly green over every part simultaneously, not merely divided into differently colored regions: I can utter the phrase, but there's what feels like a visual-modal block—no stable representation forms—so I judge it inconceivable and therefore impossible. Fifth, a mathematical theorem that is valid but essentially unsurveyable, existing only as an abstract structure that no mind could ever trace in full: I can grasp that such a structure might exist, but I cannot walk through it even in principle; I am genuinely torn, oscillating between treating this as a limit case of conceivability and treating it as a failure of conception altogether.

There is also a hard pair that resists easy reconciliation. Both the zombie case and the unsurveyable-proof case involve descriptions I can formulate but cannot fully run through. In the zombie case I lean toward conceivability and possibility; in the proof case I lean away. Meanwhile, both the constant-shift world and the currency society involve complex dynamics, yet I grant conceivability in one and deny it in the other.

I'm not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature. I want to see whether there is a precise principle, or perhaps a small family of competing candidates, that captures these verdicts. For each candidate, please state exactly which feature of a case it treats as decisive—representational stability, the presence of an epistemic friction coefficient, the distinction between symbolic formulation and dynamic modeling, or something else—explain what it predicts for cases I haven't mentioned (for instance, a conscious artificial system built on non-biological substrates, or a logical system in which the law of non-contradiction fails locally but holds globally), and be explicit about which of my verdicts it must overturn to preserve simplicity. If the best account turns out to be pluralist—different domains obey different conceivability-to-possibility mappings—say so, and tell me whether that pluralism is principled or merely an admission that my intuitions are noisy. Finally, apply your best principle back to the unsurveyable-proof case: does it resolve my oscillation, or does it show that one of my two inclinations has to be abandoned?
