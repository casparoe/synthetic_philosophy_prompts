A friend and I just spent an hour arguing about moral luck—whether Nagel’s four categories really capture what disturbs us about undeserved outcomes, and whether Bernard Williams’s agent-regret marks a genuine moral phenomenon or merely guilt with a philosophical label—and I kept returning to No Country for Old Men. Read the story through that lens, but inject one framing twist: treat the plot’s own fatal architecture as a kind of “narrative luck,” in which the genre’s demand for tragic closure imposes outcomes on Moss and Carla Jean that outstrip anything they cause or deserve. Focus on Chigurh’s coin-toss scenes: is he distributing circumstantial luck, or is he trying to evacuate his own responsibility by outsourcing it to chance? Then consider Carla Jean’s refusal to call the toss and her insistence that the coin carries no authority—that the choice remains Chigurh’s alone. Does that refusal complicate Nagel’s taxonomy, functioning as an assertion of autonomy, a denial that moral luck is real, or something else? Take Moss next: he makes a decent moral choice in returning to help the dying man, yet dies by apparently random violence in a motel. Does the narrative punish him for that decency, and if so, does the work take a position on whether moral luck undermines the very possibility of virtue ethics? I want a reading faithful in both directions—do not reduce the story to an illustration of Nagel or Williams, say clearly where the work resists the philosophical framework, and close by telling me what this particular story adds to the debate about moral luck that pure argument alone cannot supply.
