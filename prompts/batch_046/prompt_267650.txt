As a novelist who frequently constructs narratives in which protagonists face choices that violate one solemn commitment regardless of the path taken, I find myself drawn to the underlying philosophical problem: are genuine moral dilemmas possible? By this I do not mean merely difficult decisions, but situations in which an agent is subject to irreducibly conflicting moral requirements, so that whatever she does she fails to satisfy an obligation that genuinely binds her, leaving behind moral residue—appropriate guilt or regret that signals not psychological weakness but an actual, unfulfilled duty. The prevailing philosophical view, grounded in the consistency of moral obligations, denies that such conflicts can be real: careful analysis always reveals one overriding duty, or shows the conflict to have been illusory. A contrary tradition, informed by phenomenological accounts of tragic choice, treats persistent residue as normative evidence that an obligation was genuinely violated and remains unexpiated. Write an essay weighing these positions. Reach and justify a definite conclusion about whether genuine moral dilemmas exist. In your reasoning, distinguish conflicts arising from private values from what I term structural dilemmas: clashes generated by the internal norms of an institutional role—such as a magistrate simultaneously bound to procedural fairness and to substantive justice when the two diverge without institutional resolution—where the role itself provides no hierarchy to settle the conflict. Consider whether structural dilemmas strengthen the case for genuine dilemmas in general, or whether they can always be dissolved in principle by institutional redesign.
