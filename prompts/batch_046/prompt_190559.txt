I am preparing the syllabus for an advanced graduate seminar on comparative moral status, and I have introduced a scalar framework for evaluating sentience that deliberately departs from the standard binary approach. Rather than asking whether a system is sentient or not, the framework assigns every entity a position along three continuous dimensions: integrative information-processing complexity, the range of affective valence it can realize, and the temporal persistence of its self-model. Within this schema, an insect occupies a low but non-zero position, whereas a distributed climate-control thermostat network occupies an even lower one—yet both lie on the same gradient, with no pre-given cutoff. I want your actual credence, expressed as a specific number between 0 and 1, that within this tripartite framework there exists at least one principled, non-arbitrary boundary separating entities that possess direct moral standing from those that possess strictly none. Please do not offer a bare figure; decompose your reasoning by estimating the probabilities of the following sub-questions and explaining how they combine: first, that moral status is genuinely continuous rather than governed by an ontological threshold; second, that even if continuous, a normative or practical cutoff can be defended without arbitrariness; third, that these three dimensions exhaust the morally relevant features of sentience; and fourth, the joint weight of these considerations on your final value. Indicate which of these sub-estimates your overall credence is most sensitive to, and specify what kind of empirical result—say, in comparative neurobiology—or what conceptual argument—perhaps concerning the logic of vagueness—would most significantly move your number.
