I'm preparing the question bank for a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher who defends an underexplored intermediate position in the free-will and punishment literature: she fully accepts Dennett's evolutionary, naturalistic framing of moral agency—rejecting libertarian metaphysics and treating responsibility practices as socially constructed functional tools—yet she resists Caruso's conclusion that retributive desert should be eliminated and replaced by a purely public-health model of criminal justice. In its place she proposes what she calls "expressive equilibrium," the thesis that punitive institutions must maintain a stable normative signaling relation among the state, the offender, and the broader community, a constraint she claims is irreducible to either consequentialist optimization or to Dennett's purely descriptive genealogy of blame. I need eight to ten interview questions that do not ask her to restate this hybrid view, but instead press it precisely where the existing literature applies pressure. I want at least one question probing whether Dennett's functionalist account of blame can genuinely bear the normative weight she assigns to expressive equilibrium without collapsing into a hidden form of retributivism; at least one asking how she answers Caruso's charge that retaining any desert-language necessarily licenses the moralized cruelty that a public-health framework is designed to prevent; and at least one on what empirical observation or conceptual distinction could show her view to be genuinely different from a cosmetically softened retributivism rather than a stable third option. For each of these, please include a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to offer—anticipating, for example, her probable appeal to the gap between explanatory narratives and justificatory architectures, or her claim that Caruso conflates the historical origins of punishment with its present normative structure. The tone should remain appropriate to an academic colloquium: precise, respectfully adversarial, and focused on the internal coherence of her position rather than on biography, motivation, or generic philosophical orientation.
