In the socialist calculation debate, Hayek and Mises argued that central planning fails because economic knowledge is dispersed, tacit, and embedded in local practice, whereas Lange proposed that a central planning board could approximate market equilibrium through trial-and-error adjustments to prices without private ownership of the means of production. The dispute has persisted despite modern computational economics running large-scale agent-based simulations of price discovery, which neither side treats as decisive. Is the impasse actually about empirical computational limits—whether any algorithm can process dispersed information quickly enough—or about a conceptual claim that Hayekian knowledge (tacit, embodied, and transformed through action) is in principle unencodable for any planner, human or artificial? Or is a hidden normative disagreement doing the work: that market coordination derives its legitimacy not from efficiency but from the discovery procedures and incentive structures that only decentralized exchange provides? Diagnose the structure of this disagreement—whether it is verbal, substantive but hidden, or genuinely incommensurable—and identify what, if anything, could resolve it: a formal proof about the computability of preference revelation, a conceptual distinction between information processing and knowledge creation, or an argument that no single result could bridge the normative gap.
