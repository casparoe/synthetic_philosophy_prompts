I've been trying to think through Popper's idea that science is defined by being falsifiable, and I constructed an argument that now seems to prove that nothing — not physics, not biology, not even the demarcation rule itself — actually qualifies. Here are the steps as I see them:

(1) A theory is scientific only if it genuinely forbids some observable outcome; there must be something that, if we saw it, we would have to count as the theory being wrong rather than as a glitch in our setup.

(2) But any theory is applied together with a background layer of rules about measurement conditions, auxiliary models, and what counts as a valid test. Call this its insulation layer.

(3) When an apparently falsifying observation occurs, we can always choose to revise part of the insulation layer — recalibrating an instrument, refining an auxiliary equation, or adjusting a boundary condition — rather than abandoning the core theory.

(4) So for any core theory and any particular observation, there exists at least one available revision to the insulation layer that saves the theory from that observation.

(5) Therefore no core theory ever actually forbids any observation in practice, since the insulation layer can always absorb the shock.

(6) By (1), then, no core theory is scientific. This applies even to Popper's own criterion: if someone showed that astrology produces accurate predictions, a defender could always revise some auxiliary belief about what "scientific method" requires, insulating the criterion itself.

(7) Yet we clearly want to distinguish physics from astrology, so something in the chain must fail.

The mistake feels like it could be in (4) — perhaps "can revise" is not the same as "is rationally permitted to revise" — or in (5), where I move from logical possibility to practical unfalsifiability. Or perhaps (2) overstates how separable the core and the insulation are. If I have to keep any premises, I'd prefer to keep (1) and (3), because the idea that science takes real risks and that background conditions exist both seem right. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? Does saving falsificationism require me to build in a notion of cost or simplicity that the original criterion never mentioned, or is there a cleaner place to cut the argument?
