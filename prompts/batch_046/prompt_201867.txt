I am trying to characterize a particular failure mode in peer disagreement that I keep encountering in my seminars and in the literature, one that sits uneasily between conciliationism and steadfastness without being fully captured by either. The pattern runs as follows. Two interlocutors, both apparently reasonable and well-informed, reach an impasse over a specific claim—say, whether a certain kind of statistical inference licenses a normative conclusion about policy. Rather than holding firm or splitting the difference, each party lowers their confidence in the particular claim, almost conceding a local error. Yet this local concession does not diminish the overall disagreement; instead, it seems to strengthen each party’s attachment to the broader methodological or theoretical framework from which the claim was derived. The dispute then re-emerges at the framework level with greater intensity and rigidity. I have observed this in debates about causal inference in social science, in disagreements over the interpretation of phenomenological reports, and in normative disputes about collective risk. The phenomenon is not standard steadfastness, because there is genuine local revision of credence; nor is it conciliationism, because the disagreement does not dissolve but appears to migrate upward in a kind of hydraulic fashion. Is there an established concept—perhaps in social epistemology or in the philosophy of disagreement—that names this specific pattern of framework-preserving downward revision? If there is no established term, I would like a precise carving that distinguishes it from ordinary auxiliary-hypothesis protection, from framework relativism, and from simple epistemic stubbornness, together with a proposed name, its defining conditions, and its boundary cases—particularly where it collapses back into one of the more familiar dynamics. I am less interested in a bare label than in knowing exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
