In a recent discussion of metaontology, I heard a familiar dismissal applied to the debate between modal realists and their opponents: someone said, "That is just a verbal dispute." The speaker seemed to mean that the disagreement over whether other possible worlds exist in the same concrete sense as our own collapses once we recognize that the parties are merely adopting different linguistic conventions for the quantifier "there exists." I want you to treat that one-liner—"that is just a verbal dispute"—as a compressed argument that could be unpacked into several quite different theses. Lay out the menu of precise readings: one might be that the dispute is purely about the meaning of the word "real," another that both sides accept all the same modal facts and differ only in how they verbalize them, another that the existence-claim is ambiguous between a heavyweight ontological reading and a lightweight quantificational one, and another that metaontological disagreement is never substantive because ontology is always relative to a language or framework. For each reading, indicate whether it is trivially true, genuinely substantive and defensible, or plainly false, and say which of them actually supports using the slogan to end an argument about possible worlds rather than engaging with it. Be especially careful about the claim that the parties share all non-linguistic beliefs: is that a plausible description of the modal realist and the actualist, or does it misrepresent the nature of their disagreement? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want a conceptual dissection of the slogan itself, grounded in the particular work it was being asked to do in that conversation—namely, to dissolve a metaphysical dispute without adjudicating it. Please respond in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables, as if writing a thoughtful letter.
