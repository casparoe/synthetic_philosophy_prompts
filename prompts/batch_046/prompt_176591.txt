For a philosophy-of-science reading group with graduate-level familiarity with basic probability but no expectation of literature recall, construct a decision-tree taxonomy that sorts resolutions to Bertrand’s chord paradox by the commitments they actually make, not by author names or school labels. Build the tree from the logical space of answers to four concrete diagnostic questions, then locate actual, unnamed, and hypothetical positions at the leaves.

Root commitment (answerable without knowing the literature): “The paradox genuinely produces incompatible probabilities—1/2, 1/3, and 1/4—for the same event under the principle of indifference.”

Node 1 splits on whether the conflict is a failure of problem specification (the phrase “random chord” is ambiguous across parameterizations—endpoint-uniform, radius-uniform, area-uniform) or a failure of the principle of indifference itself. If specification failure, proceed to Node 2; if principle failure, proceed to Node 3.

Node 2 (specification path) asks: Once “random chord” is fully specified by one parameterization, does a uniquely correct parameterization remain determinable by geometric symmetry alone, without appealing to physical generation? Yes leads to Leaf A; No leads to Leaf B.

Node 3 (principle-failure path) asks: Must the principle of indifference be replaced by a rule that appeals to the physical or causal mechanism by which the chord is generated? Yes leads to Leaf C; No proceeds to Node 4.

Node 4 (principle failure without physical replacement) asks: Should we abandon the expectation of a unique probability and accept that rational agents may assign different values indexed to their descriptive framework? Yes leads to Leaf D; No produces an unoccupied combination. For that empty branch, diagnose why it is unoccupied and populate it with a hypothetical position using an “indexical indifference” principle—defined in your tree as weighting descriptions by their Kolmogorov-complexity cost relative to an observer’s coordinate frame—leading to Leaf E.

In addition, include Leaf F for the view that the paradox dissolves once we recognize that different parameterizations answer different well-posed questions, and Leaf G for the view that geometric invariance alone fixes the measure at 1/3 but only if we reject endpoint-uniform descriptions as mathematically illegitimate.

The tree must contain exactly seven leaves (A through G). Each leaf should state: (i) the combination of answers reaching it, (ii) its core claim in one sentence, (iii) its most awkward commitment, and (iv) whether it is defended in the literature, unnamed, or hypothetical. Each internal node should be phrased as a concrete claim about the chord problem that a student can answer without knowing which interpretation it produces.

Please flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate—particularly whether Leaf A and Leaf G collapse into the same position—and explain why the separation fails or succeeds.

Lead with the bottom line: present the complete tree—every node question, branch condition, and all seven leaves with their commitments—in the very first paragraph, with no preamble and without restating the problem. Then provide the reasoning for each split (why the two answers exhaust the logical space at that node), the diagnosis of the empty branch at Node 4’s “No,” and the explicit separation flag. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for them. If you reference an argument, paraphrase without quotation marks. Do not invoke obscure named theories that depend on memorized literature; define the “indexical indifference” idea and any other niche distinctions you need within the tree itself.
