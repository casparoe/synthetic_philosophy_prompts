Consider a philosopher working within the framework that treats the first-person pronoun not as referring to a persistent substance but as a self-referential symbol—what Douglas Hofstadter called a strange loop—where the symbol I is both inside and outside the representational system it describes. This thinker has rendered the following judgments about whether various entities possess such an I:

A large language model that generates first-person prose and maintains a context window referencing its own prior outputs does not possess an I, even when its architecture includes attention mechanisms that loop back onto earlier layers.

A chimpanzee that recognizes its reflection in a mirror and adjusts its grooming behavior accordingly does not possess an I.

A human being suffering from total retrograde amnesia who retains present-tense bodily agency and can use I to refer to her current experience does possess an I.

A hypothetical artificial system distributed across thousands of servers with a unified, real-time self-model but no localized physical boundary or dedicated sensory apparatus does not possess an I.

A human brain maintained in vitro with intact neural architecture, capable of internal self-referential symbolic processing, but lacking all sensory input and motor output, does possess an I.

Now consider a new case: a cerebral organoid grown from induced pluripotent stem cells that has never been part of a body or subjected to evolutionary selection. It exhibits sustained gamma-band synchronization, updates an internal representational state in response to patterned electrical stimulation, and shows evidence of distinguishing self-generated signals from externally induced ones, yet it has no language, no social history, and no sensory-motor loop with a surrounding environment.

What verdict should we expect this thinker to deliver? Identify at least two competing rules that could consistently explain the five judgments above—one emphasizing the formal structure of the self-referential loop regardless of substrate or embodiment, and one emphasizing that the loop must be anchored in continuous sensory-motor interaction with a world. For each rule, state what it predicts about the organoid. Then offer your best guess at the thinker's actual verdict, with the reasoning that favors one rule over the other in light of the earlier cases. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case that would decisively separate these two rules.
