I'm working through a seminar paper on classical democratic theory and came across an argument my study partner built that seems to prove far more than she intended. She wants to defend the normative weight of democratic sovereignty, yet her reasoning ends up denying that democracy can ever genuinely fail. She lays it out like this:

(1) In a pure democracy, the assembled demos is the ultimate source of legitimate authority; there is no higher tribunal—neither philosopher-king nor constitutional court—that can declare its decision unjust.

(2) If no external authority can declare a democratic decision unjust, then the very concept of "democratic failure" is incoherent, since failure requires a standard of success that the decision violates, yet the decision itself defines the standard.

(3) The Athenian assembly's vote for the Sicilian Expedition, its initial vote to execute all male citizens of Mytilene (later reversed), and its conviction of Socrates were all direct exercises of this sovereignty.

(4) Even the reversal at Mytilene does not demonstrate failure, but rather illustrates what she calls "retroactive democratic immunization": the assembly's capacity to overturn its own prior vote proves that both votes were expressions of one continuous sovereign will, so neither can be judged erroneous by any metric external to that will.

(5) Therefore, to describe these episodes as strategic overreach, genocidal impulse, or judicial murder is to presuppose an external normative framework that pure democracy rejects.

(6) But if democratic sovereignty implies that no procedural decision can be erroneous, then every atrocity committed by vote—including the execution of a philosopher—is automatically justified by its procedural origin.

(7) Hence pure democratic sovereignty is false, or else we are committed to the absurd claim that the trial of Socrates was just because the vote said so.

She insists she wants to keep both (1)—that democratic sovereignty carries real normative weight—and the judgment that Mytilene, Sicily, and the trial of Socrates represent genuine democratic failures. She suspects something slipped between (4) and (6), but she cannot name the precise move. Which step should one reject, and what does rejecting it cost for someone who wants to preserve both sovereignty and the possibility of democratic failure?
