In forums discussing quantum mechanics and consciousness, I keep encountering the claim that quantum immortality works like a video game with an infinite number of save files: every fatal event spawns a branching world in which the event did not happen, and your subjective experience is just the one save file that keeps loading. The conclusion drawn is that, from a first-person perspective, death can never occur, so one should rationally expect to survive any accident indefinitely. I would like a rigorous audit of this analogy. First, lay out the correspondence table: what in the physical picture plays the role of the save files, the player, the game engine, and the act of loading? Second, identify where the mapping breaks down—particularly whether consciousness is genuinely transferable across branches like a player switching saves, or whether each branch simply contains its own separate observer with no continuity to the others. Third, point out what the video-game picture quietly imports about personal identity, the unity of consciousness, and the nature of subjective experience that the Everett interpretation may not actually provide. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that subjective immortality is rational—survives once these disanalogies are accounted for, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument.
