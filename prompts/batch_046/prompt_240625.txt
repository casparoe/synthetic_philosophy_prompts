I'm preparing questions for a public colloquium Q&A with a philosopher defending a reconstructed, non-metaphysical theory of karma as diachronic moral desert. On her view, "karmic fittingness" is not a causal mechanism spanning rebirths, but a normative relation between an agent's moral choices and the social-structural conditions that later constrain or enable them—extending across generations without requiring personal identity continuity. She argues this preserves karma's core intuition that suffering and flourishing are not randomly distributed, while avoiding both rebirth metaphysics and the individualism of Western retributivism.

Please draft six interview questions that turn on the actual content of this position, pressing where the literature on moral desert and collective responsibility presses. For each, build in a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give:

1. On the personal-identity problem: if the recipient of a karmic outcome is not numerically the same agent, in what sense is desert satisfied rather than mere collective reward or punishment? Follow up anticipating her appeal to "moral networks" or diachronic agency.

2. On epistemic accessibility: since we observe no verifiable pattern of karmic fittingness across generations, what distinguishes her normative claim from a post-hoc rationalization of structural inequality? Follow up asking what observed social pattern, if any, could count against her view.

3. On distributive justice: her framework seems to treat inherited social conditions as deserved. How does she block the slide into justifying systemic injustice—say, caste or inherited poverty—without smuggling in hidden metaphysical causes? Follow up keyed to her likely distinction between a "karmic baseline" and an "institutional correction."

4. On proportionality: karma traditionally links specific actions to specific outcomes, but structural conditions are diffuse. How does she maintain proportional desert without a causal ledger? Follow up asking whether this collapses her view into a general consequentialist balance principle.

5. On the scope of agency: if karmic weight attaches to individual actions but outcomes depend on collective structures, how much control must an agent exercise over those structures for desert to apply? Follow up asking whether this restricts karma to agents with significant social power, undermining its claimed universality.

6. A closing question on moral luck: can her reconstructed karma accommodate cases where morally good agents suffer structurally and bad agents flourish structurally, without either abandoning desert or reintroducing the hidden causal mechanisms she rejects?

Write in a polished, formal tone suitable for a philosophy colloquium. Avoid generic biographical questions, and do not request citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
