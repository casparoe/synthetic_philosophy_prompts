Retired biologist here, got into philosphy of science lately and I'm stuck on a pattern in the literature that I can't name. You have some compound or gene that's said to carry a specific power — killing a pathogen, triggering a behavior, whatever — and the evidence always comes from very clean lab conditions. Then in the wild, with all the other stuff present, that power never actually shows up. Instead of concluding the thing doesn't really have it, people say it's being "blocked" or "masked" by the enviroment. The weird part is the blocker is never detected on its own; it's only ever defined by the missing effect. So the original claim becomes immune to failure, but nobody counts that as weakening the evidence, because each proposed blocker sounds reasonable by itself.

Two cases that bug me. One: a plant extract kills bacteria beautifully in a petri dish, but in soil it does nothing, and the paper just says "soil microbiome interactions mask the activity" without identifying which microbes or measuring them. Two: a mutation causes heart defects in pure-bred lab mice, but wild populations carrying it are fine, so they invoke "genetic background masking" without actually testing different backgrounds. In both cases the masking factor is a ghost — postulated solely because the predicted effect didn't occur.

I keep seeing mentions of "finks" and "masks" in metaphysics, but those seemed like logical puzzles about hidden powers, not about working scientists actually doing this in practice. Is there an established concept for this exact move in scientific reasoning? How is it different from ordinary confounding variables or from just adding an auxiliary hypothesis? And if there's no specific term, what should we call it — and where does it sit relative to those metaphysical ideas about dispositions being hidden? I want the carving, not just a label: what exactly this is, what it isn't, and where its edges are, especially the case where the blocker might be real but is still only ever inferred from the absence of the effect.
