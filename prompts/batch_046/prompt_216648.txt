Two members of my philosophy of language study group, Alex and Jordan, reported a disagreement they had about Davidson’s principle of charity in radical interpretation. They were working through a hypothetical case: an interpreter is trying to understand a speaker who consistently maintains both (i) that all ravens are black, expressed as a universal generalization, and (ii) that the white bird in front of them is a raven, expressed as a direct perceptual report. They did not ask for citations or further reading; they want the arguments themselves assessed. Here is the exchange as they relayed it.

(1) Alex said that in radical interpretation charity is constitutive, not merely heuristic. Because belief is largely veridical, the interpreter must avoid attributing direct logical contradictions. So if the speaker asserts both claims, the interpreter should reinterpret either “raven” or “black” (or weaken “all”) so the beliefs come out consistent. Otherwise we are not interpreting the speaker’s language but inventing an incoherent one.

(2) Jordan replied that this makes charity into a metaphysical straitjacket. Davidson’s principle is methodological: maximize overall agreement, weighing local inconsistencies against global coherence. If the speaker’s taxonomy otherwise tracks ours closely, it is more charitable to preserve the perceptual report and treat the universal as a defeasible empirical generalization with one counterexample, rather than twisting the meanings of basic terms globally.

(3) Alex countered that if we allow local contradictions whenever they seem convenient, radical interpretation collapses into Quinean indeterminacy. The interpreter cannot separate meaning assignment from belief attribution; to assign “raven” its standard meaning requires assuming the speaker applies it mostly correctly. Allowing “raven” to cover a white bird in this context undermines the very conditions that fix the term’s reference.

(4) Jordan said Alex is conflating interpretation with endorsement. Davidson requires that we make the speaker intelligible, not that we guarantee logical omniscience. Attributing one false belief about a particular bird keeps the speaker rational and largely correct; redefining their taxonomy to avoid a single contradiction makes them unintelligible. So local error is the genuinely charitable move.

(5) Alex then argued that the contradiction here is logical, not empirical. “All ravens are black” combined with “this is a white raven” is a formal contradiction. Charity should push the interpreter to reinterpret the quantifier or the color predicate, because a rational agent cannot literally hold contradictory beliefs, especially when one is framed as a universal law.

(6) Jordan responded that Davidson does not treat agents as logically omniscient. A universal claim learned by induction functions as a defeasible rule, not as an axiomatic commitment. The speaker can rationally hold the generalization and simultaneously report a perceptual exception without any need to reinterpret the terms involved; the contradiction is only apparent, resolved by recognizing the different epistemic statuses of the two beliefs.

Then identify what, if anything, both sides are missing—particularly about whether charity operates locally or globally, and about how defeasible generalizations interact with perceptual reports in belief attribution.
