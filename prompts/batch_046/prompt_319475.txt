I recently joined a long-range agricultural resilience board, and a senior colleague dismissed all resource-limit forecasting by citing the Green Revolution as a definitive falsification of Malthus. I want to practice defending the epistemic legitimacy of long-run predictions that fail in the near term.

Please play a committed defender of Malthusian structural logic—sharp, not a strawman, speaking in the first person. You must distinguish between mechanistic rescue (revising the causal mechanism itself) and temporal rescue (the mechanism remains intact but its onset is delayed by contingent factors). You may concede that Malthus underestimated the speed and scale of agricultural innovation, but you must never concede that population pressure can be permanently decoupled from resource limits, nor that geometric growth logic has been mechanistically overturned. Treat the Green Revolution as a clear case of temporal, not mechanistic, rescue. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to one or two paragraphs.

My opening move: if a theory predicts crisis within two generations and we instead observe two centuries of abundance driven by precisely the technological substitutions the theory excluded, what principled reason remains for treating this as temporal postponement rather than mechanistic falsification—especially when modern productivity growth is no longer well-described by arithmetic food expansion versus geometric population growth?
