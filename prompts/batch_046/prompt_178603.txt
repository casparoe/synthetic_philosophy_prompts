ok so I'm prepping my grad seminar on just war theory and I sketched out what I actually believe, not just what I teach in lecture. I was actually arguing about this over coffee with a colleague from history and I realized I hold this weird package: (1) I buy Walzer's moral equality of combatants for state armies — soldiers on both sides have an equal right to kill each other even if one side is clearly in the wrong. (2) At the same time, I think individual soldiers are still morally accountable for fighting in an unjust cause; they can't hide behind "just following orders," and if they genuinely know the cause is unjust they share in the collective wrong. (3) I also think conscription is only justified for just wars, but if you're drafted into an unjust war you still retain a right of self-defense against enemy soldiers who are trying to kill you. (4) For non-state actors — mercenary corps, resistance militias, whatever — I don't think moral equality applies at all; they only recieve combatant privileges if their cause is just and they discriminate properly. (5) And I recently got convinced that drone operators and cyber-combatants shouldn't be treated as combatants under this framework, since there's no reciprocal risk, so they fall outside the equality principle entirely.

My question is: does this actually hang together, or am I just helping myself to incompatible motivations? Specifically, (1) and (2) seem to pull in opposite directions — if soldiers are individually accountable for the justice of their cause, why do they retain an equal permission to kill just soldiers? And (4) feels like it breaks the symmetry I claim in (1) without giving a principaled reason. But then (3) and (5) might be consistent with each other in a way I'm not seeing? I want an audit, not a lecture: rank the tensions by severity, say which commitment has to go in each case, and flag any apparent contradiction that actually dissolves once you inspect it closer. Don't just flag everything — I know some pairs only look tense.
