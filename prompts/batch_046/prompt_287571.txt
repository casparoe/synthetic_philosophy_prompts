I serve as an appointed member of the Inter-Site Ethics Review Board for the Meridian Consortium, a five-site network running a phase-III trial of a novel immunotherapy for aggressive lymphoid malignancy. The protocol requires each site to randomize one in every ten enrolled patients to a control arm that withholds the investigational agent, using only standard chemotherapy, for the full six-month primary endpoint. Each site’s local ethics committee has flagged the same concern: assigning a patient to the control arm violates their institution’s deontological commitment never to withhold an available, potentially life-extending therapy from an individual patient for the sake of aggregate data. The paradox is collective. If all five sites honor this absolute constraint and refuse to randomize, the trial collapses, the drug never reaches regulatory review, and thousands of future patients are denied a treatment that preliminary data suggest could extend median survival by fourteen months. If, however, the sites approve the protocol, each site will have deliberately harmed one identifiable patient to benefit statistical strangers.

The consortium’s director has proposed an amendment—call it the synchronized commitment clause—under which no single site may enroll patients in the control arm unless all five sites have simultaneously activated the protocol, with a shared liability framework and a pooled compensation fund for the affected control-arm patients. The board must recommend whether to approve the original protocol, approve it only with the synchronized clause, or reject it outright and advise the sites to pursue an observational study instead.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a clear time frame for implementation or further review. The reasoning should treat the paradox of deontology seriously—do not simply resolve it by asserting that consequentialist calculus trumps duty—but should also honestly present the strongest consideration on the losing side. In particular, address whether the synchronized clause genuinely dissolves the paradox or merely disguises collective harm as distributed harm, and whether an institutional body can legitimately authorize what each individual site considers a direct wrong. Avoid bibliographic requests; the board needs actionable reasoning, not a literature review.
