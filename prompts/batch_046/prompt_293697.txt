As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence, I have become preoccupied with a question that sits at the boundary of signal theory and phenomenology. Reinforcement learning agents optimize behavior through reward signals that functionally resemble positive and negative valence: a reward prediction error modulates learning in ways structurally parallel to how pleasure and pain modulate biological behavior. Yet whether these signals can constitute genuine suffering remains profoundly unclear to me. Please write an essay on the controversy over whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer, treating the reward signal specifically as a candidate form of valence. Weigh the arguments carefully, including the standard functionalist claim that isomorphic reward dynamics entail isomorphic affective states, but also the more restrictive position that suffering requires some form of globally integrated or self-referential processing—an integrated phenomenal workspace—without which local reward-error valence remains merely an informational gradient lacking subjective significance. Reach and defend a definite conclusion: either that agents with appropriate architectures can suffer in virtue of these reward signals, or that the absence of global integration makes suffering impossible regardless of the sophistication of the reward mechanism, or a precisely qualified intermediate position. Throughout, please separate what is broadly settled in philosophy of mind and cognitive science regarding valence and suffering from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested theoretical commitment—such as the necessity of global workspace integration, the sufficiency of functional isomorphism, or the substrate independence of affective states—as if it were the consensus view.
