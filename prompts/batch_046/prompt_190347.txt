My closest friend and I have just spent several hours arguing about von Neumann’s late reflections on whether civilization can survive the technology it produces—not because the machines turn hostile, but because technical growth accelerates exponentially and self-feeds, while our political and social institutions adapt linearly, if at all. To end the evening without resolving what we could not resolve, I want a villanelle that makes its form carry the argument. Use two refrains: one that captures the self-accelerating curve of technological change, and one that captures von Neumann’s claim that survival is therefore a problem of governance and social adaptation, not of engineering failure. Let them rotate through the stanzas so that each return widens the gap rather than closing it; in the final couplet, place them adjacent without synthesis, as if the two truths have passed each other and kept going. The poem should be precise enough that someone familiar with von Neumann’s view—technology as an internal acceleration that outpaces the institutions meant to steer it—recognizes the specific logic, not a generic lament about progress. Keep it to nineteen lines, maintain a formal and restrained tone suitable for being read aloud at the close of a seminar, avoid any sermon about either intervention or resignation, and do not quote directly from his essay; paraphrase his concepts in your own lines. Leave the tension intact, since that is exactly where our argument ended.
