I've been reading about whether the no-free-lunch theorems actually do anything for Hume's problem of induction, and there's this deadlock I cant parse. On one side people say NFL proves induction is impossible without prior bias — every learner is tied on average over all target functions, so picking one inductive rule over another needs a prior that cant itself be justified, which just pushes Hume back a step. On the other side people say thats a misapplication: NFL averages over every possible target with a flat prior, but scientific induction never claims to work over literally all logically possible worlds; it assumes nature is computable or smooth, and that prior isnt arbitrary, its empirically grounded. So they talk past each other for pages.

But heres the thing that makes me think its not just verbal: both sides agree on the math, agree you need some prior to break the symetry, and agree that success can retroactively vindicait the choice. The disagreement is whether "breaking symmetry with an empirically grounded prior" counts as answering Hume or just changing the subject. The first camp treats any prior as needing its own inductive justification; the second treats computability as a working hypothesis that gets justified by predictive success. So is this a hidden disagreement about what counts as justification — circular if empirical, non-circular only if a priori? Or are they literally asking different questions: one about rational justification in general, the other about how scientists actually choose models? And theres this weird corner neither side discusses: NFL assumes symmetric loss, but real science assigns different costs to false positives and false negatives, and I dont think anyone has shown whether an asymmetric loss breaks the NFL symetry in a way that yields a non-arbitrary inductive bias without smuggling in a prior. Is that the hidden formal gap, or am I inventing a problem?

So what kind of stall is this? Bedrock intuitions about circularity? A hidden empirical/formal question about asymmetric loss that hasnt been settled? Or just two disciplines using "induction" for different jobs? And given whatever diagnosis you think fits, what would actually move the needle — a proof about asymmetric NFL, a sharp distinction between rational justification and pragmatic vindication, or should we just admit the question is ill-posed?
