As a journalist who covers the intersection of science, policy, and conceptual ethics, I have been trying to map the exact force of the nonidentity problem for long-term climate governance. I have constructed the following argument, which seems valid but lands in a conclusion I find unacceptable, and I would value a careful diagnosis.

(1) If an act is identity-affecting—that is, it changes which particular people come to exist—then it cannot harm the specific individuals who result, since for them the alternative was non-existence, and non-existence is not a harm.

(2) Large-scale climate and energy trajectories are profoundly identity-affecting: altering economic development, migration patterns, and social conditions across generations shifts the conditions of conception, so the population living under rapid decarbonization is numerically distinct from the population living under a high-emission pathway.

(3) Now consider a threshold view of well-being: a life is not wronged if it remains above the minimal level at which existence is genuinely worth living, regardless of how far it falls below an optimal or baseline quality of life.

(4) Therefore, a government that knowingly locks in severe warming—producing future generations who face ecological stress, resource scarcity, and diminished opportunity—does not wrong any particular person, provided those lives remain just above the worth-living threshold, because those exact individuals would never have existed under the alternative policy.

(5) Moreover, if moral obligations are always owed to particular, identifiable individuals, and there is no coherent moral sense in which one can wrong "future people in general" or aggregate welfare across merely possible populations without specifying actual victims, then no further moral condemnation of the policy is available.

(6) It follows that such a government commits no serious moral wrong by choosing the high-emission path, as long as future lives stay barely above the threshold.

(7) But this is intolerable; we clearly judge that knowingly locking in catastrophic conditions for centuries is a profound moral failure.

If I am determined to preserve both the core intuition in (1) and the moral judgment in (7), which premise should I abandon? Is there a hidden slide—perhaps between "not harming a particular person" and "having no moral reason to act differently"—or is the chain valid and the real price lies in rejecting either the threshold view in (3) or the particularist claim in (5)? If giving up (4) is the least damaging route, does that commit me to the idea that we can wrong people in virtue of the conditions we impose, even when those conditions are identity-affecting? I prefer not to jettison (1), but I want to understand precisely what keeping it costs me.
