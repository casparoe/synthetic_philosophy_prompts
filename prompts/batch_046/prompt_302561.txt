Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the specific philosophical lens of the torture-versus-dust-specks problem: the debate over whether an enormous number of microscopic harms (dust specks in eyes across a vast population) can aggregate to outweigh a single severe harm, and whether distributing a grave wrong across many as trivial discomforts dissolves its moral weight. In the story, one child's concentrated suffering sustains the diffuse happiness of thousands. Analyze the parade scene, the catalog of ordinary pleasures, and the moment of departure: does the narrative treat the citizens' well-being as genuinely commensurable with the child's agony on a single scale, or does its structure—especially the refusal to detail the suffering—block that comparison? Consider whether a philosopher who denies that tiny harms aggregate into a catastrophic total should read the citizens' joy as genuinely bought by torture, or as something that can be separated from it. Does the act of walking away represent a refusal of aggregation logic, or an admission that no calculation can justify staying?
