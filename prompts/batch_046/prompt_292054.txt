Case two: I'm drafting a long report with 50 factual claims, each one I'm 99% sure about, and they're independant. I think it's okay to believe each claim individually, but also okay to think "at least one of these is probably wrong," so I shouldn't treat the whole report as flawless. This is the preface paradox, right? I'm fine with that tension.

But here's where I waver: if all 15 flag at once, should the clinician believe the patient actually has the condition? One part of me says yes, since each belief is justified. Another part says no, because the cumulative false-positive risk is massive. Which instinct is right?

Case four: a weather model predicts 98% chance of rain for each of the next 5 days, calculated independantly. I'd carry an umbrella every day. I'd also think "it won't rain all five days" sounds reasonable. But then if someone asks whether to cancel an outdoor event running the whole week, I lean toward yes, cancel - yet that seems to conflict with my belief that continuous rain is unlikely. I'm going back and forth on this one.

But should the system also maintain a global belief that "roughly one part in this batch is faulty despite passing"? And if it never updates its global model but acts on every single failure, is that the same kind of rationality as my lottery belief?
