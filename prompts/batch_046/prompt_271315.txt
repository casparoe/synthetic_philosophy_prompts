I'm a phd student in cog sci and I've been stuck on something about forensic accreditation. The practice is that labs train fingerprint or bullet-striation examiners for years, then let them testify that they literally see a match—like raw observation, not inference. The accredidation bodies frame the expert report as observational, not interpretive. Meanwhile there's this procedural fix called linear sequential unmasking, where you hide contextual info so the expert's prior belief doesn't leak into the call. But even with that, the institutional framing still treats trained expert vision as a kind of transparent window on the evidence. So what theory of perception does that presupposses? Does it need a Fodor-style modular view where training just sharpens bottom-up processing? Or is it compatible with the claim that all perception is concept-penetrated, as long as we relabel the penetration expertise rather than bias? And what does the unmasking protocol implicitly commit to—maybe that penetration only comes from external context, not from the expert's own conceptual framework? I want to know if the practice is philosophically consistent or just underdetermined, and whether replacing the implicit view would actually force labs to redesign how they certify people.
