At a colloquium last week, two philosophers debated whether a genuinely self-revising normative architecture—whether institutional or artificial—must include an "inviolate level" immune to its own revision, or whether stability can arise without one. They anchored the discussion in Douglas Hofstadter's analysis of tangled hierarchies and in M.C. Escher's well-known image of two hands drawing each other.

The first speaker argued that without an inviolate foundation, the system reproduces Escher's circularity: every level is constituted by another, so nothing is genuinely grounded. If the highest-order rules can rewrite themselves, there is no terminus—only mutual dependence. Hence any coherent framework must contain at least one stratum that its own logic treats as non-revisable.

The second speaker replied that this misreads the image. The hands appear circular only inside the frame; outside it, an artist draws them on paper. Similarly, a self-modifying system need not harbor an internal inviolate level if its revisions are constrained by an embedding environment—functional demands, physical limits, or external evaluation—that breaks the loop from without.

The first speaker countered that embedding contexts are themselves interpretable and alterable by a capable system. Once the system can model and change its relation to its environment, that environment becomes just another hand in the picture. To halt the regress, there must be a level the system cannot represent as revisable—an axiomatic core that serves as a genuine terminus.

The second speaker concluded that requiring such an internal Archimedean point is unrealistic and unnecessary. Human moral and scientific frameworks lack any inviolate base—our deepest commitments evolve—yet they preserve coherence through the dynamics of revision, not static foundations. Stability comes from well-structured feedback loops that prevent self-defeating oscillation, not from an unmovable ground.
