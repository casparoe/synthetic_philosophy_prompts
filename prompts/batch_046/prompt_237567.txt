Write a short story set in a clinical research unit where a neuropsychologist administers an experimental protocol called chronometric trace revision. The protocol targets not primary sensory records but the interpolated frames that consciousness constructs—specifically analogues of the color phi phenomenon, in which a moving dot that changes color is perceived to change hue at an intermediate position that was never physically presented, binding that constructed intermediate into the temporal flow of experience before the actual change occurs. In the story, a patient retains a continuous emotional memory of reconciling with a sibling, yet neuroimaging reveals that the continuity was supplied by a phi-like interpolation across a brief gap of unconsciousness caused by a transient neurological event. The researcher must choose between an Orwellian correction—rewriting the patient’s archival transcript so that it reports discontinuous fragments—or a Stalinesque intervention—directly altering the temporal binding mechanism so the interpolated frame is never phenomenally constructed in the first place. Follow the patient’s experience of identity under each revision: does erasing the archive change the past, or does suppressing the interpolation reveal that consciousness was always an edited narrative? Ground the narrative in the specific temporal dynamics of perceptual filling-in, and treat the distinction between record and experience not as mere metaphor but as an ontological problem about whether there was ever a single, continuous phenomenal event to preserve.
