I cover philosophy and cognitive science for a long-form ideas magazine, and I am preparing an interactive taxonomy for a feature on whether Kant's moral framework can survive contact with modern comparative cognition. I need a decision tree—not a quiz about named scholars—that sorts a reader's philosophical commitments about animals in Kant's ethics into clearly separated interpretive positions.

Begin with the root commitment: Must moral standing require the capacity for autonomous legislation, or can analogical practical capacities in non-human beings ground some form of direct moral consideration?

Next, branch on how one reads Kant's prohibition on cruelty toward animals in his later ethical writings. Is it best understood as an empirical-psychological claim that brutality hardens human moral character, or as a metaphysical claim about the indirect preservation of respect for rational nature itself?

Then introduce the niche comparative-cognition case: recent work in corvid and cetacean cognition suggests proto-rational problem-solving and social negotiation. The subsequent node should ask whether such capacities permit these animals to be incorporated into a reconstructed kingdom-of-ends framework, or whether any such extension necessarily dilutes the autonomy condition beyond recognition.

Each leaf must carry a position label—use unnamed if no standard view occupies that branch—and a single sentence stating its most awkward commitment, for instance that it must either deny moral relevance to suffering in cognitively sophisticated non-humans or relax Kant's autonomy requirement in ways he never explicitly endorsed.

The tree should map the full logical space of answers: note every unoccupied combination, diagnosing each gap briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. It should also flag any pair of leaves that the tree cannot cleanly separate.

Write in polished, journalistic prose for an educated general audience. Phrase every node as a concrete yes-or-no or either-or claim that a reader can answer without knowing which interpretation it yields. Do not request citations, reading lists, bibliographies, or references to specific editions. Where position labels are unavoidable, use only widely recognized ones.
