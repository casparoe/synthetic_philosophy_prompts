Write the joint statement that a philosopher advancing a Malcolm-style conceptual-criterion objection to dream-experiences and a philosopher advancing a Dennett-style retrospective-construction account of dream reporting could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration. Address the specific question: are dreams experiences?

Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table with two columns (labeled in neutral terms that each camp accepts, with each row phrased in wording its own side would endorse), followed by a cruxes section, and ending with a brief joint assessment of verbal versus substantive disagreement.

In the numbered agreed-claims section, include shared rejections of the received view that sleep involves rich experiential episodes subsequently recalled veridically; shared observations about the unreliability, variability, and narrative structuring of reports upon awakening; and shared conditions for the legitimate application of experience-language. Exclude any claim that either party would reject.

In the disagreements table, cover at least:
- Whether the absence of intersubjective verification conditions during sleep constitutes a conceptual failure of the experience-concept or merely an empirical gap in observation.
- Whether retrospective narrative assembly upon awakening necessarily fabricates phenomenal content, or can preserve and approximate genuine fragments of sleep-time mental states.
- The epistemic status of pre-reflective or hypnagogic reports made before full wake-state narrative consolidation.

In the cruxes section, identify the sub-questions such that resolving them would shift at least one party's position. For each crux, specify a concrete case, thought experiment, or agreed-upon empirical pattern that both sides accept as bearing on it, and record each camp's explicit prediction in advance. Include:
(a) The silent-awakening protocol: a sleeper is awakened without any dream-related prompt and questioned only after a delay, versus being questioned immediately upon waking. Both sides should agree this protocol is relevant; state what each predicts about report incidence, detail, and narrative coherence.
(b) The isolated-fragment case: reports of sensory qualities (e.g., a color, pressure, or sound) that lack any narrative, spatial, or temporal structure. Both sides should agree this pattern is relevant; state whether each predicts such reports occur, and what each takes them to indicate about sleep-time mental life.
(c) The REM-correlation question: whether specific neurophysiological signatures during REM that correlate with subsequent report detail indicate retained phenomenal structures or only activated memory traces available for retrospective construction.

Conclude with a brief joint statement assessing whether any portion of their dispute is verbal—specifically, whether they assign different meanings to "experience"—and, if so, identifying precisely which remainder is genuinely substantive regarding the mental processes occurring during sleep.

Keep the formulation precise and self-contained; do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and do not invoke highly obscure technical theories outside standard philosophical and cognitive-scientific training. You may frame part of the dispute in terms of whether experience-ascription requires a wake-state verification framework, but ground the cruxes in shared conceptual and empirical considerations rather than specialized apparatus.
