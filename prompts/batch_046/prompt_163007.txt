I'm working with a regional natural-history museum and we keep getting these submissions where researchers claim they've found either a split population or that two cataloged things should actually be one. I need an actual decision procedure my team can run—like a checklist or flowchart in words—before we update our database or reccomend protection status. The theory should draw on the species-problem debates—reproductive isolation, phylogenetic clustering, ecological niche—but turned into steps someone without a phil. of bio. degree can apply in about thirty minutes using the data we actually have: morphometrics, genetic distance, habitat notes, and almost never direct breeding experiments.

Please build the procedure with ordered gates: for each one, say what counts as passing or failing, where we stop with "keep them as seperate entries," where with "merge them," and where the answer has to be kicked up to a human because it's a values call rather than a fact. Be explicit about the hard cases—phenotypic inertia where a form stays constant across environments, reproductive interface zones where hybridization is technically possible but ecologically gated, and morphological stasis paired with high genetic drift—and flag which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as rules. Then run it on three submissions: (A) two frog populations that look identical, share a breeding pond, have about 4% mtDNA divergence, but carry non-overlapping mating calls; (B) a plant group where one population is just smaller-flowered in drier soil yet grafts easily with the other; and (C) a case where the procedure outputs "merge," but doing so would strip legal protection from an endangered subpopulation—the answer I probably won't like. Finally, tell me what this procedure can't do and where it will definitely misfire, especially when conservation law and taxonomy don't line up.
