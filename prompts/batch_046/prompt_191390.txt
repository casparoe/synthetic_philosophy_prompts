Hey, I'm drafting a novel about a philosopher who realizes she's been living inside an argument she can't escape. Here's the trap she found:

(1) Scheffler's conjecture about an afterlife says extinction is awful not just for the dead but because it kills the afterlife of our work being taken up by strangers we'll never meet.
(2) If that's the core harm, then the value of any long project is essentially borrowed value from those future strangers; without them it's just vanity and we recieve nothing in return.
(3) So take someone writing a book that takes fifty years. If she knows humanity dies in a century, her book's value collapses to near zero, since nobody carries it on.
(4) But the same logic applies to parenting, science, gardening — anything that outlasts the doer. Near-extinction doesn't just sadden us; it retroactively deflates the worth of everything we've already done.
(5) That means the moral badness of extinction scales not with suffering, but with how much value it unwrites from the past. A silent extinction in 2124 makes my 2024 garden less valuable right now, which is bizarre.
(6) Worse, if borrowed-value is right, then a guaranteed distant utopia makes every current act more valuable, even harmful ones, as long as they're archived in the triumphant story. So this logic seems to let distant futures rewrite present moral facts.
(7) So either we accept that our present lives are mostly empty placeholders for strangers we'll never know, or we reject the conjecture. Where's the slip? I definately want to keep the intuition that extinction robs the future, but steps (4) and (6) feel like they prove too much. Which premise do I give up, and what's it cost me?
