I have recently been appointed the sole technical advisor to a municipal Climate Resilience Allocation Board charged with distributing a one-time $4.8 million fund within an eighteen-month window tied directly to the current electoral cycle. The board comprises elected officials with no training in cause evaluation or welfare economics, and they have directed me to build a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the importance-tractability-neglectedness framework, that my office can run on any proposal without requiring precise cross-domain quantification—our portfolio spans urban heat-island mitigation, regional wildfire-buffer management, and preparatory studies for climate-induced migration. The deliverable should be a stepwise protocol: the ordered questions to ask for any proposal, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the conditions that trigger an immediate technical rejection, and the explicit boundary conditions where the framework produces a result that conflicts with the board’s statutory equity mandate and must therefore be handed back to the members as an irreducible values judgment rather than a recommendation. The procedure must accommodate three structural complications: proposals of extreme importance whose tractability is undermined by jurisdictional limits; proposals that appear neglected only because unreliable federal funding streams exist elsewhere in the region; and proposals that score favorably on all three axes but risk concentrating infrastructure burdens on neighborhoods that have already endured repeated failures. Apply the protocol to three representative submissions—expanding the urban tree canopy in heat-vulnerable districts, subsidizing residential heat-pump retrofits across the metro area, and funding a multi-year regional study on climate-migration preparedness—including one case in which the procedure generates a technically sound recommendation that the board will almost certainly reject on political grounds. Finally, state precisely what the procedure cannot do—whether it can adjudicate trade-offs between present residents and future migrants, whether it assumes stable funding environments, and which steps remain judgment calls dressed in procedural form—and identify the points at which a practitioner without economic expertise must still make an uneliminable call.
