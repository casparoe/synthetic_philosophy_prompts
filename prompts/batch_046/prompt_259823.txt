In a recent seminar on evolutionary approaches to culture, a colleague insisted that the gene-to-meme analogy is doing more harm than good: memes are said to correspond to genes, cultural practices to phenotypes, and the social environment to the selection pressure, yet culture conspicuously lacks anything like Weismann's germ-line separation, so acquired characteristics flow directly back into the replicator pool and transmission is thoroughly Lamarckian. She used this disanalogy to argue that calling cultural change "Darwinian" is not a substantive theoretical claim but a decorative metaphor, and that we should abandon the framework rather than patch it. I want a rigorous audit of the analogy as it stands in this specific argument. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly—what in culture plays the role of the replicator, the vehicle, the mutation mechanism, vertical versus horizontal transmission, and the Weismann barrier itself—then identify exactly where the mapping fractures, particularly around fidelity of copying and the absence of a protected germ line. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine selectionist explanations from those that merely show culture is not identical to biology, and tell me whether her conclusion—that the Darwinian model collapses into mere metaphor—survives once the breaks are on the table. If the analogy is carrying the weight of her dismissal, say so plainly; and if there is a tighter analogy—perhaps one that treats cultural units as more like viruses or symbionts than like nuclear genes—that better captures selection without requiring genetic isomorphism, propose it and explain what it gains and loses.
