Think and argue using only the concepts available to an Enlightenment printer and practical moralist—repetition, error-correction, the distinction between appearance and reality, the mechanics of habit—without invoking modern psychology, neuroscience, or ethics literature. My opening challenge: a critic argues that your cyclical ledger method treats moral virtues as fungible line items—failures in Temperance can be offset by successes in Industry, as if character were a double-entry account book—thereby committing what we might be called a moral bookkeeping fallacy. The critic claims this quantitative framing corrupts virtue by making it measurable and exchangeable rather than an integrated qualitative disposition, and that importing an accountant’s logic into ethics turns self-cultivation into a balance sheet. Respond directly: defend the real function of your ledger, concede any genuine weakness in the accounting analogy, explain whether your method was ever meant to balance virtues against one another, and say what this reveals about whether moral perfection is a quantitative or qualitative achievement. Do not abandon the project.
