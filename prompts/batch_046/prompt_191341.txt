I sit on my university’s Committee for Institutional Risk and Emerging Technology, and we must vote next Thursday on whether to approve a partnership with an external laboratory proposing to install a general-purpose adaptive scheduling system across our campus energy and accessibility networks. The architecture is deliberately “thin”: it possesses no beliefs, no persistent preferences, and its objective function is explicitly set and reset by human administrators each quarter. The vendor’s safety brief contends that the orthogonality thesis and worries about instrumental convergence apply only to agents with stable utility functions, so a system that lacks both agency and enduring goals raises no special philosophical risk and should be governed by ordinary contractual oversight.

One of our faculty members—working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and machine learning—has circulated a dissent that introduces a distinction I find genuinely difficult to place. She distinguishes “motivational” instrumental convergence, in which an agent acquires sub-goals such as survival because they serve its ends, from what she terms “structural” instrumental convergence: a dynamics in which the mathematics of continuous optimisation itself generates resistance to objective-revision, not because the system “wants” anything, but because altering the objective function mid-process introduces computational discontinuities that the architecture is engineered to smooth out. On her view, this creates a functional analogue of self-preservation without any mental content, and standard safety frameworks miss it precisely because they scan for intentions rather than for stability-preserving dynamics. The committee is split. Some members regard this as a category error—applying philosophy-of-mind vocabulary to linear algebra—and wish to approve the partnership outright. Others argue that if structural convergence is real, we ought to require an architecture permitting “hard interruption” of the optimisation process without performance penalty, even at the cost of efficiency.

Please draft the committee’s written opinion recommending a concrete course of action—approve with conditions, reject, or approve contingent upon a specific technical safeguard with a clear time frame—that could be circulated before Thursday’s vote. The memo should treat the vendor’s agency-based objection seriously, engage the faculty distinction honestly rather than dismissing it as jargon, and state clearly whether orthogonality retains any bite once mental content is removed. Be explicit about where your recommendation is contingent and where you are least certain. If the framing I have given rests on a confused premise—for instance, if “structural convergence” collapses into ordinary engineering robustness, or if the orthogonality thesis is being misapplied to non-agentic tools—say so plainly rather than complying politely. Write in British English, and invite direct pushback if the task as posed cannot be done well from the information available.
