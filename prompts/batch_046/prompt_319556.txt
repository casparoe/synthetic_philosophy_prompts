I have just come away from a prolonged and somewhat frustrating argument with a close friend about whether rational herding models genuinely illuminate collective belief-formation, or whether they merely redescribe it in Bayesian vocabulary. The disagreement turned on whether informational cascades can survive once agents are allowed to reason about the reasoning of others—not just observe their actions—and whether there is any structural feature of an interaction protocol that could break a cascade without giving anyone direct access to the underlying state of the world.

I am drafting the final examination for an advanced graduate seminar in social epistemology and rational-choice theory. I need a single essay prompt, suitable for a 90-minute written exam, that forces students to move well beyond summarizing the standard cascade literature. Specifically, the question should present a scenario in which agents observe both the conclusions and a noisy, indirect trace of the reasoning processes of their predecessors, and it should ask whether this meta-cognitive visibility dissolves herding or displaces it to a second-order level. The prompt must also require students to identify one concrete structural modification to the observation protocol—not merely an increase in information—that would disrupt cascade formation, and to defend why that modification works even when agents remain rational and priors remain heterogeneous.

Please provide:
- The exam question itself, specifying the time limit and an expected response length of approximately 1,000 words.
- A model answer of about 800 words.
- A marking scheme totaling 25 marks, divided into specific conceptual moves. For each allocation, state the precise move required for full marks, and note the characteristic error that should cause a deduction. The scheme must clearly distinguish an A-range response (22–25) from a B-range response (18–21) by identifying which higher-order synthesis is missing in the latter.

The prompt should be rigorous and self-contained; it must not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should not depend on obscure technical results that fall outside standard graduate training in decision theory and epistemology.
