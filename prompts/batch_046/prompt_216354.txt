After forty years in condensed-matter physics, I have begun, in retirement, to study the philosophy of law, and I find that my scientific training makes me attentive to institutional design in ways that differ from the usual conceptual analysis. I am struck by a particular sentencing architecture that appears in several modern jurisdictions influenced by rehabilitation theory. In these systems, the actual duration of imprisonment for a given conviction is not fixed by the statute; rather, an offender remains in custody until a periodic actuarial review predicts that his recidivism probability has fallen below a jurisdiction-specific threshold. The prison term is therefore dynamically calibrated to a changing risk profile. Yet these same jurisdictions maintain a separate public registry—functionally analogous to a sex-offender registry but applied across a broader range of felonies—where the length of registration is set categorically by the original conviction class (for instance, ten years for a violent felony, five for a non-violent one) and is entirely insensitive to rehabilitation progress, risk reduction, or behavioral change during incarceration. The registry is static where the prison term is fluid.

This bifurcation puzzles me. What implicit theory of punishment does it presuppose? One might say the system has split punishment into two separable components: a consequentialist mechanism of incapacitation and rehabilitation governed by predictive risk, and a retributive mechanism of symbolic condemnation fixed to the gravity of the original act. But is that reconstruction correct, or is it too neat? Can a pure retributivist justify a prison term whose length is determined not by desert but by a statistical model of future dangerousness? Can a pure consequentialist justify a registry that continues to impose social and economic costs long after the offender’s predicted risk has dropped to the baseline population rate? Or might a unified theory—perhaps one that treats punishment as a communicative practice with both instrumental and expressive dimensions—explain both features without contradiction?

I want to know whether this practice genuinely presupposes a hybrid theory, whether its two parts are philosophically consistent with each other, and whether they could survive if the underlying theory were replaced. If we adopted a strictly desert-based view, must the risk-assessment protocol be abandoned, or could it be reinterpreted? If we adopted a strictly forward-looking view, must the categorical registry be eliminated or converted into a risk-based licensing scheme? And is there any interpretation under which the practice is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories—so that its internal tension is only apparent?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory or theories that best license this design, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it well, and indicate which feature—the dynamic prison term or the static registry—would be the more decisive point of failure for each rival account of punishment. I am less interested in empirical critiques of risk-assessment accuracy than in the conceptual commitments embedded in treating one dimension of punishment as variable and the other as fixed.
