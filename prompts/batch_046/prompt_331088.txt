After a long and somewhat heated argument with a close friend about whether moral blame can survive determinism, I realized I could not precisely locate the errors in his account of Strawson’s reactive-attitudes view. He summarized it roughly as follows. Strawson, he claimed, dissolves the free-will problem by showing that moral responsibility has nothing to do with whether an agent could have acted otherwise; it depends solely on whether it is appropriate for others to direct reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, moral indignation—toward that agent. He distinguished the participant stance, in which we treat someone as a full member of the moral community and hold them responsible through these emotional responses, from the objective stance, which is essentially a clinical or therapeutic posture we adopt toward abnormal agents such as psychopaths or those with severe neurological damage, and which involves suspending all reactive attitudes entirely. From this he drew two further conclusions. First, because the appropriateness of reactive attitudes tracks only the agent’s displayed quality of will—not any metaphysical capacity for choice—moral responsibility is a purely social-psychological phenomenon: you are responsible if and only if your community happens to feel the right emotions toward you, which implies there is no cross-cultural or objective standard of responsibility beyond local emotional consensus. Second, he argued that Strawson regards the objective stance as itself a kind of moral failure—something we should resist adopting whenever possible—and that this is precisely why Strawson rejects determinism: if determinism were true, we would be rationally compelled to extend the objective stance universally, thereby destroying the interpersonal fabric of moral life. He also added a more specific point he seemed to have drawn from a recent discussion: that reactive attitudes are not merely emotional reactions but are constitutive of interpersonal recognition, functioning as a form of moral address; this is why we do not resent young children, not because they lack free will, but because the reactive attitude literally fails to land on them as an appropriate object of address. I have the strong sense that this summary mixes genuinely accurate elements with serious distortions, particularly around determinism, the nature and value of the objective stance, and whether responsibility collapses into subjective emotional consensus, and that the “moral address” framing may conflate Strawson with later second-personal interpretations. Could you audit this characterization for me? Please separate what is genuinely faithful to Strawson’s account, what counts as a subtle misrepresentation or anachronistic conflation, what is plainly wrong, and whether his conclusions—that Strawson rejects determinism and that moral responsibility is nothing more than local emotional consensus—actually follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate.
