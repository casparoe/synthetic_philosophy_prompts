Two members of a philosophy-of-mind study group are deadlocked over whether epiphenomenalism can survive the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The first contends that if qualitative properties are causally inert, then a judgment such as 'I am now experiencing redness' cannot be caused by the redness itself; it must be produced entirely by underlying physical brain states. Consequently, the accuracy of our phenomenal judgments becomes a cosmic coincidence—our beliefs about experience are systematically disconnected from the properties they describe, which undermines any claim that we genuinely know our own phenomenology. The second responds by rejecting the premise that judgment-reference requires causal production. She proposes instead a structural homomorphism between neural dynamics and phenomenal properties: a non-causal, lawlike mapping that aligns brain-state judgments with qualitative states without conferring causal efficacy on the latter. On her view, evolutionary tuning or fundamental psychophysical laws could establish and maintain this correlation between two ontologically distinct domains, much as mathematical structures can map onto physical systems without being identical to them. This, she argues, dissolves the paradox: judgments are reliable because the mapping is stable, yet phenomenal properties remain causally inert with respect to physical effects. The first objects that structural homomorphism either collapses into identity—making phenomenal properties identical to the mapped neural structures and therefore causally relevant—or remains a brute, unexplained correlation. If the correlation is not explained by the phenomenal properties causing anything, he asks, why should it hold specifically with those judgments rather than others? He adds that invoking evolutionary tuning merely pushes the mystery back one step, since evolutionary selection operates only on physical behavior, leaving the alignment with non-physical qualities unexplained. The second counters that causation and correlation are conceptually distinct: a lawlike bridge between domains need not reduce to physical causation, and the redundancy charge fails because the qualitative properties play an essential structural role in the mapping relation even if they do not initiate physical events. The first insists that unless the phenomenal properties do genuine explanatory work in generating the correlation, they drop out as redundant, leaving only physical causation to do all the work. Adjudicate this exchange point by point: is structural homomorphism a coherent third option between direct causation and miraculous coincidence, or does it secretly depend on one? Does the evolutionary-tuning reply rescue it from the charge of being brute? And what, if anything, is each interlocutor missing about the conditions under which a judgment can be accurate without being causally produced by its object?
