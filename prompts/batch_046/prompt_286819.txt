I'm presenting the thesis of my paper, "No Parts, Only Processes," at a joint metaphysics and philosophy of biology seminar next Thursday. I defend process-integrationism: mereological composition never yields static objects, but only temporally extended process-unities with non-redundant causal profiles. On this view, organisms are not wholes composed of cells, but single causal processes that appear part-structured solely because of our spatial framing; this rejects universalism and standard mechanistic part-whole explanation without collapsing into eliminativism about organisms themselves.

The audience comprises three metaphysicians committed to classical extensional mereology who regard vagueness as the only live problem for special composition; two philosophers of biology who depend on mechanistic decomposition for functional explanation and view process ontology as a threat to empirical practice; and one formal ontologist concerned with whether "process-unity" can be axiomatised without circularity. They are sharp, skeptical of anti-mereological moves that sound like verbal redescription, and they care more about scientific coherence than about ontology for its own sake.
