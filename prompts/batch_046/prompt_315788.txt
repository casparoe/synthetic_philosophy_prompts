I'm working through my position on translation under formal constraint—specifically whether preserving rhyme, meter, and phonesthetic clustering in a short French stanzaic poem (in the mode associated with Marot) is a preservation of meaning or a betrayal of it. Here are my commitments:

(1) The formal architecture—rhyme scheme, syllabic rhythm, lineation—is not a vessel for content but part of the content, constraining interpretive possibilities.

(2) When the target language lacks phonotactic resources for the source rhyme scheme, the translator should substitute an analogous formal constraint rather than abandon form for literal paraphrase.

(3) There is no separable poem-spirit independent of its specific verbal and sonic realization; altering every lexical item to preserve rhyme yields a different poem, not a translation.

(4) The translator's primary duty is to reproduce the cognitive and affective effect on a target-language reader, which may require discarding the original formal architecture entirely if it is culturally opaque.

(5) Phonosemantic clustering—such as the concentration of labial nasals that evokes tenderness—is part of meaning, yet this sound-symbolic value is language-specific: the same phonemes do not carry identical affective charge across languages, so the cluster can only be replaced, not translated, and its replacement carries a different symbolic value.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which are only apparently so, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity. In particular, address whether (2) and (3) can be reconciled without collapsing translation into original composition, and whether (5) undermines (2) specifically for sound-pattern preservation.
