I am currently taking a course on philosophy of language and find the move from language games to actual classification practices rather disorientating, particularly when adjacent professional groups appear to deploy the same vocabulary under incompatible rules of engagement.

Please construct a thought experiment that meets the following specifications precisely. The setting must be realistic and confined to a single workplace — specifically, a small municipal archive where historians and legal clerks collaborate on digitising holdings — and the scenario itself should occupy no more than one paragraph. Introduce a single physical artefact, such as a bound ledger from the 1920s, that the historians unambiguously treat as a historical record and the clerks unambiguously treat as a legal instrument, and then introduce a second artefact that possesses every feature common to both classifications yet is rejected by both groups. The rejection must not arise from an obvious lexical ambiguity; it should fall out naturally from the different language games governing each group's practice, for instance from competing criteria of custodial responsibility or evidentiary weight, rather than from anyone misapplying a dictionary definition.

After presenting the scenario, walk through what a family-resemblance view predicts about whether there is a single correct classification, contrast this with what a classical essentialist account — one that demands necessary and sufficient conditions for membership — would be forced to say, and identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the philosophical work. Then include an honest note on how a defender of essentialism might attack the scenario, perhaps by arguing that the two groups are simply using homonymous terms with distinct hidden essences, and explain what happens to the experiment if that feature is removed. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and please avoid invoking highly specialised secondary literature; the response should be fully accessible to someone who has studied Wittgenstein's later philosophy but has no access to online sources.
