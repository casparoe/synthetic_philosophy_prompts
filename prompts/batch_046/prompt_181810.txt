I am delivering the opening remarks at a retirement symposium honoring Dr. Elena Vasquez, who spent thirty years directing university student-wellness services. The audience is split: roughly half are philosophers and clinical psychologists familiar with Stoic cognitive frameworks, and half are family members, former students, and administrative staff with no philosophical background. I want the remarks to turn on the Stoic dichotomy of control—specifically the tension between Epictetus's classical strict binary, in which judgments and impulses lie within our power while bodies, reputations, and external outcomes do not, and the modern self-help "Stoic fork," which adds a middle category of things partly in our power. Her career was animated by the belief that institutional design should actively eliminate stressors—a belief that implicitly treats external obstacles as belonging to that middle, manageable category rather than as indifferent externals. The speech must genuinely honor her concrete achievements, including crisis-intervention systems that saved lives, without endorsing that philosophical framing, and without lecturing the non-philosophers. The distinction must do real structural work: it should determine exactly which praise is appropriate and which form of comfort I must avoid offering. Please write the remarks in American English, structured in the following sections with approximate word counts: (1) "Setting the Scene" (~120 words): establish the occasion and acknowledge the mixed audience; (2) "The Framework" (~180 words): explain the binary and the fork in plain language, avoiding technical jargon that loses the family side; (3) "The Tribute, Re-framed" (~250 words): apply the distinction to her legacy, honoring what she actually controlled—institutional judgment, the decision to respond, the structure of care—while gently distinguishing that from controlling student outcomes or removing all adversity; (4) "Closing Toast" (~100 words): a brief, respectful conclusion that observes the boundary between honoring her work and claiming she conquered fortune itself. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or suggested reading. Avoid direct quotations from ancient texts; paraphrase all Stoic ideas in your own words.
