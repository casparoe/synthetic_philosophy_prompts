Following a recent clinical episode of prolonged depersonalization that left my first-person reports about qualitative experience unstable in ways standard psychiatric descriptions do not fully capture, I have come to regard the illusionism debate as having immediate personal stakes rather than being a purely academic dispute. I am commissioning a formal adversarial-collaboration document between two opposing camps.

Camp One: eliminative illusionists, who hold that phenomenal consciousness involves no actual phenomenal properties whatsoever, but rather a pervasive cognitive misrepresentation in which functional states—specifically those involved in attention, working memory, and self-modeling—are erroneously represented by the subject as possessing intrinsic, non-representational qualitative character.

Camp Two: attenuated-presence realists, who hold that phenomenal properties do genuinely exist but are radically thinner and more relational than naive introspection suggests—amounting to nothing more than the availability of representational content for global broadcasting under conditions of attentional transparency, and lacking any intrinsic, private, or ineffable dimensions.

The document should be organized into four numbered sections.

Section One—Agreed Claims: List specific propositions about the neural correlates of introspective monitoring, the conditions under which first-person reports become unreliable, and the architecture of attention and global broadcasting that both camps can endorse without reservation. These should be substantive, not vacuous.

Section Two—Exact Disagreements: State each camp's position in wording that its own proponents would accept. Identify precisely where they part ways on ontology, epistemology, and the interpretation of clinical data. Further, determine whether any portion of the dispute is verbal—particularly regarding whether terms like phenomenal presence or what-it-is-likeness denote an actual property, a theoretical posit, or a category error—and if so, specify which part and why.

Section Three—Cruxes and Predictive Tests: Isolate the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. I take it that one crux is the functional-equivalence problem: whether there exists any empirically detectable difference between a system that genuinely instantiates thin phenomenal properties and one that merely reproduces the functional profile of introspective reporting without such properties. Another crux concerns the phenomenon I will call gradated absence: the hypothetical but experimentally tractable condition in which a subject's reports of phenomenal presence scale continuously with attentional load rather than being binary—present or absent—raising the question of whether this gradation supports the illusionist's constructivist model or the realist's attenuation account. For each crux, describe a concrete experimental protocol, clinical observation, or conceptual argument that both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts the outcome or implication will be.

Section Four—Residual Open Questions: Identify only those questions both sides agree remain genuinely unresolved after this collaboration, excluding any issue that one camp considers settled but the other does not recognize as a meaningful question.

Write in formal, precise philosophical prose. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific authors or texts. Where you rely on a technical concept not universally familiar, define it within the document rather than assuming prior knowledge.
