As a journalist who covers philosophy of religion and the sciences of belief, I have been tracking the debate over divine hiddenness—particularly the argument, developed in its modern form in the 1990s, that the prevalence of non-resistant nonbelief is strong evidence against the existence of a perfectly loving, omnipotent deity. The standard exchange is now almost ritualized: evidential objections from hiddenness, skeptical-theist replies that human cognitive limits block our assessment of divine reasons, free-will defenses that relational autonomy requires epistemic distance, and the counter-reply that these defenses seem ad hoc and unfalsifiable. After decades of this choreography, the positions have not converged; if anything, they have hardened into disciplinary camps.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not mainly empirical—no experiment is going to settle it—but that the disagreement is partly verbal and partly structural. By "hiddenness," one camp means an absence of propositional evidence available to rational agents; another means a failure of relational or experiential accessibility; yet another treats it as a feature of what we might call "revelation economies," where the geographic and cultural clustering of mystical or revelatory experiences functions like a sampling bias rather than a universal signal. Meanwhile, the sides may be answering different questions: one about the rational probability of theism given total evidence, one about the moral psychology of a deity who values love over cognition, and one about whether "non-resistant" belief-formation can be defined without smuggling in culturally specific norms of rationality.

Please diagnose the impasse honestly. Is this a substantive disagreement about standards of explanation—what God owes to every rational agent? A verbal dispute about "belief," "evidence," or "hiddenness" that collapses once the terms are disambiguated? Are the sides weighing the same cases differently because they disagree about what counts as data, or are they answering entirely different questions under a single heading? Is there a hidden formal issue—perhaps about the conditions under which absence of a signal counts as evidence of absence when the expected signal is ambiguous, or about whether doxastic voluntarism is required for the free-will defense to work?

I want the diagnosis supported by what each side actually says: show where their arguments talk past one another rather than merely colliding. And tell me, given your diagnosis, whether anything could actually move the debate—a conceptual distinction that dissolves the problem, a reframing of "non-resistant" that makes the evidential argument misfire, a recognition that one side's datum is the other side's background condition, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed? Or should we expect permanent stalemate because the disagreement bottoms out in bedrock intuitions about divine goodness and human autonomy that neither side can argue for? Be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are conjectural.
