I'm preparing a presentation for my high school government and literature seminar on Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery," and I want to read it as a philosophical critique of direct democratic procedures—specifically the referendum-style decision-making seen in Swiss direct democracy, Brexit, and California ballot propositions. Most discussions treat the story as a simple allegory for the tyranny of the majority, but I want to go further: the lottery is procedurally impeccable—universal participation, transparent rules, voluntary compliance, and no hidden coercion—yet it produces a result that violates every basic right. Does this mean that direct democracy lacks an internal safeguard against morally catastrophic outcomes, or does it show that democratic legitimacy requires a prior constitutional constraint that removes certain questions from popular vote? I'm especially interested in scenes like the casual conversation before the drawing, Old Man Warner's defense of tradition, and the children's eager gathering of stones: do these dramatize a failure of deliberation, since the community never actually debates whether the lottery should continue, or do they show a success of democratic socialization, where everyone internalizes the procedure so thoroughly that dissent becomes unthinkable? I'd also like to know where the analogy to real-world referendums breaks down—no modern state votes to execute a citizen—yet whether the story still illuminates something about how direct democracy handles questions of fundamental rights that purely procedural arguments about popular sovereignty miss. Could you help me work through what Jackson's fiction adds to the philosophical debate that abstract arguments alone don't capture?
