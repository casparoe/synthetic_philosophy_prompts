I recently joined a small collective that transforms abandoned industrial water-purification plants into temporary acoustic installation spaces. Our labor is entirely unpaid; we continue it only because the environments we produce possess a particular serene, disquieting quality that seems to matter regardless of audience approval, funding, or any further consequence. My closest collaborator—someone with whom I have shared years of genuine friendship—is responsible for the sound design. When we work together, the installations achieve a coherence neither of us could create alone; the experience feels genuinely good in a way that appears independent of any practical result. Outside the studio, however, the friendship has deteriorated sharply. He is chronically unreliable with collective obligations, dismissive of other members, and has begun to invoke our shared history as leverage in artistic disputes. The collective is now considering whether he should be expelled. If I support his removal, I protect the group’s functioning and my own peace, but I also eliminate the specific combination—this friendship fused with this practice—that seems to generate a distinctive kind of worth. If I argue to keep him, I preserve the possibility of that combined good, yet I do so by sustaining a personal bond that, taken on its own, now appears to diminish rather than enrich my life. How ought I to weigh the value of the whole against the value of its parts considered separately? Can the goodness produced by this particular union of friendship and artistic practice justify maintaining a relationship that has become, in isolation, a source of harm?
