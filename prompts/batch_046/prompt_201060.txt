I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or history, but I've been working through some introductory texts on the history of science in my spare time to understand how scientific practices stabilize. In a recent discussion with a friend who studies early modern Europe, I found myself resistant to the standard tripartite division—Middle Ages, Renaissance, Scientific Revolution—because its neatness feels suspicious, almost like a version-control history that has been rebased to hide the messy intermediate commits.

The essay I want to draft argues that these three labels are not discovered discontinuities in the historical record but retrospective packaging devices—what I am tentatively calling "semantic version tags" applied to a continuous intellectual deployment. On this view, the "Middle Ages" operates as a deprecated legacy branch, the "Renaissance" as a minor release that maintained substantial backward compatibility, and the "Scientific Revolution" as a major version bump that was in fact rolled out incrementally over centuries rather than deployed in a single breaking change. I want to claim that treating these categories as real entities risks a reification error: we confuse a 19th-century curricular architecture for the territory of actual practices, texts, and institutions. At the same time, I am aware that I may be sliding illegitimately from "these categories are constructed" to "there was no significant change," which is not my intended claim, and I am unsure how to police that boundary precisely.

Could you comment on whether this framing is coherent? In particular, I worry that the software-release analogy imports an anachronistic ontology of discrete, bounded updates that may obscure the genuinely gradual but directional transformations historians document. I also wonder whether there is an established distinction—perhaps within philosophy of history or philosophy of science—between a category being socially "invented" and being straightforwardly "fictitious," and whether my argument risks collapsing that distinction. I am not asking for a bibliography or a scorecard against named scholars; I want to know whether the reasoning holds water, where its edges are blurry, and whether there is an obvious objection that my engineering-biased framing causes me to miss.
