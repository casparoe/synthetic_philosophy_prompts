I am a graduate student in philosophy of science currently taking a seminar on Kuhn’s Structure of Scientific Revolutions, and I also serve as an editorial assistant for our university’s interdisciplinary cognitive-science journal, where I must decide within forty-eight hours—without consulting outside experts—whether a submitted manuscript that rejects standard methodological assumptions should advance to peer review or be returned as incommensurable with our framework. Turn Kuhn’s account of incommensurability into a reusable decision procedure for a referee in exactly this position. The procedure must be grounded in the actual conceptual structure of Kuhn’s argument—the dependence of observation on exemplars, the absence of a neutral algorithm for theory choice, and the way taxonomic differences block direct translation—while treating local incommensurability, in which only a subset of theoretical terms shift meaning while others remain intact, as the primary practical case rather than total paradigm replacement. Include: (1) a sequential set of tests applied to the manuscript’s argumentation, not merely its conclusions, to distinguish a legitimate paradigm challenge from basic disciplinary incompetence; (2) specific indicators for identifying when a key term is being used with a different taxonomic structure versus the same structure applied to different data; (3) worked applications to three submissions—one applying quantum formalisms to social-network dynamics (likely poor science), one proposing that cognitive psychology replace its static concept of attention with a dynamic-systems analog (potentially legitimate), and one that technically passes the incommensurability tests but is methodologically sloppy in ways that should make the referee reject it anyway; (4) explicit flagging of the points where the referee’s own paradigm commitments cannot be eliminated and must be acknowledged; and (5) a clear statement of what the procedure cannot settle, such as whether the proposed paradigm is actually true or whether the journal ought to publish it. The deliverable should be usable by someone without philosophy training, should fix the stakes and time constraints of the decision, and should avoid any request for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
