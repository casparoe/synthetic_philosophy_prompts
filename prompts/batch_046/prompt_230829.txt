Debates over whether history is "locked in" frequently invoke the image of a river cutting a canyon: a slight initial slope directs the flow, erosion deepens that channel until reversing course becomes physically impossible, and the present landscape is read back as the inevitable result of those early, contingent currents. The analogy is typically used to support two claims at once — that small early differences (a missed invention, a different leader) would have diverged into radically different present outcomes, and that the actual institutional path we now inhabit is robustly stable against reversal. But the discussion rarely distinguishes this geological picture from two other frameworks that also claim irreversibility: Dollo's law in evolutionary biology, which holds that complex adaptations, once lost, are not regained in identical form, and W. Brian Arthur's formal model of competing technologies under increasing returns to adoption. How far does the river-canyon analogy actually hold when audited against these alternatives? Provide an explicit correspondence table — water, bedrock, erosion, the adjacent valleys, the point of no return — then identify the precise points of breakdown, especially regarding whether the "lock-in" at issue is physical, functional, or institutional. Separate the disanalogies that damage the claim of radical contingency from those that only damage the claim of physical inevitability. Finally, assess whether the conclusion that the same outcomes would not have arrived anyway survives once we distinguish path-dependent stabilization from true historical necessity, and indicate whether Dollo's law or an increasing-returns model offers the more honest analogy for institutional path dependence.
