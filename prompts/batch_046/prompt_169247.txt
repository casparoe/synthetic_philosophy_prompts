I've been trying to think through whether people can always be wrong about what's going on in their own minds, after stumbling across the philosophical idea that mental states might be luminous—meaning if you're in one, you automatically know it—together with a principle I understand is called the KK principle, which says that knowing something entails knowing that you know it. To see whether I really grasp the objections, I designed a hypothetical scenario for my study group: imagine a wrist-worn device that measures ambient temperature and displays the reading with exactly a five-minute delay. A person sits in a room that slowly warms from sixty to seventy-five degrees over an hour; at minute forty-five the band reads seventy-two degrees, yet the wearer reports feeling neither warm nor cold, just neutral. I asked the group: does this case show that feeling warm is not luminous? And does it undermine the KK principle, since the wearer might know the temperature via the device without knowing that they know they feel warm?

One member submitted the following answer, which I reproduce here for evaluation: Luminosity claims that if a mental state is present you know it, and here the mental state is being warm, but since the room is seventy-two degrees and the person has not noticed, luminosity is false—you can be in the state without knowing it. The KK principle says knowing p entails knowing you know p. In this case the person knows p, that the temperature is seventy-two, from the wristband, but they do not know that they feel warm, so they lack second-order knowledge about the mental state and KK is violated. Also, the argument associated with Timothy Williamson about gradual change applies here: because the warming is slow, there is no precise moment when knowledge switches on, which is exactly why anti-luminosity holds. So the device confirms that external measurements can produce first-order knowledge that never generates the corresponding second-order awareness.
