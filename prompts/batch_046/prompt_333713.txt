I serve as the senior mediator for a university Office of Community Standards, handling eight to twelve interpersonal-harm reports weekly—harassment, betrayal of confidence, exclusion, public humiliation—frequently complicated by mental-health conditions, trauma histories, neurodivergence, or steep power asymmetries. Within ninety minutes of receiving a file I must recommend either a reactive-accountability track, in which the institution treats responsive moral attitudes such as indignation or resentment as appropriate and expresses normative expectations of the party, or an objective-management track, in which the party is addressed administratively, clinically, or protectively without being made the target of institutional moral blame. I need a reusable decision procedure—ordered gates with explicit stop conditions, sufficient-answer criteria for each gate, and clearly flagged judgment points—that translates Strawson’s distinction between reactive attitudes and the objective attitude into something my office can run without requiring philosophical or psychiatric expertise. The procedure must preserve the conceptual distinctions that matter—quality of will versus causal explanation, shared normative expectation versus predictive expectation, and personal versus vicarious reactive stance—while being honest about where judgment is irreducible and where simplification risks misfiring. To keep the framework from being pure textbook repetition, build in two procedural injections not usually found in standard summaries: a Vicarious Attenuation Gate that asks whether an institutional reactive attitude is genuinely appropriate or merely a distorted proxy for personal resentment, and a Substitutability Gate that asks whether suspending reactive attitudes still permits an objective-management response that protects the victim and upholds norms. Include worked applications to three cases: (a) a staff member who sends an unambiguously hostile, insulting message after being denied promotion, with no mental-health factor present; (b) a student with documented intermittent dissociative episodes who makes unfounded public accusations against a peer causing serious harm, showing confusion and remorse between episodes; and (c) a senior faculty member whose demeaning disability-related jokes have caused documented harm, where clinical assessment indicates early-stage frontotemporal dementia degrading social inhibition but leaving core moral reasoning intact, and where the victim demands full reactive accountability including formal censure and a public apology. For case (c) the procedure should deliver the answer I will least want—objective-management with protective restructuring and victim compensation, but no moral censure—so that I can see exactly where the framework overrules intuitive demand for blame. The procedure must also state explicitly what kinds of harm or relational context fall outside its scope and what it cannot settle. Finally, in your response please state how confident you are in the framework overall, and identify the single gate, distinction, or applied judgment about which you are least certain.
