Write the opening remarks for the interdisciplinary workshop "Pain, Meaning, and Clinical Judgment," delivered to a room split evenly between pain physicians and neurologists on one side and philosophers, disability scholars, and patient advocates on the other. The remarks must set the agenda without lecturing either half, and they must begin with the speech itself, with no preface or commentary.

The guiding problem is pain asymbolia: the well-documented but clinically rare condition in which a subject registers tissue damage, reports the sensation as painful, yet shows no negative affect and no motivation to stop or avoid it. Use this not as a clinical curiosity but as a structural mirror for the whole workshop. Introduce a three-layer distinction that every session will have to navigate: nociceptive registration (the sensory signal), affective coloring (the suffering), and practical salience (the reason to act). In ordinary pain these three layers collapse into one experience; in asymbolia they come apart, and that separation should shape the ethical and clinical questions that follow.

The speech needs to hold two tensions at once. For the clinicians, it must acknowledge that their default protocol—reduce the signal and assume suffering is thereby addressed—is therapeutically justified in the vast majority of cases, and that treating asymbolia as a universal model for pain management would be a dangerous category error. For the philosophers and advocates, it must show that the collapse of the three layers is not conceptually necessary: if registration can exist without coloring or salience, then the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the sensory quality itself but resides in one or both of the other layers, and clinical ethics must ask which layer it is actually treating when it intervenes.

Because the audience is mixed, no sentence should depend on technical jargon without a brief gloss, and no sentence should reduce the conceptual problem to a platitude. The remarks should conclude by framing the workshop's task not as "understanding asymbolia," but as deciding whether pain medicine's moral authority derives from eliminating registration, coloring, or salience—and admitting that the answer may differ by patient, and that the distinction is not merely philosophical but therapeutic.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not quote any author verbatim. Keep the remarks under five minutes when spoken, and ensure the philosophical idea does real structural work in shaping what the speaker can and cannot claim about standard clinical practice.
