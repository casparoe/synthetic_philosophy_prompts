I'm drafting an essay sketch defending a position I call institutional reductionism about testimony. The central claim is that testimonial warrant is reducible, but not to a hearer's private perceptual or inferential evidence about the speaker's reliability. Instead, it reduces to the hearer's participation in a social practice structured by norms of accountability and correctability: one defers because the framework permits shared error-correction and holds speakers answerable, not because one treats the speaker as a basic epistemic source, and not because one runs an isolated Bayesian update on a track record. I mean this to split the difference between standard reductionism—which treats testimony like ordinary empirical evidence—and anti-reductionism—which takes it as an irreducible source. My specific concern is that once spelled out, the "institutional framework" may just become another input in the hearer's private reasoning, causing the view to collapse back into standard reductionism. Please comment on whether this middle position is conceptually stable, whether it avoids both horns, and whether it wrongly assumes all testimony occurs within explicit institutional settings rather than in isolated, one-off exchanges. Focus on the argument's coherence and on objections I may have missed; do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
