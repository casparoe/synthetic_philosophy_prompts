As a doctoral candidate in philosophy of action working with an autonomous-systems ethics laboratory, I am attempting to operationalize the distinction between sphexish mechanical rigidity and genuine behavioral flexibility for practical safety auditing. The familiar first-order version—an agent repeating an identical action sequence despite changed conditions—is well documented, but contemporary automated systems more often display what I term second-order sphexishness: not repetition of a single motor routine, but the rigid application of a correction meta-procedure that systematically regenerates the failure state it was designed to resolve. I need a reusable, thirty-minute audit procedure that a technically competent but philosophically untrained evaluator can apply to a documented failure loop.

The procedure should take the form of ordered diagnostic questions, each with criteria for a sufficient answer, explicit termination points yielding a classification (first-order sphexish, second-order sphexish, or adaptive flexibility constrained by resources), and clearly demarcated nodes where the distinction collapses into a normative judgment about acceptable risk rather than an empirical behavioral fact. Please construct this flowchart-in-words, specify what kinds of behavioral logs and system specifications the auditor needs in hand, flag where the procedure is likely to misclassify a resource-starved but genuinely flexible system, and then apply it to three cases: a warehouse robot that repeats an identical barcode-scanning trajectory after each failure without adjusting arm position or illumination; a clinical triage algorithm that, upon receiving contradictory vital-sign data, iteratively re-runs its internal validation subroutine without escalating to human oversight; and an autonomous navigation module that, encountering a blocked corridor, invokes its standard path-recomputation protocol in a way that resets its destination constraint and thereby regenerates the blockage.
