I have been thinking on my own about Kierkegaard's treatment of Abraham and Isaac and about what philosophers call the teleological suspension of the ethical, though I have no formal background in philosophy or theology. I came across these ideas in a secondhand translation and through long conversations with a friend who reads religious ethics as a hobby. Next Thursday evening I will present my own interpretation to a discussion circle that meets at my local library. The group is small, about eight people: two retired theology professors who are deeply familiar with existentialist writing, three people with training in moral philosophy who generally defend universalist ethical systems, and a few others who attend out of personal interest in biblical narratives. I want to defend a specific reading I have worked out. Rather than seeing Abraham's silence as proof that religious faith simply overrides universal moral law, I think the suspension is more narrowly targeted. I propose that there are two layers within the ethical itself: one layer concerns the direct prohibition against harming the innocent, which Abraham never violates in his heart, and another layer concerns the obligation to be transparent and accountable to one's family and community, which is what actually gets suspended. I call this second layer the communicative dimension of ethics, and I argue that Abraham enters a kind of covenantal space in which he is released from that communicative duty precisely so that the deeper prohibition remains intact and unspoken. This means Abraham is not a law-breaker but someone suspended from the expectation of explanation, which I think makes the story more coherent and more disturbing in the right way.
