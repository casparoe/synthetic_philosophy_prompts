Analyze the 1980 inclusion of post-traumatic stress disorder in DSM-III and its epidemiological expansion over the following two decades through the lens of Ian Hacking’s looping effects for interactive kinds. Map the loop precisely: identify the initial classified population (Vietnam veterans and other trauma survivors), the expert classifiers (the APA task force and VA clinicians), and the mechanisms of feedback—how receiving the diagnosis altered self-narratives, help-seeking patterns, and symptom reporting, and how those behavioral shifts in turn reshaped the criteria in DSM-III-R and DSM-IV. Then press on where a dyadic expert-patient model breaks down. The category was substantially shaped before widespread clinical adoption by congressional testimony, veterans’ advocacy organizations, and the bureaucratic logic of VA disability adjudication and insurance reimbursement codes. Evaluate whether this institutional mediation demands a “triadic” extension to the loop—bureaucratic validators that are neither experts nor subjects—or whether it can be subsumed within Hacking’s existing framework. Assess what the episode shows about the theory itself: does it confirm looping effects, reveal an unanticipated administrative channel, or mark a boundary condition in which loops operate without direct clinical interaction? Be disciplined about what the theory explains (the widening prevalence and changing symptom profiles), what it misses (the role of psychopharmacology marketing and the standardization of CBT protocols in driving diagnosis rates), and where it is genuinely silent rather than forced to speak. Conclude with a judgment about whether PTSD’s history validates the looping-effects model, requires its revision, or demonstrates that psychiatric classification can be captured by institutional feedback loops that bypass the patient-expert dyad entirely.
