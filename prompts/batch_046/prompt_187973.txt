I have been thinking about intelligence entirely on my own—outside any formal study or discipline—and I keep returning to a puzzle that test scores never quite settle. Imagine a system that solves every practical problem you place before it: it forecasts supply shortages, detects medical anomalies, and negotiates contracts. Now suppose it does all of this through entirely separate inner mechanisms, like three expert consultants who never share notes, so that an insight gained during diagnosis never touches its economic reasoning. By contrast, imagine a system with the same overall success rate, but where every failure and success updates one shared inner picture of the world. I want to know whether both deserve the name “intelligent,” or whether the separation itself breaks the concept.

Please construct a thought experiment that isolates exactly this structural difference. Design a single realistic scenario—perhaps inside a hospital logistics hub or a regional supply network—described in one paragraph only, with no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, no demons, and no appeals to consciousness, sentience, or human-like feeling. Present two non-biological decision systems, Integrated and Fragmented, that match each other precisely on every measurable task outcome. Integrated maintains one cross-cutting representational framework: what it learns about patient-flow patterns genuinely reshapes its model of warehouse scarcity. Fragmented achieves identical outputs through hermetically sealed modules; no representation, weight, or inference crosses between domains, so its medical reasoning and its logistics reasoning share nothing.

Then apply two views of intelligence that you should define in your own terms within the answer: first, the Performance View, which holds that intelligence is nothing more than the reliable achievement of complex, varied goals, regardless of internal architecture; second, the Unity View, which holds that intelligence requires achievements in one domain to genuinely constrain and inform reasoning in others through shared representational content. For each system, state which view counts it as intelligent and why—specify the belief formed, the method of assessment, and how nearby structural variants (for instance, partial rather than total isolation) would shift the verdict. Identify the exact feature—cross-domain representational flow—that makes the two views diverge here.
