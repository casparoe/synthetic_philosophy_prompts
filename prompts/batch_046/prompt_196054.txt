As a journalist covering the philosophy of science, I have been trying to understand whether any single account of biological function can survive contact with synthetic biology and evolutionary vestiges. Below are four concrete verdicts that feel correct to me but pull in opposing directions; I want to know what principle, if any, unites them.

Case 1 — The human appendix. Its evolutionary origin lies in digestive structures that were selected in ancestral species, but in modern humans it is largely vestigial in that role. It now serves as a reservoir for beneficial gut bacteria and contributes to immune maintenance. My verdict: Its present biological function is immune-related reservoir maintenance; the ancestral digestive selection history does not fix its current function.

Case 2 — The ostrich wing. The wing structure evolved under strong selection for flight in ancestral birds. In the ostrich it remains anatomically intact, yet it contributes nothing to locomotion, display, or thermoregulation, and has not been co-opted for any other task. My verdict: Despite its present uselessness, its biological function remains flight; the historical selection story fixes the function even in the absence of current contribution.

Case 3 — A synthetic metabolic pathway engineered into E. coli. Researchers inserted a de novo enzymatic sequence that synthesizes a compound essential for the bacterium's survival in the laboratory environment. The pathway has no evolutionary history of natural selection. My verdict: It does have a biological function—synthesis of that compound—because of its present, organizationally integrated causal contribution to survival and reproduction.

Case 4 — A malignant tumor that secretes an insulin-like growth factor. The secretion accidentally regulates host glucose metabolism in a way that benefits the host, but the tumor is not integrated into any feedback system and is not maintained by selection for that effect. My verdict: This is not a biological function of the tumor; mere beneficial causal contribution, without organizational integration or appropriate selection history, is insufficient.

These four judgments seem coherent, yet they resist easy unification: Cases 1 and 3 treat present, integrated contribution as decisive over history; Case 2 treats history as decisive over present contribution; and Case 4 denies that present contribution alone is enough, apparently requiring a further condition that Case 3 satisfies but Case 4 lacks.
