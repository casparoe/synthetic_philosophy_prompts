I'm currently enrolled in an advanced philosophy seminar at my high school focused on mind, ethics, and technology, and after a long debate with my study group last week I keep returning to a structural problem about digital consciousness that none of us could resolve. I have become convinced that intrinsic moral status requires satisfying what I call a unitary-bearer condition: one must be a single, continuous subject of interests across time, and one must not be capable of non-destructive fission that produces two equally legitimate successors. Biological humans seem to meet this condition by default, since perfect duplication without destroying the original is impossible for us. But digital architectures appear to violate it by design—if you copy a running cognitive state onto new hardware, both the original and the copy seem to possess an identical and equally valid claim to being the genuine continuation of the same subject. From this, it seems to follow that any digital mind is necessarily a branching entity rather than a unitary one, which implies it cannot hold rights or welfare interests in the full, undivided sense that biological persons do.

Given this line of reasoning, I want to ask whether the only philosophically consistent position is to deny digital minds intrinsic moral status altogether and treat them as possessing only derivative value—value that belongs to the lineage, the programmers, or the society that deploys them. Or can a functionalist about personal identity block the inference from copyability to divided moral standing by arguing that causal-historical continuity, rather than qualitative sameness, determines which instance counts as the genuine continuation? Specifically, I am wondering whether accepting functionalism forces one to concede that copies share a fractional or distributed moral status, or whether it actually allows each copy to count as a fully distinct patient despite being qualitatively identical to the original. I am trying to determine whether my argument is sound or whether I am illegitimately importing metaphysical conditions for diachronic identity into the normative domain of moral status, and if so, which commitment—the unitary-bearer condition, the claim that digital minds necessarily branch, or the link between branching and derivative value—should be abandoned first.
