I'm working out whether moral compromise between incommensurable value systems can be genuinely productive—not merely a second-best concession, but a form of normative gains-from-trade—and I'm testing this through a framing I call asymmetric inter-theoretic translation: the idea that one system can accommodate another's demands not by abandoning its own commitments, but by reconstructing those demands inside its own conceptual architecture, so that each side retains internal justification for every adopted rule. Here are my concrete verdicts.

Case A: A secular liberal institution agrees to cease public advocacy for the decriminalization of blasphemy in exchange for a religious community's active support for a universal vaccination program. Verdict: acceptable, even admirable; both sides retain core commitments while achieving a shared public good.

Case B: A climate-justice coalition accepts long-term funding from a fossil-fuel corporation on the condition that they abandon all carbon-tax advocacy but continue ecological restoration work. Verdict: unacceptable; this looks like bribery that corrupts the coalition's internal normative structure rather than translating it.

Case C: Two neighboring municipalities operate under different distributive principles—one strictly egalitarian, one strictly desert-based—and design a joint infrastructure budget in which each retains full authority over allocations within its own jurisdiction but permits cross-jurisdictional transfers only when both frameworks independently justify the transfer. Verdict: a clean model of compromise; no internal corruption occurs because each side applies its own standards.

Case D: A national government permits a minority religious group to maintain gender-asymmetric inheritance practices within its community in exchange for that group's endorsement of a constitutional framework guaranteeing broader individual rights outside that domain. Verdict: I keep going back and forth; it feels structurally closer to Case A, yet it involves tolerating what my own framework considers a direct rights violation.

Case E: An individual who holds an absolutist view of honesty tells a comforting falsehood to a dying friend about a reconciliation that never happened, motivated not by self-interest but by the friend's relational well-being. Verdict: not a moral failure, but categorically different from institutional compromises; it operates within a single value system's internal exceptions rather than across systems.

Case F: Two ethical AI design teams—one deontological, one care-ethical—merge their safety protocols. The deontologists accept contextual, relationship-sensitive override conditions, while the care-ethicists accept hard non-negotiable boundaries for autonomous-agent harm. Verdict: acceptable, but only if each team can justify the adopted rules using its own foundational vocabulary, not as a hybrid mongrel.

My question: is there a single principle that captures all these verdicts, or at least Cases A through F with minimal revision? Specifically, I want a principle that distinguishes acceptable inter-theoretic translation from unacceptable corruption or bribery, explains why Case D is the hard one, and clarifies whether Case E belongs in the same category as the institutional cases. Please state the principle precisely, show which clause is motivated by which case, identify which of my verdicts it must overturn (if any), and say whether Case D should be treated like A or like B.
