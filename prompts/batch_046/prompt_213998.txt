You serve as a termination-review auditor for the Meridian Regional Public Works Authority, which oversees brownfield infrastructure retrofits, transit-line extensions, and municipal digital-system migrations. Every quarter you review capital projects that have exceeded their original budgets by at least fifty percent and are requesting a second or subsequent supplemental appropriation; the stakes are public funds in the tens of millions, binding contractual penalties, and the authority’s political credibility. You have sixty minutes per review, access only to internal budget ledgers, change-order justifications, and engineering risk assessments, and no external consultants or web access. Your recommendation—continue with enforceable conditions, terminate with penalty assessment, or escalate to an elected oversight committee—must be co-signed by the project director, but the burden of proof for continuation rests entirely on you. The project directors and engineering staff have no training in game theory, yet they routinely deploy sunk-cost rhetoric disguised as technical justification.

Design a reusable audit procedure that translates the escalation dynamics of the dollar-auction mechanism—where rational actors continue bidding not because the prize has increased in value but because stopping means accepting a prior loss—into an ordered sequence of practical tests. For each step, specify the exact question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, the threshold that triggers an automatic termination recommendation, and the precise point where the procedure must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue is one of values rather than audit logic. The procedure must survive cases where each new supplemental request is justified by changed conditions, vendor failure, or regulatory updates, and it must explicitly flag the moment when further investment becomes a bid to avoid organizational shame rather than a bet on independent future value. Include an honest statement of what the procedure cannot resolve—particularly regarding distributive justice, long-term reputational effects, and political legitimacy.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

First, a wastewater-treatment plant upgrade in which new state discharge regulations genuinely altered the technical requirements after the initial contract was signed, but the cost curve now shows that each supplemental request references the prior expenditure as a reason not to abandon.

Second, a legacy mainframe-to-cloud migration for a county records office where vendor failure triggered cascading replacement costs, yet the new architecture has demonstrated stable performance in a pilot environment and the remaining work is clearly scoped.

Third, a seismic-retrofit of a neighborhood community center that serves an underserved district, where unforeseen soil conditions have tripled costs and the project director argues that stopping now would waste all prior expenditure and destroy public trust; here the cost-to-completion analysis shows that every supplemental justification is anchored to money already spent rather than to an independent benefit-cost ratio, and the procedure should yield a recommendation of termination despite the strong community-impact values that argue for continuation.

For each case, run every step of the procedure in order, identify where it stops or continues, mark which step involves a judgment call disguised as audit logic, and state explicitly what lies outside the procedure’s scope.
