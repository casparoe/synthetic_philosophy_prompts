I've been discussing information diets with friends in a rationalist reading group, and we keep circling back to the standard distinction between echo chambers and epistemic bubbles — the former driven by distrust of outsiders, the latter by omission of contrary evidence. But neither quite captures what we observe in highly analytical online communities where dissent isn't hidden or discredited outright; instead, opposing arguments are consistently imported and then processed through a shared interpretive framework that neutralizes them before they can trigger belief revision. Let's call this a refractive bubble: not missing information, but systematically pre-interpreted information that always arrives pre-debunked.

Please describe the most important implications of recognizing refractive bubbles as a distinct failure mode, operating alongside but differently from classic echo chambers and epistemic bubbles, for how an agent should design their information diet and peer-interaction norms. Specifically, how does this change the usual prescriptions about seeking diverse viewpoints or maintaining weak ties across ideological boundaries? What does it imply about the relative epistemic value of direct engagement with primary sources versus mediated discussion within one's own framework? And which debiasing strategies — such as pre-committed belief-updating rules, adversarial collaboration, or explicit credence tracking — actually survive when the problem is corrupted processing rather than missing data? I want the implications for practical epistemic hygiene, not sociological diagnosis.
