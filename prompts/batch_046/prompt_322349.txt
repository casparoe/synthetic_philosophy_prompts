As a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I want to understand the logical space of responses to Frankfurt-style counterexamples against the principle of alternative possibilities, but I need something I can navigate without knowing the literature by heart. Please build a decision tree with at most six branching nodes and no more than eight leaves that sorts a respondent’s actual commitments, not their self-chosen label, starting from the root question: “If an agent’s action is fully deterministically caused, can they still be morally responsible as long as they acted for reasons they endorse?”

Every internal node must be a concrete yes/no or choose-one scenario that a technically minded but philosophically untrained person could answer. You must include these three cases: (1) a standard Frankfurt case with a human controller who never actually intervenes; (2) a variant where the controller is an open-source deterministic algorithm running on a neural interface that scans the agent’s decision tree and could inject an override, yet remains inactive because the agent already chooses what it predicts; (3) a hierarchical case where a second-order monitor—human or algorithmic—could have altered the agent’s desire to want to do X, regardless of whether it does.

Fix the tree’s logical branches first, then place named positions at the leaves. Required leaves: a Frankfurt-style compatibilist view; a hard incompatibilist view that denies responsibility in all deterministic settings; a hierarchical view that distinguishes first-order and second-order volitions; a sourcehood view that cares about the causal origin of the will rather than open alternatives; and any unnamed combinations the tree allows. For each leaf, state its most awkward practical commitment in one sentence. Identify any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, and for every unoccupied combination of answers, briefly diagnose whether the gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts; keep the prompt formal, focused, and free of philosophical jargon in the questions themselves.
