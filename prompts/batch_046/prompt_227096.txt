I have been thinking lately about the way popular history writing treats every past era as though its main purpose were to become us—so that ancient republics are praised only for being "early democracies," medieval medicine is dismissed for not being modern science, and societies that pursued genuinely different goals are treated as dead ends or detours. To expose this habit by exaggerating its internal logic, please write a deadpan technical user manual of about 600 words for a fictional enterprise software suite called Progression Engine, developed by an imaginary consultancy named the Institute for Temporal Alignment. Adopt the flat, jargon-heavy voice of IT documentation. The manual’s central feature should be an invented methodology you can call Anticipatory Resonance Mapping, which scores past civilizations by measuring how closely their institutions, beliefs, and daily practices prefigure modern liberal democratic norms. Include standard sections: an installation guide; a description of the scoring algorithm, in which societies gain points for parliamentary structures and representative assemblies while losing points for achievements—such as advanced irrigation or non-representative philosophy—that do not converge on the present; a troubleshooting entry addressing why the Bronze Age receives a lower score than a mediocre modern suburb; a sidebar announcing the national holiday honoring the software’s original beta testers; and a brief FAQ in which users ask whether they can reset the present baseline to their own country and why technological innovations that lack democratic correlates receive no bonus. The satire must remain entirely in character—never breaking the corporate deadpan, never stating the criticism openly—so that a reader familiar with historiography recognizes the mockery of Whig history’s real tendencies: treating historical contingency as inevitability, judging the past exclusively by present standards, and rendering invisible any achievement that does not point toward us. Do not include references, footnotes, bibliographies, or reading lists; the piece should stand on its internal logic alone.
