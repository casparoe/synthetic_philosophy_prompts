I have been working through a puzzle that arose in a discussion about rational belief with colleagues. We started with ordinary cases of peer disagreement—say, two economists with the same data reaching opposite conclusions about whether a particular intervention will reduce inflation, or two philosophers with shared premises disagreeing about a moral intuition. In each case, one party proposes that rational agents should moderate their confidence and move toward the other’s position. But then we noticed a second-order version of the same conflict: the two parties also disagree about whether that moderation principle itself is correct—one thinks it applies universally, the other thinks it applies only to empirical domains. If they apply the moderation principle to this meta-disagreement, they should reduce confidence in the principle, which weakens the very reason they had to moderate about the original policy or intuition in the first place. That weakening can push them back toward their initial positions, which then renews the first disagreement and appears to start the loop over. I have seen this circular structure suggested in debates about scientific consensus and about normative judgment, but I am not sure whether it has been carved out as its own phenomenon.

What I am trying to identify is the precise shape of this loop, not merely a label for it. Does it differ in kind from familiar worries about being too quick to abandon one’s view or too stubborn to revise it? Is it restricted to norms about belief-revision, or can it arise for other kinds of rules—about weighing testimony, for instance, or about deference to expertise? If there is an established concept in the vicinity, I want to know how the loop I described relates to it, where the boundaries lie, and which cases fall inside versus outside. If nothing established fits, I would like a clear definition with explicit boundary conditions—what makes a loop genuinely vicious rather than merely apparent, and what kind of further case would best distinguish this structure from nearby ones. Please answer in American English, and say specifically what argument or example would change your mind about whether my described loop matches the concept you discuss or deserves to be treated as its own.
