I hold that the collapse of the Roman Republic between roughly 133 and 27 BCE is best explained not by the familiar narrative of moral decay, individual ambition, or the corruption of the citizen-militia, but by a structural constitutional failure: the Republic accumulated veto points—tribunician intercession, consular collegiality, senatorial obstruction—without any formal amendment procedure, so that every significant socio-economic conflict eventually had to be resolved outside constitutional channels. On this view, the analogies modern commentators draw to democratic "backsliding" miss the point; the closer parallel is to modern democracies suffering from institutional gridlock that invites executive aggrandizement rather than to populist decay. What would have to be true for this institutional-overload thesis to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which specific episodes—the Gracchan crisis, the Social War, the Sullan settlement, the Catilinarian conspiracy—would have to be reinterpreted, and in what direction? Which comparative cases, such as other ancient polities with dense veto structures that survived, would undermine the mechanism? Would it be fatal if historians showed that informal amendment practices actually existed and were deliberately ignored by actors? Or if the violence turned out to be driven primarily by personal patronage networks rather than by blocked institutional pathways? Sort the defeaters: which observations or arguments would genuinely force abandonment of the thesis, which would merely require qualification, and which a committed defender could absorb by refining the claim about formal versus functional veto accumulation. Finally, is there a risk that the thesis has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if every instance of violence can be read back as evidence of gridlock, and every absence of violence as evidence of successful informal adaptation—and is that insulation a problem for an explanation of this kind?
