I have been working through Hegel's Philosophy of Right and trying to apply his argument that war is not an absolute evil but rather the moment in which the state affirms its sovereignty and preserves its ethical substance against the corruption that prolonged civil peace introduces into civil society. A colleague recently suggested that this is essentially the same insight as Schmitt's claim that sovereignty is defined by the decision on the exception, only expressed in ethical rather than juridical vocabulary. If I take this comparison seriously, it seems to follow that a modern liberal state which has not, for several generations, exercised its sovereign capacity to risk the lives of its citizens in a collective conflict—relying instead on professional armies, private military contractors, and collective-security alliances—has not actually achieved the ethical actuality Hegel describes. Its sovereignty is purely formal, analogous to a corporation that holds legal title but never operates. Given this, I want to ask whether Hegel's framework entails that such states are structurally defective in a way that internal constitutional perfection or economic prosperity cannot remedy, and whether delegating the monopoly on violence to supranational bodies like NATO or to private firms represents not an evolution of the state but a regression to the abstract right of civil society that the ethical state was meant to transcend. Or is there a defensible reading—perhaps developed in Hegel's later lectures—according to which the state's ethical substance can be realized through other forms of collective risk-taking, such as compulsory national service in non-military domains, so that the universal is preserved without actual war?
