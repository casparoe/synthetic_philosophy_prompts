I'm working through a comparative argument for a seminar on cultural norms surrounding achievement, and I've constructed a chain of reasoning that seems to demonstrate that societies can hold no coherent normative stance on whether exceptional success should be celebrated or suppressed. The argument proceeds as follows. (1) The American Dream, taken as a normative framework rather than a purely descriptive narrative, holds that societies ought to celebrate exceptional achievement conspicuously, because visible rewards for merit incentivize the productive effort that generates broad prosperity and because those who contribute more have earned greater social recognition. (2) Jantelagen, together with the related tall poppy syndrome observed in several Anglophone societies, holds that societies ought to suppress conspicuous displays of exceptional achievement, because visible status hierarchies erode the egalitarian trust required for social cooperation and because singling out outliers implicitly devalues those who remain near the median. (3) Both frameworks claim universal scope: they are not confined to particular domains such as athletics or commerce, but prescribe how a society should treat anyone whose performance significantly exceeds the mean in any valued activity. (4) If both prescriptions are universally valid, then for any individual whose achievement is outstanding, society is simultaneously obligated to apply maximal celebration and maximal suppression. (5) But maximal celebration and maximal suppression are jointly unsatisfiable when directed at the same person in the same social context: one cannot simultaneously award the highest public honors and systematically diminish the recipient's standing. (6) Therefore, holding both as universal norms yields contradictory obligations in every clear case of high achievement. (7) In normative reasoning, such a contradiction typically indicates that at least one universal claim is false. (8) Yet neither claim is descriptively false—both accurately capture widely shared moral intuitions across different cultures—so they must fail specifically as universal norms. (9) The remaining option is that both are merely context-sensitive rules, but then there is no principled basis for deciding which applies where, reducing observed cultural differences to arbitrary preference. (10) Consequently, either we accept an arbitrary partition between contexts, reject both frameworks entirely, or conclude that normative theory about success is incoherent, even though we clearly do take coherent stances—for instance, praising a surgeon's skill while discouraging her from boasting—without contradiction.

I want to preserve the intuition that both frameworks contain genuine normative insights rather than discarding one wholesale, though I recognize that this preference may require me to absorb a specific cost elsewhere in the reasoning. Which step should I reject, and why does it appear plausible before collapsing? Please explain what rejecting it costs, and whether my desire to retain both insights can survive that price.
