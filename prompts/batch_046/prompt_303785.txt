I am a doctoral student in ethics and philosophy of religion. Next Thursday I present a dissertation chapter to our value-theory workshop defending what I call negative-state monism: the thesis that intrinsic welfare is constituted exhaustively by the absence of craving (tanha) and suffering (dukkha), while positively characterized mental states—eudaimonic fulfillment, affective flourishing, attachment—carry at most instrumental value as causal conditions for that absence. I argue that this renders Aristotelian flourishing frameworks redundant, since any purported positive contribution to well-being collapses into either suffering-reduction or temporary craving-suppression that does not itself constitute welfare. The audience will be six virtue ethicists who work on eudaimonia, two welfare economists who use subjective-well-being metrics, and one Abhidharma scholar who studies Buddhist phenomenology; they value conceptual parsimony but are skeptical of reductionist axiologies. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: list the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased in the vocabulary each camp actually uses, with for each what I should concede up front, what I must hold, a concise reply suitable for two minutes, and a one-line note on where that reply is weakest. Include specifically the Abhidharma scholar’s charge that I conflate mundane equanimity (upekkha in jhana) with transcendental cessation (nirodha), together with a response that protects the monist claim without misrepresenting the phenomenology. Conclude with the objection I am hoping nobody asks—likely from the economists—about whether my view entails that a permanently anesthetized brain in a vat is maximally well-off, and sketch the defense that does not rely on bare intuition-pumping.
