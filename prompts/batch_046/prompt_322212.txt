I'm drafting this for a seminar on practical reason, but the problem has become uncomfortably immediate: I am now the healthcare proxy for my mother, who has advanced frontotemporal dementia and can no longer engage in the reflective deliberation that contractualist or constructivist accounts treat as the source of normative authority. Her physicians keep asking what she would want, and I have been trying to understand whether indirect normativity—the view that a norm binds us not because we actually endorse it, but because we would endorse it under conditions of full information and rational coherence—can still apply to someone whose deliberative capacities are permanently gone. I tried to formalize the view to see whether obligations survive the loss of the very psychology that is supposed to ground them, and I ended up with an argument that seems to prove either that indirect normativity collapses into universal obligations that require the impossible, or that it collapses back into direct, capacity-indexed normativity and loses its distinctive force.

Here is the chain I constructed. (1) A norm N has indirect normative force for an actual agent A if and only if A's idealized counterpart—call her A*—would endorse N in a state of full information, logical consistency, and affective coherence. (2) If A* endorses N, then N generates a reason for A regardless of whether A can recognize or act on that reason, because the authority of N derives from the structure of rational endorsement rather than from A's actual psychology. (3) The conditions for being A* are not indexed to A's actual cognitive capacities; they are defined by a universal standard of rational coherence that applies to any being with the minimal properties that make A an agent, such as having interests and being capable of action. (4) Therefore, if N is endorsed by any A* for any agent in a relevant class, N has indirect normative force for every actual agent in that class. (5) But rational coherence under full information requires endorsing not merely local norms but the maximally coherent system of norms S that resolves all conflicts; partial endorsement is itself a failure of coherence. (6) So for any agent A, the set of norms with indirect force over A is not a subset tailored to A's capacities, but the entire system S. (7) S includes norms that require actions impossible for some actual agents—for instance, preserving every relationship that an idealized agent would preserve, or distributing resources according to a global principle that demands computational capacities no human possesses. (8) If a norm requires an impossible action, and the principle that ought implies can holds, then that norm cannot genuinely bind the agent for whom the action is impossible. (9) Therefore either (a) indirect normativity binds every agent to impossible actions, violating (8), or (b) we restrict S to what each agent can do, which abandons the universal coherence requirement in (5) and collapses indirect normativity back into direct normativity. (10) I find (9a) unacceptable and (9b) makes the view I wanted to defend disappear, so I think one of the premises must fail, but I am unsure which is least costly.

The step that worries me most is (3): it assumes idealization strips away individual differences in a way that produces a single standard. Yet if I reject (3) and index idealization to actual capacities, the view seems to say only that one should do what one, with one's limitations, would do if somewhat smarter—which sounds like direct normativity in disguise. On the other hand, if I keep (3) but reject (5), I seem to allow that an agent could rationally endorse conflicting norms without resolving them, which undermines the idea that rationality has any normative pull. I also wonder whether (2) contains a modal slide between A* endorsing N and A having a reason—perhaps the reason is available to A only if A can access the deliberative path leading to endorsement, and for agents like my mother that path is permanently blocked, so no reason is transmitted. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the project of using idealized deliberation to ground obligations—especially obligations we might have toward agents who can no longer deliberate for themselves? I would prefer to keep (1) and (8) if possible, but I want to understand what I must pay to do so.
