I am a doctoral student working on democratic legitimacy, and I have become dissatisfied with the standard framing of the paradox of voting—namely, that in large-scale elections the instrumental efficacy of any single ballot is vanishingly small, which makes widespread participation appear rationally inexplicable unless we append expressive or civic-duty benefits to an otherwise self-interested calculus. Rather than treating this as a puzzle to be solved by augmenting the voter’s utility function, I want you to argue for the stronger claim that the paradox is an artifact of a mistaken ontology: it presupposes that democratic agency is fundamentally individual, decomposable, and preference-driven, such that the rationality of voting must be adjudicated from the first-person singular standpoint. Argue instead that voting is best understood as a jointly authored performative act whose value is constituted relationally and is not reducible to the aggregation of private returns; on this view, the question “why should I vote?” misapplies the logic of individual optimization to a practice whose rational structure is collective and emergent. You should defend this relational framing against the objection that it does not solve the free-rider problem but merely relocates it to the collective level, and you should also address the concern that such an account commits liberal democratic theory to an implausible metaphysical holism about group agency that undermines individual moral standing. Please say how confident you are in the overall argument and explicitly identify the specific point—whether conceptual, normative, or metaphysical—about which you are least certain.
