I'm prepping questions for a podcast with a philosopher who's just put out a monograph arguing that the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem mostly vanishes if we stop treating belief-formation like a solo sport and start treating it as a stakeholder negotiation. She claims practical incentives—getting funded, keeping allies, avoiding institutional conflict—aren't wrong-kind reasons at all when the belief drives collective action; they're just barganing chips that set the evidential bar for the group. I want to push her hard on the slide from describing how committees work to saying that's how they should work, and I need the questions to do the work, not me. No generic "what drew you to philosophy" stuff.

Write the interview script in these sections, in this order:

1. The framing (about 100 words) — set up her stakeholder-negotiation view for listeners, with one concrete collective-belief example like a climate panel or hiring committee.

2. Opening questions (about 180 words) — three sharp questions that lock down her position. First: is she denying there's any wrong-kind reason for individual belief, or just relocating the problem? Second: what separates a legitimate barganing chip from plain corruption? Third: what happens when stakeholders have wildly unequal power—does the loudest voice set the evidence bar?

3. The deflection sequence (about 220 words) — anticipate her escapes. She'll probably say you're stuck in individulist epistemology, or that this is descriptive sociology not normative philosophy. For each, write the comeback that makes her reply harder to maintain. End with one question about what empirical finding about group decision-making would actually falsifiy her model, since she keeps calling it a research program.

4. The corruption case (about 160 words) — apply her framework to a medical board with financial ties to a drug company. Board members privately think the trial data is weak, but collectively they have strong practical reasons to approve. Force her to say whether her view describes this as legitimate negotiation or condemns it; don't let her hedge with both.

5. The closer (about 100 words) — one final question making her choose: either she saves the intuition that some reasons really are the wrong kind for belief, or she accepts every practical reason is negotiable and the distinction collapses. Make it concrete—ask what she would tell a junior researcher being pressured to tweak findings for grant money.

Take a committed stance throughout: argue that her framework either collapses back into standard wrong-kind-reason worries or ends up licensing too much institutional bad faith. Give reasons, not just a list of objections. Then add a brief section called Where this is made up (about 60 words) noting that the stakeholder-negotiation framing is my injection for this interview, not standard literature, and flagging which parts borrow from familiar WKR debates (individual versus collective belief, transparency conditions) versus which are invented here.

Keep it casual—I'm drafting this on my phone between meetings and probably misspelling a word or two.
