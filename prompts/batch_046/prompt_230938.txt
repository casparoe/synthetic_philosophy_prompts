I co-teach an advanced seminar with a colleague from our local rationalist reading group, and we are drafting the final exam for graduate students in philosophy of technology and collective decision theory. I want one essay question that pushes students past a rote recitation of the canonical unilateralist's curse and into its structural anatomy. To that end, I have sketched a variant I am calling the asymmetric-information unilateralist's curse: each of N agents can take an action that is privately known to be either net-positive or net-negative for that agent individually, but all agents share a common harm threshold—if more than k agents act, the outcome is catastrophic for everyone—while no agent knows how many others are in the net-positive category, nor whether the threshold will be crossed. The question should ask students to determine whether this information structure dissolves the curse into an ordinary coordination game, transforms it into a problem of rational ignorance, or instead reveals that the canonical curse was always driven by epistemic asymmetry rather than by the payoff structure alone. I need the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words suitable for a 90-minute closed-book sitting, and a marking scheme that separates an A answer—one that precisely locates the boundary between payoff-driven and epistemic-driven versions of the curse and applies it rigorously to the variant—from a B answer that correctly summarizes the standard case but fails to diagnose the structural shift. Please do not include any request for bibliographies, citations, or external reading lists, since the exam is closed-book and should be answerable from conceptual reasoning alone.
