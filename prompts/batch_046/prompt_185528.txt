I serve on my university's Committee on General Education Reform. We have received a proposal to restructure the first-year core curriculum according to principles drawn from Locke's essay on education and his account of mental association. The proponents argue that the current model treats students as passive receptacles of information, violating Locke's claim that genuine education forms the mind's habits through repeated experience rather than through explicit precept or lecture-based transmission. Specifically, they recommend replacing standard survey courses with a year-long associative laboratory in which students encounter paired normative and conceptual themes—such as liberty paired with responsibility, or causation paired with evidence—in varied practical contexts without upfront theoretical framing. The idea is that stable associative bonds will form implicitly, producing settled dispositions of judgment analogous to the virtue Locke describes, rather than merely depositing propositional knowledge.

Critics on the committee, including several philosophers of education, raise three objections. First, they contend that Locke's psychology of association was explanatory, not prescriptive: describing how ideas become linked does not entail that educators should engineer those links covertly. Second, they argue that concealing normative aims behind procedural design constitutes a form of indoctrination incompatible with institutional neutrality, especially since the proposed pairings embed contested liberal commitments. Third, they claim that abandoning disciplinary survey structures will leave students without the historical and methodological literacy required for advanced work, and that Locke himself—who emphasized the cultivation of reason through guided study—would reject this as miseducation.

The proponents respond that Locke's distinction between mere instruction and true education precisely demands this restructuring, and that the university's existing surveys already smuggle in normative frameworks under the guise of neutral canon-formation; at least the pilot program makes its design transparent to reviewers, if not initially to students.

Draft the committee's formal decision memo recommending whether to approve the pilot, reject it, or approve it with specific conditions. The memo should treat the Lockean philosophical issues as live concerns rather than historical curiosities: in particular, it should examine whether associative psychology can legitimately ground a pedagogical method, whether the formation of virtue through habit is compatible with a university's obligation to transparency, and whether institutional neutrality is even coherent here. It must engage the critics' indoctrination objection seriously—not dismiss it as paranoia—while also assessing whether the proponents' transparency measures are sufficient. If you recommend approval, specify the scope (which student cohort, which courses replaced, duration), the assessment mechanism for measuring whether associative habits actually form, and a mandatory review date; if rejection, specify what alternative reform, if any, the committee should pursue instead. Include an honest acknowledgment of the strongest consideration on the losing side and a concrete reply to it that does not simply restate your bottom line.
