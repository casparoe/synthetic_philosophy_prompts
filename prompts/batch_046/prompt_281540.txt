Design the explanatory architecture for a single ninety-minute session with clinical-ethics fellows—physicians without formal philosophy training—on the problem of an earlier self's authority through an advance directive when the later self exhibits evaluative discontinuity rather than mere cognitive decline. Do not explain the concept to me; instead, specify the dependency order: which distinctions (between instruction and prediction, between retained capacity and retained values, and between diachronic authority and present well-being) must be established before the authority problem becomes intelligible, and why reversing that order collapses the session into either legalism or sentimentality. Choose one carrying case: it should involve a patient who retains linguistic and social engagement, expresses present contentment, and has a documented refusal of life-sustaining intervention in severe dementia, but who—due to a progressive condition that selectively erodes long-range evaluative commitments—no longer recognizes the values that motivated the refusal. The case must contain one feature that makes the earlier directive's authority seem compelling and one feature that undermines it. Then map the misconceptions the fellows will bring or form: I anticipate at least that they will treat prior rationality as decisive authority, conflate psychological discontinuity with literal non-identity, and reduce the conflict to present happiness versus past suffering. For each, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be intercepted and the single sentence or question that defuses it. Include a final check—answerable in under three minutes—that reveals whether they understand the tension as a problem of authority rather than having simply adopted one side. Finally, explicitly list what should be omitted at this level (for instance, named metaphysical theories of personal identity, specific legal precedents, or clinical capacity-assessment instruments) and justify each omission in terms of the dependency chain.
