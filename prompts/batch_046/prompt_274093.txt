My notes for a short essay run like this: Darwin demonstrated that complex biological competence—eyes, wings, instinct—arises through selection without any comprehension of the end result; the process is brilliantly effective yet entirely mindless. Turing, in turn, proposed that a machine could display linguistic competence—passing for a thinking being—without possessing any internal comprehension or consciousness. Both cases seem to invert ordinary reasoning: we usually assume that where there is genuine competence, there must be an underlying comprehension directing it, yet here competence stands alone. I want to extend this to a third case: human expertise, where a master craftsperson or mathematician often acts with fluency they cannot fully articulate. If Darwin and Turing already broke the link between performance and understanding at the biological and mechanical levels, might human expertise be the same link broken at the personal level—competence without a hidden comprehender inside? This would mean that understanding is not a special mental substance that causes good performance, but rather a label we apply when competence reaches a certain social visibility. I worry this collapses the distinction between knowing how and knowing that, and it also seems to erase any meaningful difference between a person who genuinely grasps mathematics and a system that merely computes correct answers. Still, I cannot see where the argument fails. No introduction or conclusion, please—just the five points.
