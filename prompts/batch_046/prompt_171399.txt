You are a professional philosopher specializing in social epistemology and the rationality of disagreement. Before producing anything, take a moment to brainstorm the precise structural commitments shared by conciliationists and steadfastists, the distinctions that are often collapsed in popular treatments—such as peer disagreement about first-order beliefs versus second-order assessments of one's own reasoning, the status of private or asymmetric reasons, and the difference between rationally maintaining belief versus rationally dismissing a peer's competence—and identify which residual disagreements are genuinely substantive versus partly verbal.

Your task is to draft the joint statement that a conciliationist camp and a steadfastist camp could both sign after a rigorous adversarial collaboration on peer disagreement. The document must be meaningful without access to external sources, so do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific works, and do not ask the reader to consult outside literature.

The statement must contain the following sections in this order:

1. A brief introductory paragraph stating the purpose of the collaboration and defining the two positions in neutral language that each side would accept: conciliationists hold that discovering that an epistemic peer disagrees with one's belief about P typically provides a defeater that requires reducing confidence in P; steadfastists hold that one can rationally maintain one's belief about P despite peer disagreement, particularly when one possesses an independent, defensible reason for one's position.

2. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover shared commitments about the nature of epistemic peerhood, about what needs explaining in disagreement cases, about the formal structure of arguments often labeled the uniqueness thesis and the independence principle (without attributing them to named authors), and about the conditions under which disagreement genuinely threatens rational belief. Include agreement on what well-known formal results establish and what they leave open.

3. A disagreements table or precisely formatted section stating the exact points of residual disagreement. Each entry must phrase one side's view in wording that that side would accept as accurate, and the opposing side's view in wording it would accept. Do not use straw-man formulations.

4. A list of cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, include:
   - A concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on the crux.
   - The conciliationist's prediction or assessment of that case.
   - The steadfastist's prediction or assessment of that case.

In particular, you must address at least two specific cruxes:

A. The symmetry-breaking problem: when two peers disagree about P and each possesses a private reason—such as an internal consistency check, a direct intuition about model simplicity, or an asymmetric access to one's own reasoning process—that the other lacks, does the presence of that private reason block the conciliationist demand, or does it merely relocate the disagreement to a higher-order disagreement about peerhood itself? Use a concrete scenario, for instance two statisticians with identical training who apply different robustness checks to the same dataset.

B. Calibration under second-order uncertainty: if two peers disagree not only about P but also about whether they are genuinely epistemic peers regarding P—say, because one believes the other has overlooked a relevant methodological constraint—what rational response is required? Specify a scenario, such as two climate modelers with identical credentials and access to the same observational data who diverge because one applies a conservative outlier-exclusion rule that the other rejects. Record each camp's prediction about whether conciliation on P is required, and whether conciliation on the peerhood assessment is separately required.

5. A concluding analytical paragraph in which you evaluate whether any portion of the disagreement is verbal rather than substantive. If you identify a verbal component—such as ambiguity in the term "peer" or in "reduce confidence"—label it explicitly, explain the ambiguity, and state how resolving the linguistic point affects the substantive dispute.

Throughout, maintain neutral, precise, philosophical language. Avoid rhetorical slant. Ensure that every claim in the agreed section could genuinely be signed by both parties, and that every claim in the disagreement section is one that the respective party would endorse. Think carefully, check that your concrete cases genuinely bear on the cruxes rather than assuming the conclusion, and then present the final document in the requested format: numbered agreed claims followed by the disagreements table and crux analyses.
