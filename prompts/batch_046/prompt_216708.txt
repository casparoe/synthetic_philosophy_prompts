Write a diagnosis of the following argument, which extends Hofstadter's idea of superrationality beyond its usual symmetric prisoner's dilemmas and seems to collapse into absurdity.

(1) In a pure reasoning game—where both agents share the same logical structure and common knowledge—superrational agents always converge on the identical choice, because each can run the other’s reasoning and knows the other can do the same.

(2) Rationality is scalar: if agent A is strictly more rational than B, then every reasoning step B takes is also available to A, plus further ones B cannot take.

(3) Therefore B can model A’s reasoning up to B’s own limit, and A can model B’s reasoning exactly; the asymmetry in capacity does not break the symmetry of the reasoning loop, because both still end up predicting the same convergence point.

(4) So superrationality applies not just to equally rational agents, but to any pair of rational agents, regardless of differences in cognitive capacity or information access.

(5) Now consider a one-shot interaction with asymmetric payoffs: A can save B’s life at zero cost, but B can save A’s life only by sacrificing everything B values. By (4), both must still choose the same action.

(6) If the shared action is cooperation, B is required to make a total sacrifice for A’s free benefit; if it is self-preservation, A is forbidden from saving B for free. Both outcomes are intolerable.

(7) Hence, either superrationality is false, or it forces morally grotesque obligations in asymmetric cases.

(8) But (1) captures something deep about rational coordination; abandoning it seems to surrender the only principled reason we ever cooperate in one-shot dilemmas.

Where is the error, if there is one? I am inclined to preserve (1) and reject something in the chain leading to (4) or (5), but I want to see exactly what repair costs.

"Diagnosis of the flawed step" (about 250 words): identify the specific premise or inference that fails, explain whether it is an equivocation, a scope shift, or an invalid extension, and say why it initially looked plausible.

"Defense of premise (1)" (about 200 words): explain what (1) genuinely gets right about symmetric reasoning games and why restricting it does not destroy its value.

"The price of my preference" (about 150 words): since I want to keep (1) over (5), spell out what I must give up—specifically, whether I must deny that rationality is scalar in the relevant sense, or whether I need a boundary condition on when reasoning loops count as symmetric.

"Narrower repair" (about 150 words): propose a restricted formulation of superrationality that blocks the absurd conclusion in (6) without reverting to pure game-theoretic rationality.
