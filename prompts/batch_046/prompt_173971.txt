As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy of mind, I have been trying to work through the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness—especially Ned Block’s argument that rich subjective experience can persist even when information is not globally available to cognitive systems, and global workspace theory’s claim that consciousness just is that global broadcast across mental modules. I recently discussed this with colleagues over dinner, and we kept circling back to Jean-Dominique Bauby, the journalist who, after a brainstem stroke in the mid-1990s, was left with locked-in syndrome: clinically fully aware, yet able to move only one eyelid, which he used to dictate his memoir letter by letter through a partner reading an alphabet. The case seems to sit precisely on the fault line between the two frameworks.

Please treat Bauby’s condition as a real-world case study and analyze it through both lenses. Specifically: global workspace theory identifies consciousness with information being made available to multiple cognitive subsystems, yet Bauby’s capacity to broadcast was collapsed to a single binary output channel—his eyelid. Does this mean he retained only a severely truncated global workspace, or does the persistence of rich inner life despite near-total loss of motor and sensory broadcasting show that phenomenal consciousness can survive the collapse of global access? Conversely, using Block’s distinction, was Bauby’s retained experience primarily phenomenal consciousness without access, or did the deliberate, sequential act of selecting letters and composing sentences constitute a minimal form of access consciousness—perhaps what we might call a “unidirectional workspace,” in which intentional output is globally available to a communication partner but sensory and motor input loops are severed? I want you to be disciplined about the mapping: identify the players (Bauby’s remaining cognitive systems), the options (full consciousness, minimal consciousness, phenomenal-only consciousness, unconscious reflex), and the evidence (clinical reports of awareness, the structure of his communication protocol, the qualitative reports in his memoir). Then say explicitly where each theory is silent—whether global workspace theory has anything to say about the qualitative richness of experience when broadcast bandwidth approaches zero, and whether Block’s framework can distinguish between a genuinely conscious subject who can only blink and an unconscious subject producing reflexive blinks. Finally, does the Bauby episode function as a confirmation, an anomaly, or an unanticipated limit case for the thesis that consciousness is identical with global broadcasting?
