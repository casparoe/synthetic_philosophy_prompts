I am preparing an extended essay for my philosophy of logic course on judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma. As I understand it, the dilemma arises because a group can hold consistent individual judgments over premises P and Q and conclusion R—where R logically follows from P and Q—yet majority rule across all propositions yields an inconsistent collective set, such as accepting both premises while rejecting the conclusion. I want to defend the view that this tension is merely an artifact of binary judgment aggregation. Specifically, I propose that if each voter assigns real-valued credences between 0 and 1 to P and Q, and we aggregate these via averaging and apply a probabilistic closure rule to derive the group's credence in R, then the contradiction dissolves: the group never strictly accepts or rejects any proposition, so there is no logical inconsistency to avoid. Is this reasoning sound? Or does the discursive dilemma persist even under continuous, graded aggregation, and if so, what feature of the logical relations between premises and conclusion makes the shift from binary to graded judgments fail to resolve the structural problem?
