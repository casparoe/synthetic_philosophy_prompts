I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am facilitating a 60-minute workshop for 14 participants (mixed technical and non-technical backgrounds, no philosophy prerequisites) on technological determinism — specifically, whether artifacts have politics. Rather than rehashing well-known bridge or nuclear-power examples, I want the group to feel a more obscure variant that I will call "fallback determinism": the idea that a system's silent failure-handling protocols — retries, circuit-breakers, default values, timeout behaviors, and graceful-degradation rules — embed normative commitments that become invisible once the system is running, and that these embedded commitments are harder to overturn than explicit design choices because they are never publicly debated. I need a runnable in-room activity, not a lecture, and I want the rules and incentives arranged so that participants run into the determinism themselves before it is named.

Please design the workshop according to the following sections, with approximate lengths as indicated:

1. Setup and Materials (about 150 words): Specify physical layout, group division, and all materials needed (cards, handouts, timers, tokens). Keep it feasible for one facilitator with no special equipment.

2. Rules, Roles, and Payoff Structure (about 250 words): Describe the core simulation — small groups design a resource-allocation mechanism for a shared pool (for example, water credits or compute time) — and the hidden "failure cards" that trigger fallback behaviors. Define roles (system architect, stakeholder representative, auditor) and a non-monetary payoff scheme (points, constrained future choices, or public reputation markers) that creates real tension without requiring philosophy vocabulary.

3. The Staged Reveal (about 150 words): Explain exactly how and when the hidden normative commitments of the fallback protocols become visible to participants. Specify how the rules should be arranged so that the group discovers the determinism through their own choices and conflicts, not through facilitator explanation.

4. Debrief Mapping (about 250 words): Map specific moments from the simulation onto the concept of fallback determinism and onto the broader technological determinism debate. Distinguish this variant from explicit design politics and from ordinary unintended consequences.

5. Predictable Failures and Handling (about 150 words): State what will go wrong the first time this is run — for example, groups that fixate on the explicit allocation rules and miss the fallbacks entirely, or technical participants who dominate the design phase — and provide concrete facilitation moves to recover without lecturing.

6. Honest Limitations (about 100 words): Identify which feature of technological determinism this activity cannot reproduce — for instance, historical path dependence that unfolds over years — and state frankly that this gap must be supplied by the facilitator after the debrief.

The activity should not ask participants to read texts, cite philosophers, or use specialized terminology. The idea should emerge from what happens in the room, not from what is said afterward.
