I have come to hold a nuanced version of the view that the inefficacy objection succeeds against some collective-action cases but fails against others, and that the difference is best captured not by a simple expected-utility calculation but by a three-factor marginal-complicity index. On this view, an individual's contribution to a collective harm is morally significant only when it scores non-negligibly on (i) the elasticity of the harmful system to that individual's withdrawal—that is, whether the system responds structurally to the absence of one unit of participation rather than absorbing it through redundancy; (ii) the agent's reasonable epistemic access to that elasticity at the time of action; and (iii) a normative-signaling coefficient measuring whether the action reinforces a social practice whose stability depends on widespread, visible participation rather than on hidden infrastructure alone. Applying this index, I judge that voting in large-scale democratic elections scores near zero across all dimensions, and so the inefficacy objection is decisive there; eating meat in industrial animal agriculture scores moderately high on elasticity and signaling, making the objection weak; and driving a private combustion vehicle scores high on elasticity but low on signaling, producing an intermediate verdict. The view implies that standard expected-harm defenses miss the point, because they treat harm as linearly aggregable and ignore both overdetermination and the social-epistemic conditions of complicity.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong, or at least seriously weakened? I want an honest map of its exposure. Which empirical findings about supply elasticity—say, in energy markets versus food markets—would undermine the index's application? Which formal results in collective-action theory or decision theory could show that elasticity and signaling are the wrong dimensions altogether, or that they collapse into a simpler expected-consequentialist framework after all? Are there conceptual counterexamples—perhaps involving hidden harmful supply chains or symbolic acts with no structural effect—that expose the signaling coefficient as either too permissive or too restrictive? And from the defenders' side: which concessions would they be forced to make? Would they have to admit that the index is unfalsifiable if elasticity is always measurable post hoc? Or that it privileges agents in wealthy economies who can easily withdraw without personal cost? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish those that have arguably already appeared in existing debates from those that remain speculative, and assess whether the view has been qualified into insulation—if so, is that a virtue for a normative framework of this kind or a sign that it has been emptied of predictive content?
