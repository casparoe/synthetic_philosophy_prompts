I'm trying to determine what I actually think about dispositions, finks, and masks by testing my intuitions against concrete cases and seeing whether they fall under a single principle. Here are my verdicts.

A porcelain cup falls to the floor and shatters. Verdict: it was fragile.

The same cup is wrapped in shock-absorbing foam and struck with equal force; it does not break. Verdict: it is still fragile—the foam is a mask.

A copper wire is dead, but a hidden sensor is wired to it so that the instant it is touched it becomes live. Verdict: the wire is not live; the sensor manufactures conductivity at the moment of contact.

A reactive metallic compound is naturally coated with a stable oxide layer that prevents reaction with air. Verdict: the compound is reactive; the oxide is a superficial mask, not a removal of the disposition.

A pharmaceutical tablet is chemically inert in storage, but contains an enzyme that activates only in gastric acid, converting it into a toxin. I keep going back and forth: is the tablet disposed to poison, or is the enzyme an interfering fink that means the disposition belongs only to the activated compound?

A person is temperamentally calm, but an implanted neural chip triggers violent rage upon detecting a specific sound frequency. Verdict: the person is not irascible; the chip is an interfering mechanism, not part of their psychological disposition.

A book is printed on archival paper that will last centuries, but it is kept in a vault that will incinerate it if anyone tries to open it. Verdict: the book is durable; the vault is a mask.

The pair that is hardest for me to reconcile is the compound with its intrinsic oxide layer and the tablet with its intrinsic enzyme: in both, an internal mechanism governs whether the manifestation occurs, yet I am inclined to treat the oxide as leaving the underlying disposition intact while being genuinely unsure whether the enzyme does the same for toxicity.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps drawing on the distinction between intrinsic causal bases and interfering conditions, or on a consistent application of the mask/fink distinction across natural and artificial kinds—that captures all or most of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, identify which clause handles which case, and say what it delivers for the tablet I cannot settle. If no single principle can accommodate them all without strain, say which verdict or verdicts must be overturned, and why. Finally, apply your best principle to a case I have not listed: a software program that runs flawlessly on every operating system except one, on which it is designed to crash immediately upon launch.
