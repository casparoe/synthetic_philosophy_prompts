Write a dialogue between two philosophers—call them A and B—standing in for opposing views on whether normative reasons can be genuinely free-floating: that is, whether there can be reasons for action or belief that obtain even in worlds containing no agents, reasoners, or evaluative perspectives whatsoever. A should defend a strong realist position, arguing that reasons are structurally analogous to mathematical truths and do not depend for their existence on being recognized or acted upon; A should also address the specific objection that a reason with no possible respondent collapses into a mere causal law or descriptive regularity. B should not be a strawman anti-realist, but rather should defend a relational account: reasons exist, but only as relations between propositions and agents (or agent-like systems) with the relevant practical capacities. B should press A on whether causally inert, unrecognized reasons can play any genuine explanatory role, and should propose that advanced AI systems—lacking phenomenal consciousness but possessing robust goal-directed reasoning—could instantiate normative relations without requiring reasons to float free of all structure. The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth: B's challenge about explanatory inertness should be answered specifically rather than deflected, and A's mathematical analogy should be engaged with directly rather than ignored. Have them converge on identifying the crux: whether the possibility of artificial agents that reason without subjective experience undermines the need for free-floating reasons, or instead demonstrates that reasons were never anchored to consciousness in the first place.
