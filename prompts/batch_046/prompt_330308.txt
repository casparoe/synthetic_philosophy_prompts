Construct a thought experiment in which the Cartesian theater model and Dennett’s multiple drafts model of consciousness deliver opposing verdicts about whether there is a single, determinate temporal order to a subject’s conscious experience, and engineer the divergence to depend on one specific structural feature rather than on generic processing delay. The setting should be an ordinary perceptual-psychology laboratory with a healthy adult subject who receives two brief cross-modal stimuli—a tactile pulse to the left hand and an auditory click—separated by an interval near the threshold for temporal-order judgment. Through a stable training-and-report protocol, the subject consistently reports experiencing the touch before the click, even on trials where the neural processes that constitute the conscious content for the click reach global availability measurably earlier than those for the touch; crucially, the verbal report itself is incorporated into the working-memory draft that sets the baseline expectation for the next trial, so that correcting the subject by feedback fails because the correction becomes part of the very draft being evaluated. Design the case so that the Cartesian theater is forced to choose between locating both experiences at one canonical “showtime” that conflicts with the neural chronology—thereby requiring an implausible central editor that rewrites the stream after it has already been presented to the observer—or dismissing the stable first-person report and its behavioral coherence as illusory. Meanwhile, the multiple drafts model should accommodate the discrepancy naturally by treating the reported order as one published narrative among competing drafts, with no need for a canonical sequence independent of report. Constraints: realistic, no split-brain surgery, no exotic physics, one paragraph for the scenario, ordinary sensory modalities only, and the philosophical work must be done specifically by the feedback loop in which the reporting act is part of the draft being assessed, not by sensory latency alone. After presenting the scenario, state precisely what is at issue, walk through the reasoning each model gives and why, identify the feature that does the work, and explain what happens if that feature is removed—for instance, by replacing verbal report with an immediate non-cognitive behavioral measure that never enters working memory, causing the discrepancy to vanish and both models to converge. Finish by showing how a Cartesian-theater defender, such as a global-workspace theorist, would attack the case—perhaps by insisting that global broadcasting establishes the canonical sequence regardless of local processing-completion times—and assess whether that reply saves the theater or merely relocates the editing problem elsewhere in the architecture.
