Design a 75-minute structured seminar activity for approximately 14 graduate philosophy students that makes the force of Buridan’s ass felt through direct experience rather than through exposition. The setting is an upper-level metaphysics seminar; the group size is about 14; the time available is one session of 75 minutes. Participants should come away seeing that any deterministic tie-breaking rule applied to perfectly symmetric options necessarily introduces an asymmetry that was absent from the original choice situation, and that suspending choice in order to preserve symmetry carries a distinct cost to agency.

The deliverable should be a runnable activity with explicit rules, assigned roles, and small symbolic stakes, followed by a debrief. Structure the session in three phases, with no up-front lecture naming the paradox.

Phase one (20 minutes): a paired simulation. Divide the group into pairs. Give each pair two physically identical sealed envelopes, each containing an identical token reward, but inform them that they share a collective symbolic penalty—loss of a point that affects only their pair’s standing—if they fail to select one envelope within three minutes and justify the selection without appealing to any hidden difference between the envelopes. Arrange the rules so that participants experience either paralysis or an ungrounded leap to decision before anyone mentions Buridan’s ass. Assign rotating roles within each pair: Symmetry Defender, Agency Advocate, and Silent Observer, with the observer recording exactly what justification, if any, was actually used.

Phase two (25 minutes): a full-group deliberation. The class must collectively design a decision procedure for an autonomous agent facing two options with identical utility functions and no distinguishing features. They must choose among three architectures—(i) a deterministic lexicographic tie-breaker that always resolves in a fixed direction, (ii) a genuinely random selection with uniform distribution, and (iii) indefinite suspension of choice pending further information—and defend the choice using only the resources available to the agent itself. The facilitator should withhold the historical mapping of these architectures onto rationalist, libertarian, and skeptical interpretations of Buridan until after the vote.

Phase three (20 minutes): a debrief, followed by a brief individual reflection (10 minutes). The debrief should map the paired paralysis onto the ass’s position between hay and water; map the collective vote onto debates about rational determinacy, arbitrary will, and the conditions for action; and identify precisely where each proposed architecture smuggles in an asymmetry—whether spatial, temporal, or structural—that was not present in the symmetric situation. Include an explicit, honest note on which feature of Buridan’s original formulation—specifically the metaphysical thesis that the will can be moved only by a stronger reason, and that in the absence of such a reason action is metaphysically impossible—cannot be reproduced in a classroom setting and must be supplied directly by the facilitator. Also provide a frank warning about what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run (for example, participants will invent spurious distinctions such as envelope texture or left-right bias to avoid paralysis), together with instructions for how the facilitator should handle it without breaking the symmetry condition prematurely.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not invoke highly specialized formal results that a model without web access could not reliably reconstruct from general training. Ensure that the problem emerges from the rules and incentives of the activity itself, not from the facilitator’s explanation after the fact.
