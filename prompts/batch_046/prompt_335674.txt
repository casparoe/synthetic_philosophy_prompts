I've been coming around to the idea that we should treat major pathogenic outbreaks and volcanic climate shocks as genuine 'historical actors' — not just background causes but entities that carry explanatory weight comparable to states or social movements. Specifically, I'm drawn to the strong version: that in cases like the Justinianic Plague reshaping Byzantine labor law, or Tambora's 1816 'year without summer' altering European agricultural politics more than any contemporaneous reform movement, the germ/volcano should get causal and even agential priority. The claim is that this avoids old-school environmental determinism because it frames human societies as reactive partners in a coevolutionary drama rather than passive victims, but I'm not sure thats enough. So tell me what would actually count against this position — not just the obvious 'people still made choices' rebuttal, but which intuitions about agency it violates, whether it collapses into determinism once you formalize the causal contribution, what results from philosophy of science about explanation selection say about giving priority to non-intentional causes, and whether there's a hidden commitment to treating biological or geological processes as having strategic interests. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender of this view could genuinely concede without giving up the core idea. I'd especially like to know if there's a case — maybe a climate shock that produced no lasting institutional change, or an epidemic that was fully contained — that would genuinely break the view rather than just being an outlier they can explain away.
