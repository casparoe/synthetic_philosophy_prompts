I have been trying to work out my own position on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, largely by thinking through analogies to software architecture and incentive design, and I want to know whether the package actually holds together. Here is where I have landed:

(1) I think the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem shows that instrumental rationality by itself—treating moral conduct as an optimization problem over a preference function—cannot produce genuinely moral motivation. The reason you ought to be fair is not structurally like the reason you ought to refactor inefficient code, because the latter is always reducible to an external performance target.

(2) At the same time, I think institutional incentive structures—reputation systems, regulatory penalties, performance bonuses—are legitimate and often necessary tools for aligning collective behavior with moral outcomes, even when they operate entirely outside an agent’s internal motivational set.

(3) I am drawn to the view, associated with Bernard Williams, that reasons are genuinely reason-giving for an agent only if they connect to something already present in that agent’s motivational set. If a consideration cannot be linked to an existing concern, it is not a reason for that particular agent.

(4) Yet I also believe that moral education and social architecture can legitimately aim to reshape what people care about, not merely to reveal logical connections between existing desires and moral conclusions, but to actually alter the motivational substrate itself.

(5) Finally, I think the distinction between right-kind and wrong-kind reasons is best understood as a distinction between reasons that track the internal correctness conditions of an action—like logical consistency in a proof—versus reasons that track contingent external payoffs such as speed, resource use, or personal gain.

Do these five commitments form a consistent package? Specifically, does (3) conflict with (4), since reshaping the motivational substrate seems to require appealing to considerations that are external to the agent’s current set? Does (2) undermine (1), given that I accept external incentives as legitimate social tools even while I reject them as sources of moral motivation? And is there a hidden tension between (5) and (1): if moral reasons are analogous to internal correctness conditions, why can they not be treated as just another optimization target within an instrumental framework? If the set is not jointly tenable, I want an audit rather than a lecture: order the tensions by severity, specify for each what would have to be revised, and say what giving up that commitment would cost the rest of the package. I am not asking for bibliographic references; I want to know whether my own map is coherent.
