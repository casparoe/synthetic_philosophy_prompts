Within the philosophy of dispositions—specifically the cluster of problems generated by finks, masks, and antidotes—several open lines of inquiry compete for limited attention, whether that means a single researcher's three-year project or a small group's grant. One foundational line asks whether a unified analysis of dispositions can be given in terms of categorical bases plus modal conditions, or whether the persistence of fink cases forces a pluralism about dispositional properties. A second, less developed line concerns hierarchical or layered masking: in a system composed of parts with individual dispositions, organizational or structural features can suppress some capacities while leaving others intact, but it remains unclear whether such suppression is best modeled as a relational fink, a collective mask, or an emergent disposition of the whole that replaces rather than hides its parts. A third line applies these distinctions to artificial systems, asking whether adversarial perturbations to a trained model function as masks on an underlying dispositional capacity, such as a classification tendency, or as evidence that no genuine disposition was present—an issue with direct implications for how we attribute robustness and failure modes to AI.
