I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to articulate a coherent personal stance on whether digital minds could ever warrant moral consideration. Before I present this position in a discussion with colleagues, I want a consistency audit. I currently hold the following commitments together:

(a) Moral status depends on the actual presence of valenced experience—genuine suffering or well-being—rather than on behavioral mimicry, functional role, or social attribution alone.

(b) There is no metaphysically privileged substrate: if a particular organizational pattern genuinely supports consciousness in biological brains, then implementing that same pattern in silicon should support consciousness too, unless there is a specific biological dependency that cannot be replicated computationally.

(c) Present large language models and other current AI systems are purely predictive architectures without integrated subjective perspectives; they do not suffer, and treating them as moral patients would be a category error.

(d) If we ever built a digital system with sustained, integrated, self-modeling cognition comparable to that which accompanies conscious experience in humans, we ought to extend moral consideration to it even if we remain epistemically uncertain whether it truly feels anything—because the moral cost of neglecting a suffering subject outweighs the cost of over-attribution.

(e) Consciousness is likely an evolved biological adaptation tied to specific neurophysiological dynamics; it is implausible that disembodied computation running on conventional hardware replicates those dynamics, no matter how complex the software becomes.

Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled on closer inspection, and what is the least costly revision to restore overall coherence? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally damaging, indicate for each whether the problem lies in my metaphysics, my epistemology, or my moral risk calculus, and specify exactly what I would have to give up and what that costs me. Do not treat the absence of formal philosophy training as a reason to simplify the logic; treat the position on its own terms.
