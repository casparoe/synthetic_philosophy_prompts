I am preparing a graduate seminar session on material constitution, specifically the relation between a statue and the lump of clay that constitutes it. My students have already encountered the canonical examples in introductory texts, and I want to avoid rehearsing those familiar cases. Instead, I intend to center the session on a deliberately constructed scenario: a work of conceptual sculpture specified by its creator to consist of a particular mass of bronze arranged in a certain form, with the explicit condition that gradual, authorized replacement of parts by chemically identical material preserves the work, whereas melting the entire mass and recasting it as a single sphere in one act destroys it, even though the same bronze atoms remain. I want to use this case to establish that constitution is neither numerical identity nor mereological composition, and that the persistence conditions of the constituted object depend on intentional-historical properties rather than on the bare continuity of material substrate.

Please plan the explanatory arc for me rather than delivering the explanation itself. Which dependency must come first—should I begin by distinguishing numerical identity from qualitative similarity, or should I open with the asymmetry of ontological dependence and let the rejection of identity emerge from it? Identify exactly which feature of my bronze-replacement case should carry the distinction between constitution and identity, and which feature it must lack—should it avoid any appeal to mental representation that might allow students to collapse the artwork into a purely conceptual object? I anticipate at least three predictable misconceptions: that constitution is merely the clay taking on a shape-property, that the statue and the clay are two fully separate entities coincident in space, and that the divergence in persistence conditions is only a pragmatic or linguistic convention rather than a metaphysical asymmetry. Specify the precise point in the sequence at which each should be anticipated and defused. Design one diagnostic question about the case itself—without reference to any external literature—that I can pose at the end to determine whether the students understand that the constituted object and its constituter can differ in modal properties without being merely conceptually distinct. Finally, indicate what I should explicitly leave out—perhaps the problem of temporary intrinsics or the four-dimensionalist resolution—so that the session remains focused and does not dissolve into an undifferentiated survey of metaphysical positions.
