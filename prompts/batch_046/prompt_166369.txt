I am working through the standard democratic-boundary literature—Dahl’s affected-interests framing, Goodin’s all-subjected principle, the associative or co-author views of the demos—and I find that every existing checklist of desiderata assumes membership can be mapped onto a single jurisdictional container. I want to challenge that assumption without leaving the conceptual terrain, so I am introducing a distinction I will call structural presence: the condition of being embedded in a polity’s coercive, economic, and data architectures without holding formal membership status, or conversely holding formal membership while being structurally absent. Before I evaluate any theory that takes structural presence seriously, I need the full specification of what an adequate theory of democratic membership must deliver if it is not allowed to collapse presence into jurisdiction or vice versa.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of who belongs to the demos has to get right, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. The list should cover the familiar boundary cases—resident non-citizens, emigrant citizens, future generations, beings lacking rational agency—but it must also handle the structural-presence cases: long-term residents whose labor sustains public infrastructure but who lack formal standing; citizens whose economic and informational lives are fully governed by institutional frameworks centered elsewhere; and populations affected by algorithmic or environmental decisions made by bodies in which they have no formal voice. For each desideratum, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (symmetry between affectedness and subjection, preservation of state sovereignty, exclusion of non-rational agents, dynamic versus static membership), and flag any pairs you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable—especially tensions between accommodating structural presence and preserving the self-determination of an already-constituted demos. Then, briefly and without turning this into a literature review, indicate how the standard positions fare against your list: where they preserve the presence-jurisdiction distinction, where they collapse it, and what that collapse costs them conceptually. I do not need bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I need the conceptual architecture made explicit so I can see where the boundary problem becomes harder than the standard binary allows.
