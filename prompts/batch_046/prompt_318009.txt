I am preparing the interview guide for a philosophy podcast episode in which I will speak with an unnamed philosopher—let us call her Dr. A—who has recently advanced a particular interpretation of Buddhist anatta. Rather than treating the doctrine as a purely negative claim that no enduring self exists, she defends what she terms a process-relationist account: personal identity is not anchored in a substance or in bare psychological continuity, but in a dynamically maintained, causally integrated pattern of the five skandhas, sustained through upadana and structured by paticcasamuppada. She argues that this framework avoids the too-many-thinkers objection that plagues both psychological-continuity and animalist theories in Western metaphysics, and offers a more coherent response to Parfit-style reductionism, since it denies that identity requires either a further fact or a substantial bearer without collapsing into mereological nihilism.

I want a sequence of six to eight interview questions that turn on the actual content of her position rather than asking her to restate it. Specifically, please include at least one question addressing how her process-relationist reading accommodates the first-person phenomenology of persistence—the persistent sense of self that subjects report—given that anatta denies a subject behind experience; a second probing whether upadana functions as a hidden substrate or relational glue that reintroduces something self-like, anticipating her likely reply that clinging is itself impermanent and dependently originated; and a third on what her view implies for ethical responsibility across temporal parts or rebirth, particularly if the pattern can be disrupted or duplicated. For each, include a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The tone should be rigorous but conversational, suitable for listeners with some philosophical literacy. Please write the response in British English, and please state how confident you are in the framing and diagnosis offered, explicitly identifying the point about which you are least certain.
