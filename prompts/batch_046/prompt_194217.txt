I'm studying Hume's treatment of miracles in Section X of his Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding. In the core argument he defines a miracle as a transgression of a law of nature established by firm and unalterable experience, then maintains that uniform experience amounts to a direct and full proof against any miracle occurring. He concludes that testimony can establish a miracle only if the falsehood of that testimony is more miraculous—more contrary to experience—than the event it asserts.

What I cannot see clearly is how this weighing of probabilities is meant to sit with Hume's general claim that belief is not an act of reason but a feeling or custom-produced vivacity attached to an idea. If belief strength tracks habitual association rather than explicit comparison of evidential weights, then by what mechanism does the mind perform the comparative judgment Hume requires? Is the argument implicitly smuggling in a rationalist calculus of belief that conflicts with his own associationist psychology, or can the comparison be reconstructed as just one more customary inference about the relative frequency of deception versus natural violations? I would appreciate help working through this specific tension.
