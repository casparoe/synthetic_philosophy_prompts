Having spent thirty years in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy, I have come to treat classical Chinese texts not as antiquarian relics but as conceptual experiments whose internal logic can be tested against the structure of perception. Next Thursday I present to the interdepartmental seminar on philosophy of language and metaphysics—an audience comprising analytic metaphysicians, logicians with a specialist interest in the School of Names, and one textual historian of pre-Qin thought—my thesis that the White Horse dialogue traditionally attributed to Gongsun Long ought to be read neither as a fallacious equivocation on universals and particulars, nor as a proto-logical puzzle about mass nouns, but rather as an early phenomenological analysis of perceptual synthesis. On my reading, when the interlocutor denies that a white horse is a horse, he is not committing a category error; he is isolating the fact that form and colour are given in distinct intentional acts. The term horse names the formal structure grasped in one mode of seeing, whereas white horse names the compound object in which chromatic determination is already fused with that form. The dialogue thus shows, I claim, that predication depends on the prior synthesis of perceptual fields rather than on logical subsumption alone.

Given that several members of this audience have published on identity conditions and on Mingjia interpretation, I should like you to prepare me for the objections they will actually raise, ordered by how likely and damaging they are. For each objection, please identify the likely speaker—the analytic metaphysician concerned with anachronism, the logician defending the mass-noun reading, or the textual historian—phrase the objection in the precise vocabulary that audience member would employ, and advise me which elements to concede outright, which to defend, and where the weakest point in my reply lies. I am particularly anxious about the charge that I am smuggling Husserlian phenomenology into a Warring States logical debate; please treat that as the objection I am hoping nobody asks, yet include it with the concise reply I ought to have ready for a seminar setting rather than a monograph. The response should acknowledge the limits of what a retired experimentalist can legitimately claim about textual scholarship, and should avoid asking me to cite editions or page numbers that I could not verify without library access.
