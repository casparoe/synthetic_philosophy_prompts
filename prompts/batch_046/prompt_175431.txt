Consider the following notes for a short essay on Gongsun Long’s white-horse dialogue and its place within the School of Names (Mingjia).

The standard reconstruction treats the argument as either a confusion between universal and particular classes, or as a deliberate sophism about compound versus simple terms. I want to propose instead that the dialogue is best read as an early investigation into predicative exclusion: the claim that adding a contingent determinable, such as colour, to a kind term does not merely restrict extension but alters logical behaviour in a non-conjunctive way. On this view, horse functions as a kind-referring expression neutral with respect to accidental properties, whereas white horse operates as a filtered description in which the determinable white excludes other colour determinates. The School of Names is not literally denying that white horses are horses; rather, it isolates the fact that once a contingent property is built into the referring expression, the conditions for successful reference shift. This resembles, very roughly, the modern distinction between kind terms and definite descriptions, except that here the added predicate acts exclusionarily rather than conjunctively—specifying one value in a dimension implicitly rules out others, thereby changing what counts as a match.

If this framing holds, then the dialogue concerns the mechanics of reference-fixing more than ontology in the Aristotelian sense.

Please comment on the interpretation. In particular: (1) Is the predicative-exclusion reading historically plausible for the Mingjia, or does it import anachronistic machinery? (2) Does the comparison with modern theories of reference illuminate the dialogue, or does it obscure its original purpose? (3) Do specific moves in the dialogue—such as the exchange about yellow horse and black horse—support the claim that colour predicates function as exclusionary filters rather than ordinary conjunctive modifiers? I am especially interested in whether the argument survives if we do not treat the School of Names as proto-logicians in the modern sense.
