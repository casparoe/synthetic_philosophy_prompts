I recently joined the ethics review board at my regional hospital consortium, where I am drafting the normative framework for our triage and resource-allocation protocols. During our last meeting, two senior members—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—became locked in a disagreement about whether T. M. Scanlon’s contractualism can coherently guide institutional decisions that involve trade-offs across groups of patients. Because I am responsible for writing the final proposal, I need a rigorous adjudication of their exchange, but I want to be sure I am not missing a structural point about contractualist justification itself.

Elena opened by arguing that contractualism correctly solves the aggregation problem by treating each person’s complaint separately rather than summing them. She said that if our board adopted a principle permitting us to sacrifice one critically ill patient to save five others, the one patient could reasonably reject that principle, and since contractualism requires that our actions be justifiable to each affected individual, the principle must be abandoned. She insisted this is not a failure of the theory but its central virtue: it respects the separateness of persons. Marcus responded that this renders contractualism paralyzed in any real allocation context, because every institutional rule affects groups. He proposed that we should work with compound principles—general rules that specify conditions under which aggregation is permitted—rather than applying the rejection test to bare outcomes. He suggested that a principle allowing aggregation only above an extreme disparity, say saving fifty lives at the cost of one, could not be reasonably rejected even by the potential victim, because any principle that absolutely forbade such trade-offs would itself be rejectable by those who might be among the fifty saved. Elena replied that Marcus was smuggling consequentialist weighting back in: once the magnitude of harm to others influences whether a complaint counts as reasonable, we are no longer treating each person as an end but as part of an aggregate sum. Marcus then introduced what he called an ex ante institutional framing: in designing triage rules, we choose principles from behind a partial veil of ignorance about whether we will be the single victim or one of the many beneficiaries. From that standpoint, he argued, a high-threshold aggregation principle might be the one that no one could reasonably reject, whereas Elena’s absolute prohibition might be rejectable by anyone who considers the possibility of being among the larger group.

Please adopt the role of a professional moral philosopher specializing in distributive justice and contractualist ethics, and treat me as a fellow board member seeking analytical clarity rather than rhetorical victory. Before delivering your verdict, briefly brainstorm the architecture of Scanlonian justification—distinguishing individual-level justifiability from institutional-level principle selection, and noting where the strength of a complaint interacts with the content of the principle being tested. Then adjudicate the exchange move by move: assess whether Elena is right that allowing disparity to influence reasonable rejection abandons the separateness of persons; evaluate whether Marcus’s ex ante framing genuinely rescues contractualism or instead collapses it into a form of rule-consequentialism or Rawlsian constructivism; and identify what both interlocutors are missing, particularly regarding whether compound principles can be formulated without consequentialist baggage and whether Scanlon’s own distinction between personal and impersonal reasons bears on institutional design. Conclude with an assessment of who is more right overall and what your recommendation for our framework proposal should be. Think carefully, avoid strawmanning either position, and do not rely on obscure secondary literature—work from the conceptual structure of contractualism itself.
