I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on political judgment and have been arguing with peers about whether Hannah Arendt's portrait of Adolf Eichmann in the Jerusalem trial holds up once you take seriously the moments when he actually invoked moral language—his mangled reference to Kant's categorical imperative, his talk of duty, and his insistence that he had never acted from hatred. The standard commentary treats these as either transparent lies or as superficial inconsistencies that confirm his "thoughtlessness" (Gedankenlosigkeit). But I want to push past that reading. I propose that we distinguish two phenomena that Arendt tends to collapse: first, cognitive thoughtlessness, meaning the genuine failure to exercise reflective judgment about one's actions and their meaning for others; and second, performative bureaucratic identity, meaning the deliberate cultivation of a persona in which administrative compliance is treated as a moral virtue in itself. These are not the same, and the trial record gives us material to test whether Eichmann exhibited one, both, or a strategic oscillation between them.

Analyze the 1961 trial of Adolf Eichmann in Jerusalem through the lens of Arendt's concept of the banality of evil, but apply the distinction above as your organizing framework.
