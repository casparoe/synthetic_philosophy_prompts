I spent the better part of last evening arguing with a close friend about Erwin Schrödinger’s late philosophical writings—specifically the Vedanta-inflected monism he develops in essays such as Mind and Matter and elsewhere—where he proposes that the plurality of individual consciousnesses is an illusion sustained by the spatial and temporal boundaries of bodies, and that at the deepest level there is only one universal consciousness, with quantum mechanical indistinguishability serving not as a proof but as a structural analogy: just as identical particles lack individual identity outside of measurement context, so too do human minds lack metaphysical separateness outside of embodied perspective. I found the view more coherent than my friend allowed, particularly once one distinguishes the claim as metaphysical rather than empirical, but I want to stress-test it properly rather than defend it by default.

What would actually count against this position—not merely what sounds implausible to a materialist ear, but what observations, conceptual results, or formal arguments should move a defender? I am especially interested in the so-called division problem for monistic consciousness: if there is genuinely only one subject of experience, what mechanism—causal, phenomenal, or logical—accounts for the apparent fragmentation into non-overlapping first-person perspectives, and does neuroscience’s localization of phenomenal binding (say, split-brain cases or dissociative pathologies) provide decisive counter-evidence or merely an awkward descriptive challenge? Likewise, I want an honest appraisal of Schrödinger’s quantum analogy: once decoherence suppresses macroscopic superposition and environmental entanglement is traced out, does the indistinguishability argument collapse into a category mistake, or can it be reformulated in terms of information-theoretic identity? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, indicate which ones a committed Vedantic monist would concede as genuinely damaging versus which they would dismiss by retreating to the unfalsifiability of metaphysical first principles, and assess whether such insulation renders the view intellectually empty or whether that immunity is appropriate for a claim about the ultimate nature of consciousness. I do not want bibliographies or citations; I want the map of exposure—what this view actually risks, and where it stands exposed.
