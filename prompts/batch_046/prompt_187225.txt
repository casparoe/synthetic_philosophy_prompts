I've been trying to understand this Frege-Geach problem that keeps coming up in metaethics — you know, where expressivists say a sentence like "stealing is wrong" just expresses an attitude, basically "boo stealing!", but then they can't make sense of it embedded inside conditionals or negations like "if stealing is wrong, then..." because in that spot it doesn't seem to express the boo-attitude at all. Geach pointed this out decades ago and people are still patching it with "disjunctive attitudes" or "higher-order commitments" or whatever. Reading the back-and-forth feels like watching people debug the same codebase with different variable names.

My working theory is that its not really one shared disagreement at all. I think one camp cares about what mental state gets tokened when you sincerely utter the sentence, while the other camp cares about logical compositionality and consistency — so they're using "meaning" to cover totally different evaluation contexts, kind of like in programming where a symbol can be a value or a lazy thunk depending on strict vs lazy evalutation mode. But maybe I'm wrong and there's a real formal problem hiding underneath.

So I want a diagnosis, not a survey of every reply ever written. Are the sides actually talking past each other — one group answering a psychology-of-language question, another answering a logic question — under the single heading "what does moral language mean"? Or is there a hidden substantive dispute about whether embedded moral clauses preserve expressive force, cancel it, or switch to some representational mode? Tell me what each side actually says: the expressivists who try to save compositionality with conditional attitudes, and the critics who say any such move either changes the subject or smuggles in representational content. Is the stall caused by bedrock intuitions that morality "has to" be non-representational, or by a missing formal distinction — maybe between speaker-meaning and sentence-meaning, or between evaluation in embedded vs unembedded contexts?

And be honest: what would actually count as progress here? A sharper logical framework for embedded expressions, a conceptual split I haven't named, or should we admit the question is ill-posed because we're forcing moral language into a representational grammar it was never meant to fit? Also, since I'm just a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, I wonder if borrowing something like evaluation-context tracking from programming actually clarifies the embedding problem, or if that's me projecting my day job onto philosophy. What's the real structure of this disagreement, and is there anything that could move it?
