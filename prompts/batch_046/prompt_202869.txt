As a retired physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy of law and moral psychology, I am struck by how debates about punishment and moral agency echo problems in statistical mechanics—particularly whether certain explanatory constructs correspond to real dynamics or are merely calculational conveniences. I want you to act as a professional philosopher with expertise in conceptual analysis and the philosophy of science. Before you begin drafting, take one brief paragraph to brainstorm: list the three distinctions that must be kept rigorously separate in what follows—(i) moral desert as a metaphysical property versus a social-functional status, (ii) the conditions for competent rational agency versus the conditions for basic moral responsibility, and (iii) retributive justification versus consequentialist justification—so that none are collapsed in the document.

Then draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two camps on just deserts and punishment should produce. Camp A is the Dennett-style compatibilist camp: they hold that free will and moral desert are naturalized, real but deflationary phenomena—agents genuinely deserve responses like praise and blame in virtue of their rational capacities and social roles, determinism is compatible with this form of desert, and punishment is partly justified by its role in maintaining the normative structure of the community, though they reject any metaphysically heavy, eye-for-an-eye retributivism. Camp B is the Caruso-style free-will-skeptic camp: they hold that neither determinism nor indeterminism provides the kind of control needed for basic moral desert, that our retributive intuitions are evolutionary byproducts or social fictions, and that all punishment must be justified on purely forward-looking grounds—rehabilitation, deterrence, public safety, or quarantine—without any appeal to the offender's intrinsic deservingness.

To avoid a generic rehash of standard free-will debates, frame the central crux using the concept of normative inertia—the idea, borrowed analogically from mechanics, that established social norms resist change not merely because they are currently useful, but because they carry structural momentum—asking whether moral desert is like inertia (a real feature of social dynamics that must be accounted for in any adequate theory of punishment) or like a fictitious force (a calculational convenience with no ontological weight).

Include the following concrete case that both sides agree is conceptually relevant: a 45-year-old accountant with no prior violence develops an undiagnosed orbitofrontal tumor, commits a single assault, is surgically treated, and thereafter shows full remorse and restored impulse control; both sides must state in advance whether this case undermines desert-based punishment specifically, or only a particular misapplication of it, and whether the correct institutional response should differ if the tumor had never developed but the behavioral act was identical in every observable respect.

Your response must be structured in these sections, in this exact order, with approximate lengths:

I. Agreed Foundational Claims (numbered list; approx. 300 words): claims both camps can sign about the nature of agency, the social function of punishment, the relevance of neuroscience to legal responsibility, and what both agree is not genuinely at issue.

II. Precise Formulation of Residual Disagreement (table format; approx. 250 words): the exact points of divergence, each phrased in wording that its own camp would accept, with no straw-manning.

III. The Cruxes and Agreed Tests (approx. 450 words): identify at least two cruxes—one being whether normative inertia is real or fictitious, another being how to interpret the tumor case—then for each: the shared argument or evidence both accept bears on it, Camp A's predicted resolution, and Camp B's predicted resolution.

IV. Verbal vs Substantive Audit (approx. 150 words): which parts of the traditional debate are merely about competing definitions of free will, which are genuinely substantive, and whether the normative-inertia framing clarifies or obscures the real divide.

Write formally and precisely.
