I've been arguing with my friend about prioritarianism, specifically whether the leveling-down objection actually sticks if you use a threshhold-sensitive version where benefits below a welfare floor get exponentially weighted but anything above gets linear treatment. The usual reply is that prioritarianism doesn't value equality for it's own sake, just helping the worst off, so leveling down doesn't help anyone and isn't required. But my friend thinks that's a bit too quick—she says if the weighting function is continuous and monotonic, there's still a weird asymetry: destroying goods for the well-off never improves the priority score of the worst-off unless resources are actually redirected, but the formalism doesn't encode redistribution mechanics, only evaluation. So the position I'm defending is that threshhold-sensitive prioritarianism with an explicit non-transferability constraint—the weighting applies only to direct benefits, not comparative losses—is genuinely immune to leveling-down, not just conceptually but structurally, because the objection requires a comparative metric that the view explicitely rejects.

What would actually count against this? I'm not asking for a generic restatement—I'm asking specifically: what case, formal result, or intuition pump shows that even with the non-transferability constraint and threshhold weighting, the view still implicitly commits me to leveling down? Are there hidden comparative premises in the weighting function itself? Could someone construct a distribution where, because of the exponential floor-weighting, reducing the best-off person from 100 to 99 technically changes the aggregate priority score in a way that looks like comparative evaluation? Or is the insulation complete? Also, if defenders themselves concede that some comparative element is unavoidable—maybe just in identifying who is "worse off"—does that open a back door? I'd like an honest map of where this specific formulation is exposed, what's merely awkward versus genuinely damaging, and whether there's a version of the objection that doesn't rely on equality being intrinsic but still bites.
