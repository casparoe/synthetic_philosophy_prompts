In debates over workfare and benefit conditionality, defenders often liken unemployment support to a private insurance contract: national insurance contributions are the premiums, jobseeker’s allowance is the payout, work-search requirements are proof-of-loss clauses, and benefit fraud is filing a false claim. The analogy is used to justify strict sanctions and to cast non-compliant claimants as “undeserving” — people breaking a contract they freely entered. Lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the insurer, the policyholder, the premium, the covered risk, the deductible or excess, and the claims investigator? Then identify where the mapping fractures — for example, whether tax-funded benefits are genuinely voluntary contracts, whether the state can be treated as a profit-maximising insurer, whether citizenship creates obligations analogous to policy terms, and whether administrative error in welfare claims maps onto intentional deception in private insurance. Does the analogy smuggle in a transactional theory of citizenship that treats social rights as earned rather than unconditional? Once those disanalogies are exposed, does the conclusion that conditionality is merely “enforcing the terms” survive, or was the moral stigma of the “undeserving poor” being carried by the insurance picture itself? If that analogy collapses, is there a better one — say, a mutual-aid pool or a public-health quarantine — that could capture conditionality without presupposing contractual desert?
