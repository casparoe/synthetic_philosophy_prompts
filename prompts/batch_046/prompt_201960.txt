I've been chewing on that old rationalist framing where Darwin and Turing are like mirror-image inversions—Darwin gives you design-level competence with literally zero comprehension in the thing doing the selecting, and Turing gives you a process that looks like reasoning running on a substrate that doesn't understand a word of it. I'm on an evaluation team at a small AI lab and we're constantly running into systems that either solve hard tasks without "getting it" or seem to understand stuff but fall over on basic execution, and our reviewers keep mixing the two up. I want a reusable procedue my team can actually run—like a 20-minute checklist—when we're assessing whether a new model or algorithm is running on one of these inversions, both, or neither.

Specifically, turn the Darwin-Turing inversion idea into an ordered practitioner flowchart: first determine if there's observable competence on a task that normally requires conceptual reasoning; second, check whether the mechanism has access to the conceptual structures that usually ground that reasoning (allowing for black-box access and limited compute—say what counts as sufficient evidence when we can't inspect weights); third, classify which inversion applies—Darwin-style selection/search with no internal model, Turing-style computation over representations ungrounded in substrate comprehension, a double inversion where both apply, or neither. The checklist needs to flag every point where we're smuggling a judgment call into a binary yes/no, and it has to say what to do when the black-box outputs are identical across categories.

Then run it on three cases: an evolutionary algorithm that discovers novel circuit layouts, a 3B-param LLM that does chain-of-thought math but collapses on slight rephrasings, and one case where the procedure dumps the result into "pure Darwinian competence" that my team will probably hate—maybe a human crowd-sourced prediction market that aggregates accurate forecasts in a domain where no individual forecaster understands the mechanics. Also be honest about what the procedure can't settle, especially the line between shallow pattern-matching and genuine Turing-style symbol manipulation when we only have outputs to go on. Keep it practical enough that someone without the philosophy background can apply it, but don't dumb down the parts where the theory actually bites.
