Consider a political philosopher who has offered the following judgments about voting ethics. First, she holds that in a two-candidate race, voting for the less objectionable candidate is not merely permitted but required when the alternative poses a serious moral harm, even if the voter finds both candidates deeply flawed. Second, she maintains that abstaining is permissible—though not laudable—when every available option violates the voter’s core moral commitments and no meaningful lesser-evil distinction applies. Third, she rejects strategic crossover voting in a primary for the opposing party’s weakest candidate, arguing that such behavior treats the democratic process as a purely instrumental game and corrupts the expressive function of the vote. Fourth, she affirms that casting a ballot in a safe seat where the outcome is predetermined remains an act of civic solidarity and should not be dismissed as futile. Fifth, she allows that supporting a small-party candidate with no realistic path to victory is sometimes the right choice, but only when the major parties are both committed to policies the voter regards as gravely unjust, not merely suboptimal.

Now consider a new institutional design: a referendum-style election offers only two candidates, X and Y. The voter regards X as gravely unjust, Y as merely bad but not gravely unjust, and neither as worthy of positive endorsement. Crucially, the ballot permits a “negative vote”—a formal vote cast solely against one candidate, without conferring any positive support on the other—counted separately from abstention and designed so that the candidate with the fewest negative votes wins.

What should our philosopher conclude if she wishes to be consistent with her earlier judgments? Identify the closest analog among her prior verdicts, explain whether her record genuinely underdetermines the case, and if so, state the principle she would be adopting with each possible choice: casting a negative vote against X, casting a positive vote for Y, or abstaining entirely. Finally, specify which further case—involving a different voting mechanism or a different structural relationship between the voter’s preferences and the available options—would best distinguish the competing rules that fit her earlier track record.
