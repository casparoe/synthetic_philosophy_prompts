I have been thinking, without any formal training, about Aristotle’s distinction between acquiring goods for household use and acquiring them without limit through exchange—especially the idea that money, invented to make trade easier, becomes unnatural when it is used to breed more of itself rather than to serve real needs. I would like a short story that makes this tension concrete through an invented world rather than through historical examples. Imagine a small harbor town where the currency is not metal but living tide-tokens: each one is born from the moon’s pull and can be traded for fish, nets, or repairs. If a person hoards more tokens than she needs to live, they begin to multiply in her chest and eventually drown the very household goods she actually uses; yet the town measures honor and security by how high the tide stands in one’s vault. Write the story of a woman who suddenly realizes that her accumulated tokens are not wealth but a kind of flooding, and who must make a single, visible decision—either releasing them back to the sea or keeping them as an end in themselves. Let the world and that final choice carry the philosophical point: whether she treats money as a tool with a natural limit tied to use, or as something that grows beyond need and consumes its owner. Do not include any explicit philosophical explanation in the narration; let the situation and the consequence speak.
