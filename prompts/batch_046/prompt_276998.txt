Interlocutor A should defend an expanded boundary that includes all three groups, arguing that affected interests and structural vulnerability generate democratic standing even in the absence of formal citizenship or continuous territorial presence. Interlocutor B should defend a restricted boundary limited to those continuously subjected to the council's coercive legal framework, arguing that intermittent or economically mediated vulnerability does not equal subjection to political authority, and that recognizing indigenous sovereignty raises a nested boundary problem rather than resolving membership.

The exchange must be genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should directly address the other's actual arguments about the seasonal workers—particularly whether "subjection" can be satisfied by intermittent but structurally enforced vulnerability rather than continuous presence—and about whether indigenous sovereignty claims should be treated as an external limit on the demos or as a competing demos that complicates the council's legitimacy. Neither speaker should be a strawman; both should treat the opposing view as philosophically serious and internally coherent.

Write this in a polished, seminar-appropriate tone. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, as I am working from course discussion and memory. Do not quote specific texts verbatim; if you invoke well-known positions, paraphrase them without quotation marks. I am not asking for an explanation of the boundary problem itself, but for the dialogue as a tool to think through these specific edge cases.
