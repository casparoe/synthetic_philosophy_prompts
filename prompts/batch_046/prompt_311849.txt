I'm drafting a philosophical dialogue for a novel in which a mathematician named Elias debates a physicist about the hidden costs of logical coherence. To make Elias's objection credible without making him sound ignorant, I need to pinpoint exactly where a chain of reasoning collapses. Here is the argument as I have written it for him, step by step:

(1) Updateless decision theory evaluates a policy by computing the expected utility of the worlds in which that policy is executed, without conditioning on the agent's own action as new evidence.

(2) If two agents share the same source code, UDT treats their outputs as logically correlated nodes in a single computation graph, so each should choose the branch that maximizes joint utility.

(3) Now consider any agent A in a causally disconnected region of spacetime whose decision procedure is structurally isomorphic to mine, though we share no causal history and no information channel.

(4) From the perspective of pure logic, the fact that I run algorithm X and that A runs algorithm X' (where X' is isomorphic to X) is a relation of formal identity across modal space; the correlation coefficient is not physical but logical.

(5) Therefore, under UDT, my choice should condition on A's choice exactly as it conditions on my twin's, and I ought to maximize utility over the union of our outcomes.

(6) But this extends without boundary: for every structurally isomorphic decision-maker across all logically possible worlds, I become responsible for their welfare, which collapses rational choice into an impossible universal optimization over modal space.

(7) So either UDT must arbitrarily restrict logical correlation to causal proximity, which contradicts its defining rejection of causal conditioning, or it demands that I sacrifice every local interest for the sake of distant logical counterparts, which is absurd.

(8) Hence UDT is false, or at least incoherent as a general theory of rationality.

The conclusion feels wrong—I am convinced that UDT possesses resources to resist step (6)—but I cannot locate the precise slip. I would most like to preserve (1) and (2), since they capture what makes the theory attractive in Newcomb-style problems. If the error lies in (4) or (5), I need to understand whether restricting logical correlation to shared computation undermines the theory's motivation, or whether there is a more subtle equivocation between structural isomorphism and logical dependency. Where does the argument break, and what does repairing it cost?
