I am applying for a nine-month independent research fellowship to develop a conceptual framework for collective cognition, and I want the proposal to center on a question that has circulated in philosophy of mind since Hofstadter’s dialogue featuring the ant colony Aunt Hillary, but which standard treatments have left genuinely open: can an ant colony be a mind, and if it can, what kind of mind must it be? The existing literature tends to force a binary—either the colony is a single conscious subject whose parts are like neurons in a brain, or it is a purely mechanical aggregate with no mental properties at all. Both sides, I suspect, share a hidden assumption: that wherever a mind exists, there must be a unified, continuous point of view or narrative center that owns its experiences. My project aims to show that this assumption is doing all the work, and that dissolving it opens a third option.

My specific angle is to construct what I will call a heterochronic distributed-mind framework. Rather than asking whether the colony “feels like something” in the human sense, I will examine how cognitive labor in ant colonies is partitioned across radically disjointed temporal scales—some processes unfold in seconds through pheromone signaling, others across months through caste turnover and queen replacement—without being integrated into a single phenomenal present. I will then test whether human institutions that operate on similarly fractured timelines, such as multi-generational scientific collaborations or long-lived democratic bodies, might also instantiate minds that are genuinely mental yet structurally polycentric, lacking any one “now” that binds all their parts together. The deliverable will be a set of precise criteria for attributing distributed heterochronic minds, with explicit conditions under which such an attribution is warranted and conditions under which it collapses back into mere aggregate behavior.

The approach is philosophical rather than empirical: months one through three will be devoted to mapping the conceptual architecture of the Aunt Hillary scenario onto analogical cases in human collective practice; months four through six will develop the heterochronic framework and subject it to objections from both functionalist and phenomenological camps; months seven through nine will produce the final paper, including a clear account of what counts as failure—specifically, if the distinction between unitary and distributed minds collapses into a metaphor, or if any system meeting my criteria turns out to be automatically reducible to its individual parts, leaving the original binary untouched. This framing should make progress precisely where current debates stall, because it shifts the burden from proving that ants have an “inner life” to asking whether our concept of mind has been illegitimately tied to monological subjectivity.
