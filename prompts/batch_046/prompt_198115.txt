I am preparing a unit on social ontology and want to use a literary text that forces students to see institutional facts as lived realities rather than abstractions. I have in mind Shakespeare’s The Merchant of Venice, read through the question of whether race, gender, and money are socially constructed kinds. The play makes money explicit—Antonio’s credit, Shylock’s bond, the ducat—and it makes religious and gender identity explicit in Shylock’s forced conversion and Portia’s legal disguise. I want a reading that does not reduce the play to an illustration of the social-construction thesis but takes the thesis seriously enough to ask where the play complicates it.

Specifically: treat the contract for a pound of flesh as a case study in institutional reality. The bond is valid only because the legal community recognizes it, yet Shylock insists on its literal enforcement, treating the human body as if it were outside the system of collective recognition—as a biological fact that can serve as collateral. At the same time, Shylock’s Jewishness and Portia’s gender are presented as identities that the community can enforce, disguise, or convert by decree. Examine whether the play draws a line between kinds that depend on collective acceptance (money, legal status, gender performance) and kinds that depend on non-institutional reality (the body, perhaps biological sex), and whether that line holds up or collapses in the trial scene and in Shylock’s final forced conversion.

Please address specific moments: the negotiation of the bond, Portia’s cross-dressing and her authority in the courtroom, and the resolution in which Shylock must become Christian and forfeit his wealth. Where does the play show that these categories are constructed and enforced? Where does it treat them as fixed? And does the play suggest that social constructions have real, violent effects precisely because they are treated as natural? I am especially interested in whether there is a hidden parallel between the circulation of money and the circulation of identity in Venice—both requiring collective belief to function, both capable of being withdrawn or converted—and whether recognizing that parallel changes how we read Shylock’s tragedy. End by saying what this literary case adds to the philosophical debate about constructed kinds that an abstract argument alone does not capture.
