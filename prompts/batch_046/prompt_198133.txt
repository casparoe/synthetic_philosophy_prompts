I have recently moved with my partner into a small, self-sufficient observatory in the highlands, constructed upon a slowly rotating platform designed to maintain constant solar alignment. Within the structure, every fixture, surface, and occupant shifts in perfect concert; there is no perceptible motion relative to the walls or to each other. Nevertheless, the water in our washbasin consistently settles into a pronounced parabola, and both of us experience a subtle but persistent vestibular pull towards the periphery. My partner maintains that, since nothing inside moves relative to anything else inside, we are not genuinely in motion at all—merely altering our orientation with respect to distant landmarks or celestial bodies, which is no different in principle from standing still upon the rotating Earth. I feel that the curvature of the water and the bodily sensation of pull indicate that something real is occurring to us, not merely a change in relational position. Yet I cannot articulate whether this commits me to the existence of space as an independent substance through which we are genuinely travelling, or whether I am simply misinterpreting physical effects. Is my partner's relational account adequate to explain why the water behaves as it does, and is there a philosophically coherent defence of the view that our rotation is absolute—without relying on entities that cannot be observed?
