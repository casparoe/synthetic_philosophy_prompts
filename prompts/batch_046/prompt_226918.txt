Consider an institutional practice—already piloted in some tech-sector ethics boards—called Reflective Preference Weighting. Before participating, members complete an audit that classifies each moral judgment they hold as either a “stable preference” (it survives a forty-eight-hour cooling period and exposure to the strongest counter-argument) or a “bias residue” (it weakens or vanishes under the same conditions). The board then weights each member’s input proportionally to their aggregate preference-to-bias ratio. In exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long and with no introduction or conclusion, reconstruct: (1) the implicit theory of moral psychology this practice assumes about access to one’s own mental states; (2) the metaethical claim about the normative authority of reflective endorsement over spontaneous affect; (3) whether the preference/bias distinction can be maintained without collapsing into circularity or question-begging; (4) which competing accounts of moral judgment—e.g., those treating all stable affect as evidential data or all reflection as post-hoc rationalization—this framework quietly rules out; (5) what a decision procedure that rejected the split entirely would have to look like and what it would cost us in terms of moral self-knowledge.
