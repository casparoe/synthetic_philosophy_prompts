For a course on digital ontology and applied ethics, I have settled on the following package of commitments about virtual worlds, but I am unsure whether they form a coherent whole and would like an audit before I present them. Here is where I stand: (1) Virtual entities in persistent worlds are institutional artifacts—analogous to chess pieces or currency—whose existence depends on collective recognition rather than physical substrate, and they possess no intrinsic moral status or phenomenal consciousness. (2) Deeply personalized avatars can nonetheless function as genuine extensions of personal identity, so that severe harm directed at one's avatar—such as virtual torture or non-consensual virtual sexual violence—can wrong the real-world user in a way structurally analogous to direct bodily harm. (3) I reject both panpsychism about digital systems and the "magic circle" thesis that gameplay is morally quarantined; virtual actions are continuous with real-world social practices rather than isolated fictions. (4) The wrongness of virtual harm should be evaluated contractually: an act is wrong if it violates norms that affected human participants could not reasonably reject, rather than by measuring psychological damage as a consequentialist metric. (5) Virtual property rights are legitimate only when they protect a player's real-world investment of labor or capital; there is no independent moral claim to virtual goods that exist purely within the fiction. (6) I am skeptical of empirical psychological studies linking virtual violence to real-world aggression, yet I maintain that deceptive manipulation of a co-player's emotional attachment to a shared virtual world is seriously wrong.

Please examine these for consistency. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict but dissolve on inspection, and what is the least costly revision for each real conflict? In particular, I am worried that my institutional ontology in (1) undermines the identity-extension claim in (2), and that (2) pulls toward a rights-based view that is incompatible with the contractualist framework in (4). I also wonder whether (4) and (5) conflict over whether internal virtual norms can generate property claims that do not track real-world labor. Rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what would have to give, and indicate which single revision would clean up the most problems without collapsing the overall view. I am not asking for a bibliography or empirical literature review—just a conceptual audit of whether these six commitments hang together.
