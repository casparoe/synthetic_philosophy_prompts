I'm preparing a position paper on consciousness and first-person knowledge and want a consistency check before I finalize my view. Here is the package I currently hold:

(1) Epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness is correct: qualitative properties have no causal efficacy with respect to physical events, including neural processes and behavior.

(2) The paradox of phenomenal judgment—that if qualia do not cause judgments about them, those judgments are unreliable—is a genuine structural difficulty, not one that disappears simply by rejecting a causal theory of epistemic justification.

(3) Nevertheless, I reject causal theories of justification specifically for phenomenal knowledge: direct first-person awareness of one's own consciousness does not depend on a causal chain running from the qualia to the belief.

(4) I instead defend a constitutive relation: the phenomenal state and the judging subject are not fully distinct; the judgment is an articulation or expression of the state rather than a separate representation caused by it.

(5) I am a strict physicalist about events and causation: every physical occurrence has a complete physical causal explanation, downward causation never occurs, and the causal closure of the physical domain is inviolable.

(6) At the same time, I deny metaphysical supervenience of the phenomenal on the physical; phenomenal properties are genuinely non-physical properties that supervene only nomologically.

(7) I also think evolutionary biology supports the reliability of our phenomenal judgments: natural selection would have eliminated lineages in which judgments about internal states were systematically decoupled from the states themselves, because such decoupling would carry direct behavioral costs.

Please audit these commitments. Which ones genuinely pull against each other, which tensions are only apparent, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Rank the conflicts by severity, and be specific about what must be abandoned or weakened—whether physicalism, epiphenomenalism, the constitutive account, or the evolutionary argument. In particular, I am worried that (4) fails to solve the problem in (2) without implicitly reintroducing causal efficacy; that (6) conflicts with (5) once we distinguish causal closure of events from ontological closure of properties; and that (7) presupposes precisely the causal tracking between qualia and judgments that (1) denies.
