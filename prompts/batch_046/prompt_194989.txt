I am a journalist covering science and ideas for a long-form magazine, and I am working on a feature about whether relational models of moral personhood can be made sense of in the language of contemporary biology. I am fluent in evolutionary developmental biology—gene-regulatory networks, phenotypic plasticity, niche construction, and homeostatic feedback loops—but I am not trained in philosophy, and I need a precise translation rather than a popular summary. Please restate the core position of Confucian role ethics—specifically the claim that moral personhood is not an internal, pre-social substance but is constituted through reciprocal relational roles (the five cardinal relationships) and enacted through ritual propriety (li)—in that biological vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. Then give me the residue report: which elements map cleanly onto regulatory interaction, plastic response to environmental cues, or niche construction; which require distortion or stretching to fit; and which elements, if any, have no biological counterpart whatsoever. In particular, address whether the normative dimension—that one ought to cultivate these roles and perform these rituals—survives the translation or is lost, and whether that absence reveals a genuine limitation of the biological framework or suggests that the normative claim in the original was less clearly articulated than it appears. Where interpreters disagree—for instance, on whether Confucianism presupposes a metaphysically thick self that is subsequently shaped by roles, or denies such a prior self altogether—separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus view.
