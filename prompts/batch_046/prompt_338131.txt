hey, I'm in this philosphy of science seminar and we were talking about Popper's poverty of historicism — specifically whether there can be laws of history at all. My study group came up with this argument that feels like it breaks Popper's own system, and I can't see where the leak is. We invented this idea of an "institutional half-life" for democracies — basically the claim that every democratic system carries an internal decay curve and must collapse into authoritarianism within about three centuries, no matter how it reforms — and then we ran it through his demarcation criteron.

1. Popper says a claim is scientific only if there's some observable state of affairs that could contradict it.
2. Historicism, on his definition, is the view that history has an inevitable course governed by discoverable laws — like our made-up half-life decay law.
3. Now suppose we observe a democracy that survives past its predicted three-century mark, keeps adapting its institutions, and shows no authoritarian backsliding.
4. That observation directly contradicts the predicted decay.
5. So historicist predictions are falsifiable in principle; by (1) they should count as scientific.
6. But Popper's whole poverty of historicism argument is that historicism is pseudoscience precisely because it evades falsification.
7. Therefore either Popper must admit historicism is science (which he refuses), or he has to drop falsifiability as his demarcation criterion.
8. Both options feel wrong, especially (7). Where is the hidden slide?

If I had to defend one claim, I'd keep (1) and (2) intact — I think Popper's criterion and his characterization of historicism are basically right. So I want to know what's wrong with (3)-(6). Is there an equivocation about what "falsifiable" means for long-range trends? Or does the historicist have some built-in rescue mechanism I'm missing that makes (4) not actually a contradiction? I need the diagnosis, not just "Popper said so" — and I want to know exactly what rejecting the weak step costs me.
