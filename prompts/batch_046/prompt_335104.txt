I retired from condensed matter physics a few years back and have since fallen into philosphy of science, mostly because I got obsessed with this ontology identification problem — you know, the idea that if two theories make all the same predictions inside our observable region but postulate different stuff beyond it, we can't tell which ontology is right. Lately I've been thinking about cosmological horizons and how information past them is permanently inaccessable, so I came up with what I call the "informational closure principle": if we can never recieve data about region X, then there's no ontological fact about X, not even an unknown one — the ontology is just undefined there.

My reasoning is that ontology identification needs empirical access to do its job, and where access is closed, identification fails, which should mean we can't commit to anything existant beyond the horizon. But then a colleague told me I'm sliding from "we can't know" to "there's nothing to know," which sounds like the old verificationist mistake. I still don't quite see the gap though. If two cosmological models are empirically equivilent inside the horizon but one has extra universes outside and the other doesn't, and we'll never observe the difference, why isn't the divergence just a notational choice? Where exactly does the argument that ontology still exists but is hidden break down if identification is genuinely impossible?
