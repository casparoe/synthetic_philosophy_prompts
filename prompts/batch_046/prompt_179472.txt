For a final exam in an advanced undergraduate course on Kant’s moral philosophy, draft one essay question that students can answer in 75 minutes (target answer length: about 800 words). The question must engage Kant’s argument in his response to Benjamin Constant—specifically, the claim that lying is always wrong, even to save an innocent person from a murderer—but it should not be answerable by summarizing that argument. To put it in the weeds, construct a modified “murderer at the door” scenario in which the potential victim has already fled (unknown to the murderer), and present the agent with three distinct response options: (i) an explicit falsehood; (ii) a technically true but pragmatically misleading utterance that exploits the murderer’s false presupposition; and (iii) silence. Require students to determine, using Kant’s distinction between perfect and imperfect duties and applying the Formula of Universal Law to the maxim of each option, whether each response violates the duty of truthfulness; to explain whether the technically true utterance counts as a lie for Kant or as a permissible mental reservation; to assess whether Kant’s “unpredictable consequences” defense of lying survives once the agent knows the friend is safe; and to say whether silence breaches the imperfect duty of beneficence. Provide a model answer of approximately 700 words that reconstructs the universalizability of each maxim, distinguishes falsity from deception, and addresses the interaction between the perfect duty of truthfulness and the imperfect duty to aid others. Then provide a marking scheme out of 20 points built around five must-hit moves, with marks allocated to each and a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks for each. The separation between an A and a B answer should turn on whether the student applies the machinery precisely to the modified case—especially the status of technically true misleading speech—rather than on recalling standard labels about Kant and lying.
