As a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun studying philosophy with any rigor, I am trying to determine whether Buddhist ethics is best interpreted as a form of consequentialism or as something structurally different, given its emphasis on cetanā (intentional mental state) rather than on outcome alone. Rather than asking for a bibliography, I want to test my own intuitions through reflective equilibrium on a set of concrete cases.

Here are my present verdicts. Case one: a surgeon operates with flawless technique and genuinely compassionate intent, yet the patient dies from an unforeseeable genetic reaction; I judge the surgeon’s act to have been morally sound. Case two: a surgeon operates negligently, yet the patient survives and thrives because the negligence accidentally corrects a hidden condition; I judge the act to have been morally bad. Case three: a climate researcher deliberately suppresses a minor data irregularity in order to prevent public panic, and this succeeds in preserving funding for mitigation policies that save thousands of lives; I judge the suppression wrong despite the beneficial result. Case four: the same researcher publishes her raw data with full transparency, but policymakers misinterpret it and enact harmful regulations; I judge her act right despite the harmful downstream effects. Case five: a donor gives anonymously to a medical charity out of pure compassion, but the charity’s administrator embezzles the funds, so no lives are saved; I still judge the donation meritorious. Case six: another donor gives to the same charity solely to enhance her public reputation, yet the funds are used properly and thousands are saved; I judge this donation to be significantly less meritorious, perhaps not meritorious at all. Case seven: a parent tells a young child that a deceased goldfish has been released into a large pond rather than explaining mortality; the child never learns the truth and avoids lasting trauma, yet I keep reversing my judgment—sometimes it seems permissible, sometimes a failure of respect. Case eight: a physician administers a life-saving drug to an unconscious patient without consent; I judge this not fine. Case nine: a physician withholds a terminal diagnosis from a conscious patient who had previously and clearly requested not to be told, allowing the patient to live out his remaining months in peace; I judge this fine.

I have been attempting to formulate a principle that captures these judgments, tentatively something like: the moral quality of an act is fixed primarily by the purity of its root intention together with the agent’s epistemic diligence, while outcomes alter the evaluation only when they fall within the agent’s foreseeable sphere of influence, and outcomes that are mediated by conditions outside that sphere—what one might call conditionally ripened results (upakāra) rather than direct fruits (phala)—should not reverse the evaluation of the act itself. But this formulation struggles with cases eight and nine, since both involve withholding information or acting without present consent, yet I want opposite verdicts. Does any precise principle—perhaps one that distinguishes respect for a prior autonomous directive from unilateral paternalism—preserve all nine verdicts, or must one be sacrificed for simplicity? Specifically, what does your best candidate principle say about case seven, and about an unconsidered case: a physician administers a standard vaccine with perfect procedure and benevolent intent, but an unknown interaction with a rare genetic variant causes harm in exactly one patient?

Please state the principle or small set of candidate principles precisely, identify which of my verdicts each preserves and which it overturns, apply it to case seven and to the vaccine-interaction case, and indicate whether saving every verdict requires an undesirable complexity.
