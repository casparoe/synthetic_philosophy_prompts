I'm putting together the final exam for my upper-level seminar on 19th-century moral phil, and I need one essay prompt that gets students into the weeds of Bentham's sexual morality without letting them just recite the standard line that he was an early utilitarian defender of gay rights. The class is mostly juniors and seniors who've read excerpts from the Introduction and some secondary commentary, but they haven't dug into the manuscript history, so I want the question to hinge on reasoning they can do with the tools they've already got.

Here's what I'm after: present a hypothetical legislature debating whether to decriminalize consensual non-procreative acts between adults. Ask the students to reconstruct Bentham's core argument—that these acts produce pleasure without assignable harm to non-consenting parties—then force them to wrestle with a tension inside his own felicific calculus. Specifically, they need to deal with the fecundity dimension (that good pleasure should tend to produce further pleasure) and the fact that these acts don't generate offspring, plus they have to address whether widespread social disgust can be dismissed as irrational prejudice without smuggling in a non-utilitarian distinction between "authentic" and "corrupt" pleasures. I want them to decide whether a rigorous application of the calculus actually supports decriminalization or whether it collapses once disgust-based disutility and long-term social-stability costs are entered on the ledger. Can you draft the exam question, then a model answer, plus a marking scheme? The scheme should have five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how candidates usually botch it. The whole thing should be doable in about 90 minutes and shouldn't ask for citations, bibliographies, or anything they couldn't answer from memory and reasoning alone. Oh, and please don't lean on obscure manuscript titles—just keep it to the conceptual machinery they actually have.
