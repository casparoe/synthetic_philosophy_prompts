I am working through a passage from David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, specifically Book III, Part I, Section I, in which he observes that moral authors proceed for some time in the ordinary manner of reasoning—establishing the being of God, describing human affairs, and linking propositions involving is and is not—before suddenly, and without explanation, introducing propositions involving ought or ought not. Hume expresses surprise at this imperceptible change and insists that the new relation or affirmation requires observation and explanation, asking how such a normative relation can be deduced from premises that are entirely different in kind.

I am trying to understand whether Hume is identifying what I will call a modal entailment gap: a failure of deductive logic in which normative conclusions lack the modal status to follow from purely descriptive premises. Alternatively, he may be identifying a directive speech-act rupture: an unmarked shift in the type of linguistic act being performed, from describing states of affairs to issuing prescriptions, without any explicit justification for the change in force.

Could you help me determine which of these two readings—modal entailment gap or directive speech-act rupture—is more faithful to Hume's text? Does Hume anticipate Moore's charge that a natural property is being illicitly identified with the non-natural property of goodness, or is Hume's concern structurally different—perhaps focused on the inferential architecture of moral reasoning rather than on definitional confusion?
