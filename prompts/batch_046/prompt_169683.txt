I'm delivering the opening remarks at the inaugural session of an interdisciplinary workshop my department is hosting—titled Accountability and Catastrophe—and I need the speech to establish the conceptual framework rather than merely welcome people. The audience will include political philosophers, economic historians, development economists, and first-year graduate students who may never have encountered Amartya Sen's entitlement argument in any detail. I want the talk to feel like an occasion, not a syllabus, and I want the philosophical claim to shape what I can and cannot say: specifically, I must avoid any language that treats famine as a meteorological event with human casualties, since that vocabulary is precisely what the workshop aims to dismantle.

This has become personally urgent over the past year. While organizing my late grandmother's estate, I discovered her unpublished field notes from 1959, written during her work with resettlement efforts in West Bengal in the aftermath of the 1943 famine. What struck me was not her description of hunger itself—she describes administrative denial, the reclassification of starvation deaths as ordinary mortality, and the absence of democratic pressure—precisely the pattern Sen identifies when he argues that famine is rarely a failure of food availability and almost always a failure of entitlement. Reading her notes, I realized she had witnessed a political crime she had no vocabulary to name. I want the remarks to honor that silence without making her the subject of the speech, and I ask you not to quote her notes verbatim; please paraphrase any observations drawn from them in my own voice.

The remarks need to navigate Sen's distinction between availability decline and entitlement collapse, apply it to three specific cases—the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the food crisis during China's Great Leap Forward—without treating them as interchangeable illustrations. They must show why authoritarian information suppression produces a different mechanism of entitlement failure than democratic neglect, and why that distinction matters for assigning responsibility. At the same time, they must avoid jargon that loses the students, avoid any bibliography or citation list, and never invoke inevitable tragedy or natural disaster framing. Please write the remarks—approximately eight to ten minutes, formal but accessible—ending not with a call to action but with a conceptual clarification: what it actually means to classify a famine as a political crime rather than a natural disaster, and how that reclassification alters the structure of moral obligation.
