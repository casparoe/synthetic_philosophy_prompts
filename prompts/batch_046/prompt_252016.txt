Consider the published verdicts of the philosopher Dr. Elena Vasquez on observer-selection puzzles. First, she rejects the inference that cosmological theories predicting vastly larger populations of observers should receive higher credence merely because we exist; she terms this the presumptuous-philosopher error and denies that self-indication reasoning applies across cosmological models lacking a well-defined reference class. Second, she accepts self-sampling updates within a fixed, empirically specified set: if one hundred subjectively identical copies of an agent run an identical trial and only one observes result R, that copy should revise its beliefs about the underlying mechanism. Third, she denies that one's birth rank supports the Doomsday argument, on the grounds that all possible human births across every history fails to constitute a valid sampling frame.

Now consider the Branching-Protocol case. An external mechanism selects either Protocol A, which conducts ten independent trials each containing a single observer, or Protocol B, which conducts one trial containing ten observers in identical subjective states; the selection is unknown to the participants. You are one observer who has just experienced outcome O, and you know that O occurs with different frequencies under Protocol A and Protocol B. Vasquez must advise whether your credence about which protocol was selected ought to depend on whether you are one among several co-observers or a solitary observer.

What should Vasquez advise if she wishes to remain consistent with her earlier verdicts? Identify the closest analog among her three precedents, say whether her rules genuinely underdetermine the case, and if they do, state the principle she would be adopting with each possible ruling. Finally, specify one further case that would distinguish the competing interpretations of her position.
