I'm trying to classify a communicative move I keep encountering in both public argument and private conversation, and I'm unsure whether pragmatics already has a precise name for it or whether it falls between existing categories in Grice's account of speaker meaning.

Consider two instances. In the first, a speaker is pressed on whether they endorse a specific controversial policy. Rather than answering directly, they invoke a sweeping generalization — for example, that no institutional design can eliminate all trade-offs — delivered with clear emotional emphasis on the particular case under discussion. The audience naturally takes this as a reason to reject the specific policy. But if the speaker is challenged, they retreat to the literal content: they only stated a banal structural fact, they say, and never claimed anything about the particular policy. The utterance was useless unless heard as specific, yet its literal form is so universal that it applies to every policy equally.

In the second instance, during a disagreement about personal responsibility, one party says, "We all choose our paths," immediately after the other has described being pressured into a decision by an authority figure. The statement functions in context as an accusation of voluntary complicity. Yet when objected to, the speaker insists they merely expressed a truism about human agency, denies any targeted application, and points out that the sentence contains no reference to the authority figure or the specific decision.

What strikes me is not just ambiguity, but a kind of structural asymmetry: the speaker relies on the audience computing a specific, context-bound message — one that carries the conversational weight — while preserving a literal form so generic that it can be defended as entirely unrelated to that message. This is not irony, since there is no pretense of saying one thing while meaning its opposite. It is not standard cancellation of an implicature, because the speaker does not add a clarifying clause; instead, they appeal to the literal meaning being the only meaning they ever carried. And it is not quite strategic ambiguity or political dog-whistling in the usual sense, because the two interpretations do not map onto separate audiences; rather, the same audience is expected to hold both, using one for interpretation and the other for defense.

Does this pattern have an established name in the literature on implicature and speaker meaning? If it falls under one of Grice's familiar distinctions — say, between what is said and what is implicated, or between exploiting and violating a maxim — I want to know exactly where, and more importantly where the fit breaks down. If it does not fit, I want it carved precisely: what are its necessary conditions, what distinguishes it from nearby phenomena like plausible deniability, equivocation, or the motte-and-bailey, and what should we call it? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know whether the phenomenon is genuinely distinct, what its edges are, and whether Grice's framework of recoverable speaker intention can accommodate a case where the speaker appears to intend both the specific implicature and the ability to deny having intended it.
