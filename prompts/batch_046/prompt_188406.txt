So I've been thinking about pain asymbolia and the whole is-pain-necessarily-bad debate. You know the cases — people who report feeling pain but don't care about it, don't avoid it, sometimes even laugh. The philosophers split into two camps: one says this proves pain's badness is contingent on the affective response, the other says these people just have a broken evalutive module and the pain is still bad for them, just like a silent alarm is still an alarm. But here's the thing: this argument hasn't moved in like fifteeen years, and both sides keep citing the same few clinical reports. My working theory is that it's not really about pain at all, but about whether bad for me can be defined without referance to what I'd actually want to avoid — basically, whether there's a gap between first-order feeling and second-order concern that the badness debate is smuggling in. Is that right? Or is there acutally a hidden empirical question here, like whether asymbolics still have subconscious avoidance learning? And if it's just conceptual, is there any distinction or experiment that could actually break the stalemate, or are we just stuck with bedrock intuitions about suffering?
