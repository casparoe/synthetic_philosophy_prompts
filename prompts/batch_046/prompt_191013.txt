Write a dialogue between two political philosophers, A and B, debating whether Sen's liberal paradox exposes an unavoidable tension between minimal liberal rights and collective welfare. A holds that the paradox is robust: even a minimal commitment to individual liberty over genuinely personal domains can conflict with Pareto-efficient outcomes, and this tension is not resolvable by redefining the domains. B contends that the paradox is an artifact of an overly expansive conception of liberal rights, and that if we restrict protected choices to those that are strictly self-regarding—where the agent's preference does not depend on others' concurrent choices—the contradiction disappears.

Ground their exchange in a concrete institutional design problem: a municipal zoning board grants every resident an absolute veto over any zoning change that technically affects only her parcel, yet every resident privately prefers a coordinated development plan that each individual veto blocks. A should argue that narrowing or eliminating the veto to achieve the collectively preferred outcome abandons liberalism's core protection of personal domains, regardless of interdependence. B should counter that zoning preferences are not genuinely self-regarding because property values, sightlines, and neighborhood character are structurally interdependent, so the veto was never a protected liberal right in the first place; removing it therefore does not violate liberalism but corrects a category error.

Require real back-and-forth: A must address B's interdependence claim directly, asking whether B's criterion for "self-regarding" is drawn independently or tailored precisely to exclude cases that generate the paradox. B must respond to A's charge of ad hocness, explaining how to distinguish legitimate interdependence from spurious dependency without collapsing all personal choice into collective choice. Both should be equally rigorous; neither should be a strawman. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or requests for named sources.
