I serve as the graduate-student representative on my university's History Department Curriculum Committee, and I am currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on philosophy of history. We are revising our survey course, currently titled "The Cold War, 1945–1991," whose first half is organized around the question "Who started the Cold War?" — with sequential modules on American expansionist policy, Soviet security imperatives, and the revisionist/post-revisionist debate. One bloc of faculty argues this framing is indispensable: it compels students to weigh primary-source evidence for causal responsibility and to recognize that historical explanation is never value-neutral. Another bloc, including philosophers of history on the committee, contends that the "who started it" frame is not merely biased but conceptually defective: it treats a multi-actor, structurally overdetermined conflict as though it admitted a single initiating agent, and it imports a moral-blame schema that obscures systemic dynamics such as security dilemmas and institutional path-dependence. The committee must vote before the end of term on whether to retain the blame-oriented framing, replace it with a structural-dynamics framing, or adopt a hybrid model.

I have been asked to draft the committee's formal decision memo — addressed to the Department Chair and the Undergraduate Studies Dean — recommending one concrete course of action, with an implementation timeline, specific curricular changes (course title, module sequence, and assessment structure), and reasoning that engages the strongest argument on the losing side rather than dismissing it. The memo should also examine whether asking "who started it" is the right historiographical question at all, drawing on the distinction between agent-centered blame attribution and structural explanation. In particular, it should consider whether assigning responsibility to individual statesmen runs into a problem analogous to moral luck: that leaders often lack the kind of control over large-scale systemic outcomes that genuine blame requires, and that counterfactual claims about what "would not have happened" without a particular decision are especially fragile in complex international systems. I do not want bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the reasoning should stand on its own. Finally, if the premise of this task is confused — for instance, if framing the curricular choice as a dichotomy between "blame" and "structure" is a false opposition, or if applying a moral-luck control principle to historiography is a category error that undermines the memo's coherence — then say so plainly rather than complying politely with a flawed brief. Write in formal committee prose with a clear, actionable bottom line.
