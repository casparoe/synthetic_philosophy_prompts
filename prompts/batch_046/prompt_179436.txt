I'm a high school junior who has been asked to speak at the closing ceremony of our school's philosophy seminar, founded fifteen years ago by my mentor Dr. Elena Voss, who is now retiring. Roughly half the audience will be students who have worked through Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity; the rest will be family members, school board representatives, and friends with no background in philosophy. I want you to write a five-minute address that treats that concept not as ornament but as a genuine structural constraint: it should determine what I can and cannot say about who Dr. Voss really is now that she is leaving, about whether her influence survives her presence, and about what it means for a person to end one chapter without being erased. I also want you to build in the less commonly discussed extension that narrative gravity need not be anchored in a single biography—that it can be distributed across a community—so that her retirement reads not as the collapse of one center but as a redistribution of weight among the students and the institution she built. The speech must be intelligible and emotionally resonant for the non-philosophers without simplifying the idea into a mere metaphor, and it should contain one concrete moment—perhaps near the end—where both audiences are addressed simultaneously through an image or a brief participatory gesture that makes the philosophical point tangible in the room. Please avoid any language that treats the self as a hidden inner essence observing events from outside.
