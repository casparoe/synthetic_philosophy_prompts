I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar I am leading on practical reason and virtue, and a recurring move in our discussions has begun to trouble me. Several participants have started analogizing Benjamin Franklin's well-known scheme for moral perfection — his catalog of thirteen virtues, the daily self-examination, the incremental tracking of failures in a private ledger — to a reinforcement-learning loop. In this mapping, the nightly audit serves as the reward signal, the virtues function as policy parameters being tuned through repeated exposure, and the entire apparatus is said to anticipate a behaviorist, algorithmic model of character formation. The analogy is deployed specifically to license a reductionist conclusion: that Franklin's view of moral improvement lacks any genuinely normative or reflective dimension, reducing virtue to optimized habit and treating the self as a system to be calibrated rather than an agent to be persuaded.

I want to push my students — and myself — to examine how far this comparison actually holds rather than letting it do argumentative work by stealth. Could you lay out the explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the agent, the reward function, the environment, the training epoch, and the convergence criterion — and then identify the points of disanalogy that matter for the intended philosophical conclusion? In particular, I am interested in whether the analogy quietly imports features of modern algorithmic optimization that Franklin's eighteenth-century framework lacks, for instance a separation between designer and system, or an absence of first-person normative judgment, and whether those imported features are essential to the reductionist verdict. Please also say whether a better analogy might be available — perhaps to craft apprenticeship, to physical conditioning, or to financial accounting — and whether the conclusion that Franklin's ethics is merely behaviorist survives once the mapping is audited rather than assumed.

Finally, I ask that your response be self-critical about its own reasoning: indicate which step in your analysis is most vulnerable to error, and specify what argument or textual consideration about Franklin's actual practice — for example, a closer reading of how he describes the role of reason in selecting virtues — would cause you to revise your verdict about whether the analogy succeeds or collapses.
