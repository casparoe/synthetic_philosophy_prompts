I recently lost an uncle to respiratory illness, and in settling his estate I discovered that he had quietly funded climate litigation for a decade while continuing to fly to family gatherings without apology; the tension has made the problem of individual efficacy suddenly personal. At the memorial dinner, two cousins of mine—call them R and S—fell into a sustained disagreement about whether personal choices like forgoing air travel or industrial meat can be morally obligatory when each choice is causally negligible inside massive collective systems. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander with no settled view, and I ask you to adjudicate it move by move rather than simply declare a winner.

R began by pressing the inefficacy objection: in climate change and factory farming, individual contributions are swamped by scale. One round-trip flight or one steak is a rounding error in global emissions or slaughter counts, and supply chains do not respond discretely to my absence but aggregate over millions of actors. The standard comparison is voting—my ballot almost never decides an election—yet there an institutional aggregation mechanism and a civic duty of democratic participation justify the act. For climate and meat, R argued, no analogous aggregation procedure exists, so the voting analogy fails and there is no moral reason to change behavior beyond personal preference. S replied that R ignores both threshold effects and structural complicity: in some supply chains orders are filled in discrete truckloads, so if aggregate demand hovers near a threshold, one purchase can probabilistically tip production of an additional unit; more fundamentally, purchasing expresses endorsement of a harmful practice and integrates the buyer into it, regardless of discrete causation. S added that the voting analogy actually supports broad abstention, since democratic legitimacy requires widespread participation, and similarly reducing collective harm requires widespread reduction—my act belongs to the pattern that, if universalized, eliminates the harm. R countered that S smuggles universalization in through the back door: universalization arguments work for coordination problems, not for harms that are structurally overdetermined regardless of my participation. R also noted that S’s batching claim is speculative without evidence about actual supply logistics, and that the complicity move proves too much—paying taxes in a state that funds harmful wars makes one complicit, yet tax refusal is not generally obligatory—so the expressive value of abstaining belongs to personal virtue, not to strict moral obligation, unless S can identify a specific wrong done. S then refined their position: the tax comparison is inapt because taxation is coerced and non-optional, whereas meat and flight are voluntary market choices with available alternatives. But S conceded that universalization alone does not ground obligation here, and proposed instead what they called modal contribution—moral evaluation should attend not only to actual-world counterfactuals but to whether my choice is part of the causal basis for harm reduction across the nearest possible worlds in which people like me abstain. This, S claimed, avoids overdetermination without requiring discrete batching. R responded that modal contribution sounds sophisticated but collapses back into actual-world inefficacy unless S can supply a principled rule for selecting which modal worlds matter—say, one tied to reasonable expectation or institutional role—without which S is merely relabeling the intuition that one should feel bad. I do not deny that intuition, R concluded, but I deny that it grounds a specific duty; without either a direct causal link or a legitimate collective decision procedure that assigns me a role, the obligation is at best aspirational.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: rate each of the four turns from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating, and then say who is more right overall and what both parties are missing—whether that is a distinction between obligations of justice and obligations of virtue, the role of institutional design in mediating individual efficacy, or something else that neither has introduced.
