As a novelist constructing a character with a complete callosal section, I have been mapping my own intuitions about the unity of consciousness across a series of concrete scenarios—some drawn from clinical descriptions, others invented to test the edges of my imagination. I want to know whether there is a single principle behind my verdicts, which ones must be abandoned if simplicity demands it, and what that principle predicts for a case I have not considered.

Here are my judgments.

First, the classic divided-field case: the left hemisphere, controlling the right visual field and speech, names an object shown to the right; the right hemisphere, controlling the left hand and left visual field, selects the corresponding object with the left hand, yet the speaking left hemisphere confabulates an explanation for the left hand's movement. I judge that there are two genuinely distinct streams of conscious awareness, not one consciousness with a blind spot.

Second, an emotional-integration case: a threatening image is presented solely to the right hemisphere, producing measurable autonomic arousal, and the left hemisphere reports feeling suddenly uneasy without being able to identify the cause. I judge that emotional consciousness remains unified across the hemispheres even when cognitive awareness is divided.

Third, a partial-section case—only the splenium severed, leaving anterior pathways intact—in which the patient describes herself as having one mood and one will, but two separate vocabularies for describing her experience. I judge that unity is not monolithic; it can be decomposed into distinct functional dimensions.

Fourth, a confabulation case: the right hemisphere directs the left hand to pick up a specific tool, and the left hemisphere, unaware of the directive, sincerely reports that it chose the tool in order to complete a task it had been planning. I judge that this narrative fabrication creates only the appearance of unity; the underlying awareness is still bifurcated.

Fifth, a volitional-gate case—purely hypothetical for my narrative—where both hands are engaged in separate drawing tasks, yet both stop instantaneously at an auditory cue and neither resumes until a single verbal command is given. I judge that there remains one volitional center capable of governing both hemispheres simultaneously, even if cognitive content is split.

These verdicts seem to pull in different directions. The first and fourth suggest deep division masked by narrative illusion; the second and fifth suggest genuine unity in affect and will; the third suggests that both can be true in different registers. Is there a coherent principle that accommodates all five? If not, which verdict should I overturn—the division in case one, the unity in case five, the decomposition in case three, or the illusion-reading in case four? And whatever principle survives, what should it say about a sixth case: a split-brain patient who, when each hemisphere is separately asked to rate her own happiness on a scale from one to ten, gives different answers, yet both answers shift in the same direction after a shared pleasant experience?
