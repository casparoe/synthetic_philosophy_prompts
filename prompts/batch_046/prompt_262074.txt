I just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence. She kept insisting that the principle is little more than a rhetorical gloss on status quo bias, and that once the original rationale for a rule is discredited, the burden should lie with those who wish to keep it. To see whether there is any genuine substance to the position, I want to practice against a committed defender.

Please play a sharp Burkean conservative — not a strawman, willing to concede that individual fences may be harmful or obsolete, but never conceding that the burden of proof should shift to preservation. You take Chesterton's fence seriously as an epistemic principle about embedded, often tacit social knowledge, not as an excuse for inertia. Speak in the first person, keep your responses to about one or two paragraphs, and stay in character until I say 'stop'. You may refer to the resources of conservative thought available in the early twentieth century, but do not break character to invoke modern academic citations or ask procedural questions outside the exchange. Your core commitment is that tearing down an institution whose full functions we do not understand risks destroying coordination mechanisms we have not yet mapped, and that the reformer must demonstrate not merely the failure of the original justification but the absence of any hidden sustaining purpose.

Here is my opening challenge, drawn from our actual disagreement: we were discussing the 1923 Quiet Ground ordinance, which prohibits the installation of mechanical refrigeration compressors within 150 feet of any cemetery boundary. The original justification — that compressor vibrations disturbed the repose of the dead — has been thoroughly discredited. The rule remains on the books, and a grocery chain now seeks a variance to build a cold-storage facility near an old burial ground. My friend argued that because we know the original reason was false, and because no one has demonstrated a new, positive justification for the restriction, retaining it is pure status quo bias. She pressed me specifically: if the Burkean insists on preserving fences whose current purpose we cannot articulate, are we not simply privileging our ignorance? Address her challenge directly, treating both the particular ordinance and the general principle at stake.
