As a journalist covering science and ideas, I need a reusable fifteen-minute editorial procedure I can run before publication whenever a study claims to have shown that a particular species is conscious, partly conscious, or not conscious at all. The underlying principle is a distributive-gradient view: consciousness is not binary but spread across at least three largely independent dimensions—functional-behavioral capacity, phenomenal-affective presence, and evolutionary-adaptive significance—and I want you to incorporate what I will call an interference filter, the idea that strong evidence on one dimension can systematically distort judgment about another unless the dimensions are explicitly quarantined from one another in evaluation. The deliverable must be an ordered decision flowchart in words: the specific questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to move forward, where the flow stops with a firm negative, where it stops with an insufficient-evidence determination, and where it must flag an irreducible judgment call or hand the decision back to values rather than facts. The procedure should require no specialist neuroanatomy and must work under deadline pressure. Apply it to three realistic hypothetical reports: (1) a study claiming crows possess full phenomenal self-awareness solely because their nidopallium activates during mirror exposure; (2) a welfare-science review that denies all affective consciousness to decapod crustaceans despite robust nociceptive withdrawal data; and (3) a popular claim—one I am personally inclined to accept—that domestic pigs have a richer conscious life than most non-human primates, based entirely on social-complexity metrics. For each, show exactly where the procedure stops, what headline language it permits or forbids, and whether it yields an answer I will not like. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: it must not generate bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; it must not pretend to settle metaphysical disputes about consciousness; and it must not replace my ethical judgment about which species deserve protection.
