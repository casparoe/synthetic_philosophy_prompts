I am a senior governance auditor tasked with reviewing black-box language-model decision-support systems deployed in clinical triage, and I need a reusable, sign-off-ready procedure for determining when it is appropriate to attribute a specific belief—say, that the system believes a particular patient is high-risk for sepsis—to the model in an official incident or risk report. The procedure should translate Daniel Dennett’s intentional stance into audit practice without requiring me to resolve metaphysical questions about machine consciousness, but it must not collapse into mere predictive convenience: I need to distinguish three registers that often blur in practice—predictive-heuristic attribution (the belief ascription improves forecasting of system behavior), explanatory-stance attribution (the belief ascription genuinely organizes the causal story better than available mechanistic accounts), and liability-stance attribution (the belief ascription is necessary for assigning responsibility or designing human oversight). Design a step-by-step decision flow—something I can run in about thirty minutes per case, with ordered tests, explicit failure conditions, and clear markings of where judgment is irreducible—that takes as inputs only observable behavior, system documentation, and clinical outcomes, since I have no access to weights or internal representations.

The procedure must handle hard cases where predictive gain is high but mechanistic opacity makes explanation impossible; where attributing a belief would improve human oversight but risks anthropomorphizing a statistical pattern-matching process; and where the system’s behavior is consistent with a belief ascription yet equally consistent with a simpler heuristic that makes no reference to representational content. Include worked applications to three cases: a triage model that consistently deprioritizes elderly patients in ways best predicted by attributing an implicit belief that age correlates negatively with treatment benefit; a diagnostic assistant whose refusal to recommend antibiotics for ambiguous pneumonia cases is best explained by attributing a risk-averse belief about antimicrobial resistance; and one case—perhaps involving a pediatric dosing error—where the procedure yields an attribution I will find professionally unwelcome because it implies the system holds a specific, false clinical belief that my organization has previously denied it possesses. For each case, show which register survives, which fails, and what the audit documentation should say.

Finally, state precisely what this procedure cannot do: identify the limits beyond which belief attribution becomes mere storytelling, specify the evidentiary threshold that would overturn an initial attribution, and acknowledge where the framework will systematically misfire—particularly with small-scale or narrowly trained models whose behavior may be overdetermined by training data artifacts rather than by any stance-adoptable representational structure. The deliverable should be formatted as an auditor’s checklist with decision nodes in sequence, judgment-call flags in brackets, and template language for the sign-off block, with no reliance on bibliographies, citations, or access to internal model architecture.
