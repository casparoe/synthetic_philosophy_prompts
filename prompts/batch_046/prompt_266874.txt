I’m writing a short essay for a graduate seminar on moral obligation, and I’d like precise, critical feedback on its argument rather than stylistic editing. The piece begins from Peter Singer’s well-known drowning-child scenario but quickly moves into territory that I think is underexplored: the interaction between rescue duties and collective-action structures where individual contributions are non-linearly related to outcomes. I construct a modified case—call it the distributed-pond network—in which a large number of children are each in a separate shallow pond, and rescue requires simultaneous action by at least N agents; if fewer than N act, every attempt fails and no child is saved. I argue that in this setting, the individual agent does not have an unconditional duty to jump in, because the action is futile absent coordination. Rather, the duty is conditional: one must contribute if and only if one has reasonable grounds to believe the threshold N is reachable. This, I suggest, introduces a kind of 'threshold-sensitive obligation' that complicates Singer’s claim that physical proximity and direct causation are morally irrelevant—here, the structural conditions of causation seem to modify the obligation itself.

The second half of the essay introduces what I term a 'visibility-bias' constraint on rescue ethics. In Singer’s original pond, the victim is phenomenally present; the rescuer sees the child. In the distributed-pond network, by contrast, no particular victim is present to any agent. I propose that the intuitive force of the rescue duty may depend not merely on the objective fact of suffering but on phenomenal presence—the direct perceptual availability of the victim. If so, then extending the duty to distant, statistically described suffering (famine, climate displacement) requires an auxiliary argument that Singer does not fully provide. The essay concludes that either Singer’s principle must incorporate phenomenal presence as a moderating condition—which weakens its universal reach—or it must supply an independent account of why statistical suffering triggers the same duty despite phenomenal absence. I am especially concerned about two points: first, whether my conditional-duty argument illegitimately imports a consequentialist aggregation that Singer’s deontological framing should reject; and second, whether the visibility-bias claim is genuinely distinct from the standard psychological-distance objection or merely restates it in phenomenological language. Please respond in no more than 250 words, divided into three parts labeled (A), (B), and (C): (A) accuracy of my reconstruction of Singer’s position; (B) soundness of the threshold-sensitive obligation argument; (C) novelty and coherence of the visibility-bias point.
