I'm preparing to defend a thesis at an interdisciplinary seminar on aesthetic cognition: that the paradox of fiction is not a single paradox but a family of confusions produced by treating emotional responses to fiction as if they fall under the same belief-commitment conditions as emotional responses to reality. My specific claim is that fiction-engagement activates what I call "suspended commitment protocols"—cognitive frames in which representational content is processed for affective resonance without being routed through the belief-formation systems that normally trigger action-oriented emotional evaluation—so that feeling genuine fear for a fictional character, knowing she is not real, and lacking the behavioral dispositions of real fear are not contradictory but structurally decoupled by design. The audience includes analytic philosophers of art (several committed to some version of Kendall Walton's make-believe account or Noël Carroll's thought-theory reply), a cognitive psychologist who studies emotional inhibition during narrative immersion, and a phenomenologist skeptical of any mechanistic "protocol" vocabulary. I need a prep sheet for the question period: the objections in the order they will come, phrased as each camp would phrase them; for each, what I should concede up front, what I must hold, where my reply is weakest, and how long I have to make it land. In particular, I need to be ready for the objection I am hoping nobody raises—that if fiction-emotions are genuinely decoupled from belief and action-disposition, then we lose any normative basis for distinguishing appropriate from inappropriate emotional responses to immoral fictions, which threatens not just my framework but the practice of aesthetic criticism itself. Please include that hidden objection, note which feature of my position it targets, and tell me where I am genuinely weakest rather than where I merely sound weakest.
