I have recently begun mentoring first-generation college students through a nonprofit that pairs them with older volunteers, and I have watched the standard character-education curriculum fail repeatedly: modules built on abstract virtues and isolated heroic biographies leave students either unmoved or intimidated, because the exemplars are too perfect and the principles too detached from pressure. Please argue that moral education should revive a specifically comparative, Plutarchan model of biography—not as hagiography, but as what we might call counter-exemplar pairing. By this I mean presenting two lives that faced structurally identical temptations—say, the same institutional pressure to compromise integrity for loyalty or advancement—where one yielded partially and the other resisted, with neither rendered as pure saint or pure villain. Make the case that this paired framing does three things that isolated biography and abstract principle-teaching cannot: it makes moral failure visible as a contingent choice within a shared context rather than an essential character flaw; it blocks the audience from identifying with an unreachable ideal, since both figures remain recognizably flawed; and it trains practical judgment by forcing the learner to compare responses across parallel situations rather than imitate a single model. Address the objection that exposing young people to vicious or compromised lives risks normalizing vice, and defend the claim that normalization is actually prevented by the comparative structure itself—because vice is never presented in isolation, but always held in tension with a parallel act of resistance. Ground the argument in the mechanics of narrative identification and moral learning, and avoid treating the biographical subjects as mere illustrations of pre-existing ethical rules.
