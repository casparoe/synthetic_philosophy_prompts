I'm a student in an advanced political philosophy seminar, and I'm preparing to present a view I'm tentatively calling "fallible anti-partisanship." Before I defend it, I'd like a consistency audit of my commitments. Here is where I stand:

(1) I accept Simone Weil's diagnosis that political parties are essentially machines for manufacturing collective passions and suppressing individual judgment; I think this critique applies to any organized collective that demands ideological loyalty.

(2) I understand democratic legitimacy in roughly Deweyan experimental terms: collective decisions are legitimate insofar as they function as revisable social inquiries into shared problems, not as expressions of a fixed popular will.

(3) I believe that algorithmic recommendation systems operate as what I call "parties without members"—they homogenize information exposure, generate collective emotional alignments, and suppress heterodox inputs, all without requiring explicit ideological commitment from users—so Weil's critique extends to them.

(4) I hold that genuine democratic inquiry requires institutionalized error-correction, which historically has depended on persistent, organized opposition capable of challenging governing assumptions.

(5) I want to propose abolishing traditional parties and replacing them with non-partisan, randomly selected deliberative bodies (sortition), while subjecting algorithmic curation to democratic experimental oversight rather than abolishing it outright.

(6) I deny that organized opposition must take the form of permanent, ideologically cohesive parties; distributed, episodic dissent within deliberative bodies can provide sufficient error-correction.

Which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension, and which only look incompatible? For each real tension, how severe is it, and what is the least costly revision? Is there one revision that resolves the most problems at once? Specifically, does the apparent conflict between (1) and (4)—that Weil's anti-party stance might undermine the institutionalized dissent required by (2)—actually dissolve given (6), or does a deeper problem remain, perhaps involving (3) and (5)?
