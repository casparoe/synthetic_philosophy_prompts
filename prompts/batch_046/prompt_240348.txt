Here are my reflective verdicts on when honoring sunk costs is rational or irrational, and I am trying to determine whether they cohere around a single principle or whether some must be sacrificed.

Case one: A diner, already full, forces herself to finish an expensive, mediocre entrée solely because she has paid for it and leaving food on the plate would waste the money. Verdict: plainly irrational; the expenditure is irrecoverable, and only present and future enjoyment should govern her choice.

Case two: A city council continues funding a bridge project that independent engineers agree will never be completed and will serve no transportation need, reasoning that taxpayers have already spent two hundred million dollars and abandoning it now would waste that investment. Verdict: plainly irrational; past costs are irrelevant to the comparison of future costs and benefits.

Case three: A scholar, three years into a monograph she now regards as fundamentally mistaken and with no prospect of publication, spends another eighteen months finishing it, explaining that if she stops now all those years of work will have been for nothing. Verdict: irrational; finishing cannot retroactively justify the labor already expended.

Case four: A veteran attends every annual reunion of his military unit despite severe emotional distress and no instrumental benefit, saying that the bonds formed and the sacrifices made together obligate him to keep showing up, and that to stop would be to betray what they built—not because the past labor must be redeemed, but because that shared history has made him the kind of person for whom this presence is now constitutive. Verdict: not irrational; there is something genuinely reason-giving here that is not reducible to recovering a lost investment.

Case five: A manufacturing firm keeps an obsolete factory line running at a continuous loss, rejecting modernization because shutting it down would dishonor the generations of workers who built it and erase their legacy. The operation serves no economic, social, or commemorative function beyond being maintained as a monument to past labor. Verdict: irrational, and structurally parallel to case two, yet I want to say it differs from case four and struggle to articulate the distinction without appealing to my own emotional response.

Case six: A musician with chronic tendon damage refuses to abandon an instrument she has practiced for thirty years, insisting that to quit now would render all that practice worthless. Verdict: irrational, though I feel less certain here than in case three, because bodily mastery might seem to create a present identity that is disrupted by quitting rather than merely redeemed.

My tentative thought is that honoring sunk costs is rational only when the past expenditure has constituted a present practical commitment or identity that generates independent, forward-looking reasons—reasons that do not aim at justifying the past investment but flow from who one has become—whereas it is irrational whenever the sole motivation is retrospective redemption of the expenditure itself. But I am unsure whether this principle genuinely distinguishes case four from case five, or whether it forces me to overturn my verdict on case six.

Please provide a precise principle, or a small family of competing principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each principle, state the clause that corresponds to each case, indicate what it predicts about a case I have not listed—say, a parent who continues to read a long, dull bedtime story to a child every night not for the child's present enjoyment but because they have read every page of this book together for three years, and stopping now would break the continuity of their shared ritual—and be explicit about which of my verdicts, especially case four, five, or six, it cannot accommodate without revision. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want an analysis that could be conducted without access to external sources.
