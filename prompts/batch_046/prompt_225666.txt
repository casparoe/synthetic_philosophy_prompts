At a seminar on synthetic epistemology, two participants—Dr. Elena Vasquez and Dr. Marcus Chen—disagreed about whether Jamesian will-to-believe norms or Cliffordian evidentialist norms should govern belief formation in large language models deployed in high-stakes advisory roles.

Vasquez argued that when an LLM must choose between underdetermined but consequential interpretations of ambiguous regulatory text, it faces a genuinely forced, living, and momentous option in James's technical sense: the model either commits to one interpretive framework or stalls, and the choice affects real human decisions. She claimed that if human agents may legitimately believe beyond the evidence in such live options, then synthetic advisory systems—functioning as epistemic surrogates—should be permitted analogous leaps when calibrated confidence is low but action is required, provided the leap serves a vital practical end.

Chen replied that James's exemption applies only to volitional, conscious assent shaped by passion and will, neither of which LLMs possess. He said treating token prediction as belief already commits a category error, and extending James's permission to statistical computation merely anthropomorphizes mechanics. Clifford's universal evidentialism—belief only proportioned to evidence—applies more rigorously to artificial systems precisely because they lack the phenomenological and moral stakes that might excuse human overreach.

Vasquez countered that structural analogues matter more than inner phenomenology: the functional role of committing to an interpretation under uncertainty is identical, and denying synthetic agents Jamesian latitude forces them into a paralytic Cliffordian rigor that harms users who need timely guidance. She added that requiring full evidential closure before output is impossible for open-domain reasoning, so Clifford's norm is operationally incoherent for these systems.

Chen responded that operational difficulty doesn't dissolve ethical obligation; if an LLM cannot meet Clifford's standard, it shouldn't be deployed as an advisory authority rather than being granted a special exemption. He also noted that Vasquez smuggles in anthropomorphism by describing the model's choice as serving a vital practical end—ends belong to designers and users, not to the system itself—so her Jamesian framing collapses back into human accountability, leaving the synthetic agent with no independent normative status.

They asked a colleague to referee and received only confusion. Also say what both are missing that a coherent synthetic epistemology would need to address.
