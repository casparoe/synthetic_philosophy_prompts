I am working through a philosophy of biology seminar and find that discussions of the species problem tend to collapse quickly into advocacy for one concept—biological, phylogenetic, morphological, ecological—without first asking what work a theory of species is actually supposed to do. Before I evaluate particular accounts, I want a rigorous specification of the desiderata: the full list of requirements any adequate theory must satisfy, each anchored to the concrete case or biological result that motivates it. The list should cover the classic tensions—reproductive isolation versus genealogical continuity, morphological stasis versus genetic divergence, the treatment of hybrid zones and ring species such as the Ensatina salamanders, and the status of asexual and parthenogenetic lineages such as the whiptail lizards—but it should also include a less commonly discussed requirement: a theory must explain whether and how species can persist through anagenesis, where a lineage transforms gradually without branching, and whether this forces us to accept vague temporal boundaries for species membership analogous to sorites-style indeterminacy. For each desideratum, please indicate whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or whether it reflects a partisan commitment of one research tradition; flag any pair that you think cannot be jointly satisfied; and state which single requirement, if enforced strictly, eliminates the largest number of existing candidates. Finally, briefly score the biological species concept, the phylogenetic species concept, and the ecological species concept against your list, noting for each whether it fails on a non-negotiable item or only on a negotiable one, and conclude by saying whether the remaining viable accounts converge on a pluralist view or whether one survives intact.
