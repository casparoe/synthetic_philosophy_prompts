I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or animal ethics, but I have been reflecting on welfare issues in aquaculture after a conversation with a colleague who works in marine systems biology. She raised a point that I find both intriguing and unsettling: because decapod crustaceans undergo periodic moulting during which parts of their nervous system are regenerated, some researchers suggest that subjective experience in these animals might be discontinuous across moult cycles. On this view, chronic stress experienced over months might not accumulate into a single, continuous stream of suffering in the way it presumably does for vertebrates such as salmon or trout. I do not know whether this distinction holds up, but it keeps interfering with my intuitions about the following cases, and I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle behind them or whether I must abandon some of my judgements.

Here are my gut verdicts. First, stunning farmed Atlantic salmon with electrical shock immediately before slaughter strikes me as ethically acceptable, provided the insensibility is genuinely instantaneous and irreversible. Second, boiling live lobsters without prior stunning strikes me as clearly wrong, regardless of culinary tradition. Third, maintaining shrimp in high-density recirculating aquaculture systems where they display chronic stress behaviours — cannibalism, reduced feeding, elevated mortality — for the entire grow-out period, and then applying a humane stunning method at harvest, still strikes me as wrong; the endpoint does not seem to redeem the conditions. Fourth, selectively breeding trout for accelerated growth that produces spinal deformities and reduced mobility in a significant minority, thereby shortening the production cycle by several weeks, also strikes me as wrong, though I am genuinely unsure whether it is worse or less bad than the shrimp case. If suffering is primarily about intensity, the deformity case might be more severe; if it is primarily about duration, the shrimp case should be worse. I lean toward judging the shrimp case more seriously, but I cannot articulate why without appealing to something like continuity of consciousness — which brings me back to my colleague's point about moulting.

Is there one principle, or a small family of principles, that captures all four verdicts without excessive complexity? If not, which verdict should I overturn to reach a simpler account? I would also like you to apply the resulting principle to a case I have not listed: using rapid chilling to induce torpor in crabs before processing, knowing that the method occasionally fails and requires a second application, versus using a slower chemical bath that is more reliable but extends handling time. Please state your principle precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, and be explicit about which of my intuitions it cannot accommodate. Finally, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and identify specifically the point at which you are least certain.
