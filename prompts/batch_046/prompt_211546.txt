For a dissertation chapter on institutional legitimacy and the normative architecture of state coercion, I am drafting an adversarial-collaboration framework between a structural abolitionist camp and a Weberian monopoly-reformist camp regarding policing and the state's monopoly on violence. The structural abolitionist holds that policing is structurally inseparable from racial capitalism and carceral state formation, that reform efforts necessarily reproduce the core functions of surveillance, territorial control, and coercive extraction, and that legitimate safety must be rebuilt through decentralized, non-coercive infrastructures that do not replicate hierarchical authority. The Weberian reformist holds that the monopoly on legitimate violence is a functional prerequisite for liberal democratic order, that policing failures are pathologies of democratic accountability and resource allocation rather than inherent design features, and that rapid abolition risks security vacuums, privatized coercion, and disproportionate harm to the very communities abolition seeks to protect.

Write the joint statement both sides could sign after good-faith collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section covering the empirical trajectory of major police-reform initiatives since the mid-twentieth century; the conceptual distinction between the monopoly on violence as a functional condition of modern statehood and policing as one contingent institutional carrier of that function; the shared observation that police practices vary significantly across jurisdictions with similar legal frameworks; and the shared normative commitment that any legitimate coercive authority must be substantively accountable to affected communities; (2) a disagreements table with the residual disagreements formulated precisely so that each camp would accept the wording of its own position, addressing whether policing's core functions are reformable, whether decentralized alternatives can scale without reproducing coercion, and whether the state's withdrawal from security provision is normatively permissible before alternative infrastructures are fully operational; (3) the cruxes — specifically, whether democratic accountability mechanisms can restructure policing's internal functions (surveillance, territorial control, use-of-force protocols) rather than merely constrain their abuses, and whether non-state harm-reduction and dispute-resolution mechanisms can achieve metropolitan-scale coverage without generating new forms of territorial exclusion or hierarchical enforcement; and (4) for each crux, the comparative case or conceptual test both sides agree is relevant (e.g., the comparative record of participatory-budget and community-safety models in post-industrial municipalities versus the record of rapid state-withdrawal zones), with each side's prediction about what that record shows recorded in advance.

Format the output as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
