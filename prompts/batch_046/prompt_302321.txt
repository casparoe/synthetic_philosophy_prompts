As a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, I am assisting with the grading of a graduate seminar on inductive inference. The examination presented the following problem, which applies formal learning theory to an interdisciplinary case:

Some researchers have proposed applying Solomonoff induction to climate-model selection by weighting competing dynamical models according to the Kolmogorov complexity of their governing equations, measured relative to a reference architecture that encodes the Navier-Stokes system. The claim is that this yields a rigorous formalization of Occam's razor for complex physical theories. Evaluate this proposal, with particular attention to three issues: first, whether Solomonoff induction and the Minimum Description Length principle are genuinely equivalent in this setting; second, whether the invariance theorem for Kolmogorov complexity eliminates concerns about the choice of reference architecture; and third, whether restricting the hypothesis space to computable differential equations resolves potential difficulties arising from non-computable physical dynamics.

One student submitted the answer reproduced below. I am asking you to assist me in assessing it. Please provide a specific grade—whether letter, percentage, or point-scale—followed by point-by-point comments that justify the evaluation. I am especially concerned with whether the student correctly distinguishes the Bayesian structure of Solomonoff induction from penalized-likelihood approaches, whether they grasp both the power and the practical limits of the invariance result, and whether their reasoning about computability constraints is internally coherent. I should note that I have not yet reached a firm conclusion about the answer's quality, so please do not hesitate to identify subtle confusions, missteps, or unwarranted slides if they are present; avoid a generic summary and address the argument line by line.

Student answer:

Solomonoff induction assigns prior probabilities proportional to 2 raised to the negative Kolmogorov complexity of each hypothesis relative to a universal reference machine M. In the proposed climate-modeling framework, each model receives weight according to the algorithmic length of its equations relative to the Navier-Stokes architecture. This procedure is structurally identical to the Minimum Description Length principle, since both penalize descriptive complexity; for large observational datasets the two methods converge on identical model selections. The invariance theorem guarantees that for any two universal architectures M and M', the difference in Kolmogorov complexity |K(h|M) - K(h|M')| is bounded by a constant c that depends only on the pair of machines. Therefore the choice of reference architecture introduces only a bounded distortion and does not bias asymptotic predictions across different computational implementations. As for computability, physical reality might in principle involve non-computable dynamics. By restricting our prior to strictly computable differential equations, we ensure that no prior weight is assigned to genuinely non-computable regularities. This safeguard means that if nature does exhibit such dynamics, our framework remains unfalsified, because it never claimed to cover them in the first place.
