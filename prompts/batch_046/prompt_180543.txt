I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy working on value incommensurability, and I'm trying to map a conceptual tension in lexical threshold negative utilitarianism that the standard literature tends to pass over quickly. The view holds that suffering has lexical priority over pleasure—no quantity of pleasure can outweigh any non-negligible suffering—but it introduces a threshold parameter, call it θ, below which suffering is phenomenologically negligible and can be traded against pleasure within an ordinary cardinal framework, while above θ suffering triggers absolute lexical dominance. My worry is that justifying the location of θ seems to presuppose the very commensurability the view is meant to deny. If θ is fixed as the point where pain becomes structurally disruptive to practical reasoning—disrupting preference coherence or deliberative continuity—then measuring that disruption requires comparing the cost of pain against the benefit of pleasure within a unified metric of deliberative efficiency, which looks like cardinal commensurability rather than lexical separation. If, on the other hand, θ is stipulated as a bare normative boundary with no comparative grounding, it risks being ad hoc.

Please explain whether this tension is genuinely resolvable or only apparent. Specifically, can the distinction between cardinal incommensurability (no shared quantitative scale) and ordinal lexicality (ranking without quantitative comparison) do real defensive work here? Or does introducing any threshold parameter necessarily collapse lexical negative utilitarianism back into a weighted cardinal view with an arbitrarily steep suffering-weight? I'm particularly interested in whether there is a phenomenologically grounded way to maintain that pleasure and pain remain incommensurable above θ without implicitly relying on a common currency—say, a "disruption-cost"—to set the boundary in the first place. I don't need bibliographic references, citations, or a reading list; I'm looking for a conceptual analysis I can evaluate on its own terms.
