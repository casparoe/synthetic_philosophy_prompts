I have been trying to map the logical structure of the socialist calculation debate, and I keep returning to what seems like a hidden symmetry. Mises argued that rational economic calculation requires market prices formed through exchange on private property; Lange responded with a trial-and-error procedure in which a central board sets prices, observes shortages and surpluses, and adjusts; Hayek then objected that central planning cannot access the dispersed, tacit knowledge embedded in local circumstances. But if we reinterpret Lange's procedure as a genuinely distributed algorithm—one in which local managers, not a central computer, perform the iterative adjustments using contextual knowledge that is never fully articulated upward—then the mechanism appears structurally parallel to Hayek's market coordination. Both systems, on this reading, rely on local agents processing information that resists aggregation. Given this, why is Hayek's critique still regarded as a decisive, independent blow to socialist planning rather than as a redescription of Lange's own mechanism? Is the residual difference purely motivational—namely, the absence of private-property incentives—or is there an epistemic gap, perhaps involving the entrepreneurial discovery of new production possibilities rather than the mere correction of parameter mismatches, that the distributed-algorithm interpretation of Lange necessarily fails to capture?
