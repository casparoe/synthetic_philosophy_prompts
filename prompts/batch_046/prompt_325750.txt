I have been revisiting the relationship between deterministic chaos and metaphysical determinism in my graduate metaphysics seminar, and I want to frame next week's discussion around a misconception that keeps resurfacing among my students. They observe that in chaotic dynamical systems—where Lyapunov exponents are positive—any observer embedded within the system cannot, even in principle, predict future states beyond a short horizon due to the exponential amplification of initial measurement errors. From this, several students have concluded that chaos introduces a genuine form of ontological indeterminacy, not merely practical unpredictability, and that Laplace's demon fails not just because of epistemic limits but because determinism itself is incompatible with embedded observation. One particularly sharp student pressed the point further by invoking a heuristic argument: she claimed that if we apply the standard presumption of independence between an observer's predictive apparatus and the system's microstates, then any deterministic account must treat the divergence of trajectories as causally open, which she takes to collapse determinism into a purely formal claim with no empirical content.

I want to address this head-on, but I am struggling to pinpoint the precise confusion. Is the error primarily a category mistake—conflating epistemic inaccessibility with ontological openness—or does it involve a misapplication of the presumption of independence to deterministic dynamics? More specifically, if we reject the idea that embedded observation introduces a novel form of predictive indeterminacy that is neither quantum nor merely practical, what exactly is wrong with reasoning from heuristic independence to the failure of determinism? And how should I structure the rebuttal so that it does not simply assert that determinism concerns the equations rather than our predictions, but actually explains why the heuristic move is illegitimate in this domain?
