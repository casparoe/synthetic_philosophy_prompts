As a journalist covering the sociology of knowledge, I have been trying to formalize my own judgments about when collective belief-formation counts as rational herding and when it collapses into a pathological information cascade. Here are my verdicts on specific scenarios. Diners who join a long queue for one restaurant rather than entering an empty neighboring venue seem rational: they treat others’ revealed preferences as noisy signals about quality. Social-media users who share a viral headline they have not read, purely because it is trending, seem caught in a cascade; they are not updating beliefs but mimicking visibility. Junior researchers who adopt an elaborate statistical correction after seeing it in high-impact journals, despite privately finding it overly conservative, seem rationally deferring to expertise—provided the correction was originally justified by data rather than by popularity alone. Investors who purchase an asset they privately believe is overvalued, expecting only that others will keep buying, are in an irrational cascade: their action directly contradicts their own belief. Here is where I hesitate: a small community of climate modelers gradually abandons a legacy parameterization not because any new study refutes it, but because funding agencies now require citation of the newer framework; back-tests actually show the legacy model predicting extreme events more accurately, yet every grant proposal must align with the institutional standard. Is this rational herding—deferring to aggregated institutional expertise—or a suppressive cascade that penalizes dissent?

What principle, if any, captures these distinctions? Does the dividing line depend on whether agents hold contradictory private evidence, on whether the initial adopters possessed domain-specific expertise, or on whether reversing the collective choice would be costless? Apply your best principle to a case I have not listed: a group of historians gradually rejects an established chronology of Bronze Age trade routes after one well-funded museum exhibition popularizes a revised timeline, even though the primary-source specialists who study the tablets remain skeptical and no new excavation supports the change. Would your principle classify this convergence as rational or pathological?
