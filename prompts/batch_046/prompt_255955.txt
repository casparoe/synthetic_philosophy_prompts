I'm drafting a position paper for an undergraduate philosophy-of-history seminar in which I argue that the standard tripartite division—"the Middle Ages were dark, the Renaissance rebirthed antiquity, and the Scientific Revolution turned on the lights"—does more rhetorical work than historiographical work. A classmate used that exact line to dismiss my observation that medieval Islamic and late-Scholastic mechanics contained sophisticated empirical practices, insisting that "everyone knows" these periods are categorically different in kind. Rather than debating chronology point by point, I want to dissect the slogan itself.

Can you lay out, as a menu of distinct precise theses, the different claims this one-liner could actually be expressing? For each reading, specify whether it asserts (i) an institutional rupture versus continuity in universities, patronage, or textual transmission; (ii) a shift in epistemic norms, methodological self-consciousness, or the social organization of knowledge; (iii) a retrospective labeling convention invented or hardened by later historians for pedagogical convenience; or (iv) an implicit normative ranking that treats "light" as intellectual virtue and "darkness" as absence. Indicate which versions are near-truisms, which are substantive but defensible, which are empirically contestable, and which are Whiggish myths; then say which reading my classmate is most likely invoking to dismiss pre-modern science, and whether that specific reading actually licenses his dismissal. I am particularly interested in how the slogan treats the "negative space" between these periods—what it renders invisible—so address whether any defensible version can survive once we account for cross-cultural transmission and gradual methodological change rather than epochal rebirth.

Please also be explicit about where your reasoning is weakest: tell me what specific counter-example, conceptual distinction, or recent historiographical finding would most likely cause you to revise your verdict about which of these readings remain defensible, and what evidence would convince you that the triadic schema captures something real that a continuity thesis misses.
