At a seminar dinner on revolutionary history, two participants—Elena and Marcus—fell into a sustained dispute about whether the French Revolution “devoured its children” and, more broadly, how to explain the Terror. Elena argued that the trope is essentially a Thermidorian moral fable: after 9 Thermidor, moderates needed to justify Robespierre’s overthrow without admitting a reactionary coup against popular democracy. By framing Robespierre, Danton, and even the Girondins as offspring of a single revolutionary family turned cannibalistic, they transformed a political conflict into an internal family tragedy. Elena insisted that the purges were not “vertical” (a rising proletariat consuming bourgeois parents) but “horizontal”: lawyers, journalists, and minor officials within the same petty-bourgeois stratum destroying one another over administrative control and wartime policy. Because there was never a unified revolutionary subject, the family metaphor obscures more than it reveals.

Marcus replied that Elena’s horizontal-devouring framework ignores the structural corridor within which these conflicts operated. Even if Committee members shared social origins, the revolutionary state faced an irreducible choice: either enforce the Maximum and requisition grain to supply Paris and the armies, or concede to market freedom and risk sans-culotte insurrection and military collapse. The sequential elimination of the Hébertists, then Danton’s Indulgents, and finally Robespierre was not random factional housekeeping. Each step responded to the pressure of aligning state power with the material demands of the urban popular classes. Marcus granted that contingency mattered—Robespierre’s rigidity, the military situation in 1794—but maintained it only operated inside this corridor; without class-struggle dynamics, the disputes would have remained parliamentary rather than lethal.

Elena countered that Marcus’s corridor is unfalsifiable. If Robespierre had survived and moderated, Marcus would call that the corridor widening; if he had pushed left and fallen to the sections, Marcus would say the corridor had snapped. She proposed that ideology and contingency do the real work: the Committee’s Rousseauist commitment to an indivisible general will made dissent appear not as disagreement but as criminal corruption. Once that ideological frame combined with wartime paranoia, lethal purges followed regardless of precise class position. She conceded that class interests shaped who was available to scapegoat—wealthy Girondins were easier targets than Paris artisans—but held that the mechanism of elimination was ideological, not structural.

Marcus answered that ideology alone cannot explain the sequence. If the general will were the sole driver, why were the ultra-radical Hébertists executed in March 1794, months before the moderate Indulgents in April? The order makes sense only if the Committee was navigating between popular insurrection from below and bourgeois restoration from above—exactly the class-struggle corridor. He added that Elena herself smuggles in contingency by appealing to “wartime paranoia” and Robespierre’s personality, neither of which is a class variable.

They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: assess Elena’s claim about the Thermidorian myth, her horizontal-versus-vertical distinction, and her critique of the corridor; then assess Marcus’s defense of the corridor, his sequencing argument about the Hébertists and Indulgents, and his charge that Elena relies on hidden contingency. Finally, say who is more right overall about whether the Revolution devoured its children, and identify what both parties are missing—perhaps regarding institutional design, such as the Law of 22 Prairial and the composition of the Revolutionary Tribunal’s jury, or regarding the specific social background of the representatives-on-mission who implemented the Terror in the provinces. Do not provide citations or reading lists; simply deliver the adjudication on the arguments as made.
