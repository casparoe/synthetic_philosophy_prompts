I recently helped draft consent forms for a small participatory research project and realized how often a signed document satisfies institutional rules while failing to capture genuine authorization. For a philosophy seminar of about 14 students (90 minutes), design a classroom game—structured roles, hidden information, and a simple payoff scheme—around what makes consent valid. The scenario should involve groups evaluating simulated agreements where two of the three dimensions—informational disclosure, absence of coercion, and decision-making competence—are visibly satisfied, but hidden cards reveal that the third is undermined in a non-obvious way (for example, a disclosure that is technically complete yet structured to induce a predictable misunderstanding, or a choice that is uncoerced in form but made under an unrecognized dependency that compromises competence). Arrange the rules so that participants repeatedly approve agreements, then discover retrospectively that they have authorized something defective; only after this experience should the facilitator name deception, coercion, and competence failures. Include a timeline, roles, materials, the debrief mapping each hidden failure to the corresponding philosophical condition, an honest note on which feature of consent theory the activity cannot reproduce, and a prediction of what will go wrong on the first run. Please say how confident you are in the design’s ability to make these failures feel live rather than merely theoretical, and identify the specific point—probably about hidden competence deficits—where your confidence is lowest.
