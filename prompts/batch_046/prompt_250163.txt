I'm working through an argument I constructed for a seminar on moral responsibility in organizational design, and I can't locate the slip that makes the conclusion feel wrong. The argument runs like this. (1) In Frankfurt's original cases, an agent is responsible for acting well even though a hidden controller would have forced the action if the agent had wavered; since the controller never intervenes, the absence of an alternative possibility doesn't excuse the agent. (2) Now consider what I will call a distributed Frankfurt architecture: a large institution in which every role is embedded in layered oversight—automated checks, peer review, and hierarchical veto—such that any individual deviation from protocol would be corrected before it affected the outcome, yet these mechanisms never activate because agents always comply. (3) For any single agent in this architecture, there is no available alternative course of action that would actually reach implementation; every path to a different result is blocked by a dormant safeguard. (4) By Frankfurt's reasoning, because these safeguards do not cause the actual choices, each agent remains fully responsible for her specific contribution. (5) But then aggregate across the institution: since every agent lacks an implemented alternative, the collective also lacks any implemented alternative to the institutional outcome. (6) Nevertheless, we routinely invoke systemic failure or structural incentives to partially excuse individuals within badly functioning institutions, treating the system itself as bearing some of the responsibility. (7) If Frankfurt's rejection of the principle of alternative possibilities is correct, such systemic exculpation is conceptually incoherent—either the safeguards cause the outcomes (in which case no one is responsible) or they don't (in which case everyone is fully responsible), with no middle ground for distributed blame. (8) Therefore, accepting Frankfurt's view either eliminates the concept of institutional or systemic responsibility altogether, or forces us to say that well-designed institutions make every member maximally responsible for every organizational result, including those produced by safeguards they never triggered. This seems to prove too much. Which step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I would especially like to keep (1) and (6), but I am open to paying elsewhere if that is where the error lies.
