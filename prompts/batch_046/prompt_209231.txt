I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of science, and last week we worked through Milton Friedman's well-known methodological defense of unrealistic assumptions—the argument that false premises in a model are acceptable, even desirable, so long as the model's predictions are accurate. The discussion stayed largely within economics and forecasting, and I want to push the framework into a domain where models are used not merely to predict but to design urgent interventions.

Consider the standard compartmental SIR framework—susceptible, infected, recovered—and its widespread deployment by governments during the first half of 2020. The baseline versions of this model assume homogeneous mixing across populations, uniform infection rates, and the absence of any network structure or superspreading dynamics; all of these assumptions are manifestly false in ways that epidemiologists recognized immediately. Nevertheless, variants of the model yielded estimates of the reproduction number R0, projections of peak hospital demand, and assessments of how social-distancing measures might flatten the curve—outputs that directly shaped lockdown policies.

Analyze this episode through the lens of Friedman's F-twist. First, map the model with precision: state the specific unrealistic structural assumptions, identify the predictions they actually generated (about epidemic thresholds, peak timing, and the linear scaling of transmission with contact reduction), and distinguish those predictions that held approximately true from those that failed—particularly the failures related to superspreading events, heterogeneous contact networks, and rapid behavioral adaptation that altered transmission patterns. Be disciplined about the mapping: do not treat every predictive miss as automatically damaging to Friedman's defense.

Second, run the analysis in the other direction. Ask whether policymakers treated the SIR framework as Friedman recommends—as an instrumental black box whose false premises are harmless if the outputs are sound—or whether they implicitly treated the homogeneous-mixing assumption as a structural description of social reality. Specifically, examine whether the unrealistic assumptions corrupted the intervention design not by yielding inaccurate numbers, but by framing the problem in a way that made uniform, population-wide restrictions seem naturally appropriate and made targeted strategies—such as protecting high-contact nodes or shielding specific network clusters—conceptually invisible. 

Finally, tell me what this case reveals about the limits of the F-twist. Does the episode show that Friedman needs a supplementary condition—perhaps that unrealistic assumptions must be causally inert with respect to the mechanisms through which policy actually operates—or is this simply an anomalous case where a forecasting tool was misapplied? I want an honest account of where the theory illuminates the episode, where it is silent, and whether the episode forces a revision of the theory itself.
