I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on transcendental arguments in political philosophy, and I've reconstructed a version of the particularist challenge that seems to collapse both sides of the debate into contradiction. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Any normative judgment—say, that compatriots have special moral claims—must be made from within a practical standpoint that renders the judgment intelligible as a reason for action. (2) A practical standpoint is not merely a set of beliefs but a framework of mutual recognition: it fixes whom I can address as responsible and whom I can recognize as making valid claims on me. (3) On the transcendental reading, belonging to a particular political community is not one contingent belief among others but a constitutive condition of possessing any practical standpoint at all; without such embedding, there is no "I" for whom obligations could be binding. (4) So when a cosmopolitan denies that compatriots have special claims, she issues a normative judgment that presupposes the very particularistic framework she rejects—this is not mere hypocrisy but a performative contradiction of the form "within F, I deny that F is necessary." (5) Apply the structure symmetrically to nationalism: the nationalist affirms that obligations are confined to her own community, which means she denies that outsiders fall within her framework of recognition. (6) Yet her judgment is offered as a universal normative claim—"only compatriots matter"—and universal addressability requires a standpoint that recognizes all rational agents, including outsiders, which presupposes the universal framework she denies. (7) Hence both cosmopolitanism and nationalism are self-undermining; neither can be rationally affirmed. (8) But this is absurd, since the dispute between them is a live, rational disagreement. (9) I am inclined to hold onto (1) through (3), and I think (4) correctly diagnoses the cosmopolitan's difficulty, but I cannot see which step breaks the symmetry in (5)-(6) without paying a high price. Is the flaw an equivocation between the conditions for having a standpoint and the content of judgments made within it? Or must I reject (2) or (6), and what would that cost for the transcendental strategy?
