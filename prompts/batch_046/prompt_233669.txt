I serve on the Institutional Ethics Review Board of a university-affiliated long-term neuropsychiatric care center, and we have a split decision on a pilot-study proposal from the clinical-psychology and AI-ethics groups. The proposal is to deploy an algorithmic “narrative continuity system” for in-patients with severe episodic-memory loss—principally advanced Alzheimer’s and Korsakoff-type conditions—who can no longer sustain a coherent autobiographical thread. The system aggregates family interviews, behavioral telemetry, and archival biographical fragments to generate and maintain a first-person narrative that is presented back to the patient and used by staff to preserve relational consistency. The investigators argue that, since personal identity is best understood—following Dennett’s well-known conception—as a center of narrative gravity rather than as a substantive soul or Cartesian ego, constructing such a center is not an ontological deception but a therapeutic restoration of the conditions for personhood. A dissenting faction on the board, led by a phenomenologically oriented clinician, insists that the patient’s present, discontinuous experiential flow is being overwritten by an externally authored fiction, and that substituting an institutional narrative for whatever residual subjectivity remains violates respect for persons regardless of metaphysical commitments about the self.

The board must reach an actionable decision: approve outright, reject outright, or approve with a strict monitoring and withdrawal framework. I need a formal decision memo—no more than about three pages in standard policy format—recommending one of these outcomes. The memo must include explicit criteria reviewers could apply in future analogous cases; a clear bottom line with reasoning that engages the metaphysical claim about narrative identity seriously rather than treating it as philosophical garnish; and an honest, non-caricatured treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side. It should also address whether the disagreement is genuinely ontological—turning on incompatible conceptions of the self—or whether it masks hidden empirical disagreements about residual consciousness, or normative disagreements about what counts as autonomous consent in conditions of profound cognitive discontinuity. The researchers must be able to respond to the reasoning in practical terms.

Please do not include a bibliography, citation list, suggested reading, or appeals to authority through named texts; the board needs reasoning it can use in closed session without external lookup. And please do not treat the philosophical disagreement as purely verbal—diagnose whether the impasse is substantive, empirical, or normative, and show how that diagnosis affects the recommended conditions or prohibition.
