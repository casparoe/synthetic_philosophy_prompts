The philosophical debate over whether knowing-how reduces to knowing-that—exemplified by anti-intellectualist claims that skilled action requires no propositional attitude and by intellectualist analyses that cast skill as knowledge of propositions under practical modes of presentation—has cycled through arguments about mental format, ability conditions, and cognitive architecture for decades without clear resolution. I want a diagnosis, not an adjudication. My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is partly taxonomic: the literature conflates procedural fluency, a graded embodied capacity for routine execution, with adaptive expertise, the ability to restructure performance in response to novel perturbations. If that distinction holds, are the opposing camps answering different questions—one about representational format in stereotyped action, the other about the architecture of flexible skill—under a single heading? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about cognitive science, or a verbal dispute over what counts as knowing? Assess the structure of the disagreement: is it about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases are canonical, or about starting intuitions that neither side can independently justify? And specify what would count as genuine progress—whether a conceptual distinction, a result from cognitive psychology, a formal model of practical reasoning, or a reframing of the question—or whether the impasse signals that the original problem is ill-posed.
