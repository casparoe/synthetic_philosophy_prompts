Design a near-future variation on Dennett’s classic brain-transplant scenario—often framed around the question “where am I?”—in which separation is not abrupt but gradual and distributed. Over the course of a year, every neuron in the patient’s head is replaced one-by-one by a synthetic analog that preserves all psychological properties, including memory, personality, and reasoning. Crucially, each synthetic neuron participates not only in the cranial network but also, via a sustained wireless link, in a remote humanoid robot. By the end of the replacement, the original biological body (except for the synthetic cranium) lies alive but unconscious in a hospital bed, while the same synthetic neural dynamics run concurrently inside the skull and inside the robot, both supporting what subjectively feels like a single continuous first-person predictive model. At any point the wireless link can be severed.

Your task is to build this case to precise specifications. First, engineer the scenario so that three theories of personal identity deliver genuinely different verdicts about where the person is: (a) a psychological-continuity theory that tracks causal-psychological connections and therefore assigns the self to the synthetic network wherever its strongest functional chain lies; (b) a bodily-continuity theory that assigns the self to the original biological body regardless of neural replacement; and (c) a “distributed predictive-self” view—introduced here as a novel framing, not drawn from any named school—that assigns the self to neither substrate alone but to the functional system spanning both skull and robot, predicting that identity dissolves into ambiguity once the link is cut. Second, for each theory state the specific belief about location, the identification method (first-person report, third-person causal tracing, or observational behavior), and the relevant nearby possible worlds (link intact versus link severed; robot destroyed versus biological body destroyed), explaining exactly why the theory gives the verdict it does. Third, isolate the single structural feature of the case—likely the concurrent dual instantiation with shared causal-psychological access—that forces the three verdicts apart, and describe what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed (for instance, if the synthetic neurons can operate in only one substrate at a time). Fourth, include an honest defensive reply: show how a proponent of bodily continuity could attack the case as cheating by building psychological preservation into the synthetic substrate by stipulation, and show how a proponent of the distributed view could counter that bodily continuity was already undermined by the gradual replacement itself. Keep all technology within realistic near-future neuroscience and robotics; avoid teleportation, perfect copying, omniscient predictors, or any appeal to unnamed exotic physics. The deliverable should be the scenario followed by the step-by-step check for each theory, the feature-isolation analysis, and the paired defensive attacks.
