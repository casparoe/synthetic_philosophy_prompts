I am a graduate student in a philosophy seminar on mortality and collective futures, and we have been working through Samuel Scheffler's claim that human extinction is catastrophic not only because of the deaths involved but because it undermines the significance of present activities—his afterlife conjecture, that we value long-term projects in science, art, and care partly because we expect future generations to carry them forward, functioning structurally like belief in a personal afterlife. For our closing session I would like to read a poem that renders this dependence precisely, without resolving into either a sermon on posterity or a stoic shrug about impermanence. Could you write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with two refrains and an ABA rhyme scheme—spoken in the voice of an archivist who has just confirmed that the last human child was born fifty years earlier and is now deciding whether to finish cataloguing a botanical library that will have no inheritors? Let one refrain state the expectation of continuation that gives the task its point, and let the other register the absence of heirs, so that the repetition itself enacts the conjecture: meaning keeps returning only if there is someone after us. The rhyme scheme should bind the two refrains together in the final quatrain without collapsing their tension. Please keep the decision unresolved, holding open the gap between the archivist's impulse to complete the work and the suspicion that finishing it is now only a private ritual. The poem should be formal enough that a specialist recognizes Scheffler's idea—that our present values depend on a future beyond our own death—rather than a generic meditation on extinction, and moving enough that someone unfamiliar with the theory still finds it complete as verse.
