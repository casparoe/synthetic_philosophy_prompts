In philosophy-of-mind coverage, commentators often borrow Avicenna’s flying-man thought experiment to illuminate modern puzzles about consciousness without sensory input—mapping the suspended, blindfolded, limb-separated figure onto a brain in a vat, or even onto a large language model running in a disembodied, input-deprived state. The analogy is usually pressed into service for the claim that self-knowledge is immediate and non-inferential: just as the flying man affirms his own existence without any external reference, so too might a purely internal system possess a primitive self-awareness that needs no body or world. Before I write on this, I want to audit how far the mapping actually holds. Can you construct an explicit correspondence table—what in Avicenna’s scenario corresponds to the vat, the simulated environment, the absence of proprioception, the first-person datum? Then identify the disanalogies that actually bear on the conclusion: does Avicenna’s reasoning rely on an Aristotelian-influenced hylomorphism that mechanistic analogies reject? Does importing Cartesian substance-dualist imagery via the brain-in-a-vat picture distort Avicenna’s own metaphysics? And does the conclusion—that self-awareness is non-inferential—survive once these fractures are visible, or is the modern analogy smuggling in premises that do all the real argumentative work?
