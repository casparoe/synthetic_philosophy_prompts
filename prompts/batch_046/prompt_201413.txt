I'm giving the opening remarks at tomorrow's municipal forum on whether long-term resident non-citizens should vote in school-board elections. The issue turned personally urgent three months ago, when I returned to my hometown after fifteen years abroad and learned that a residency technicality excludes me from the local rolls despite my owning property and paying taxes here; my partner, a permanent resident, is excluded on parallel grounds. The audience will be split between democratic theorists familiar with the boundary problem and local residents and officials for whom it is entirely new. Write a five-minute speech in which the boundary problem does real structural work: specifically, use the bootstrap paradox—that any rule defining who belongs to the demos already presupposes a legitimate demos to enact it—to constrain what I can claim about "the people," to block any appeal to an obvious pre-political community, and to show that including or excluding my partner and me is not an administrative fix but a constitutive decision about who "we" are. The speech should be intelligible and emotionally resonant for the non-experts without becoming a lecture, and it should land precisely for the theorists. After the speech, please include a brief note separating what is settled in this framing from what is genuinely contested—such as whether separating jurisdiction from membership resolves the paradox or merely relocates it, or whether the all-affected-interests principle addresses membership at all—and flag where the speech takes liberties with standard accounts or leans on a contested reading, without presenting that reading as the consensus.
