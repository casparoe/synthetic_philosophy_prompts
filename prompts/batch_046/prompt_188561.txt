I am preparing to defend a particular package of views on literary interpretation in a philosophy of language seminar, and I would like a consistency audit before I present. My commitments are as follows. First, I subscribe to a form of hypothetical intentionalism: the meaning of a text is determined not by the mental states of its actual historical author, but by the intentions that a rational, well-informed reader would attribute to an implied author constructed solely from the work's internal features and its available historical context. Second, I hold that once a text is published, the actual author's mental states become both causally and epistemically irrelevant to its semantic properties; the author has no special interpretive authority, not merely as a normative claim about ownership but as a claim about what fixes meaning. Third, I nevertheless maintain that texts can bear meanings that neither the actual author nor any implied author intended—meanings that arise from the interaction between the text's linguistic structures and the interpretive conventions of the communities that receive it. Fourth, I reject the strong thesis that the author is dead, in that I believe there is always a genuine constraint on interpretation coming from the text's internal coherence and the historical linguistic framework in which it was produced; however, I deny that this constraint is best explained by appeal to authorial intention of either the actual or the hypothetical variety. Fifth, I take interpretation to be fundamentally an act of recovery—discovering what the text says—rather than an act of construction—projecting onto it what we want to find; yet I allow that in cases of radical semantic ambiguity, such as when a term's meaning has shifted between the text's era and ours, different interpretive communities may arrive at different but equally legitimate interpretations of the same passage. Sixth, I think that when a fictional text introduces a proper name that accidentally coincides with a real historical figure unknown to the author, the correct interpretation should follow the reference-fixing conventions available to the reader's community rather than the author's private mental reference, if any.

Please examine this set for internal tension. Which pairs genuinely conflict, which conflicts are only apparent, and what is the least costly revision for each genuine tension? I am especially concerned about whether my hypothetical intentionalism can survive my allowance for community-generated meanings, and whether my claim that actual authors lack authority undermines my insistence that interpretation is recovery rather than construction. Rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what commitment would have to be modified or abandoned and at what philosophical cost, and indicate whether any single revision could resolve more than one problem.
