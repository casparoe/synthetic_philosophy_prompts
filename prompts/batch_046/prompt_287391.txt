After a long and rather heated argument with a close friend last evening about whether a nation can genuinely be guilty for actions committed long before most of its current citizens were born, I have decided to defend a more nuanced position in an upcoming seminar presentation. I am arguing that what we call collective guilt across generations is best reconstructed not as inherited psychological blame — which I agree is conceptually incoherent — but as a form of diachronic civic liability: a moral status that attaches to a continuing institutional agent rather than to discrete individuals, sustained by constitutional continuity and participatory membership rather than by blood or ethnicity. To make this concrete, I propose what I call the Constitutional Continuity Test (CCT): a polity retains the same moral agency, and therefore the same unfulfilled obligations, only if there is an unbroken chain of legitimate institutional recognition linking the present constitutional order to the order that authorised or failed to prevent the original wrong. I take this to be a revision of the familiar taxonomy associated with Jaspers — criminal, political, moral and metaphysical guilt — but stripped of any essentialist or biologised notion of national character, and limited strictly to institutional moral agency.

Next Tuesday I am presenting this as a twenty-minute paper to a small but formidable audience: a joint philosophy-law seminar consisting mainly of analytic moral philosophers committed to methodological individualism, two transitional justice scholars who are deeply sceptical of any ascription of moral properties to groups, and a historian of political thought who is sympathetic to collective responsibility but insists that any such account must survive rigorous tests of anachronism. They care intensely about avoiding essentialism, about whether the view can do practical work in reparative debates without licensing collective punishment, and about whether the distinction between individual and institutional agency is being smuggled in rather than argued for. I need a prep sheet, not a polished defence: please set out the objections they are most likely to raise, phrased in the vocabulary they would actually use — for instance, worries about reification, moral individualism, anachronistic moral projection, or functional equivalence to collective punishment. For each objection, indicate how serious it is, what I should concede up front, and where my reply is weakest. Please also include the one objection I am secretly hoping nobody asks — perhaps the challenge that my CCT collapses into a purely legal criterion and therefore fails to generate genuinely moral obligations — and tell me exactly how to reply in the two minutes I would have. Finally, please advise me on the single reply that must land if the proposal is to survive the session, and write your response in British English.
