I've been trying to wrap my head around the procreation asymmetry -- the idea that we have strong moral reasons not to bring suffering people into existence, but basicly no reason to bring happy ones into existence. In a thread I was reading, someone kept comparing the choice to have a child to deciding whether to invite strangers to a party you're already throwing. The logic goes: if you don't invite anyone extra, nobody is harmed or missed out; but if you do invite them and the party is awful, you've wronged them. So there's no duty to send out invitations (create happy lives), but there is a duty not to invite people to a disaster (create suffering lives).

I'm not sure this party analogy actually holds up once you press on it, and I want to audit it properly. What exactly maps onto what -- the host is the prospective parent? The invitation is conception? The party experience is the life lived? Where does the mapping break? For instance, does the analogy quietly import that the "guest" has no interests before being invited, which might beg the question against views that potential people have some kind of modal standing? And does it smuggle in a value theory where the whole (the party as an event) is just instrumentally valuable, rather than the parts (the guests) having intrinsic value? There's also this wrinkle people rarely talk about: if we take the analogy seriously, should we think that adding a "great" partygoer who enjoys themselves immensely creates value that the host should care about, or does the asymmetry still hold because there's no "guest" there to be benefitted? I want to know whether the party analogy can actually carry the asymmetry to its conclusion, or if the disanalogies -- especially around the temporality of interests and whether absence can be a harm or benefit -- kill it. If it fails, is there a better analogy that doesn't smuggle in host-guest power dynamics?
