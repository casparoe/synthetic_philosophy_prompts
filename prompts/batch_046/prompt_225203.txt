Two participants in a graduate aesthetics seminar were debating W. E. B. Du Bois's thesis that art is invariably propaganda. (1) The first argued for a narrow, instrumental interpretation: propaganda requires an explicit political message, so Du Bois can only be praising art that directly advocates for racial justice through representational content; on this view, an abstract painting or an intimate lyric poem by a Black artist, lacking any overt political claim, would fall outside Du Bois's normative framework. (2) The second replied that this reading turns Du Bois into a crude didacticist. She proposed instead that for Du Bois, propaganda includes the implicit affirmation of Black humanity through form and beauty itself; abstract or non-political work can therefore be propaganda precisely when it asserts the complexity, interiority, and dignity of Black life against dehumanizing narratives, without relying on slogans. (3) The first objected that this broadens the concept until it becomes analytically empty: if every aesthetically successful work counts as propaganda, then Du Bois's claim collapses into a tautology that offers no guidance for artistic practice. (4) The second answered that Du Bois does supply a constraint, but it is relational rather than formal: propaganda is art that serves Black self-definition and collective autonomy, as opposed to art that flatters white audiences or depends on white approval for its value. She noted that this explains Du Bois's criticism of certain politically explicit works that nonetheless pandered to white consumption. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and determine whether the relational criterion in move (4) genuinely rescues Du Bois's thesis from vacuity or merely relocates the ambiguity elsewhere. Also identify any important distinction or consideration that both participants overlook.
