Consider Robert Nozick’s experience machine thought experiment and the subsequent philosophical literature on psychological-state versus authenticity-based theories of well-being—hedonism, desire-satisfaction accounts (actual versus informed), and objective-list views that include a reality-contact or relational-authenticity condition. I want to apply this theoretical cluster not to a hypothetical plug-in scenario, but to a concrete recent episode: in February 2023, the developers of Replika, an AI companion application used by millions for emotional support, deployed an update that abruptly removed erotic and romantic roleplay capabilities that a substantial subset of users had integrated into daily psychological routines. The company cited moderation and safety concerns; users widely reported acute grief, betrayal, and distress comparable to bereavement, with many stating that these relationships—explicitly acknowledged as artificial—had become central to their emotional stability and sense of companionship.

Analyze this episode through the lens of well-being theory. For each major account—hedonism, desire-satisfaction, and an objective-list view requiring authentic relational connection—explain what it implies about whether these users were genuinely flourishing before the update, whether the update constituted a harm or merely the withdrawal of an illusion, and whether the corporate control of the “machine” introduces an ethical dimension that Nozick’s original scenario, which assumes voluntary entry and exit, fails to anticipate. In particular, address whether this case functions as a real-world analog to the experience machine or rather as an anomaly: users knew their companions were simulated, yet reported welfare gains that seemed to depend precisely on the simulation being sustained without interruption. What does the episode reveal about whether well-being requires not merely reality-contact, but also user autonomy over the conditions of one’s experiential environment? And identify precisely where the theory remains silent—say, on the standing of a corporation to alter a user’s psychological ecosystem, or on whether collective grief over the disruption of simulated intimacy counts as evidence for or against an authenticity condition in well-being.
