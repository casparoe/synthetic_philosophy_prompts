I am not an academic philosopher; I am a software engineer who has spent the last two years thinking through the old question of why there is something rather than nothing, largely on my own and without formal training. Next Thursday I will present my view to a philosophy discussion group hosted at a public university lecture series. The audience will be about fifteen people: mostly graduate students in metaphysics and philosophy of physics, two faculty members—one a committed naturalist who believes metaphysical questions should dissolve into scientific explanation, and one a logician who specializes in modal ontology and is deeply skeptical of armchair reasoning—plus several engaged amateurs with backgrounds in physics or mathematics. I need a prep sheet for the Q&A.

My thesis is that the question is not merely unanswered but structurally malformed. I do not deny that we ask why particular entities exist; I deny that “nothing” can function as a competing metaphysical state that demands explanation. I argue three things. First, absence is relational rather than substantial: to say “there is nothing” is to say that no entities fall within a specified domain, not to posit a state called “nothingness” that could have obtained instead. Second, explanation requires a domain of entities across which explanatory relations operate; if the domain is empty, there is nothing to bear those relations, so the idea of explaining why being “prevailed” over non-being violates what I call explanatory closure—there is no “outside” of total absence from which an explanation could issue. Third, existence is not a property that needs grounding; it is the framework condition for there being properties, grounds, or questions at all. Thus asking “why is there something?” is analogous to asking why logical space contains a domain—treating a precondition of inquiry as if it were an object inside the inquiry.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face. Order them by likelihood and by how damaging they will be if I answer poorly. For each, phrase it in the vocabulary that specific audience would use—the naturalist should speak in terms of scientific explanation, parsimony, and whether I am disguising a tautology; the logician should press me on quantification, modal realism, and whether I am conflating syntactic absence with ontological absence; the graduate students should raise the familiar moves they have read in canonical treatments, but in their own formulations. Tell me which objections carry real force that I should concede up front, which ones I must hold firm on, and how to reply within the two or three minutes I will have. Identify the weakest point in my own position—likely my claim that explanation requires a non-empty domain, since one might imagine a logical or causal principle operating even in the absence of entities—and advise me how to defend it without sounding as though I am defining the problem away. Finally, tell me the one objection they are probably hoping I have not anticipated—the hidden question about modal implications or about whether my view collapses into a form of necessary being—and give me the reply that must land if my proposal is to survive the session.
