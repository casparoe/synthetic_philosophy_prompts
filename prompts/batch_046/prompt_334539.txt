You chair the clinical ethics board at a large teaching hospital. Your board receives recurring referrals about staff who display extreme moral commitment—clinicians who consistently prioritize patient welfare over rest, relationships, or career advancement—to the point where colleagues worry either that this is exemplary or that it signals burnout, self-neglect, or a problematic narrowing of character. You want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the philosophical argument that maximal moral goodness may not be desirable if it systematically displaces non-moral virtues (such as humor, aesthetic engagement, intellectual curiosity, or reciprocal friendship), but translated so that board members without philosophical training can apply it consistently in about fifteen minutes.

Write a procedure your board can run on any referral. It should take the form of an ordered sequence of questions, with clear criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, explicit stopping points where the board issues a clear finding—support, modify, or flag for intervention—and explicit hand-off points where the question is one of values rather than observable fact and must be resolved by the chair together with an external reviewer. The procedure should distinguish between integrative moral excellence, which coexists with and even enhances other human capacities, and displacing moral excellence, which crowds them out; include at least four observable dimensions against which displacement is checked (for example, emotional range, relational reciprocity, playful or aesthetic engagement, and epistemic openness). Specify what behavioral or institutional evidence counts, and do not rely on staff self-report alone.

Then apply the procedure to three cases:

1. A senior nurse beloved by patients who voluntarily takes every holiday shift, declines a promotion that would reduce bedside hours, and is described by peers as warm but always on duty, with no known hobbies, close friendships outside work, or non-medical interests, yet with excellent clinical outcomes and no signs of distress.
2. A surgeon who refuses all non-medical social interaction, states that personal enjoyment is a distraction from the moral imperative, has outstanding surgical outcomes, is dismissive of colleagues' concerns, and shows no evidence of reciprocal relationships or openness to revising clinical judgments.
3. A resident who donates a substantial portion of income to patient-relief funds, works long hours, but maintains an active amateur music practice, close friendships, and openly revises clinical opinions when challenged.

For each case, show the answers at each step, note where the procedure stops or hands off, and identify any point where judgment is unavoidable despite the procedure. Include at least one case where the procedure yields a finding—likely flag or modify—that the board will be reluctant to accept because the clinician is otherwise admired and effective. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot determine: where it will misfire, what kinds of cultural or personal variation it ignores, and which ethical questions remain outside its scope.
