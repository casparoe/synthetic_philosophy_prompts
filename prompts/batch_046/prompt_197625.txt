ok so heres something that always seemed weird to me about how we treat binding yourself in advance. in constitutional law we have these entrenchment clauses - like you need a supermajority to change certain provisions - and lawyers justify them as Ulysses contracts where one generation ties future generations to the mast so democracy doesnt drift into tyranny. but then courts also say that if you call a constituent assembly, the people are acting in their original sovereign capacity and those entrenchment rules just dont apply, because you cant bind the constituent power. meanwhile in medical law we totally let people write living wills that constrain their future medical choices even if they later change their mind, and we treat that as respecting autonomy rather than violating it. so what view of personal and collective identity over time is this whole framework actually presupposing? does the democratic community have a future self that the present can legitimately constrain, or is that just a metaphor that breaks down when we notice generations are different people? and if we say individual future selves are literally the same person but democratic majorities are not, why does that license one person to bind their own future medical choices but prevent one democratic generation from binding the next? is there a hidden rationality commitment here - like we only honor the precommitment when we think the present self was more rational than the future one - and does that sit comfortably with the idea that sovereignty belongs to the people at all times?
