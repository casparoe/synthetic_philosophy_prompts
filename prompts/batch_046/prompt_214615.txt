In philosophy-of-mind seminars I keep hearing the claim that 'a private language is impossible,' usually deployed to end discussion—most recently to reject the possibility that an isolated cognitive system, one trained exclusively on its own internal state-transitions with no shared symbolic environment or intersubjective correction, could develop genuinely private phenomenal concepts that are in principle incommunicable. Rather than treating this as a single thesis, I want it disambiguated. Lay out the precise claims the slogan could be expressing: (a) a definitional claim that language by concept requires public criteria, so 'private language' is contradictory; (b) an epistemic claim that a rule-following practice confined to one mind cannot distinguish correct application from error; (c) a semantic claim that terms for sensations or inner states acquire determinacy only through publicly observable criteria of correct use; (d) a metaphysical claim that subjective experience is conceptually incoherent without a community of speakers; and (e) any other serious reading I have missed. For each, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible Wittgensteinian position, or an overstatement that is actually false. Then tell me which of these readings actually does the work required to deny my isolated-system case its private vocabulary, and which ones—despite sounding decisive—leave that possibility intact. Present the answer as a menu of readings with verdicts, not as a defense of a single interpretation.
