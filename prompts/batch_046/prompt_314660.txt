I have been thinking on my own about the philosophical worry that scientific evidence never uniquely determines which theory is true, because one can always protect a favored theory by adjusting some auxiliary belief or background assumption. In the usual philosophical presentation, theories appear as free-floating logical structures that can be endlessly rewritten to fit the data. But it seems to me that real scientific theories are embedded in specific instruments, calibration routines, and institutional practices—laboratory protocols, shared measurement standards, training regimes—that do not travel with the theory like detachable accessories. If you change the core theory, you often have to rebuild the apparatus, retrain the practitioners, and renegotiate the institutional norms that make the measurements meaningful in the first place. I want to argue that this material and social embeddedness blocks the kind of limitless theoretical flexibility that philosophers assume, so that global underdetermination is not a live threat to scientific knowledge but a category mistake that confuses abstract logical possibility with practical scientific revision. Please construct a detailed argument for this position: show how institutional and instrumental constraints localize and limit theory change in ways the standard formulation ignores, and defend the claim that recognizing this embeddedness dissolves rather than solves the traditional problem. In doing so, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain.
