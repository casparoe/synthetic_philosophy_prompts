I have been thinking on my own about when directness is a virtue and when politeness or indirectness becomes something worse, comparing the Dutch habit of saying exactly what one means with Japanese practices of avoiding explicit refusal and leaving meaning to be inferred from context. I have also been trying to decide whether white lies are ever genuinely wrong, or whether they are merely a form of kindness. I do not have a formal theory, but I have formed strong gut judgments about particular situations, and I want to see whether they point to a single principle or whether some of them must be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts. First, on directness and indirectness. When a Dutch colleague tells me in private that my presentation was poorly structured and factually wrong, I think she is being respectful, not cruel; I would rather hear this clearly than be left to guess. When a Japanese colleague, after a presentation that clearly failed, sends me a book on public speaking with a note that it might be useful for future growth, I think this is thoughtful and not wrong. But when the same indirect style produces a workplace situation in which my proposal is greeted with the phrase "That is a very interesting direction" and is then silently dropped, leaving me with no explanation and no idea whether it was rejected for being foolish or merely premature, I think something has gone wrong. It feels closer to deception than to courtesy, even though no one uttered an explicit falsehood.

Now on white lies. Telling my aunt that I love the ugly sweater she knitted, when I do not, seems fine. Telling a murderer at the door that my friend is not home, when she is, also seems fine. Telling a job interviewer that I left my previous position voluntarily, when in fact I was fired for incompetence, seems clearly wrong. Telling a dying patient who asks about her estranged son that he called, when he has not, seems wrong too, even though it would bring her comfort in her final hours. Telling a friend whose business plan has obvious fatal flaws that the plan is sound seems wrong. There is also one case I keep returning to: telling my young child that our dead dog went to live on a farm. Sometimes I think this is a harmless, protective fiction suited to her age; other times I think it teaches that reality can be rewritten to suit our feelings, and that this is a bad lesson to give.

My tentative guess is that wrongness tracks whether one actually asserts something false, not whether one is direct or indirect. But this does not quite fit my judgments. The silent cancellation of my proposal involves no explicit false sentence, yet I judge it wrong, while the sweater lie is literally false and yet I judge it acceptable. Meanwhile, the farm case and the dying-patient case are both false and both protective, yet my intuition is firm on one and wavers on the other.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all, or nearly all, of these judgments? If so, please state it precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case above, predict what it says about a situation I have not listed—say, a doctor who tells a patient with a terminal diagnosis that "many people live for years with this condition," knowing the statistical prognosis is months and knowing the patient will take the remark as personal hope rather than as a general statistical claim—and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to overturn. If no single principle can accommodate them all, present the two simplest competing principles, indicate which verdict each must sacrifice, and say which one you think is closer to the truth.
