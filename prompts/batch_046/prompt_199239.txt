I'm in a philosophy of mind course and we've been stuck on pain asymbolia for weeks—those cases where someone reports feeling pain but doesn't avoid it or seem bothered, sometimes even smiling when you ask. Half the class thinks this shows pain isn't intrinsically bad, that the "badness" is just an emotional add-on that can get seperated from the raw sensation. The other half says it's just a broken motivational circuit, the pain is still bad but they can't act on the evaluation. It feels like we're talking past each other, but I'm not sure if it's a verbal confusion over "badness" or a real empirical disagreement about whether affect is necessary for pain to exist at all.

My hunch is that one side means prudential disvalue—what gives you reason to avoid something—while the other means representational content—what the state says about tissue damage. But maybe there's a formal issue hiding in there about how we individuate mental states, and I can't tell. I want an honest diagnosis: is this disagreement mostly about words, about hidden empirical commitments, or about which cases count as data? Could a new distinction actually help, or are we just going to keep spinning? I also made up this hypothetical case I'm calling reverse asymbolia—full emotional suffering and avoidance behavior but with zero localized sensory quality reported. If neurologists actually found something like that, would it settle whether pain's badness lives in the feel or in the motivation? Don't just list interpretations; tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, whether anything could end it, and whether my prudential-vs-representational split is useful or just shuffles the deck.
