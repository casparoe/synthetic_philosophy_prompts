Consider a hypothetical International Commission on Historical Military Ethics that has issued the following rulings. First, it judged the February 1945 Dresden firebombing unjustified: the direct military targets were limited, the European war was near its end, and the civilian casualties were grossly disproportionate to any strategic gain. Second, it judged a 1943 attack on a fortified rail junction in an occupied industrial zone to be justified, despite unavoidable civilian harm, because the target was of direct strategic importance and the attackers made credible efforts to limit collateral damage. Third, it judged the March 1945 Tokyo incendiary campaign unjustified, even accepting its potential to shorten the war, because the operation deliberately aimed at residential neighborhoods rather than at distinguishable military objectives. Fourth, on Hiroshima in August 1945, the Commission issued a split but majority opinion that the attack was conditionally justified: the weapon was unprecedented, its use was intended as a singular shock to avert a costly invasion, and the city contained legitimate military and industrial targets; however, the majority added that this justification depended on treating the weapon as exceptional and non-repeatable.

The Commission now faces Nagasaki, bombed on August 9, 1945—three days after Hiroshima and after the Soviet Union had entered the war against Japan. The primary target had been obscured by smoke, so the weapon was delivered on Nagasaki, a city with a major shipyard and torpedo works but also a dense civilian population. The strategic rationale offered was that Japan had not yet surrendered and that a second demonstration might hasten capitulation.

What should the Commission decide about Nagasaki if it wishes to be consistent with its own track record? Identify the closest analog among the four prior rulings. Then reconstruct at least two distinct principles that could jointly explain all four prior verdicts, state what each principle predicts for Nagasaki and why, and give your best recommendation for the Commission with explicit reasoning. Be clear about which principle the Commission would be adopting if it followed your recommendation versus if it chose the opposite. Finally, propose one well-defined hypothetical further case—different from Nagasaki in a specific, clearly described respect—that would best distinguish the leading principles from one another.
