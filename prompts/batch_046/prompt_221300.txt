I have noticed a persistent asymmetry in philosophical treatments of location and identity that I do not have a name for. Consider two scenarios with formally identical structures of evidence and possibility: in one, ten physically identical copies of a person exist simultaneously in separate, indistinguishable rooms, and each copy must reason about which room contains it; in the other, one person is questioned ten successive times in the same room, with all memory of prior sessions erased, and must reason about which session this is—closely analogous to the Sleeping Beauty problem. In the room case, the natural answer seems to be an even division of belief across the ten locations, with little philosophical controversy; in the awakening case, that same even division is hotly disputed, with special theoretical machinery invoked that has no obvious analog for the rooms. Is there an established term for this specific divergence, where temporal repetition triggers contested reasoning about one's position that spatial duplication does not, despite the two being structurally parallel? How should it be separated from ordinary framing effects, and if no term exists, what precise definition—including boundary conditions like whether continuous awareness removes the effect—would capture it?
