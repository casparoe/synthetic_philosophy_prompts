I've been working through the modal-metaphysics literature on how Lewis-style counterpart theory and Kripkean transworld identity actually diverge, and I keep finding myself pulled toward what looks like an unstable hybrid position. As I understand it, Lewis treats worlds as causally isolated concrete totalities and individuals as strictly world-bound; cross-world talk is mediated by counterpart relations governed by qualitative similarity and importance. That makes him an anti-haecceitist about worlds—there is no bare fact about which world is "actual" beyond its qualitative profile—yet he seems to treat actual-world individuals as uniquely anchored to this world by their intrinsic properties. So when I say "I could have been left-handed," the analysis is that in some other concrete world, a counterpart of me is left-handed, and that makes my modal claim true. But here is the tension: for there to be a fact about which other-worldly musician is my counterpart rather than someone else's, it seems there must be a fact about my actual-world haecceity—my being this particular individual—that determines the relation. If so, doesn't counterpart theory secretly depend on a thin form of transworld identity for the actual me, the very thing it was meant to eliminate? Without that anchoring "me," the counterpart relation looks arbitrary, and "possibly left-handed" collapses into a claim about strangers who happen to resemble me.

More specifically, I worry that treating modal properties as extrinsic—like siblinghood, which depends on relations to others—makes possibility fundamentally different from the intrinsic capacities we ordinarily take it to be. If being possibly left-handed is just standing in a similarity relation to a left-handed counterpart, then it is no closer to my nature than having a sister in Paris. So it seems the counterpart theorist must either concede that ordinary modal discourse is systematically misleading about the kind of property possibility is, or admit that there is a persistent "relatum"—me—who bears the counterpart relation across worlds, which sounds like Kripke's view in thinner clothing. Can you lay out exactly where this line of reasoning misfires: is the error in thinking that counterpart relations require a cross-world relatum, in misunderstanding how anti-haecceitism applies to individuals versus worlds, or in assuming that extrinsic modal properties cannot ground genuine de re possibilities?
