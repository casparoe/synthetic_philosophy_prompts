You are a professional philosopher of art and African American intellectual history, with particular expertise in aesthetics, propaganda theory, and museum ethics. Before drafting, think carefully and step by step: first, identify the precise tension in W. E. B. Du Bois's position between the inevitability of propaganda and the demand for beauty; second, list two common misreadings of that position that a speaker addressing both scholars and laypeople should avoid; third, determine exactly what that position permits and forbids saying about an artwork whose technical excellence is inseparable from an unethical origin.

I am delivering the opening remarks at the launch of "Synthetic Archives: Machine Vision and the Great Migration," an exhibition of images generated by a model trained—without the knowledge or consent of the families—on archival photographs documenting the Great Migration. The audience is split evenly between digital-humanities researchers, art historians, and community members whose relatives appear in the source material. The curators want the remarks to acknowledge that the images are technically accomplished yet ethically unstable, and they have asked me to ground the speech in Du Bois's argument that art is necessarily propaganda—that is, that beauty is never separable from the moral and political conditions of its making and circulation. Use that idea to do real structural work: it should shape what I can and cannot claim about aesthetic value in this room. The speech must honor the community members' grievance without reducing the artists or engineers to mere villains, and it must speak to the researchers without turning the occasion into a methodology lecture. Do not allow the speech to pretend that aesthetic experience can be purified of its material origins, and do not allow it to claim that technical excellence cancels ethical failure.

Write the speech. Begin with the speech itself, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of the prompt. The first sentence should establish the central framing claim that governs everything that follows.
