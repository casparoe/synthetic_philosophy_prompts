I sit on the clinical-ethics review board of a hospital network in a region where Buddhist frameworks inform institutional policy but are rarely operationalized. We need a reusable decision procedure—something a reviewer can run in about thirty minutes per proposal, without computing metaphysical weights—that evaluates interventions in which harm is probabilistic, distributed, and small-scale, while benefit is large-scale but diffuse. Ground the procedure in the actual conceptual tension within Buddhist ethics between consequence-sensitive reasoning (the suffering-calculus tradition running from Jataka narratives to Shantideva’s exchange-of-self arguments) and precept-based constraints anchored in cetana (volitional intention) and the first, second, and fourth precepts. Structure it as ordered questions with explicit stopping points (where the answer is no), explicit judgment points (where values rather than facts decide, and the reviewer must document the choice), and a small set of diagnostic sentences—applied to concrete scenarios—that distinguish direct harm, indirect harm, and structural harm. Apply the procedure to three proposals: (a) a mandatory vaccination program projected to prevent 200 deaths but cause five severe adverse reactions; (b) an age-weighted triage protocol that withholds ICU admission from patients over eighty during surge conditions; and (c) a community mental-health screening initiative that identifies at-risk individuals but exposes them to documented social stigma. For each case, show where the procedure delivers an answer, where it forces an unavoidable judgment call, and where it produces a result that a secular consequentialist reviewer will find intolerable. Include a section on misfire conditions: specifically, cases where the skillful-means (upaya) exception might appear to override a precept, and state clearly what the procedure cannot settle—particularly long-range karmic consequences that fall outside institutional time horizons and collective-action problems where individual volition is diffuse. Do not ask reviewers to assign numerical karmic weights; instead, require them to map the volitional structure of the proposed harm and to demonstrate that no less harmful means achieves the same benefit.
