I have been preparing for an advanced seminar on scientific realism and find myself caught on a point that standard introductions seem to gloss over. While working through Pierre Duhem's 1906 treatise on the aim and structure of physical theory, I encountered his argument that a physical theory should not be understood as an explanatory picture of hidden reality but as a symbolic apparatus for classifying experimental laws. To fix the idea, consider this reconstructed passage from his discussion of theoretical change: a theory is a system of mathematical relations that groups observed phenomena under economical headings; when it is superseded, the supersession is not analogous to discovering that a portrait was of the wrong subject, but rather to adopting a more efficient filing system. The entities postulated by the older theory—caloric, phlogiston, the luminiferous ether—are not refuted in the sense that their existence is experimentally disproved; rather, they become redundant within the new symbolic framework, and the mathematical structures that once supported them may survive only as approximate limiting cases. I am trying to understand how this symbolic-classification view interacts with the pessimistic meta-induction. Specifically, I want to explore whether there is a hidden realist premise in Laudan's induction—namely, that theory change involves the falsification of ontological commitments rather than merely the retirement of representational conveniences. If Duhem is right, then the abandonment of past entities might not support the anti-realist conclusion that scientists were systematically wrong about what exists; instead, it might support what I am tentatively calling a "symbolic stability thesis," according to which the functional role of old theoretical terms is preserved in successor theories even when their ontological interpretation is discarded.

Could you help me see exactly where this hidden premise operates, whether Duhem's view genuinely blocks the induction or merely transforms it into an argument about representational economy, and how one might rigorously distinguish between saying that an entity "was never real" and saying that "its symbolic role has been absorbed into a more comprehensive classification"? I am particularly unsure whether this distinction collapses under scrutiny, or whether it opens up a third position—neither straightforward realism nor standard anti-realism—that treats theoretical continuity as a matter of symbolic inheritance rather than approximate truth.
