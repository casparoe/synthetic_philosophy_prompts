After thirty years in biochemistry, I have only recently begun reading philosophy with any real seriousness. I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at a one-day workshop titled "Moral Attention in Scientific Practice," with an audience of working scientists and philosophers of science. I want the remarks to be structured around George Eliot's secular humanist conviction that the loss of orthodox religious belief does not entail a loss of moral gravity, because disciplined sympathy with particular, concrete others can serve as an independent ethical foundation. The speech should not romanticize her position, should not imply that she secretly retained religious commitments, and should avoid treating sympathy as mere sentiment. To make the idea concrete for this audience, I would like to develop a comparison—something I have been calling "sympathetic calibration"—between the way an experimentalist adjusts an instrument against an external standard and the way Eliot describes a moral agent adjusting her feelings against the irreducible particularity of another person's situation. The remarks must remain accessible to scientists, respectful to philosophers, and must not invoke specific scriptural texts or claim that modern science validates her view. Please write these remarks.
