Hey, I'm prepping discussion questions for my grad seminar on conservative political thought, and there's an argumentative move I've seen a bunch lately that I don't have a clean label for. It usually starts with the Chesterton fence point — don't tear down a fence until you understand why it was put up — used to block reform of some old institution, like a legacy admission rule or a zoning ordinance that everyone now admits is weird. That's standard Burkean caution about unintended consequences, and I get it.

But here's the twist: when you actually do the historical work and show the fence was built for reasons that are now pretty discreditd — say, explicit racial exclusion or pure guild protectionism — the defender doesn't say okay, tear it down. Instead they pivot: the original purpose is dead, sure, but the fence might now serve some hidden, stabilizing function we can't fully see, so removing it risks unknown harms. Meanwhile the epistemic burden never shifts back; the reformer is still expected to prove there's no hidden value, while the defender doesn't have to demonstrate that any hidden value actually exists. I've watched this happen in debates over university governence structures, old licensing boards, even certain common-law doctrines, and it feels like a distinct failure mode rather than just ordinary status-quo bias. Is there an established term for this specific asymmetry — where Chesterton's fence is first used as a demand for historical knowledge, and then, once that knowledge reveals a bad origin, as a generic appeal to mysterious utility? How does it relate to Burke's idea of presumption in favor of existing arrangements, and if there's no precise name, how should we distinguish it from a motte-and-bailey or plain equivocation? I want my students to spot it, but I'm not sure whether I'm describing a recognized concept or just inventing one.
