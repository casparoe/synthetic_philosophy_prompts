For a colloquium on political ontology I have been asked to set out my philosophical commitments regarding the left-right distinction, and I should like a consistency check before I submit them. Here is my position, as precisely as I can state it. (1) I take the distinction to be real but not a natural kind: it tracks a historically sedimented cluster of responses to equality and tradition, best understood through family resemblance rather than shared essence. (2) I endorse what one might call an asymmetry thesis: the left is unified by a negative core—opposition to arbitrary or unearned hierarchy—whereas the right possesses no analogous internal principle and is largely reactive, defined chiefly by resistance to left projects. (3) Despite this asymmetry, I reject multi-axis replacements (for instance, frameworks separating economic from social liberty) and maintain that the single left-right axis remains the most powerful heuristic for political orientation. (4) I grant that in contemporary democratic politics the distinction has become increasingly performative, with actors adopting positions for strategic advantage rather than from deep ideological conviction; yet I continue to deploy the labels with full confidence in my own analytical and pedagogical work. (5) I accept that if the right truly lacks internal coherence, then appeals to "authentic conservatism" or accusations that a figure has "betrayed conservative principles" are conceptually confused; notwithstanding this logical consequence, I have recently argued in print that a prominent statesman betrayed the authentic conservative tradition by abandoning institutional continuity in favour of populist disruption. I should be grateful for an audit of these commitments. Which pairs pull against each other, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicating for each what must be adjusted and at what price, and identify the single change that would restore the greatest consistency.
