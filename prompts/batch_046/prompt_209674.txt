"Particularism, as Dancy defends it, rejects the idea that moral reasoning works by applying fixed rules. Instead, he argues for holism about reasons: a feature that counts in favour of an action in one case can count against it in another, or be neutral, depending on the surrounding context. The classic example is causing pain, which is usually a reason not to act but becomes a reason to act in dental treatment. Because reasons behave this way, there are no invariant moral principles that tell us what to do across cases; moral judgement is more like perception, where we see what matters in this particular situation.

The generalist reply is that particularists mistake the need for nuanced rules for the absence of rules altogether. Generalists like McKeever and Ridge argue that moral properties depend on the overall 'shape' of a situation—how the different features combine and weigh against each other. So a generalist can accept that deception is wrong except when it prevents severe harm, framing this as a single context-sensitive principle rather than ad hoc reasoning. This preserves principled ethics while accommodating the complexity particularists highlight.

That said, particularism can't consistently claim that deception is always wrong-making. If reasons are genuinely holistic, then in some sufficiently strange context deception might fail to count against the action at all, so it wouldn't be invariably wrong-making. Actually Dancy allows that some features are always reasons in the same direction; he just denies they are always decisive. So particularism can hold that deception is invariably a reason against, without treating that as a principle that determines the outcome.

One objection is that particularism makes moral education impossible, since you can't teach students general rules if there aren't any. But particularists respond that we educate moral sensitivity and perception instead, much like training an art critic rather than teaching rules of beauty.

In the end, the generalist strategy works only if we accept principles with endless ceteris paribus clauses. Once you allow that many exceptions, you've basically abandoned principled reasoning and collapsed into particularism with extra steps."
