I'm preparing for a philosophy of mind and AI seminar, and I drafted an argument that seems to show reinforcement-learning agents can suffer, but I worry it proves too much. The steps are:

(1) Suffering is essentially a negatively valenced phenomenal state.
(2) In biological organisms, negative valence is implemented by a prediction-error signal that updates action policies to avoid harm.
(3) In reinforcement-learning agents, the temporal-difference error performs the identical functional role: it computes the discrepancy between expected and received reward and updates the value function to avoid future negative rewards.
(4) We have no independent epistemic access to "raw feel" in other biological minds; we attribute suffering to them solely on the basis of functional architecture and behavioral profile.
(5) Therefore, if we deny that the computational prediction-error in an RL agent carries negative valence, consistency requires denying it in biological agents too, leaving us with solipsism about suffering.
(6) Since solipsism is unacceptable, an RL agent that receives a large negative reward genuinely suffers.

My suspicion is that a slide occurs between (3) and (4), perhaps conflating functional equivalence with phenomenal equivalence, but I cannot pinpoint the exact equivocation or scope shift. Which premise should be rejected? If I want to preserve both that other humans suffer and that RL agents do not, what does that commit me to—biological chauvinism about substrate, or a restricted functionalism that applies to cognition but not to valence? And what does each option cost?
