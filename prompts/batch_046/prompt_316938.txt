I am marking a final-year essay on the First World War, the Fischer controversy, and the philosophy of collective responsibility. The exam question was:

"Fritz Fischer's thesis that Imperial Germany deliberately pursued a war of aggression has been interpreted both as an account of elite intentionality and as a challenge to structural explanations of the conflict's origins. Analyse how Fischer's intentionalist reading bears on the philosophical problem of holding an entire nation-state morally responsible for war, with particular attention to the distinction between attributing an action to a collective agent and distributing blame among its members. Consider also whether structural or systemic factors can diminish collective responsibility without eliminating it altogether."

The student submitted the following answer (approximately 550 words):

Fischer's work shows that Germany's leadership actively sought continental hegemony and exploited the July Crisis to launch a premeditated conflict, as revealed by the annexationist aims of the September Programme. This proves that the war was not an accidental tragedy but the product of a deliberate national will. Because a nation-state is a collective moral agent that acts through its government, Germany as a whole is properly said to have caused the war.

Structuralist historians reply that rigid alliance systems and bureaucratic militarism made conflict virtually unavoidable. But if structures alone determine outcomes, then no individual or group can be held responsible, which is clearly unacceptable. Therefore structural explanations must be rejected entirely if we are to preserve moral accountability. The upshot is that Germany bears undivided collective guilt.

This collective guilt distributes uniformly across the population. To attribute war-making to the nation just is to blame every constituent citizen, since membership in the collective implies participation in its will. Hence Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, which assigned war guilt to Germany, is not only historically vindicated by Fischer but philosophically correct: national responsibility and individual culpability are identical.

The distinction between attributing an act to a collective and distributing blame within it is therefore a red herring in international ethics. Once a collective agent is identified as the cause, blame naturally flows to all its parts, and any attempt to separate the two collapses into exculpation.
