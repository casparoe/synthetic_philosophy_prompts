In rationalist spaces and philosophy of mind forums there's this recurring deadlock about transcendental arguments for a unified self-model — the idea that any coherent reasoning system needs a unified apperceptive centre (the Kantian I think) accompanying representations, rather than just distributed processes. One side (mostly Kant scholars and some AI alignment people) says it's a substantive condition on intelegibility: if you split the self-model across seperate modules with no central integration, you've lost the conditions for experience, not just changed the architecture. The other side (mostly cognitive scientists and some effective altruists who read too much Dennett) says this is just a verbal trick: unity just means whatever functional integration we observe, and split-brain or distributed AI cases show the transcendental claim is either false or tautological. They've been going in circles for years — every new brain-splitting study or distributed LLM architecture gets cited, but nobody moves. My working theory is that they're not actually disagreeing about brains or AI, but about whether a transcendental argument can ever be falsifed by empirical cases, or whether it's protected by definitional fiat. Is that right? Or is there a hidden formal disagreement about what counts as a representation? I want a diagnosis of the stall, not another round of examples, and an honest take on whether anything — a formal result about distributed computation, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — could actually unstick it.
