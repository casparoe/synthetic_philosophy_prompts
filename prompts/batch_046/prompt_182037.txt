I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but after a long discussion with colleagues about whether our forecasting pipelines could ever be made perfect, I started thinking about determinism and chaos in a way that feels different from the usual butterfly-effect examples. I would like you to write a short story—around 500 to 800 words—set in a near-future research lab where a small team has built a fully deterministic, closed-world simulator of a single neighborhood. Every rule inside is fixed; there is no randomness, no quantum noise, no hidden variable, and no measurement error beyond ordinary floating-point limits. The protagonist is an engineer on the team who discovers that the system is nevertheless becoming radically unpredictable. The cause is not disorder or external perturbation, but a structural feature of the architecture: the prediction module must feed its own output back into the input stream it is forecasting. The loop is entirely deterministic, yet it never settles into a periodic orbit or a stable equilibrium; it generates behavior that looks chaotic despite the absence of any standard physical source of chaos.

Please construct the narrative around this specific conceptual tension. The world inside the simulator should remain deterministically governed at every point, yet the engineers should find themselves unable to forecast it in practice and, more importantly, unable to do so in principle because any prediction changes the very state being predicted. I want you to avoid framing the failure as mere sensitivity to initial conditions or as a disguised random number generator. Instead, explore whether the protagonist interprets this as determinism being false, as determinism being true but empty of predictive value, or as a sign that our concept of predictability implicitly assumes an impossible vantage point outside the system. You are free to invent the characters, the dialogue, and the precise technical breakdown, but keep the core idea intact: chaos arising from deterministic self-reference rather than from chance or disorder. I am especially interested in how the engineer—someone who thinks in terms of code, state spaces, and feedback loops—articulates the philosophical problem without relying on technical philosophical vocabulary, and whether the story ends with resignation, a redesigned system, or a changed understanding of what determinism was ever supposed to guarantee.
