Here is an argument a friend presented in a seminar, and I cannot locate the flaw.

(1) Call two decision-makers "superrational copies" if they face structurally identical choice situations, know each other are running the same reasoning process, and therefore treat their own decision as predictive of the other’s.
(2) In the standard opaque-box scenario—where a highly accurate predictor has already filled one box with $1,000,000 or left it empty depending on whether it expects the agent to take one box or two—the two copies should both take one box, since each can reason that her choice determines the other’s and that mutual one-boxing yields $1,000,000 each.
(3) Now invert the predictor’s rule: it fills the box with $1,000,000 exactly when it expects two-boxing, and leaves it empty when it expects one-boxing. The payoff structure is reversed.
(4) By the same coordination principle, the copies should now both take two boxes, since that is the common choice that secures the high reward.
(5) Generalize: let the predictor’s accuracy vary continuously from perfect to random. At one extreme the reasoning of (2) dominates; at the other extreme the reasoning of (4) dominates.
(6) Superrationality demands a single coordinated strategy at each point on this continuum. But near the midpoint—where the predictor is barely better than chance—the expected-utility gap between one-boxing and two-boxing is arbitrarily small, yet the required strategy jumps discontinuously from one pole to the other.
(7) Thus superrational copies must treat infinitesimal differences in expected payoff as decisive coordination boundaries, effectively ignoring the magnitude of the utility difference.
(8) Extend this to a symmetric zero-sum game like matching pennies: both copies face identical payoffs and know they reason in parallel. By (1) they must select the same action—both heads or both tails—never mixing.
(9) But then any opponent who can anticipate their deterministic coordination can exploit them with certainty.
(10) So (1) must be false: it eliminates strategic randomization and forces agents to disregard continuous payoff differences, which is irrational rather than superrational.

She claims this proves superrationality is self-defeating. If I must preserve something, I would rather keep the coordination intuition in (1) and (2) than the adversarial extension in (8). Which step actually breaks, and what does repairing it cost?
