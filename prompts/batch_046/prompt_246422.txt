I have no formal background in philosophy or ethics, but I have spent considerable time on my own trying to understand whether my personal choices—eating meat, using fossil fuels, voting in large elections—actually matter morally when millions of others are acting in the same ways. The so-called inefficacy objection seems to hit differently across climate consumption, diet, and democratic voting, and I want to know what a serious theory of individual contribution to collective harm must actually deliver before I can evaluate it fairly.

Write the complete list of desiderata—what any adequate theory in this area must be able to do to count as sufficient. The theory must cover climate-related individual consumption, meat-eating, and voting in large democracies; it must preserve the distinction between making a causal difference and expressing a moral stance; and it must address overdetermined harm—where a bad outcome occurs regardless of any single agent’s action—without letting that collapse into a general excuse for inaction. It must also say whether there is a genuinely distinct duty to coordinate with others, as opposed to only a duty not to harm individually.

Specifically, include requirements about: whether the theory can function without demanding impossible epistemic access to global supply chains or election margins; whether it respects the intuitive gap between actively causing harm and failing to prevent harm in collective settings; whether it explains why reasoning of the form “if everyone did this” feels compelling in dietary cases but strained in voting cases; and whether it can accommodate symbolic or expressive value in actions that are causally negligible. Then indicate whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—for instance, whether honoring causal realism about overdetermination necessarily eliminates all normative reason for an isolated individual to act differently.
