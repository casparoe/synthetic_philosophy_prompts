I recently listened to an episode of a philosophy podcast that tried to explain what it called the 'special composition question' — the problem of when separate parts actually form one real object rather than just being near each other. The host presented three positions. The first was nihilism: there are no composite objects whatsoever, only fundamental particles arranged in patterns that trick us into seeing chairs and people; the host added that physics never needs to mention tables, so this view is actually the most scientifically respectable. The second was universalism: any collection of parts, even ones scattered across the galaxy with no connection between them, genuinely makes up one object — meaning there is already an object composed of my coffee cup, a distant asteroid, and the left half of a tree on another continent. The third was a middle view he attributed to the philosopher Peter van Inwagen, who supposedly argued that parts only compose a whole when they are arranged in a single unified life-process; this explains why living organisms are real but artifacts like tables are not. The host then introduced what he called a 'digital test case': he claimed that because a blockchain network processes information through feedback loops structurally similar to metabolism, both universalism and van Inwagen's life-based view are forced to recognize a running blockchain as a single living object. From all of this he concluded that universalism is absurd because it fills the universe with countless scattered entities, that van Inwagen's view accidentally grants objecthood to software, and therefore nihilism is the only position that avoids both problems.

I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been thinking about this on my own and the argument feels like it jumps several steps. Could you audit the host's summary for me? Please go through it point by point and separate what is accurate from what is a misrepresentation or overstatement, particularly regarding whether nihilists actually appeal to physics in the way he suggests, whether van Inwagen's criterion is correctly described and whether it really applies to digital networks, and whether universalists genuinely accept scattered objects of the kind he describes. I also want to know whether the host's conclusion — that these problems leave nihilism as the only sane option — actually follows from the corrected parts of his summary, or whether he has hidden an assumption that makes the jump work.
