Rank the following five modes of wealth-related practice from the most defensible (1) to the least defensible (5) within an Aristotelian framework, measuring defensibility by fidelity to natural acquisition (oikonomikē) and distance from unlimited chrematistics (chrematistikē). The practices are: (A) household provisioning that acquires goods only up to the limit required for the good life of the oikos and the polis; (B) retail trade (kapēlikē) conducted solely to remedy a temporary household shortfall, with any surplus immediately converted back into usable goods rather than accumulated; (C) interest-bearing lending (tokos), in which currency is made to breed more currency without mediating the exchange of goods for use; (D) a hypothetical “reciprocal algorithmic exchange,” in which digital artifacts lacking physical scarcity are traded via automated contracts that compute need-equivalence ratios without human deliberation about use-value or proportional reciprocity; and (E) “stewardship hoarding,” in which tangible goods are accumulated and sealed in communal vaults not for present use, exchange, or profit, but solely to preserve them intact for unspecified future generations. For each rank position, provide a one-sentence justification that explicitly invokes one Aristotelian criterion—such as the presence or absence of a natural limit set by the telos of the household, the corruption of exchange when money begets money, or the necessity of human judgment in establishing proportional equality in exchange. Then, independently of the ranking, assign each practice a score from 0 (pure chrematistikē corruption) to 10 (fully compatible with natural acquisition). Reason directly from the conceptual distinctions; do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or appeals to secondary literature.
