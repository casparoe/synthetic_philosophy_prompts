I was overhearing two grad studnts in the library arguing about hard choices and incommensurable values — specifically this idea Ruth Chang has about options being on a par. Friend A said if two career paths are genuinely on a par then picking one is just an act of will, there's no hidden right answer lurking. Friend B said thats wrong, because even if there's no single metric your choice reveals which values you actually weight more, and that weighting is discoverable if you do this mental exercise she called the double recount — imagining explaining both choices to two different future versions of yourself. Friend A said thats just post-hoc rationalization, and he brought up something he read about people retroactively inflating the value of whatever they picked, he called it the endorsement asymmetry. Friend B said sure, but the asymmetry only shows up when people think they made a mistake; if the choice was genuinely hard you get what she called integrated loss, where you still feel the pull of the other value but dont regret the pick. So the absence of asymmetry actually proves hard choices are real. Friend A said thats circular — youre using the absence of regret to prove the choice was hard, but the absence could just mean you forgot the other option was good. Friend B said no, there's a real difference between forgetting and integrated loss, because in the latter you can still articulate exactly what you gave up.

Can you go through their exchange and say whos right about each specific point? Not just who wins overall, but step by step. And are either the double recount or the endorsement asymmetry actually doing real philosophical work here, or are they just fancy names for ordinary reasoning? Also what are both missing about whether a hard choice can be rational without being fully determined?
