hey, i'm a phd student in moral philosophy (well, technically ethics and social theory) and i'm trying to sort out my actual intuitions about whether real moral dilemmas exist, not just the textbook ones. i keep going back and forth, so i figured i'd lay out my gut verdicts and see if there's a principle that holds them together, or if i'm just rationalizing.

case one: you're a surgeon with one compatible heart and two patients who will die within hours without it. one is your partner, one is a random stranger with the same prognosis. you save your partner. afterwards you feel guilty about the stranger, but you don't think you actually did wrong. my verdict: hard choice, but not a genuine moral dilemma -- the spousal obligation doesn't conflict with medical duty in a symetrical, binding way.

case two: same surgeon, but now both patients are strangers with identical needs and you literally flip a coin. patient A lives, B dies. do you feel appropriate moral residue? i think no -- there's no leftover obligation you violated, just bad luck. so also not a genuine dilemma.

case three: you're a public defender and your client confesses he did commit the crime, but the only evidence is illegally obtained and will be suppressed. if you stay silent, he goes free and might hurt someone; if you reveal the confession, you violate attorney-client priviledge and your professional duty. both obligations seem fully binding, and whichever you do, you've genuinely wronged someone specific. my verdict: this IS a genuine moral dilemma, and you'd be justified in feeling guilty either way.

case four: you promised your kid you'd be at her recital, but on the way there you see a car crash and you're the only one who can administer first aid. you miss the recital and save a life. verdict: not a dilemma, just one obligation clearly overriding another. no appropriate residue about missing the recital.

case five: you're on a funding committee with exactly enough money for one grant. both applicants are equally deserving, but one studies climate mitigation and the other studies a rare linguistic preservation project. you pick climate. do you wrong the linguist? i think yes, somewhat, but it's not a moral dilemma in the strong sense -- it's more like a tragedy of scarcity rather than a conflict of binding duties with specific claimants.

so my pattern seems to be: genuine dilemmas only occur when two agent-relative obligations are genuinely symetrical and neither can be overridden, especially when each has a specific claimant you've wronged. but then case three and case five feel different, even though both involve institutional roles. in case five there's no particular person you've violated a duty to, just a lost opportunity; in case three you've actively breached a specific duty to either client or potential victim.

is there a principle here? maybe something about whether the conflict is between obligations that each have a direct claimant, versus conflicts between general values or institutional goals? and what does it say about the case i keep flipping on: you're a parent and your adult child asks you to lie to their employer about why they missed work (they were hungover). if you lie, you violate honesty; if you tell the truth, you violate loyalty or care? is that a genuine dilemma, or just a failure of character on the kid's part?

also, check consistency: if my principle is right, then in a case where a doctor is the assigned physician for both her own child and a stranger, and she can only save one, does the professional duty neutralize the spousal one? or does the presence of two direct claimants turn it into a real dilemma then?

sorry for the rambling -- just need to know if i'm onto something or if some of these verdicts have to go.
