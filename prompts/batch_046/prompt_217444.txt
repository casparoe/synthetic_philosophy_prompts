Here is an argument I constructed about Ubuntu and relational personhood that seems to prove communities are singular moral agents with no internal justice. (1) On Ubuntu—umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu—personhood is constituted by reciprocal recognition relations (R) rather than by intrinsic properties. (2) If R is genuinely constitutive and not merely causal, then the relata form parts of a single metaphysical unit, analogous to how brain hemispheres compose one mind despite spatial separation. (3) R is transitive across a community: if A stands in R to B and B to C, then A and C are linked within the same relational network. (4) So an entire village linked by R is one distributed person, not a collection of separate ones. (5) If harm occurs within that network—say, A injures B—it is internal damage to the distributed person, like a hand striking its own body. (6) Internal harm is not inter-personal wrong; there is no distinct victim with independent rights, only damage to be repaired for the health of the whole. (7) But Ubuntu's restorative-justice practices presuppose that wrongdoers and victims are distinct moral agents with separate dignity. (8) Therefore relational personhood either dissolves internal moral rights—making punishment and restitution conceptually incoherent—or R is not genuinely constitutive, reducing Ubuntu to a causal-social thesis. I'd like to keep (1) and (7), but I don't see how without giving up either the constitutive reading in (2) or the transitivity in (3). Where is the slip—an equivocation between metaphysical constitution and moral agency, a failure of transitivity for recognition relations, or something else? And what does each rejection cost?
