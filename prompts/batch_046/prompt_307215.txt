In neuroscience popularizations I keep encountering the line that "in split-brain patients the left hemisphere is just an interpreter confabulating explanations," usually deployed to argue that the feeling of being one unified consciousness is always a post-hoc fiction. Can you separate the precise theses this slogan might stand for—e.g., (i) that the left hemisphere invents false causal stories for actions initiated by the right; (ii) that it constructs a first-person self-model without direct access to the motor source of behavior; (iii) that narrative coherence rather than causal integration is what produces personal unity; and (iv) that isolating this interpreter experimentally proves the self is generally an illusion? For each reading, assess whether it is empirically supported in commissurotomy cases, whether it applies defensibly to intact brains, and whether it actually yields the anti-unity conclusion the speaker wants. I am especially interested in whether there is a narrower, defensible intermediate claim—say, that the interpreter generates a single temporal indexical ("I-now") that can become dissociated from its action-source without being false—and whether that captures the clinical phenomenon without collapsing into full-blown eliminativism about the self. Where this slogan is used to shut down discussion of personal identity or free will, tell me which reading is actually carrying the argumentative weight.
