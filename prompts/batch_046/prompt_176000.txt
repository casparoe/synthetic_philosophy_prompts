I'm helping organize a graduate reading group on moral psychology, and during our session on moral worth two members—Riley and Sam—got stuck in a debate about a hypothetical case they constructed together. They imagined a senior researcher who spends every weekend volunteering at a free clinic. She genuinely believes that access to basic medical care is a matter of justice and finds the work deeply meaningful, but she also knows that her visible commitment strengthens her candidacy for a prestigious national ethics board. The specific detail they added is this: she carefully tracks her hours and admits that if she were entirely certain the board would never learn of her service, she would still volunteer, but only about half as often; the extra weekend shifts are genuinely driven by the visibility, yet the base commitment of the initial shifts remains intact and sincerely motivated by concern for patients. Riley argued that this undermines her moral worth for those extra hours because the right reason—respect for human dignity—fails what she called a counterfactual independence test: without the career incentive, those specific actions would not occur, so the moral reason is not actually doing the causal or justificatory work for them. Sam replied that this standard is impossibly austere; nearly every sustained moral action in real life is overdetermined by mixed motives, and demanding that the right reason be counterfactually sufficient on its own would imply that parents who work partly to provide for their children lack moral worth for those efforts, or that anyone who enjoys helping others is thereby tainted. Riley then refined her position: the issue is not global counterfactual sufficiency, but what she termed deliberative transparency—the agent must be able to cite the moral reason alone as adequate justification in her own practical reasoning, without smuggling in the self-interested reason as a necessary supplement for the specific action in question. Sam countered that this mistakes the architecture of mature moral agency; what actually matters is reason integration, where moral and personal reasons form a coherent practical identity rather than competing in isolation. The volunteer is not deceiving herself—she knows both motives are present—but the moral reason retains normative authority within that integrated structure, and requiring motives to be isolable misunderstands how good agents actually deliberate. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not settle it. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: assess Riley's initial counterfactual independence test, Sam's overdetermination reply, Riley's deliberative transparency refinement, and Sam's reason-integration response. For each move, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify your rating. Then say who is more right overall about moral worth in this case, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly regarding whether their dispute is genuinely about the conditions for moral worth itself, or instead about how we ought to describe the psychology of agents who act well.
