I'm writing from a philosophy of mind seminar where, after a session on first-person authority, two participants—Nadia and Elias—continued a debate in the corridor that I found genuinely difficult to resolve. They were discussing whether introspection is trustworthy about the sources of our own judgments, using a hypothetical case study rather than any standard textbook example. I was a bystander and have no stake in either side; I would like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange, including what each is missing and who is more right overall.

Nadia opened with a specific scenario: imagine a decision-maker who consistently chooses charitable options presented in ascending monetary order, yet when asked introspectively reports a sophisticated moral rationale about structural fairness, with no awareness of the ordering effect. Nadia argued that this reveals a systematic unreliability in introspection about motivational causes—the subject accesses a confabulated narrative that feels like direct observation but is actually a post-hoc reconstruction. Elias replied that Nadia conflates causal etiology with rational justification; he claimed the subject's report accurately captures her conscious reasoning process, which genuinely operates through moral framing, and that the spatial trigger is a subpersonal causal fact introspection was never meant to track. Nadia pushed back: if introspection cannot distinguish a genuine moral reason from a spatially induced rationalization that perfectly mimics one, then calling it reliable about motives empties the term—reliability requires correlation with causal origin, not mere internal coherence. Elias then proposed what he termed an epistemic echo distinction: introspection reliably reports the echo of a mental state—its cognitive aftermath, including available rationalizations—while remaining silent on the state's original causal properties. He insisted this is not failure but a boundary condition; introspection delivers access to the conscious workspace, not to subpersonal history. Nadia responded that this saves introspection only by redefining its object, noting that in ordinary contexts when we ask someone why they acted, we seek causal explanation, not merely the narrative present to working memory. Elias conceded that ordinary questions are ambiguous, but maintained that recognizing this ambiguity resolves the tension rather than exposing a flaw in first-person access.

Please adjudicate their moves in sequence: assess Nadia's initial case, Elias's distinction between causal and rational targets, Nadia's coherence objection, the epistemic echo proposal itself, and Nadia's charge that it redefines rather than defends introspection, followed by Elias's final concession and claim. For each step, indicate whether it is clearly correct, partially correct, or clearly mistaken, and say what either party overlooks—particularly whether the echo distinction genuinely protects introspection or merely relocates its failure. Finally, state who is more right overall and on which specific points they should concede ground.
