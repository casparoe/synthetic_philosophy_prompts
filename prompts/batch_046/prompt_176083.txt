I'm preparing a single ninety-minute session for an adult philosophy reading group with no formal training, on whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational. The members will likely arrive with the pop-psychology version: that clinging to a failing course of action is pure emotional weakness, and rationality just means cutting losses. I want to complicate this by introducing the idea that some effort-based sunk-cost commitments carry expressive rather than instrumental value—continuing not because future payoff justifies past loss, but because abandoning now severs a narrative connection to one's earlier self—without using decision-theory formalisms or drifting into economics textbooks. Plan the explanatory arc: which conceptual distinction has to be secured before the expressive-value claim can even register; what single concrete example should carry the session (it must involve a non-monetary, effort-based sunk cost and should fail if replaced by a purely financial case, and you should say exactly what the example must have and lack); the misconceptions the group will predictably form—I'm anticipating that this is just rationalization, that expressive value licenses any stubbornness, and that economists already accounted for this—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off; one closing question that reveals whether they've grasped the distinction rather than merely adopted a more tolerant attitude toward persistence; and what should be deliberately left out at this level with an explicit reason.
