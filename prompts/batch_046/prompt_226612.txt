I'm prepping material for my undergrad seminar on Dewey's experimentalism—specifically that democracy is a mode of social inquiry rather than just ballot-casting, and that knowing and doing aren't really seperate activities in a healthy community. My students always ask for a concrete picture, and I want something that doesn't just rehash the usual textbook examples.

Could you write a short story—say around 700 words—set in a crumbling coastal settlement called the Drift, where residents maintain an old desalination rig that also happens to process shared sensory data? The rig starts emitting contradictory perceptions, and the community has to respond not by deferring to a single technician or taking a simple majority vote, but by running small-scale experiments, treating every explanation as a working hypothesis that might fail, and revising their collective understanding as they go. Focus on an ordinary maintainer—someone who wouldn't call herself a thinker—who gradually realizes that figuring out what's wrong with the rig and physically repairing it are literally the same activity, not two steps. Let the story show that Deweyan democratic-inquiry vibe through the architecture of the place and the rhythm of the labor, not through exposition or direct references. Keep the resolution genuinely open-ended: the problem shouldn't be fully solved, just moved into a new phase of experimentation. And don't worry about being too neat—if there's a typo or rough edge, that's actually perfect for the exercise.
