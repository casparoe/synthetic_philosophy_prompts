Within the literature on cross-cultural differences in folk morality—particularly the WEIRD psychology findings that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic populations exhibit systematically different moral intuitions from much of the global population—there remains an unresolved tension about what these variations imply for moral objectivity. Rather than rehearse the generic universalism-versus-relativism framing, I want to focus on a more granular structural problem: the phenomenon of asymmetrical normative export, in which moral frameworks dominant in WEIRD societies are treated within normative ethics as universal default positions, while non-WEIRD frameworks are treated primarily as descriptive anthropological data rather than as live normative competitors. Assuming a research group with limited resources—say, one senior philosopher, one cognitive anthropologist, and five years—what are the three or four most important open questions or lines of empirical and philosophical work that actually matter for determining whether this asymmetry is a genuine philosophical problem about objectivity or merely a sociological artifact of disciplinary power? Please rank them in order of priority, defend the ordering with explicit criteria such as tractability with current methods, dependency (how many other debates hinge on the answer), and practical import for how normative ethics is practiced, and identify which familiar but lower-yield lines of inquiry you would deliberately deprioritize because resolving them would not substantially change the objectivity debate or is intractable with present tools.
