After a long and somewhat circular argument with a close friend about whether "Western values" names anything real, I tried to formalize my position for a discussion group and realized I may be holding incompatible views; I want an internal consistency audit rather than an external defense. Here are my commitments. First, the phrase lacks any essential definition: any inventory—constitutional democracy, individual rights against the state, secular public reason, free expression, market economies—either includes societies not conventionally called Western or excludes societies that are, so the category operates as a family-resemblance cluster with no necessary and sufficient conditions. Second, despite that lack of essence, there is a historically specific normative cluster—limited government, rights-based legalism, and a form of secular public reason—that was articulated, codified, and institutionally defended primarily within Western European and North American contexts from the early modern period onward. Third, this cluster is defensible on universalist grounds; nothing in its content ties it to European ethnicity or Christian heritage, and presenting it as the proprietary possession of "the West" is both genealogically misleading—similar commitments appear in other traditions—and politically dangerous. Fourth, nevertheless, in practical normative argument—especially regarding democratic backsliding in states that formally aspire to liberal-institutional membership—invoking "Western values" serves a legitimate strategic function: it names an institutional aspiration that those polities have adopted, not an ethnocultural identity being externally imposed. Fifth, I therefore hold that the phrase should be rigorously avoided in descriptive philosophy and comparative social science, yet is permissible and sometimes necessary in advocacy and policy rhetoric, provided it is explicitly framed as referring to an institutional genealogy rather than a civilizational essence.

Please examine these five commitments for joint tenability. I am particularly concerned that the denial of essence in the first commitment undermines the specificity claimed in the second; that the universalist claim in the third conflicts with the strategic use permitted in the fourth, since strategic invocation seems to trade on exactly the civilizational framing I reject; and that my fifth commitment tries to split the difference in a way that collapses into contradiction. For each pair that is genuinely in tension—not merely apparently so—rank them by severity, specify what has to give, and identify the least costly revision. Also tell me whether there is one single change that resolves the worst conflicts, or whether repairing one tension exacerbates another. Do not treat all conflicts as equal; discriminate carefully, and flag where my reasoning depends on a distinction—such as between genealogical origin and normative justification—that might not do the work I need it to do.
