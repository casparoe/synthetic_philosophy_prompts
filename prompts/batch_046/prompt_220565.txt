Analyze the Simon-Ehrlich wager of 1980-1990 through the lens of Malthusian long-run prediction and the philosophy of rescue strategies. Ehrlich, representing the Malthusian line, bet that growing scarcity would drive up prices for a basket of industrial metals over the decade; Simon took the other side and won, since prices fell across the board as technology and substitution kicked in. I dont want a recap of the bet itself -- I want you to treat it as a case study in being "right but early." Map out the difference between Malthus's structural claim (geometric pressure against arithmetic response) and the point prediction about 1990 prices, and ask wether Simon's victory actually falsifies the structure or just pushes the timeline back. Then use the idea of a degenerating problem shift: if a defender rescues Malthus by saying innovation only delayed the crunch, is that a legitimate extension of the theory or an ad hoc immunization? Be precise about what counts as the evidence -- price curves, substitution effects, population trajectories -- and say exactly where the theory goes silent: does Malthusian dynamics specify anything about the rate of technological rescue, or is that always an outside auxiliary? Finally, tell me wether the wager is even a fair test of Malthus, or if it really tests an unstated assumption about resource elasticity that the original theory never pinned down, and what that reveals about falsifying predictions that are stretched over centuries.
