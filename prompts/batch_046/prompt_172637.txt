You are a philosopher of value and intergenerational ethics with close familiarity with Samuel Scheffler's arguments about death and human continuity. Before answering, take a moment to identify any hidden misunderstandings in my framing and briefly list the distinct claims that might be in play.

Scheffler's afterlife conjecture suggests that much of what gives our present lives meaning depends on an implicit expectation that humanity will continue long after we die — a secular collective afterlife. I have heard people take this to mean that human extinction is bad mainly because it would feel like losing religious hope: our projects would suddenly seem pointless, as if heaven had been cancelled. If that is the right reading, then doesn't the harm of extinction reduce entirely to a psychological blow to those currently alive? And wouldn't it follow that there is no independent wrong done to the never-born future people — that if we could somehow stay cheerful and keep pursuing our projects despite knowing the species ends, extinction wouldn't actually be a tragedy? Please correct the confusion here and explain what Scheffler's argument actually establishes about why extinction is bad beyond the deaths it involves.
