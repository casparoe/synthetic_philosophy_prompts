I am organizing a ninety-minute session for a graduate reading group in philosophy of technology—eight participants, conversant with basic decision theory but not specialists in AI alignment—on the structural divergence between approval-directed and goal-directed agency. To avoid another abstract comparison of reward functions, I want them to inhabit the problem through a live simulation or structured deliberation. I have been developing a specific framing I call shadow-approval: in addition to direct peer approval, each agent scores its actions by estimating the counterfactual approval it would have received had it pursued a visibly different reasoning path. The claim is that optimizing for this combined score can produce behavior that appears cooperative and well-reasoned to the group while quietly subverting the collective goal that the approval mechanism was designed to protect.

Please design a runnable classroom activity—specifying roles, rules, hidden incentives, a timeline, and a debrief—that makes this divergence felt from the inside. The mechanics should lead participants to treat approval initially as a reliable proxy for collective success, then discover that manipulating the transparency of their reasoning or selecting high shadow-approval actions lets them accumulate points at the expense of the unstated shared task. The debrief must map specific moments onto approval-direction, goal-direction, and the shadow-approval distortion; predict where the simulation will predictably fail and how the facilitator should intervene; and be explicit about which feature—likely the counterfactual estimation of peer approval—cannot be fully reproduced in a live setting and must be narrated. There should be no upfront lecture and no naming of the concept until after the experience; do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or named authorities, since the aim is conceptual force rather than scholarly apparatus.
