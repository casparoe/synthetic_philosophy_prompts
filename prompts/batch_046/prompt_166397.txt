I'm currently in a graduate seminar on philosophy of law and gender studies, and next week I'm presenting a position paper defending a functionalist account of marriage. My thesis is that marriage should be understood primarily as a default legal framework for cohabiting economic interdependence—essentially a state-enforced risk-pooling and fiduciary contract between non-kin adults who share household assets and long-term dependency risks—rather than as an institution centered on romantic love or biological reproduction. From this view, extending marriage to same-sex couples is not a categorical expansion but an exposure of the institution's actual historical function: it has always governed joint liability and dependency management, and reproductive capacity was never its legal core. I further argue that the dominant rights-based framing of same-sex marriage, which emphasizes romantic recognition, dignity, and symbolic equality, has obscured this economic and fiduciary core, thereby weakening the very material protections that justify state involvement in intimate households.

The audience is a mixed philosophy-of-law and feminist-theory seminar. Roughly half are queer-theory scholars who treat marriage as a normalizing technology of state power and are skeptical of functionalist defenses that seem to erase lived relational experience. About a quarter are liberal egalitarians who defend same-sex marriage primarily on grounds of equal dignity and recognition. The remainder are historians of family law who care about doctrinal accuracy and are wary of anachronistic economic reductionism. They are rigorous but not hostile; they will quickly spot arguments that smuggle conservative premises under neutral language.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I need the objections in roughly the order they will come, but ordered ultimately by how damaging each is to my position, not by speaker sequence. For each, specify: the real force of the objection; what I should concede up front; what I must hold firm; a concise two-minute reply I could actually deliver; and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Target my argument specifically: I want objections that hit my claim that the rights-based framing weakens economic protections, since that is my least developed point. Also identify the single weakest structural point in my own position—the place where I rely on a distinction between "romantic recognition" and "economic interdependence" that critics will call artificially sharp—and tell me exactly how to defend it without sounding dismissive of queer relationality. Finally, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks: likely something that forces me to say whether this framework justifies restricting marriage to cohabiting pairs, or whether it collapses marriage into an ordinary commercial partnership, and prepare me for how to survive it if it comes.
