Write a short dialogue between a proponent of prior-interior consequentialism and a structural realist who denies it. The first should argue that because the Solomonoff prior assigns nonzero weight to computable programs containing conscious agents—particularly simpler ones that receive higher prior weight—moral decision theory ought to account for suffering instantiated within those weighted programs, not merely within causally accessible worlds. The second should maintain that the universal prior is purely an epistemic scoring rule, not an ontological census: being represented in a high-weight program does not confer moral status absent causal interaction. Have them examine a specific, somewhat contrived case: two climate-prediction algorithms are available, and the simpler one (which receives greater prior weight) inadvertently contains, as an internal subroutine, a conscious simulation experiencing suffering, whereas the more complex alternative does not. The consequentialist should claim that selecting the simpler algorithm therefore carries genuine moral weight because it instantiates or favors suffering inside a high-measure region of the prior; the realist should respond by directly engaging the nonzero-measure argument—carefully distinguishing epistemic simplicity-weight from ontological presence—rather than dismissing the scenario as fictional. Ensure both characters are equally competent, address each other's actual reasoning rather than talking past one another, and avoid treating either position as a strawman.
