I have been studying the metaphysical debate about whether a statue is identical to the lump of clay that makes it up. The usual argument notes that they share the same matter and location, yet differ in modal properties—the clay survives being squashed into a ball, while the statue does not—so Leibniz's law implies they are distinct. I understand that some philosophers, such as Lynne Rudder Baker, propose a middle path called constitution, where the clay constitutes the statue without being identical to it. My difficulty is this: if constitution is a real physical relation rather than a mere shape-description, then the clay ought to be a proper part of the statue, comparable to an engine being part of a car; yet if the clay is a proper part, then altering the statue's shape should damage that part, which it clearly does not, since the clay remains intact. Does this mean that constitution is not a part-whole relation at all, but rather something like causal dependence, where the statue exists only so long as the clay maintains a certain form? I am particularly unsure how this applies when one block of clay is simultaneously arranged into two separate sculptures in a shared installation: if the clay is not part of either work, how can both works genuinely be made of it?
