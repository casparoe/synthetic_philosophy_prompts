Exam question: Consider a hypothetical direct-democracy system modeled partly on Swiss cantonal practice, governed by three rules: (i) constitutional amendments pass by simple popular majority; (ii) an initiative qualifies for the ballot if signatures reach 5% of registered voters; (iii) an amendment may include a binding protocol that changes the rules for future amendments, but that protocol takes effect only if the amendment itself passes.

In Year 1, Initiative A lowers the signature threshold to 2%. In Year 2, Initiative B is filed under that 2% rule. B proposes to restore the 5% threshold and to add a district-majority protocol, which requires both a popular majority and a majority of cantonal districts for any future change to the signature rule. B receives 53% of the popular vote but carries only 4 of 12 districts.

Does Initiative B take effect? Once in force, what rules govern a subsequent Initiative C that seeks to lower the threshold again? Be precise about temporal application and about whether B's own qualification is affected by the rules it enacts.

Student answer:

Initiative B takes effect, since 53% satisfies the simple-majority requirement in rule (i). The district-majority protocol embedded in B applies prospectively to future amendments, so B's failure to win a majority of districts does not block its own passage. This tracks the distinction—familiar from Rousseau's discussion of the general will—between the procedural conditions for enacting a law and the conditions for its legitimate administration across territory, where geographic uniformity matters for the latter but not for the former.

Once B is enacted, the threshold returns to 5% and the binding protocol is active, so Initiative C must collect signatures equal to 5% of registered voters and must win both a popular majority and a district majority. There is, however, a consistency tension: B was qualified using the 2% rule that it immediately repeals. If we apply the principle that an initiative must satisfy the procedural standards it establishes, B implicitly subjects itself to the restored 5% qualification standard, which means B never should have reached the ballot. Under that view, B fails, the 2% rule survives, and C could qualify under the lower bar without facing the district requirement. Still, procedural rules ought to be assessed as they stand at the time of qualification and vote, not as retroactively altered by the amendment's content, so B stands and the stricter regime governs C.
