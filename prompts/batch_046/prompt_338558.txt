I was chatting with my friend over coffee about this education policy I'm covering for the science-and-ideas desk, and I think there's a hidden philosophy problem buried in the district paperwork. There's this new mandate — they call it the Heritage Curriculum Rule — that forces every high school history program to split its budget 45/45/0 across two tracks with nothing left over. Track one is pure Monumental: giant portraits and inspirational quotes from 'great minds' meant to motivate current students. Track two is pure Antiquarian: they have to preserve every student lab notebook, every local newspaper clipping about science, every broken beaker in climate-controlled storage, with no selection criteria except 'it existed'. But the Critical track — where kids actually compare past values to present ones and ask if those great minds were wrong — is banned outright; teachers can loose their jobs if they assign grades based on moral judgment of historical figures. The board claims preservation and inspiration are self-evident goods, yet they treat evaluation as actively dangerous. So what Nietzschean theory of history's uses and disadvantages does that 45/45/0 split actually presuppose? Does it assume monumental and antiquarian history can thrive without critical history, or does it hide a seperate view about knowledge and progress? Reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments behind the policy, distinguish what it strictly needs from what merely fits, and say what a school actually built on critical history would have to change structurally.
