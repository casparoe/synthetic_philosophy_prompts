Restate Strawson’s distinction between reactive attitudes and the objective attitude—specifically his claim that excuses operate inside the framework of interpersonal moral demand while exemptions (e.g., severe mental disorder) suspend that framework altogether—using only the vocabulary of contract law: breach, excuse, justification, contractual capacity, strict liability, and vicarious liability. Assume fluency in contract doctrine but no background in philosophy. Then deliver the residue report: which reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation) map cleanly onto legal responses to breach and which only distort; whether the objective attitude corresponds to treating a party as lacking capacity, to applying strict liability, or to something else entirely; and whether anything essential is lost—particularly Strawson’s point that adopting the objective attitude is not a morally superior stance but a withdrawal from interpersonal engagement—when the translation is done in institutional legal categories that are already normative and rule-governed. In particular, examine whether contract law’s distinction between excuse and exemption genuinely captures Strawson’s distinction, or whether the legal framework’s built-in objectivity obscures the phenomenological shift he describes.
