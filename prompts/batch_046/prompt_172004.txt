I'm organizing a graduate reading group on philosophy of religion, and I've noticed that discussions of divine hiddenness tend to collapse several distinct argumentative structures into a single complaint about God's absence. To keep our analysis precise, I want to evaluate five formulations separately: (A) the evidential formulation, which treats widespread reasonable non-belief as Bayesian counter-evidence to theism; (B) the deontological formulation, which holds that a maximally loving being would have an obligation to secure belief-conditions for all capable agents, and that the failure to do so undermines either omnibenevolence or existence; (C) the relational formulation, which claims that hiddenness frustrates the possibility of the reciprocal personal relationship that many theistic traditions take to be central to religious life; (D) the epistemic-justice formulation, which argues that hiddenness distributes epistemic access unevenly across cultural and cognitive contexts in ways incompatible with divine fairness; and (E) the existential formulation, which contends that hiddenness generates a specific form of existential anxiety or meaning-instability that undermines the value of religious commitment itself.

Please rank these five formulations by their logical independence from auxiliary theological premises—specifically, by how little they require prior assumptions about the nature of divine love, human cognitive capacities, or the goods of belief. Assign each to a tier (Top / Middle / Bottom), and in one sentence explain why it belongs there. Then, separately, rate each formulation on a scale of 0 to 1 according to how effectively the soul-making theodicy—according to which hiddenness is necessary for genuine moral and spiritual development—actually addresses it, with a brief reason for each score.
