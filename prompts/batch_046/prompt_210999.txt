Below are notes for a short essay I am drafting. The central claim is that guilt is the more defensible primary moral emotion for societies committed to individual moral agency and reform, but that the choice between guilt and shame is not purely psychological—it is partly institutional, mediated by what I call "moral emotional architecture": the ways legal, educational, and religious institutions channel emotional responses toward either action-specific or identity-constitutive evaluation. I argue that shame is not inherently toxic but becomes so when it lacks structural boundaries; I point to certain restorative justice frameworks in Indigenous legal traditions as cases where shame operates within formal procedural limits and thus functions more like a targeted sanction than an identity verdict. I then ask whether a culture can deliberately shift its emotional architecture—say, through penal reform—without merely substituting one coercive emotional regime for another.

Please comment on these notes. Specifically: Is the distinction between action-targeting and identity-targeting robust enough to do the work I assign it, or does it collapse once we examine the restorative justice examples? Does my framing of "moral emotional architecture" add anything beyond standard sociological accounts of moral culture? And is there an internal tension in arguing both that guilt is generally preferable and that shame can be rehabilitated through institutional design?

In your response, please separate what is well-established in the moral psychology and comparative legal literature from what is genuinely contested or speculative—particularly regarding whether cultures can "choose" dominant moral emotions and whether restorative practices genuinely deploy shame in a bounded way. Do not present any contested interpretive claim about these traditions or about institutional determinism as if it were consensus.
