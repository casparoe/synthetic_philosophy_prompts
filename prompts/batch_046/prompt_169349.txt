I was arguing with a friend in my enviro-history seminar about whether volcanoes and germs can be "historical actors" without us just falling back into environmental determinism. We kept circling this double-bind: if you deny agency to climate or microbes you're being anthropocentric, but if you grant it you seem to treat human politics as structurally inevitable, especially when historians borrow intentional language like "the eruption forced the treaty negotiations" but then deny the volcano any moral responsibility. I want an exam question for an upper-level philosophy of history course that makes students navigate this precisely, not just summarize Diamond or Latour.

Please draft a 90-minute essay question asking students whether attributing historical agency to non-human forces—specifically the 1815 Tambora eruption, the 1816 climate anomalies, and concurrent cholera dynamics—inevitably collapses into determinism, or if there's a coherent middle ground. They must distinguish causal efficacy from intentional agency, address what I'd call the "asymmetric agency problem" (where intentional vocabulary gets applied to microbes and aerosols but human responses are still treated as predetermined), and explain why invoking "distributed causation" alone doesnt solve the determinism worry. The prompt should be answerable without citing specific texts, and it shouldnt ask for a bibliography or reading list since this is a closed-book exam.

Then write a model answer of about 700 words that actually does the philosophical work rather than just labeling positions, and include a marking scheme with five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth totaling 20, and a one-line note on how candidates typically botch each point. Make sure the difficulty comes from having to hold the distinction steady under pressure, not from reciting jargon they memorized.
