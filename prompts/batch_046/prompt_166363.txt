At a recent workshop, two attendees—Mira and Theo—debated illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. Mira opened by defending the view: she said that reports of “what it’s like” to feel pain or see red misrepresent purely physical states as bearing an irreducible phenomenal quality, and that neuroscience can eventually explain this misrepresentation without leaving any explanatory gap. Theo objected that illusionism must deny not just phenomenal properties but the very “seeming” itself, because any intentional seeming already carries phenomenal character—so illusionists end up denying the data they claim to explain. Mira replied that “seeming” can be analyzed as a purely cognitive disposition to form false judgments, analogous to how static film frames seem to move without actual motion being present. Theo countered that the film analogy fails: in that case there is still a visual experience with phenomenal character (successive frames appear continuous), whereas illusionism eliminates phenomenal character entirely, leaving nothing analogous to the experience. Mira then argued that this misses the point—illusionism treats phenomenal reports like obsolete scientific posits such as phlogiston, and that the “explanatory gap” is just an artifact of our conceptual framework. Theo responded that phlogiston was an explanatory posit, whereas phenomenal consciousness is a presentational given; comparing them confuses appearance with its mechanism, and that illusionists therefore collapse the appearance/reality distinction in a way that renders their view unfalsifiable and conceptually incoherent. They asked me to referee. Please adjudicate move by move—Mira’s opening defense, Theo’s first objection about seeming requiring phenomenal character, Mira’s film/disposition reply, Theo’s distinction between film experience and illusionism, Mira’s phlogiston analogy, and Theo’s presentational-given reply—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing about how illusionism relates to the transparency of introspection and the possibility of a self-model that represents itself as having experiences it lacks.
