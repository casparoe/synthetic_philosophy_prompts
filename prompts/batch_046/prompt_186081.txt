I have recently begun studying Madhyamaka philosophy with some urgency after a close friend, who practices within the Tibetan Gelug tradition, received a serious medical prognosis; I want to understand the conceptual framework through which she understands impermanence and suffering, but I am conscious that my analytic training may distort the material. A fellow student in philosophy offered me the following summary of Nāgārjuna's view: Nāgārjuna deploys the tetralemma—affirmation, negation, both, neither—to demonstrate that every entity is literally non-existent, so that emptiness (śūnyatā) is equivalent to absolute nothingness. On this reading, the four corners represent exhaustive logical possibilities, and Nāgārjuna rejects all four in order to show that logic itself collapses, leaving only an inexpressible mystical silence rather than any philosophical thesis. The student adds that Madhyamaka therefore denies both causation and conventional reality, since if nothing exists then nothing can arise or depend upon anything else, and that contemporary scholars who speak of emptiness of inherent existence (svabhāva) are merely diluting Nāgārjuna's radical nihilism to render it intellectually respectable. Which elements of this account would a careful Madhyamaka scholar accept, and which are subtle misrepresentations—specifically with regard to the relation between emptiness and conventional truth (saṃvṛti-satya), the function of the tetralemma as a dialectical instrument (prasaṅga) rather than a formal logical grid, and the distinction between lacking svabhāva and lacking all existence whatsoever? Does the conclusion that Madhyamaka is an irremediable nihilism actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary, and if not, what is the precise argumentative structure—particularly the doctrine of the emptiness of emptiness itself—that blocks the slide from absence of inherent nature to total non-being?
