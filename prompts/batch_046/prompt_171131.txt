I'm prepping interview questions for a podcast episode tuesday with an unamed historian—she'd rather stay anonymous since she's still in peer review—who just published a book called The Server Bed: Infrastuctural Geology and the Digital Longue Durée. The core idea is Braudel's old distinction between geography, climate, and those slow-moving structures that outlast events, except she's applying it to submarine fiber-optic routes, the sedimentary layers of data storage, and the thermal footprint of server farms. She argues that treating election cycles or viral news storms as primary histroy is the same mistake Braudel called histoire événementielle, only now the slow geography is made of silicon, copper, and cooling water.

I need about eight sharp questions that actually engage the argument, not generic "how did you get interested in philosophy of histroy?" stuff. At least one should press whether digital infrastructure is genuinely slow enough—fiber gets rerouted, data centers are decommissioned in a decade, which feels like event-time, not Braudel's centuries-long rhythms. Another should tackle individual agency: if a whistleblower leaks classified documents, is that an event bubbling up against the structure, or is the leak itself structurally predetermined by server layout and access protocols? For each of those, I want a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give—say, if she claims the physical substrate still moves on geological time, ask whether she's just smuggling climate determinism back in through the back door. I also want one question on climate change itself: is warming part of the longue durée, or has carbon feedback pushed it into accelerated event-time?
