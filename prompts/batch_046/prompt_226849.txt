I am currently taking an upper-level course on philosophies of history, and I have drafted a short essay comparing cyclical accounts of historical change. Rather than surveying the standard taxonomy, I have tried to isolate a mechanism that might link two apparently different cyclical models. Before I develop it further, I would welcome critical comments on whether the comparison is sound, whether my framing introduces anachronism, and whether I have overlooked an obvious counter-example.

Here is the core of the draft.

Both Polybius and Ibn Khaldun present history as governed by a recurring pattern rather than by linear progress. In Polybius’s account of the anacyclosis, constitutions rotate through monarchy, kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy and ochlocracy, eventually collapsing back into monarchy when the citizens, corrupted by private interest, forget the civic virtue that originally sustained balanced government. Ibn Khaldun’s cycle, by contrast, tracks the rise and fall of dynasties through the waxing and waning of ʿaṣabiyyah: tribal solidarity generates conquest and state-building; urban luxury and administrative centralisation then erode that solidarity; a weaker dynasty falls to a fresh tribal group with stronger cohesion. The conventional comparison treats these as parallel biological or moral metaphors—constitutions ageing like organisms, dynasties decaying like bodies.

My essay proposes that both cycles are driven less by a natural law of ageing than by a failure of institutional memory, which I call “institutional amnesia.” In Polybius, the corruption of democracy into ochlocracy occurs not merely because citizens become greedy, but because successive generations forget why constitutional checks were originally instituted: they inherit the form of popular rule without the collective memory of tyranny that made it necessary. In Ibn Khaldun, the urbanised descendants of conquerors forget the material hardship and mutual dependence that produced tribal loyalty; luxury is not just a moral failure but an erasure of the conditions that made solidarity intelligible. If this framing holds, the cyclical pattern is not an iron law of nature but a recursive failure to transmit the contextual knowledge that made an institution viable.

I then ask whether Spengler and Toynbee break or extend this model. Spengler’s “civilisation” phase looks like extreme institutional amnesia: the culture forgets its formative creative energy and enters a rationalised, decadent winter. Toynbee’s “challenge-and-response” framework, however, seems to allow linear escape: if a civilisation responds creatively rather than mechanically, it might avoid recurrence. So my tentative conclusion is that Polybius and Ibn Khaldun share an amnesia-driven cycle, Spengler radicalises it into cultural fatalism, and Toynbee introduces a linear escape hatch that depends on whether institutional memory can be actively reconstructed rather than passively inherited.

My questions are these. First, is the “institutional amnesia” framing defensible, or does it anachronistically import modern concepts of collective memory into ancient and medieval texts? Second, have I flattened an important distinction—perhaps Polybius’s constitutionalism is normative in a way that Ibn Khaldun’s sociology is not—so that comparing them through a single mechanism misrepresents one or both? Third, does Toynbee’s notion of “creative response” genuinely interrupt the cycle, or is it better read as just another phase in a larger recurrence? Finally, is the essay interesting enough to justify developing, or does it collapse into a vague metaphor?

Please respond in British English, and feel free to address whichever parts seem most in need of correction. I will add proper citations and references myself once the argument is settled.
