You serve as the chair of an institutional review board for a large interdisciplinary research university. Every quarter you must evaluate pairs of competing grant proposals, departmental restructuring plans, or peer-review panels, and sign a binding recommendation: either the two traditions represent incommensurable Kuhnian paradigms that require separate institutional tracks, funding streams, and evaluation criteria, or they are normal-science disagreements resolvable within a single shared framework. You have forty-five minutes per pair, access only to the submitted documents and your own notes, no external literature search, and you must justify the recommendation in writing to a non-philosopher provost.

Write a reusable decision procedure—ordered tests, a flowchart in words, and a concise set of applied questions—that operationalizes Kuhn’s account of paradigm shifts and incommensurability for this administrative role. The procedure must distinguish genuine incommensurability from mere interdisciplinary rivalry, semantic drift, or political resistance, and it must handle the hard cases: vocabulary that overlaps but carries divergent meanings; different standards for what counts as an explanatory success; and situations in which each side treats the other’s central achievements as anomalies rather than solutions.

Embed within the procedure a specific “reciprocal formalization stress-test”: require each camp to state the opposing camp’s central anomaly, core explanatory target, and criterion of success using only the opponent’s own technical vocabulary, accepted methods, and explicitly endorsed ontological commitments. Define precisely what result of this test indicates incommensurability (successful formalization paired with principled rejection), what indicates translation failure (inability to formalize without contradiction or category error), and what indicates resolvable disagreement (successful formalization followed by agreement on the problem’s importance but disagreement about its weight).

Provide worked applications to three cases. First, two climate-modeling groups that share differential equations and datasets but disagree on whether predictive accuracy or mechanistic transparency is the non-negotiable criterion of success, with each treating the other’s best results as methodologically compromised. Second, two neuroscience proposals—one reductionist, one enactivist—that both invoke “cognitive architecture” but embed the term in incompatible ontological frameworks, so that one camp’s solved problem is the other’s misdescribed phenomenon. Third, an unwelcome case in which the board has strong political and budgetary incentives to merge two traditions, yet the procedure returns that they are incommensurable and must be institutionally separated; show the signed recommendation and explain what safeguards prevent the board from overriding the result.

For each case, run the ordered tests step by step, apply the stress-test, record each side’s formalization attempt, and state the final recommendation with its justification. Mark explicitly every point where judgment cannot be eliminated, every condition under which the procedure is designed to misfire (e.g., strategic vocabulary manipulation by one camp, hidden shared metaphysical commitments), and every institutional constraint—time pressure, lack of technical expertise, political sign-off—that limits reliability. Ground the procedure in what Kuhn’s framework actually implies about lexical structures, anomaly recognition, and the absence of a neutral algorithm for theory choice, but translate those implications into actions a non-philosopher administrator can execute without consulting external texts. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or requests for further sources.
