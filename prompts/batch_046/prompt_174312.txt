I'm trying to clarify my view on church-state arrangements by checking whether my gut judgments share a principle. Where citizens register with a denomination and the state collects a designated tax for it—without doctrinal control or general-revenue subsidy—I think that's permissible cooperation. Where a state bans visible religious expression in public schools to enforce a secular public sphere, I think it's wrong: it substitutes one comprehensive doctrine for another. Where one church is formally established—the monarch as its head, bishops in the legislature, legal monopolies on rites for its members—I think that's unjust, even if membership is voluntary. Where tax exemptions and public access are granted to religious bodies but denied to secular ethical communities that reject supernatural claims, I think that's hidden privilege, not neutrality. I'm also uncertain about a proposal to maintain church buildings over four centuries old with general tax revenue, but only for architectural preservation and secular cultural programming, with worship explicitly excluded—sometimes it strikes me as harmless heritage policy, sometimes as privileging religious architecture. Is there a single principle that captures the first four verdicts, what does it say about the heritage case, and which judgment must I abandon if the principle is to remain simple? Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: state the principle, identify the clause that distinguishes the acceptable tax collection from formal establishment, identify the clause that condemns both exclusionary secularism and religious tax privilege, state the verdict on the heritage proposal, and name which of my original judgments the simplest version of the principle cannot accommodate.
