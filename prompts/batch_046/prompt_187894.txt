I am leading a single ninety-minute seminar for first-year philosophy-of-science graduate students drawn from biology, physics, and history of science. Most arrive believing that Karl Popper rejected Darwinian evolution as an unscientific tautology and that this judgment demonstrates a flaw in his falsifiability criterion. I want a teaching plan for the session—not the lecture itself.

Structure the plan as follows. First, state the dependency chain: which concept must be secured before the distinction between a metaphysical research programme and a falsifiable theory can even be introduced, and why Popper's demarcation criterion should not be presented initially as a value judgment on evolutionary biology. Second, identify the single concrete, constructed case—perhaps a quantitative laboratory prediction about the rate of adaptive mutation under directional selection—that should carry the entire session; specify exactly what this case must possess (a genuinely risky predictive claim derived from selection theory) and what it must lack (dependence on the tautological formulation that survivors are by definition the fittest) in order to do that work. Third, map the misconceptions in the order students are likely to voice them—beginning with the belief that Popper was simply anti-Darwin, moving through the confusion that unfalsifiable equals pseudoscientific, and ending with the assumption that Popper later performed a complete reversal rather than a refinement—and indicate the precise point in the explanatory arc where each should be intercepted. Fourth, propose one specific closing question that would reveal whether a student understands that Darwinism can operate simultaneously as a metaphysical framework and as a generator of testable science, as distinct from merely repeating that Popper changed his view. Fifth, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—such as the technical details of Popper's later essays on evolutionary epistemology or any bibliographic recommendations—and say aloud why omitting it protects the core distinction. Keep the plan rigorous, avoid quoting specific texts verbatim, and do not ask for readings.
