I'm a novelist preparing the opening remarks for a three-day workshop I'm co-hosting with a philosopher of language; the audience is split evenly between fiction writers and philosophers, with a few editors and critics mixed in. I want the speech—about eight minutes—to take David Lewis's analysis of convention as its structural spine, not as decoration. Specifically, I want to work with the idea that conventions are arbitrary solutions to coordination problems that stabilize through mutual expectation rather than design or explicit agreement, and that once they're entrenched they become invisible precisely because everyone expects everyone else to expect them.

The constraint this must impose on what I can say: I should be able to explain why certain narrative conventions—say, the unreliability of a first-person narrator being signaled through contradiction rather than through an explicit flag—feel inevitable to experienced readers even though they were never legislated, and why breaking them requires more than individual talent; it requires shifting a shared expectation. At the same time, I must avoid saying anything that treats these conventions as natural laws or as freely chosen rules, because Lewis's framework rules out both.

To keep this from being a lecture, I need a concrete, somewhat invented case study woven through the remarks: imagine a small, insular literary community—say, a regional magazine collective—where over two decades an unstated convention has emerged that any flashback in a story must be grammatically signaled by a sudden shift to the past perfect continuous, never by typographical markers like italics, line breaks, or headers. The convention is arbitrary in its specific form, yet rational in its function; newcomers learn it not through a manual but through the shock of violating mutual expectation. I want to use this to show how a convention can be simultaneously invisible to insiders and painfully obvious to outsiders, and why noticing it doesn't automatically dissolve its grip.

The speech should not include any bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or named texts; assume the philosophers in the room recognize the framework, and don't burden the writers with names or dates. It should also avoid treating the workshop itself as a place to "break rules" in a naive sense—instead, the closing should frame our purpose as making visible the conventions we've stopped noticing, while acknowledging that visibility alone doesn't free us from them. Write the remarks in a voice appropriate for a novelist addressing peers: literate, slightly formal, but not academic. Include one moment where the philosophical distinction genuinely changes what I can claim about writing—where the structure of the argument forces me to qualify or withdraw something that would otherwise be tempting to say.
