You serve on the Institutional Review and Funding Ethics Board of the Meridian Foundation for Cognitive Science. The board must vote on Proposal 2024-CL-77, titled "The Strange Loop Exhibit: Engineering a Self-Referential Symbol In Silico." The applicants—an interdisciplinary team of computational neuroscientists and philosophers of mind—seek both research funding and permission to install a running system in a public museum wing. Their technical aim is to realize Douglas Hofstadter's conception of the self as a strange loop: not mere first-order state tracking, but the emergence within the architecture of a symbol, designated I*, that refers back to the system's own symbol-generating processes as a unified agent. To distinguish this from conventional self-monitoring, the applicants propose a "recursive symbol-binding architecture" (RSBA) that generates what they call a "strange-loop index"—a second-order symbol that binds the system's representations of itself into a single referential loop.

Your co-chair, a philosopher of science, urges rejection. She argues that RSBA may be technically impressive, but attributing an "I" to the output is an interpretive projection rather than an engineered achievement; she warns that funding the project risks institutionalizing anthropomorphism in AI ethics and blurring the line between simulating self-reference and instantiating it. A board member from philosophy of mind counters that the proposal offers a rare empirical-philosophical test case: if a system can maintain a stable I* that functions as a self-referential symbol, we must take seriously whether self-reference alone is sufficient for the kind of unified agency associated with a self. He argues that rejecting the proposal on metaphysical grounds would prejudge an open question.

Draft a formal policy memo addressed to the full board recommending a specific, actionable course—approval with conditions, conditional approval pending further review, or rejection. The memo should:

1. Articulate the philosophical stakes as they apply specifically to this proposal, using the applicants' own distinction between first-order self-monitoring and the second-order strange-loop index (I*) generated by RSBA, without reducing the issue to a generic debate about whether large language models are conscious.

2. State your recommendation clearly and specify the concrete conditions—technical, ethical, or institutional—that must be satisfied for the recommendation to hold, including criteria by which reviewers can determine whether I* is functioning as a genuine self-referential symbol rather than as a syntactic label or marketing term.

3. Present, in its strongest form, the consideration that supports the opposite recommendation—particularly the concern that RSBA presupposes rather than demonstrates the emergence of a self—and explain precisely why that concern does not carry the day, or, if it does, why it nonetheless justifies your recommended outcome.

4. Frame the board's decision so that it does not require resolving the metaphysics of selfhood definitively; the recommendation should turn on institutional risk, scientific merit, and ethical safeguards, with any metaphysical ambiguity treated as a reason for conditional oversight rather than as a veto.

Keep the memo concise, suitable for a vote, and suitable for members who are not specialists in philosophy of mind. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. Do not quote from any published text; paraphrase Hofstadter's concept of the strange loop in your own terms. Ensure the document treats both the technical architecture and the museum-public-interaction component.
