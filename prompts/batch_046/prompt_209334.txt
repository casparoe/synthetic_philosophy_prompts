As a high school student studying philosophy of mind, I have adopted what I call the continuous-causal-inheritance thesis regarding gradual mind uploading. The view holds that if a biological brain is replaced neuron-by-neuron with synthetic analogues—each integrated into the active network before its biological predecessor is removed—then numerical personal identity is preserved, provided there is never even a millisecond-long gap in which the global workspace of consciousness is causally disconnected from its immediate prior state. I take this unbroken causal thread to be sufficient for identity, with narrative psychological continuity being a welcome but secondary feature. Consequently, the Ship of Theseus problem seems to dissolve: there is no discrete moment of replacement, only a continuous substrate transition. But I want an honest assessment of what would count against this position. Would it be defeated if empirical neuroscience showed that consciousness depends on specific biological dynamics—such as particular glial-cell modulation patterns or neurotransmitter diffusion gradients—that synthetic substrates could replicate functionally but never exactly? Or is that merely an awkward limitation? More structurally, suppose a split-replacement procedure created two parallel synthetic streams, both maintaining direct causal links back to the original brain; if my view implies there are now two numerically identical persons, is that fatal, or can it be absorbed? Additionally, what weight should be given to first-person reports: if subjects who underwent partial replacement consistently described feeling like an entirely different person despite an intact causal chain, should that be treated as decisive evidence, or can a defender dismiss it as confabulation? Please distinguish the objections that would force abandonment of the thesis from those that merely require refinement, rank them from fatal to awkward, and indicate which ones even a committed defender would have to concede as genuinely damaging.
