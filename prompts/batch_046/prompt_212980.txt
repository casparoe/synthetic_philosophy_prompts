Consider Aristotle’s distinction, developed in the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics, between oikonomike—household management directed at natural use and bounded by need—and chrematistike—the acquisition of wealth without natural limit. He treats money (nomisma) as a conventional instrument for exchange, yet condemns usury (tokos) because currency, unlike crops or animals, is barren: it produces no natural offspring, so charging for its use violates proportional justice by demanding more than was given.

Now imagine Aristotle confronted with two phenomena that did not exist in the fourth century BCE: first, a purely digital currency maintained on distributed ledgers with no state backing and no commodity anchor, functioning solely as a socially agreed unit of account; second, high-frequency algorithmic trading in which automated agents exchange these digital units millions of times per second solely to capture microsecond price differentials, generating returns without producing goods, services, or even conventional credit.

How would he classify this system? Would he regard algorithmic spread-capture and decentralized fiat-like tokens as extreme, perhaps infinitely extendable forms of unnatural chrematistike—since they extract offspring from a barren medium at machine speed—or would his conventionalist view of money allow him to accept them as legitimate if socially sanctioned? More specifically, does the absence of a physical substrate and the complete automation of exchange alter the moral structure of his critique, or are they merely quantitative accelerations of the same unnatural practice? Reason from his actual commitments about nature, limit, proportional equality, and the purpose of exchange, rather than applying generic ancient wisdom.

If the premise is confused—for example, if applying Aristotle’s concept of barrenness to algorithmic trading requires an anachronistic stretch that breaks the analogy, or if his conventionalism about money actually undermines any normative critique of these modern instruments—say so plainly. Do not force an answer if the historical framework genuinely cannot be extended to these cases without distortion.
