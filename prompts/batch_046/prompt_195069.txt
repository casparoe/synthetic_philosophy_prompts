I have noticed a recurring mental occurrence that I cannot place within the standard taxonomy of affective states. Consider two cases. In the first, I work through a mathematical argument that is formally valid, yet at one particular inference I experience a focused resistance or discomfort—without any identifiable bodily arousal, without forming the explicit belief that an error exists, and without perceiving any visible flaw—yet this response reliably directs my attention until I locate a hidden ambiguity. In the second, I observe a brief, neutral facial expression in a conversation partner and feel an immediate, object-directed caution; again, there is no pronounced bodily change, no conscious judgment that I have been deceived, and no sensory property like dishonesty that I take myself to perceive, though my behavior shifts defensively. Both cases share a structure: an evaluative orientation toward a specific object or proposition, motivating attention or action, but lacking both the explicit propositional content associated with judgment-based accounts and the distinctive bodily profile associated with feeling-based accounts, while also failing to meet the conditions for being a direct perception of a value-laden property in the world. Does this configuration have an established name? How should it be situated with respect to the view that emotions are judgments, perceptions, or feelings? And if it does not map cleanly onto any of these, what exactly distinguishes it from intuition, subliminal belief, or mood, and where should its boundaries be drawn?
