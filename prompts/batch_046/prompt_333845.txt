I am preparing a position paper for a graduate seminar on classical Indian epistemology that treats Nyāya not as a museum piece but as a live framework for philosophical engineering. Our instructor has asked us to take the traditional four pramāṇas—perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), testimony (śabda), and comparison (upamāna)—and test whether they can accommodate a form of knowledge that the classical commentators discuss only obliquely, if at all. For the purpose of this exercise, I want to focus on what I will call "indexical calibration knowledge": the knowledge a cognizing subject possesses not of any particular object, but of being the unique, non-transferable boundary condition within which perceptual judgments occur—the implicit awareness that this phenomenal field is anchored here rather than there, without being itself an object of perception.

The controversy divides the seminar. One camp argues that Nyāya's scheme is fully sufficient: such self-location can be analyzed either as internal perception (mānasapratyakṣa) directed at the self, or as an inferential conclusion (anumāna) drawn from the constant conjunction of cognition with the ego-sense (ahaṅkāra). The opposing camp holds that either reduction violates the subject-object (viṣaya-viṣayī) structure required for valid cognition (pramā), or else it illicitly imports a non-Nyāya conception of pure subjectivity that the pramāṇas were never designed to capture. A further dispute concerns whether upamāna can play any legitimate, non-circular role in grounding this calibration—whether comparing one's own situatedness to an analogical model actually yields knowledge, or merely projects structure onto an already-assumed self.

Write an essay that weighs these competing interpretations with philosophical precision. Your essay should examine in detail whether reducing indexical calibration to mānasapratyakṣa or anumāna preserves the integrity of Nyāya's epistemic architecture, and whether upamāna offers a genuinely independent pathway or collapses into one of the other pramāṇas. Do not end with a non-committal survey: defend a specific verdict—whether Nyāya's fourfold scheme requires reinterpretation, explicit expansion, or can remain intact as it stands—and justify that verdict by showing which reduction, if any, avoids both circularity and the smuggling-in of foreign metaphysics.
