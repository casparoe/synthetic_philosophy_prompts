I've been thinking on my own about whether history is just stories people agree on or whether there's a real past that can be recovered, and I watched Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon. I know there's a philosophical disagreement about this — Ranke's idea that history should show how things actually happened, versus E.H. Carr's argument that facts never speak for themselves and Hayden White's claim that all historical writing imposes narrative form and that the past has no inherent meaning apart from it. I'd like you to read Rashomon through that debate.

The film gives four contradictory accounts of the same death and assault in a grove. Read it as criticism, not just illustration: does the film's structure argue that a single true event lies hidden behind the contradictions, or does it suggest that the event is nothing but the conflicting tellings? Focus on the woodcutter's account near the end, the medium's testimony, and the small physical details that shift across versions — the dagger's disappearance, the woman's posture, the bandit's description of the fight. There's also the final scene with the abandoned baby and the woodcutter's decision to take it in. Some viewers treat that as a moral redemption that makes his version more credible; others treat it as just another self-serving frame.

Here's the specific angle I want you to take. There's a distinction I keep returning to: is Rashomon showing that the past is epistemically hidden — we can't reach it because witnesses lie and evidence rots — or is it showing something stronger, that the past has no single form until someone narrates it? I also want you to use an idea I'm calling narrative residue: those small details in each account that don't obviously serve the speaker's self-image, like the fact that the dagger is stolen rather than lost. Do these residues work like Rankean footholds — fragments that resist full narrative shaping — or are they constructed too, only less visibly?

Don't lay out both sides and leave me to pick. Take a committed position: does Rashomon ultimately defend the possibility of objective history, destroy it, or show that the question itself is framed wrong? Give your reasons, point to the scenes that support your reading, and say honestly where the film resists being turned into a philosophical example — for instance, whether its real subject is human shame rather than historiography. Finally, tell me what Rashomon adds to the debate among Ranke, Carr, and White that pure argument cannot provide: something about the moral need for a story that flatters the self, or about the ethics of listening, that should change how we think about historical objectivity.
