Two students in my philosophy-of-mind seminar have been arguing about a twist on the causal exclusion problem that our instructor only mentioned in passing. I am writing to ask for an adjudication of their exchange, since neither will concede anything and I need to sort out the logic for a paper I am drafting.

The disagreement began after we read about Kim’s causal exclusion argument. Alex claimed that the argument works only if we assume that causal completeness applies to properties individuated in a functional or causal vocabulary. He proposed a scenario in which the physical base is described in purely structural or geometric terms—say, as a field of metric relations and connectivity patterns—without any reference to forces, dispositions, or causal powers. In that framework, mental properties (such as the property of representing danger) are not redundant with any single geometric descriptor, because the geometric description carries no causal information at all. Therefore, Alex said, mental properties can do genuine causal work without competing with the physical properties that realize them; the exclusion problem dissolves once we see that “the physical” can be carved up in ways that leave causal gaps for the mental to fill.

Blake replied that this misses the point of causal completeness entirely. The principle, she said, is not about vocabulary but about the causal powers of the physical world: every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, regardless of how we describe those causes. Redescribing neural processes geometrically does not strip them of their causal efficacy; it merely hides that efficacy behind a different notation. So if mental properties are realized by those neural processes, the mental properties are still causally redundant, and Alex has only performed a notational shift, not solved an ontological problem.

Alex came back with an interventionist move. He said that on an interventionist account of causation—where whether X causes Y depends on whether intervening on X makes a difference to Y within a specified model—the question of causal relevance is framework-relative. If our model includes mental variables and holds geometric variables fixed in the right way, mental properties can be causally relevant; if our model includes only geometric variables, they are not. So exclusion is not absolute but model-dependent, and non-reductive physicalism can be defended by insisting that mental properties belong in the correct explanatory model of behavior, not by denying physical realization.

Blake answered that this makes mental causation far too cheap. If one can always preserve mental causal relevance by selecting a model in which mental variables are included and physical ones are backgrounded, then non-reductive physicalism collapses into a trivial claim about representational convenience. The view was supposed to maintain that mental properties are real and causally efficacious in virtue of being physically realized, not in virtue of being useful modeling choices. By making causation framework-dependent in this way, Alex saves mental causation only by sacrificing the substantive metaphysical thesis that made it interesting in the first place.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. For each of the four moves—Alex’s geometric-redescription claim, Blake’s response about causal powers versus notation, Alex’s interventionist framework-relativity point, and Blake’s “too cheap” objection—rate whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right but overstated, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, what both are missing about how structural descriptions relate to causal explanation, and whether there is a stable middle position that preserves non-reductive physicalism without either dissolving into notational trickery or collapsing into epiphenomenalism.
