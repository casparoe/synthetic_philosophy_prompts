Consider the following record of verdicts delivered by a moral philosopher on questions of abortion ethics. She judges that, in Thomson's violinist scenario, disconnection is morally permissible but not obligatory; she rejects the future-like-ours argument on the grounds that mere potentiality for a future like ours does not confer a present moral right to continued gestation; she permits termination in cases of severe foetal anomaly regardless of gestational age; she denies that viability alone establishes foetal personhood in the absence of conscious experience; she holds that a pregnant person's right to bodily autonomy always outweighs the foetus's claim to life when the two conflict directly; yet she deems elective termination for purely socio-economic reasons in the third trimester impermissible.

Now examine this new case: a pregnant person at twenty-four weeks requests termination on documented, severe, but non-life-threatening psychological grounds; the foetus is neurologically intact and free from anomaly; and local law permits termination only on medical or psychological indications.

Predict the philosopher's verdict, articulate the implicit rule—or competing rules—governing her earlier decisions (for instance, a threshold-based potentiality view, a weighted autonomy principle, or a hybrid framework), indicate which of these rules diverge on the present case, and identify the further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between them. Please present your answer in formal British English, without offering bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations.
