I'm drafting a short essay on whether a nation can be guilty across generations, engaging Karl Jaspers's 1946 work Die Schuldfrage and his distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. I argue that collective guilt fails because guilt requires subjective consciousness that later generations do not share with perpetrators. To preserve continuity without attributing mental states, I propose a framework I call successive moral tenancy: citizens inherit not guilt itself but an institutional obligation analogous to assuming ownership of property with known structural defects—the duty is to repair or acknowledge embedded harm, not to feel culpable. This is meant to differ from Jaspers's metaphysical guilt, which remains individually borne, and from ordinary legal successor liability, since moral tenancy demands active institutional acknowledgment rather than mere compensation. I am concerned, however, that the property analogy may smuggle in methodological individualism precisely where a genuinely collective concept is needed, and that once the institutional mechanism is fully specified the view collapses into a variant of political guilt.

Please comment on: (1) whether my characterization of Jaspers's four categories, especially the boundary between political and metaphysical guilt, is accurate; (2) whether successive moral tenancy is genuinely distinct from existing accounts of collective responsibility or institutional inheritance, or merely renames them; (3) whether the property analogy undermines the collective dimension rather than supporting it; and (4) where the conceptual structure is weakest. End with a one-sentence summary of your overall assessment as the very last line on its own.
