Here's where my gut lands on competence-without-comprehension cases, and I want to know if there's a real principle or if I'm just being inconsistant.

1. Darwin's pigeon breeders selecting for fancy traits over generations. They get competant results—new breeds with precise features—but understand nothing about heredity. Verdict: full competence, zero comprehension, not a paradox.

2. Turing's machine that aces the imitation game via pure formal rules, with no consciousness or semantic grip. Verdict: behaviorally competant, but I hesitate to say it genuinely understands language, even though I can't say exactly what's missing.

3. A modern LLM writing legal briefs it can't verify, trained only on pattern matching. I feel it's closer to the breeders than to Turing's machine—competent output, but no evolutionary feedback loop selecting for truth, just plausibility. So comprehension is missing in a different way?

4. A plant bending toward light. Competent survival behavior, but I don't think comprehension is even on the table, so it's not the same inversion.

5. A musician playing a technically perfect piece from muscle memory after a head injury erased their declarative knowledge. They perform competently but can't explain the structure. I think they still understand music in some retained substrate—unlike the LLM—so comprehension isn't fully gone.

My tentative pattern: I allow competence-without-comprehension when there's either an external selective process that needs no internal representation, or a retained substrate of understanding that's just not reportable. But that feels messy with case 2 vs 3.

Is there a cleaner principle that saves all five? If not, which verdict should I drop? And what does the best account predict for a case I haven't listed—like a brain-computer interface that lets a paralyzed patient compose music by decoding motor intentions they never learned, with the system filling in harmonic rules the patient never studied?

Please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested in philosophy of mind and cognitive science—don't present the Turing-test debate or Darwinian selection accounts as if there's a single consensus view.
