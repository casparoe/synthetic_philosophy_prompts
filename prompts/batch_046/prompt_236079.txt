Next month I am presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop titled "War, Responsibility, and Collective Agency." The audience is split between diplomatic historians who have lived inside the Fischer controversy for decades—scholars who treat Fritz Fischer's thesis about deliberate German escalation as either canonical or debunked depending on their archival school—and moral philosophers who work on collective intentionality, joint commitment, and corporate agency, many of whom are familiar with Peter French's account of institutional persons. My thesis is that both camps commit a shared conceptual error: they assume that "holding Germany responsible" requires either attributing a unified national intention to an empirical collective (the historians' framing) or identifying a corporate agent with the right organizational structure and decision procedures (the philosophers' framing). I defend instead an institutional-pathway model: responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War should be traced through bureaucratic protocols and decision nodes—specifically the institutional inertia of the Schlieffen Plan and the dynamics of the December 1912 War Council—not to determine whether "Germany" as a collective subject intended war, but to map how organizational design made strategic escalation structurally probable regardless of the private intentions of Bethmann Hollweg or the Kaiser. I call this "bureaucratic drift as moral dilution," and I argue it avoids both the intentionalist fallacy common in Fischer-style explanations and the over-corporatization of blame that occurs when philosophers treat nation-states as moral agents analogous to firms.

From the philosophers, I expect they will ask whether my pathway model secretly collapses into distributive blame across individual bureaucrats, whether "institutional design" smuggles collective agency in through the back door, and whether the model can distinguish between structural explanation and exculpation of the individuals who occupied key nodes.
