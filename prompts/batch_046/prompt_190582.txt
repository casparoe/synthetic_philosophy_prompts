The debate over expected-value fanaticism—whether rational agents should accept arbitrarily large sacrifices for sufficiently small probabilities of sufficiently large goods—seems to stall once both sides agree on cardinal probabilities and values. Defenders treat risk-neutrality as the default rational posture; critics invoke lexical priorities, decision paralysis, or a breakdown in the commensurability of reasons. My hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not about risk aversion but about whether practical reasoning requires what we might call a closure condition on tradeoffs: if there exists any pair of goods such that no quantity of one can justify losing a fixed unit of the other, then expected-value maximization is not merely extreme but structurally incompatible with the logic of reasons. Is this diagnosis correct, or is the stall hiding a dispute about the linearity of value across domains, about bounded rationality, or about whether a decision procedure should optimize, satisfice, or preserve agential integrity? Diagnose the disagreement using concrete cases—such as a St. Petersburg-style moral gamble in which saving one life requires risking the destruction of all human value with vanishing probability—and say what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result about utility bounds, a distinction in the concept of reason, or an argument that the question itself is ill-posed.
