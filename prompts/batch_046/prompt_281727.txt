Consider the institutional pattern common in jurisdictions that permit assisted dying only in self-administered or withdrawn-treatment forms: a competent patient may refuse a ventilator or ingest prescribed lethal medication alone, but if progressive paralysis removes the physical ability to lift the cup, neither a physician nor a caregiver may assist in the physical act of ingestion, even though decision-making capacity and the severity of suffering remain unchanged. At the same time, these institutions routinely honor requests for invasive bodily interventions—amputations, sterilizations, high-risk surgeries—that involve far greater physical intrusion and are performed entirely by the physician. What philosophical account of bodily autonomy, causal agency, or the moral distinction between acting and being acted upon does this asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view that makes the self-administration boundary coherent, ask whether it can also justify the differential treatment of withdrawal requests, and determine whether the practice is strictly committed to one theory or is philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival accounts of consent and harm. Then describe exactly which protocols and eligibility rules would have to be revised or abandoned if the institution adopted a framework on which only informed consent and the minimization of suffering carry moral weight.
