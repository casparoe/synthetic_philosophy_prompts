Next week I present to a joint classics and philosophy seminar the thesis that Cicero's De Senectute should not be read as a straightforward Stoic consolatio that applies the doctrine of indifferents to the four explicit objections—loss of pleasure, physical strength, mental acuity, and proximity to death. Instead, I argue that Cicero tacitly introduces a fifth, unstated objection: that old age threatens the temporal continuity required for Roman civic authority, specifically the coherence of auctoritas and fides over time, because the aging senator's embodied memory becomes fragile and his past commitments seem disconnected from his present judgment. I claim his reply depends not on Stoic apatheia or materialist psychology but on a reconstructed conception of the senex as a living archive whose authority derives precisely from the vulnerability of embodied, diachronic memory. I further contend that this commits Cicero, despite his usual Stoic sympathies, to a narrative theory of personal identity that is incompatible with the materialist account of the soul he elsewhere endorses.

The audience is precisely this: three Stoic specialists who treat De Senectute as a popularization of orthodox Stoic ethics and who know the standard four-objection structure and its decision-theoretic parallels inside out; two Roman political historians who study auctoritas and fides in republican institutions and who are deeply skeptical of importing modern philosophy of personal identity into first-century BCE texts; one contemporary philosopher of personal identity who works on narrative theories but is suspicious of anachronistic application; and several classics graduate students who know the surface argument but not the secondary literature on Stoic materialism.

Prepare me for the question period. Order the objections by how likely they are to come and how damaging they would be if unanswered. For each, phrase it as that specific interlocutor would phrase it—use the vocabulary the Stoic scholars, the Roman historians, and the narrative-theory philosopher would actually use. Note which objections have real force, which I should concede up front, and which I must hold without qualification. For each reply I might give, flag its weakest point rather than presenting it as decisive. Include the one objection I am most hoping nobody raises—the one that targets the unstated fifth objection directly and forces me to defend the anachronism charge in front of the historians—and tell me exactly how to reply in the limited time available, including what to concede in the first thirty seconds so the rest of the reply can land. I will have about twenty minutes for questions and need to protect the core claim that the narrative-identity reading explains textual details the Stoic reading ignores, without being forced into defending full-blown anachronism.
