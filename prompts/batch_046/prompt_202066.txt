I am preparing a 75-minute session for sixteen philosophy undergraduates on whether everyday conventions—tipping, queuing, jaywalking—generate genuine moral obligations for visitors, or remain purely instrumental coordination devices that outsiders may disregard without fault. Rather than another round of case analysis, I would like you to design a runnable classroom simulation or structured role-play in which participants themselves generate the tension between universal moral reasoning and local normative practice, before the facilitator explicitly names the philosophical distinction.

The scenario should be set in a fictional society, Velora, where café culture depends upon a silent closure gesture—briefly placing one’s palm flat on the table edge before leaving—which locals treat as a coordination signal allowing staff to manage seating and resources, but which visitors experience as an arbitrary ritual lacking intrinsic meaning. Divide the group into locals, visitors, and observers, assigning small but real stakes: for example, locals accumulate collective points only when a sufficient proportion of visitors perform the gesture, thereby enabling efficient table turnover, while visitors accumulate individual points for rapid departure. You should embed the concept of normative grafting, whereby a visitor who adopts the gesture purely to preserve the locals’ trust infrastructure—without endorsing its value or feeling cultural deference—receives a different moral evaluation in the debrief from one who refuses outright or one who performs it out of respect for local custom. Arrange the rules so that participants discover for themselves whether refusal causes a genuine moral harm, eroding the shared expectations upon which local cooperation depends, or merely produces an unfortunate practical externality that falls on others.

Please provide a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, explicit roles with payoffs, the debrief questions that map each stage of the activity onto the philosophical issues, and an honest assessment of which feature of the moral-obligation debate—perhaps the long-term, intergenerational decay of conventions that this single-session simulation cannot capture—the facilitator must supply directly. Write in formal British English, contain all fictional rules and background within your description, and do not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or research into real-world customs.
