Hey, I'm writing this out for my study group since we had a long argument over coffee about whether Gettier-style reasoning can be pushed into full-blown skepticism, and my friend Maya wrote up this chain that now looks suspicious to me. Here's her argument, more or less: (1) Knowledge is justified true belief that isn't true merely by accident, which is the core Gettier intuition. (2) Take any ordinary empirical belief, like that there's a coffee cup on the desk in front of you. For any such belief, there's a nearby possible world where a prankster moves the cup right after you check, or your visual system glitched for a split second, but your internal jusitification stays exactly the same. (3) So in every actual case there's a nearby failure-world with identical justification but false belief. (4) That means every empirical belief is true only by luck relative to its justification, which is precisely the Gettier condition applied across the board. (5) Therefore nobody actually knows any empirical fact, since all our beliefs are Gettier-vulnerable in this modal sense. (6) But that's absurd, because then science and everyday life are built on sand.

I really want to keep (1) since it feels like the standard anti-luck requirement, and (2) also seems okay since modal proximity is real, but I feel like something slips between (3) and (4) about what counts as the same justification, or maybe about how close these worlds really are. Where does the argument actually break? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
