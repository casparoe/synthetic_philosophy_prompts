My colleague in the philosophy of literature has ruled consistently across interpretation disputes: when an author's private journal assigns a symbol a personal meaning not recoverable from the text, she holds that public convention governs and the journal is biographically but not interpretively relevant; when a second edition adds a footnote resolving an ambiguity present in the first, she treats the footnote as authoritative for the work; when an author uses a word in an idiosyncratic private sense unknown to readers and unmarked in the text, she insists on the standard lexical meaning; when corrected proofs restore a stage direction missing from the first printing, she counts that direction as part of the work; and when a later sequel contradicts an event in the first novel, she maintains that the first text's internal meaning is not retroactively revised. Now consider a novelist who publishes a novel containing one chapter written in an invented cipher based on a lattice structure she has never published; she subsequently publishes the decoding key in an unrelated mathematics journal, such that without the key the chapter reads as surrealist prose, but with it the chapter encodes a precise narrative sequence. Predict her verdict on whether the chapter's meaning includes that encoded narrative, identify her closest prior analog, lay out the two competing rules that both fit her record yet diverge on this case, state which rule she likely applies with your reasoning, and specify one further case that would distinguish the rules. Respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
