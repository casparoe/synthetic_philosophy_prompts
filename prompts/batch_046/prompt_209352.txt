I'm assembling my commitments on collective intentionality and joint action for a philosophy-of-action seminar, and I want a consistency audit before I present them. Here is my current package:

(1) Joint actions—such as two people carrying a table up stairs together—are ontologically irreducible: they are not decomposable into individual actions plus coordination conditions without loss, because the success conditions are genuinely shared.

(2) I reject all group-mind hypotheses: there is no collective subject of consciousness, no phenomenal character to being a corporation or committee, and mental-state attributions to groups are always metaphorical or distributive shorthand.

(3) Joint action requires we-intentions—psychologically real shared intentions that take the form we intend to J—which are causally efficacious in each participant and have genuinely collective intentional objects that cannot be reconstructed from individual intentions alone.

(4) Nevertheless, I hold that we-intentions are metaphysically reducible to interlocking individual intentions together with mutual belief and common-knowledge conditions; there is no primitive collective mental content, only distributed individual psychology.

(5) I treat spontaneous coordination—e.g., two strangers who, without prior communication, begin pushing the same stuck car out of snow—as a genuine joint action, despite the absence of any prior shared plan.

(6) Methodologically, I think social ontology should be built solely from individual psychology and rational-choice theory, with no appeal to emergent group-level properties or downward causation.

Please rank the tensions by severity. Which pairs are genuinely incompatible, which only appear to conflict, and what is the least costly revision in each case? Specifically: does holding (3) together with (4) force me to concede that the mental content in (3) is not genuinely shared, thereby undermining (2) or weakening (1)? Can (1) be maintained alongside (6) if the irreducibility is purely structural rather than explanatory? Does (5) falsify the necessity claim embedded in (3)? For each tension, identify the cheapest fix and what it costs—whether abandoning ontological anti-reductionism, allowing emergent mental properties, treating spontaneous cases as merely parallel individual actions, or revising the psychological-reality claim in (3). Do not treat all conflicts as equally serious; discriminate sharply between surface contradictions and deeper incompatibilities of motivation.
