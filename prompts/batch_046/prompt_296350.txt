You serve as the methodological referee for an interdisciplinary quarterly that receives submissions in cognitive science, theoretical physics, and philosophy of mind. A recurring problem is manuscripts that invoke Gödel’s first or second incompleteness theorem to argue for sweeping conclusions outside mathematics—e.g., that human minds transcend computation, that any future physical theory is necessarily incomplete, or that an artificial system cannot verify its own safety properties. You need a reusable evaluation procedure you can run in under twenty minutes per manuscript, without consulting external sources, that determines whether the Gödelian move is structurally legitimate or a category error. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of gates or tests, with explicit stopping conditions and judgment handoffs, plus worked applications.

Write the procedure with the following structure. For each gate, specify: (i) the precise conceptual distinction being tested—distinguishing, for example, formal systems with sufficient arithmetic strength from colloquial “systems of belief,” syntactic self-reference from metaphorical self-awareness, and consistency in the logical sense from practical coherence; (ii) what counts as passing, failing, or being indeterminate; (iii) whether failure yields an immediate procedural rejection, or whether the gate hands the decision to human judgment because the dispute concerns interpretive charity rather than logical form; and (iv) the characteristic misfires—false positives where authors smuggle in colloquial senses of “incomplete,” “consistent,” or “undecidable,” and false negatives where a technically sound analogy is dismissed because the referee confuses formalization with empirical modeling.

Then apply the procedure to three submissions:
A. A manuscript arguing that because the human brain can represent its own mental states, Gödel’s first theorem proves no computer can fully replicate human reasoning.
B. A physics manuscript claiming that any “theory of everything” containing a formal representation of all measurable quantities is necessarily incomplete, since it can encode arithmetic and therefore harbor an undecidable statement about measurement outcomes.
C. A case where the Gödelian machinery is applied with technical correctness but you think the ultimate conclusion is still wrong—perhaps an AI-safety argument using the second theorem to claim that an advanced system cannot prove its own non-harmfulness—so that the procedure returns “structurally sound analogy” yet still requires an independent rejection of the inference.

Conclude by stating explicitly what the procedure cannot do: which judgments remain irreducibly philosophical rather than procedural; what kinds of purely rhetorical or metaphorical invocations of “incompleteness” fall entirely outside its scope; and where a referee must still exercise domain-specific expertise that no checklist can replace. Keep the tone formal and precise; do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations.
