I'm writing from a philosophy of mind course where we are examining whether functionalism genuinely licenses substrate independence. In seminar discussions of multiple realisability, people constantly invoke the analogy that mental states stand to brains as software stands to hardware: the same functional specification can run on different physical substrates, so consciousness ought to be portable across silicon, neural tissue, or synthetic architectures. The analogy is usually deployed to argue that once the functional architecture is fully mapped, the underlying physics becomes metaphysically inert. How far does it actually hold? Please give me an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the source code, the compiler, the operating system, the debugging cycle, and the hardware failure modes — then map the disanalogies that matter specifically for mental states, such as whether a brain can suffer partial lesion without total system collapse in ways that have no software analogue, or whether functional duplication in distributed biological signalling networks introduces continuous dynamics that a discrete software model suppresses. Does the conclusion that substrate independence is unproblematic survive once these breaks are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the entire argument? If the software-hardware comparison collapses under scrutiny, suggest whether a different analogy — perhaps drawn from an orchestral score to its performances, or from gene regulatory networks to phenotypic expression — would capture multiple realisability without smuggling in the false assumption that mental properties are fully abstractable from their continuous physical dynamics.
