Carroll's dialogue involves Achilles and the Tortoise working through a Euclidean proof. They agree on premises stating that things equal to the same thing are equal to each other, and that two sides of a triangle share this relation. From these they should derive that the two sides are equal to each other. The Tortoise accepts the premises but refuses the conclusion unless Achilles adds a hypothetical premise stating that if the first two premises hold, then the conclusion must hold. Once Achilles writes this down as a further premise, the Tortoise accepts all three but still refuses the conclusion unless another premise is added linking the three premises to the conclusion. This generates an infinite regress, showing that modus ponens cannot be justified as an ordinary premise within the system it governs.

This problem is structurally identical to Hume's problem of induction: both demonstrate that justifying a reasoning pattern requires employing that very pattern, producing circularity. Because of this parallel, some philosophers—especially the logical positivists—argued that inference rules should be treated not as premises but as normative entitlements. On this view, we are entitled to apply modus ponens not because we have derived it, but because it is constitutive of rational belief revision. Once we adopt this framing, the Tortoise's challenge is fully resolved. The entitlement carries primitive normative authority, so demanding its justification is a category mistake, like asking why we should trust perception. Thus Carroll's dialogue ultimately supports the claim that deduction rests on irreducible normative commitments rather than on provable premises.
