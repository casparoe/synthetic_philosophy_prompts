As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying moral philosophy, I have been trying to apply disciplined conceptual mapping to political episodes that resist tidy analysis. Take the 2011 NATO-led intervention in Libya, authorized by the UN to protect civilians but quickly evolving into regime change and leaving a prolonged institutional vacuum. Analyze this episode through the lens of what I call asymmetric moral bookkeeping: the principle that the justification for entering an intervention is assessed against a discrete protective baseline, while the justification for its outcome is assessed against a counterfactual welfare gradient weighted differently across populations. First, map the critical decision points—enforcing the no-fly zone, arming opposition factions, and the eventual collapse of the Gaddafi government—onto these two frames, identifying which actions appear warranted under the discrete baseline but not under the gradient comparison, and vice versa. Second, specify which actors—the NATO coalition, the Arab League endorsers, the abstaining Security Council members, or the Libyan opposition—operated under which frame, and whether the frames were applied consistently or instrumentally. Third, indicate where this distinction clarifies the familiar gap between the protective mandate and the restructuring result, and where it breaks down, especially regarding Libya's post-2011 fragmentation. Finally, say whether the case supports or undermines the broader philosophical claim that humanitarian intervention should be governed by separate entry and exit criteria rather than a single unified consequentialist calculus.
