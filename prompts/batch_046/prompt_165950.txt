I am a novelist drafting a chapter in which an appellate judge must rule between two defendants whose evidentiary profiles are perfectly symmetrical in every legally relevant respect; I want her paralysis to be philosophically serious rather than merely dramatic, so I have constructed the following argument, only to find that its conclusion dissolves not just her particular deadlock but the very idea of a genuine moral dilemma. (1) A rational agent can choose option A over option B only if there is some feature F, relevant to the choice, that A possesses and B lacks. (2) If A and B are identical in all choice-relevant respects, then no such F exists. (3) Therefore rational choice between genuinely identical options is impossible. (4) Buridan's ass, facing two bales of hay that are equally nourishing and equally accessible, confronts exactly this kind of identity. (5) So the ass cannot rationally choose; any movement it makes is arbitrary rather than justified. (6) Now, if arbitrary resolution in cases of perfect symmetry is practically indistinguishable from rational resolution—since in both cases an option is selected—then the boundary between rational choice and random selection collapses whenever options are sufficiently close. (7) By continuity, for any apparently balanced pair, some imperceptible asymmetry—spatial position, temporal ordering, neural noise—does the actual selecting. (8) Hence there is never truly choice without preference; what looks like indifference is merely ignorance of microscopic differences. (9) It follows that genuine moral dilemmas, in which reasons genuinely balance without hidden resolution, are conceptually impossible. (10) But ordinary moral experience and legal practice treat such dilemmas as real and sometimes tragic. (11) Therefore premise (1) must be false.

Something slips, I think, between (6) and (7), or perhaps in the move from action theory to moral psychology, but I cannot name the exact failure. Which step should I reject if I wish to preserve the possibility that my judge faces a true deadlock without being irrational or merely flipping a coin? And what does rejecting it cost—must I accept that rationality permits arbitrary selection, or that there can be choice without reasons, or that microscopic asymmetries never determine moral outcomes? I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: identify the failing premise or inference, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives so I can decide what kind of rational agent my character is.
