I've been thinking about the philosophical implications of pain asymbolia — the clinical cases in which patients report intact nociceptive discrimination, localized sensation, and even injury-avoidance behavior, yet deny that the experience is unpleasant or bad in any way — and I'm trying to work out whether this phenomenon undermines the standard representationalist claim that pain is intrinsically bad in virtue of representing tissue damage. The dissociation seems to show that the sensory-discriminative content can exist without the affective-motivational layer, which suggests that "badness" isn't part of what pain represents but rather an independent evaluative tag that can fail to attach. If that's correct, then it looks like pain is not, after all, the paradigm of intrinsic suffering; it's a neutral representational state that usually gets paired with suffering, but need not be. My question is this: does this mean that moral arguments which treat nociception as automatically generating negative utility — for instance, in discussions of wild-animal suffering or in negative-utilitarian frameworks — are committing a category error? Are they conflating the presence of a sensory representation with the presence of an actual badness? Or is there a way for the representationalist to maintain that pain is essentially bad even while allowing that the affective component can be stripped away in pathology, without simply defining the pathology away?
