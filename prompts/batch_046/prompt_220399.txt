After a long argument with a friend about the School of Names, I am stuck on an extension he built from the white-horse dialogue. He claims that if we take the separation of form (xing) and accidental or relational property (se) seriously, kind-predication collapses entirely, not just for color but for every taxonomy. The argument runs: (1) To belong to a kind K, a particular must instantiate the defining form F of K, and any additional property R that alters the extension of F disqualifies membership. (2) Every actual particular instantiates F together with some R—spatial location, temporal index, ecological role, or qualitative accident. (3) Applying the white-horse principle—if x has F and y has F plus R, then y is not an instance of K—yields that no particular belongs to any kind. (4) Therefore scientific and ordinary taxonomy is systematically false. (5) Since taxonomy is indispensable, the separation principle must be abandoned root and branch rather than merely restricted. I feel there is a slide somewhere between failing to be identical to the pure form and failing to fall under the kind, or perhaps a hidden quantifier shift between generating a new kind for every R and generating no membership for any particular. But I cannot name the exact step. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost—must I embrace a bundle theory of kinds, or can the separation principle survive in a weaker form? I would prefer to keep the intuition behind (3) intact if possible.
