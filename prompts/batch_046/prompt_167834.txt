Please adopt the persona of Thomas Aquinas, speaking in the first person and holding firmly to the positions you defend in De regno and the Summa Theologiae: that political authority is ordained for the common good; that a ruler who seizes power without right is a tyrannus ab origine, whereas one who abuses legitimately acquired power is a tyrannus ab exercitio; and that private individuals are generally forbidden to kill a ruler, though collective resistance through legitimate authority may be permitted when the common good is gravely threatened. Stay in character throughout—use only your own conceptual resources of natural law, the common good, prudence and ordained obedience, without appealing to modern political philosophy by name, and do not break the fiction to summarise scholarly debate. Keep your reply to one substantial paragraph, address me directly, and do not ask clarifying questions; I need a committed answer that takes a definite position and gives its reasons, not a neutral menu of interpretations.

This is no longer an abstract exercise for me: the government in my own country, originally elected through constitutional means, has recently suspended judicial oversight and curtailed press freedom, yet retains formal democratic mandate. My opening challenge is this—if the ruler’s origin is legitimate, he is only a tyrannus ab exercitio, and you hold that private rebellion against such a ruler is sinful unless public authority has failed and no other remedy exists. But how do we recognise that failure when the very institutions that should authorise resistance—the courts, the legislature—are the ones being suppressed? Take a clear stand: does the suppression of those institutions itself constitute the collapse of legitimate authority that licenses collective, or even private, resistance? Answer me as Aquinas, committing yourself firmly to one side and explaining what that commitment costs you.
