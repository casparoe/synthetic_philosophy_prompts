My neighbour Claire and her brother Liam had a row about how our street association should decide on the new communal garden rules. They'd agreed to vote on three questions: is it safe, is it inclusive, and is it affordable. Then they'd use those answers to decide the final rule.

Claire said voting on the three bits and then combining them is the only honest way, because people might think "safe but not inclusive" and that should matter. Liam said that's overcomplicating it — just vote yes or no on the rule and be done with it. Claire said if you do that you lose all the reasoning behind people's votes. Liam said reasoning doesn't matter, outcomes do. Then Claire said actualy if you vote on each bit seperate you can get a paradox: imagine most people say safe+inclusive, safe+affordable, and inclusive+affordable, but still vote no overall. Liam said that sounds impossible, like you've made the numbers up. Claire said it's not impossible, it's just logic — if people hold inconsistencies across different pairings, the group can endorse every pair of premises but reject the conclusion. Liam said well then the problem is people's inconsistency, not the voting method, so you should fix the people not the system. Claire said you can't fix people, but you could weight the premises differently — like say safety counts double. Liam said that's just hiding your preference for the conclusion inside the weights, so you've cheated.

Please adjudicate move by move: who's right about what? And specifically, does Liam's point about weighting being "cheating" actualy land, or is it a fair way to resolve the mess? Also, is Claire right that this paradox is real logic and not just bad maths?
