I sit on the acquisitions commitee for our digital typography archive, and we're deadlocked on a weird submission. There's this AI font called Morpho-Sans where every 'a' is readable in context but none share the same actual shape — some are single-story, some double, some have no tail, some have two bowls, etc. The designers claim they built it around something they call "morphological debt," the idea that a letter can violate canonical form in one place if it over-pays recognizability elsewhere, like through aperture size or the shape of neighboring letters. This feels like that old Letter Spirit problem — is there one true "a"-ness, or is being an 'a' just being recognized as one?

Our catalog rules require "consistent morphological identity" for a type family, but nobody can say what that means now. Half the commitee wants to reject Morpho-Sans outright, arguing that without shared essential features across instances we've just got noise, not an alphabet. The other half says essence-talk is nostalgia and recognition has always been the real criterion. I need a short decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — recommending what we do before Thursday's vote. The memo should actually engage the strongest argument for the side that loses, not just dismiss it. Please also seperate what's genuinely settled about categorization and letter recognition from what's actually contested among people who know this stuff — don't present either view as the obvious consensus. Write in American English, keep it casual, and don't bother with citations or a reading list since I'm working without web access. Oh, and feel free to leave a typo or two in the framing — just make the memo itself readable.
