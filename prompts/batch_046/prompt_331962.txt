I am part of a neighbourhood cooperative that lends specialised equipment among its members. There is an unwritten understanding that items should be returned promptly once the borrower has finished using them, and that no one should treat the shared pool as an extension of their personal property. Last month I borrowed an expensive power tool that was rarely requested by others. I kept it for six weeks, using it intermittently for several home repairs, and I refused to return it when another member asked for it over a weekend, on the grounds that I still had legitimate unfinished work and that interrupting my project would cause me significant inconvenience. When challenged, I defended my action by formulating my personal principle very narrowly: I will retain borrowed, low-demand equipment beyond the usual period only when I have active, unfinished projects and when releasing it would disrupt my work for only minor benefit to the other party. I argued that if everyone followed this precisely qualified rule, the cooperative would not collapse; at worst, borrowing times would lengthen, but the system of mutual aid would remain intact. I added that if my principle were stated more generally—simply that anyone may keep shared items as long as they wish—then it would clearly fail, but my carefully restricted version avoids that failure. Several members have told me that this defence misses the point entirely: they claim I am treating a strict obligation as though it were a flexible guideline, that I have loaded my principle with conditions precisely to make it immune from rejection, and that the fundamental problem is not whether the logistics of lending still work, but whether my principle undermines the very idea of shared ownership that makes the cooperative possible. I cannot see why my qualifications should not save me, nor why the kind of obligation matters if the practical arrangement remains sustainable. Can you identify the specific flaw in how I have constructed my principle and in how I have tested it, and explain whether the problem lies in logical impossibility, in what I could consistently will, or in something else altogether?
