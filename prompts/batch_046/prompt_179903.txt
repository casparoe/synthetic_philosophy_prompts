I run a two-hour graduate seminar with twelve philosophy students on long-term ethics and epistemic risk, meeting in a seminar room with tables and no computers. Design a runnable multi-round deliberation game—complete with hidden role cards, a token economy, voting rules, and a randomly triggered termination condition—that makes participants feel whether uncertainty about being in a computer simulation undermines fanatical commitment to the far future, without anyone naming Bostrom, the simulation argument, or longtermism until the debrief. Inject an ontological-escrow framing: each student receives a concealed nesting depth (base reality, first-order simulation, or second-order simulation) and must vote each round whether to allocate tokens to immediate coordination (guaranteed payout this round regardless of depth) or deep legacy (pays out multiplicatively only if the player is at base reality and survives an unpredictable discontinuity that ends the session). Arrange the rules and hidden information so that participants themselves discover, through their own shifting allocations, that extreme deep-legacy investment collapses once they recognize they cannot verify their depth and face unknown termination risk. Then provide the debrief: map each round and mechanic onto an element of the interaction between simulation uncertainty and far-future fanaticism; say what will predictably fail in facilitation (for example, students fixating on game-theoretic dominance rather than epistemic humility) and how to correct it; and be explicit about which feature—such as the self-indication assumption or the reference-class problem—this physical game cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally. Before the design, state what would change your view: which specific argument or empirical finding would lead you to conclude that simulation uncertainty actually strengthens rather than dampens extreme long-term commitment, and identify the point in your reasoning most likely to fail. Begin your response with the activity design itself—no introductory paragraph, no restatement of the prompt—then present the debrief, and conclude with your self-critical note.
