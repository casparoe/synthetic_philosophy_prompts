As a journalist covering neuroscience and the philosophy of identity, I have been collecting intuitive judgments about synthetic brain replacement, and my own verdicts refuse to align neatly. Here is where I stand. First, if every neuron in a patient's brain is replaced over thirty years with synthetic analogues that preserve synaptic weights, firing dynamics, and biochemical signaling pathways—so that memory, personality, and conscious experience flow without interruption—I judge that the original person survives; the substrate has changed, but continuity is intact. Second, if the identical total replacement is executed in a seventy-two-hour surgical window, I judge that the original person dies and a psychologically identical successor is activated; speed seems to break something essential. Third, a non-destructive scan followed by destruction of the biological brain and instantiation of a digital emulation on a server, with no temporal overlap, strikes me as producing a copy, not as survival. Fourth, during a gradual thirty-year replacement, if the synthetic neurons gradually diverge by roughly three percent in noise patterns—leaving explicit memories intact but slowly altering emotional temperament, so that by year twenty the patient reports feeling like a muted version of herself—I feel this is not abrupt death but a kind of ontological fading, and I cannot say whether she is the same person. Fifth, if that same gradual replacement is paused for six months—substrate preserved, dynamics frozen, then resumed—I find the interruption feels like death, even though the physical basis was maintained. Sixth, if the replacement uses lab-grown biological tissue rather than silicon, preserving biochemical dynamics precisely, my intuition is that survival is assured even if the procedure is rapid.

The tension I cannot resolve is between the first and second cases: the total physical change is identical, yet temporal distribution transforms my verdict from survival to death. Meanwhile, the fifth case suggests that uninterrupted functional integration matters more than substrate preservation, yet the second case suggests that speed alone can defeat survival even with functional continuity.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps involving substrate-transition velocity, the continuity of conscious dynamics, or the absence of temporal gaps in functional integration—that captures these judgments? If so, please state it with the specific clause that each case motivates. Then apply it to two scenarios I have not considered: a replacement performed over one year rather than thirty, and a distributed upload in which consciousness is split across three bodies with shared access to a single synthetic memory architecture. Finally, be explicit about which of my six verdicts the principle must reject if simplicity is to be preserved, and whether that rejection is more plausible than complicating the principle to save every intuition.
