Consider a hybrid climate-policy framework that imposes a steep, non-rebated carbon tax alongside direct regulatory caps on fossil-fuel extraction; the revenue is sequestered into a public decommissioning fund rather than rebated to households, and success is evaluated not by GDP or emissions alone, but by a degrowth-adjusted welfare metric that weights energy-throughput reduction against distributive equity. What are the most important implications of this specific design for the debate between carbon-pricing advocates and degrowth proponents? Specifically, does it resolve the efficiency-versus-planning tension that divides the two camps, or does it generate new contradictions—fiscal drag, political-economy resistance, or measurement circularity—that neither side has adequately addressed?
