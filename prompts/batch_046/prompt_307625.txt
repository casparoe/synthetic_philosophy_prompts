Consider the following argument about the moral authority of advance directives in cases of severe dementia.

(1) For a decision to bind me now, it must be made by an agent who stands in an appropriate authority relation to my present situation: either it is me deciding for myself (shared identity) or I have legitimately delegated that authority to another.

(2) Severe dementia breaks psychological continuity with the author of the directive; the values, reasoning capacities, and narrative identity that supported the original decision are no longer present in the patient.

(3) If psychological continuity is necessary for personal identity, then the demented patient is not the same agent who authored the directive. The directive is therefore an instruction from a now-defunct agent about the care of a numerically different agent who happens to occupy the same body.

(4) A defunct agent possesses no present authority to command medical treatment for a living stranger; hence the directive lacks binding force.

(5) If, on the other hand, bodily continuity is sufficient for identity, then the patient is numerically identical to the author. But then the patient's present preferences—however fragmentary or inarticulate—carry exactly the same normative weight as the past preferences recorded in the document.

(6) To treat the past preference as overriding the present preference in this case is therefore a form of paternalism directed at one continuous person, structurally identical to overriding the current expressed wishes of any other adult who retains some minimal capacity for preference.

(7) So, on either account of personal identity, advance directives in severe dementia are either non-binding (because identity is broken) or illegitimately coercive (because identity is preserved but past preferences dominate present ones).

(8) The same reasoning structure applies to any long-range commitment—contracts signed in youth, vows made decades earlier, or prior consent to risky procedures—whenever substantial psychological change has occurred in the interim.

(9) Yet we routinely treat such commitments as valid and do not regard them as void or coercive merely because the present self has different values; we recognize conditions for legitimate revocation, not automatic nullification.

(10) Therefore, either the chain in (1) through (7) is flawed, or we must reject the validity of long-range commitments altogether, which is unacceptable.

If forced to choose, I would preserve (1) and (2) rather than (9), but I want a precise diagnosis: which step contains the hidden equivocation, scope shift, or modal slide, why it seemed plausible, and what giving it up costs for our understanding of diachronic authority and retrospective consent. In particular, does the flaw lie in treating authority as requiring the continued existence of the deciding agent, or in assuming that temporal parts of one person must always have equal normative standing?
