I spent much of last evening in a frustrating argument with a close friend who studies democratic theory, and I am still trying to understand why we made no progress despite both of us being reasonably well-read on the subject. The dispute concerned populist claims to embody "the will of the people." My friend defended what she called aggregative legitimacy: a populist movement genuinely expresses the popular will whenever its demands align with the majority preference ordering over available policy options, regardless of whether the electorate holds any coherent shared ideology. I countered with what I termed the performative coherence objection: that populist rhetoric does not describe a pre-existing collective will but rather constructs one by treating heterogeneous grievances as if they emanated from a unified subject, and that this is democratically questionable not because it misrepresents individual preferences but because it suppresses the internal pluralism that democratic authorization requires. She then replied that all representative democracy involves precisely this kind of constructive unification—representatives necessarily speak for constituencies that do not literally share one mind—so my objection, if taken seriously, would undermine representative democracy itself rather than isolating populism.

We circled for hours. My working hypothesis is that we are answering different questions under a single heading: she is asking whether populism satisfies minimal conditions for democratic authorization, while I am asking whether the rhetorical form of populism falsifies the ontology of the electorate in ways that corrupt deliberative legitimacy. But I cannot tell whether this is a verbal disagreement about the term "will," a substantive disagreement about democratic norms, or a hidden metaphysical disagreement about whether collective intentionality is coherent in the absence of shared deliberative structures. There might even be an empirical question buried inside—about whether populist voters actually share enough common cause to ground a collective agent—that neither of us has addressed because we have been treating the issue as purely conceptual. Could you diagnose what kind of impasse this actually is? Specifically: are we talking past each other because "the will of the people" functions ambiguously across normative, metaphysical, and empirical registers? Is there a genuine disagreement about facts or standards of evidence disguised by our rhetoric, or are our starting intuitions about democratic legitimacy simply bedrock and unarguable? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—would it require a conceptual distinction separating authorization from representation, an empirical study of voter coherence that both sides would accept as bearing on the dispute, a formal argument about collective agency, or should we conclude that the framing is ill-posed and abandon it?
