I have recently been asked to help design an introductory logic module for a philosophy curriculum, and in returning to Carroll’s dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise I have become preoccupied with the specific structure of the regress: not a generic skepticism about reason, but the precise difficulty that licensing an inference requires transforming a meta-level rule into an object-level premise, which then demands its own license. For the opening session I want a villanelle that enacts this regress through its form rather than merely describing it. One refrain should carry Achilles’ conviction that if the premises hold, the conclusion must follow; the other should carry the Tortoise’s refusal to grant that conditional without a further premise that authorizes the step itself—distinguishing carefully between accepting “A and B” and accepting the rule that permits the move from A and B to Z. Through the repeating stanzas the poem should deepen this object-level versus meta-level distinction, so that at the volta the reader recognizes that the regress applies not only to this syllogism but to any attempt to ground deduction in something prior. The final couplet should bring the two refrains into adjacency without resolving them, leaving the tension exactly where the logical problem leaves it. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of logic recognizes the specific mechanism—the instantiation of modus ponens as a premise that itself requires justification—rather than treating logic as decorative metaphor. I am not seeking a pastiche of Carroll’s prose, nor rhyme for its own sake; I need the villanelle’s unavoidable circular returns to do the philosophical work of showing why the justification of deduction never closes.
