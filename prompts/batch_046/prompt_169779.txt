I'm drafting a long-form post on societal collapse and modern risk, and before I publish I want a consistency audit on the package I actually hold. Here it is:

1. I reserve "collapse" for rapid, involuntary reductions in sociopolitical complexity—not for gradual transformation or local adaptation.
2. The Late Bronze Age crisis is best explained as a cascade failure in interdependent palace economies, with climate stress and migration as amplifiers rather than root causes.
3. Easter Island's deforestation was driven mainly by introduced Polynesian rats; the "ecocide" narrative attributing it to human overexploitation is largely a modern projection.
4. The Classic Maya decline was political decentralization with sustained populations; by my own definition in (1), calling it a collapse is technically wrong.
5. Societies rarely "fail" in any normative sense; they usually adapt to lower net energy capture by simplifying, and that simplification is locally adaptive rather than catastrophic.
6. Direct analogies between ancient collapses and modern civilization are structurally flawed, because modern complexity is maintained by transregional, non-substitutable networks that have no Bronze Age or Maya equivalent.
7. Despite (6), the collapse framework is indispensable for modern risk analysis, because tightly coupled global systems exhibit nonlinear vulnerability that the concept captures well.
8. I accept a demographic metabolism view—complexity tracks net energy capture per capita—but I deny it implies cultural determinism or energy monocausality.
9. I regularly use the Bronze Age palace-network cascade as a direct structural analog for modern supply-chain fragility in semiconductors and food distribution.
