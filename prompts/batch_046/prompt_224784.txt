I'm running a session for a small reading group of non-specialist adults — lawyers, engineers, and a secondary-school teacher — who have read some popular psychology but no academic moral philosophy. I want them to grasp not merely the distinction between guilt and shame, but the stronger claim that guilt is the more morally productive emotion, and the further claim that a culture can, through deliberate institutional design, shift its dominant moral emotional regime from one to the other. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself: the order in which the conceptual components must be introduced and why each depends on the previous; the single carrying example that makes the functional difference visible; the misconceptions they will bring or form in sequence; where in the arc each misconception should be intercepted; the check for understanding that reveals whether they have seen that the normative claim depends on the functional analysis rather than on personal preference; and what should be deliberately omitted at this level, with an explicit note that it has been left out.

The audience will arrive thinking that guilt is private and shame is public, that "shame cultures" are uniformly oppressive, that emotional regimes are like policies that can be switched overnight, and that if guilt is healthier then shame is always bad. Choose one carrying example involving collective rather than purely individual harm — it must display both the reparative potential of guilt and the status-destructive risk of shame without collapsing either into caricature, and it must work for people with no philosophical training. Map the dependency structure: what idea has to be in place before the normative comparison even makes sense, and what idea has to be in place before the cultural-choice question is intelligible?
