In debates about rational agency and artificial self-modification, I keep encountering the maxim: 'If you will not trust your own reasoning, you possess no instrument with which to correct it.' The phrase is routinely invoked to resist demands that an agent suspend confidence in its own consistency, particularly in discussions of the Löbian obstacle for self-trusting systems. I want the slogan pulled apart rather than repeated. Set out the distinct precise theses it could express—from the near-tautology that revision requires some retained standard of evaluation, through the substantive claim that a sound reasoning system must be able to prove its own consistency for every derivable theorem, to the false claim that any localised self-doubt necessarily collapses into global scepticism. For each reading, state whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and specify which version a speaker actually needs if they are using the maxim to defend embedding a direct reflective endorsement loop inside an artificial agent—where the agent conditions present updates on a proof that its future belief states will not diverge beyond a bounded region.

Then take a committed position: on the best defensible reading, does this maxim actually address the Löbian obstacle for agents that update on predictions of their own future beliefs, or does it conflate procedural reliability with unconditional self-endorsement? Give your verdict with reasons, not a balanced survey that leaves the choice to me. In particular, say whether injecting such a niche reflective mechanism makes the slogan more or less applicable, and mark clearly where your own reasoning is weakest.
