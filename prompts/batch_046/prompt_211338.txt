Construct a thought experiment set in a realistic institutional context—specifically, a public-health committee charged with allocating a fixed, non-expandable budget between a preventive mental-health program and an acute trauma-surgery unit—engineered to isolate the divergence between value-level incommensurability, in the Berlin-style pluralist tradition, and reason-level incommensurability, in the Raz-style tradition where reasons themselves fail to combine into an all-things-considered ordering.

The scenario must satisfy the following specifications. First, both programs serve populations of identical size and comparable demographic risk, so that straightforward utilitarian aggregation is held constant and cannot resolve the tie. Second, the committee must adopt a binding, irreversible allocation rule for a five-year term; institutional rules prohibit deferral, splitting, or revisiting the decision. Third, the goods at stake—long-term psychological flourishing distributed across a population versus immediate, catastrophic bodily rescue—must be such that they are not merely assigned different weights on a shared well-being scale but involve structurally different normative logics: one is probabilistic and temporal, the other is deterministic and present-centered. Fourth, the case must generate the verdict that value-level pluralism permits a rational, non-arbitrary institutional choice, perhaps through a secondary procedural value such as democratic legitimacy or institutional integrity, whereas reason-level incommensurability denies that any resolution is rational, because the reasons generated by each value do not form a comparable set and therefore yield no all-things-considered judgment. Fifth, present the scenario in a single paragraph, then demonstrate that it meets each specification: state precisely what pluralism says and why, what reason-level incommensurability says and why, and identify the exact feature of the case—most plausibly the combination of irreversibility with non-aggregable reasons—that forces the divergence. Sixth, explain what happens if that feature is removed: for instance, if the decision becomes reversible or if the reasons can be mapped onto a shared procedural metric, does the divergence collapse, and if so, which view absorbs the other? Seventh, provide an honest assessment of how a defender of the view that comes out poorly—likely reason-level incommensurability, since it renders institutional decision-making rationally groundless—would attack the case, and whether that attack succeeds without presupposing the comparability it needs to establish. The purpose of the case is to determine whether pluralism's appeal to procedural resolution is a substantive philosophical move or a hidden collapse into comparativism. Keep the scenario entirely realistic: no science fiction, no perfect predictors, no demonic agents, and no appeals to specific page citations or bibliographic lists. Do not provide a bibliography; the analysis should stand on its conceptual structure alone.
