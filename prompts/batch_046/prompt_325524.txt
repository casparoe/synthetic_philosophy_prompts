Write an essay on whether Jackson's knowledge argument, as illustrated by the Mary scenario, succeeds in establishing anti-physicalism when extended through an artificial-system analog. In the standard formulation, Mary is a neuroscientist who possesses complete physical information about color vision yet has lived in an entirely monochromatic environment; upon leaving and seeing red for the first time, she allegedly acquires knowledge she previously lacked—namely, what it is like to experience red. Now consider a modified case: an artificial system is constructed with a complete structural and functional model of human color processing, including opponent-process dynamics and the neural correlates associated with subjective reports, but it has never instantiated these processes within an embodied, perceptual context, and it lacks any indexical-recognitional capacity to apply color concepts to its own internal states. Does this analog strengthen the anti-physicalist conclusion, weaken it, or leave it unchanged? In particular, weigh whether the gap between Mary's prior knowledge and her later experience is best explained by (i) the existence of non-physical phenomenal properties, (ii) the acquisition of a phenomenal concept that requires a first-person, embodied mode of presentation that remains fully compatible with physicalism, or (iii) a merely conceptual or indexical limitation that applies equally to the artificial system and therefore reveals nothing about ontology. Your essay should address the objection that phenomenal concepts are not merely missing descriptions but missing recognitional capacities formed through embodied interaction, and it should consider whether the AI analog demonstrates that such capacities are themselves physically implementable in principle, even if not yet instantiated. Reach and justify a definite verdict: does Mary's argument, even in this extended form, demonstrate that there are facts about consciousness that necessarily escape any complete physical description?
