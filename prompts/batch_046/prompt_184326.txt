I work as a software engineer with no formal training in moral philosophy, but I have been reading about ethical reasoning in automated systems because it comes up in discussions of AI alignment and recommendation-system design. A colleague recently summarized the debate over whether AI should reason as utilitarians or as deontologists, and while his account sounded coherent, I suspect it may be an engineering caricature that smooths over important philosophical distinctions. He said that utilitarian reasoning for an AI means computing a single aggregate welfare function across all affected users and always selecting the action that maximizes total good, which necessarily treats individual rights as purely instrumental—so a genuinely utilitarian system ought to be willing, in principle, to violate one user's privacy or even disable one account if that produces a net gain for millions of others. By contrast, he described deontological reasoning as encoding an absolute, flat list of prohibitions—never deceive, never delete without explicit consent, never discriminate—such that the system checks each proposed action against these rules and rejects it if any rule is violated, regardless of the outcome. He then claimed that deontology is computationally intractable at scale because the rule set is potentially infinite and conflicts between rules have no built-in resolution procedure. He also mentioned something called threshold deontology, in which rules may be overridden once consequences cross a catastrophic boundary, but he dismissed this as essentially utilitarianism with an added safety margin that philosophers themselves reject. I am not sure which parts of this are fair representations of the philosophical positions and which parts conflate doctrine with naive algorithmic implementation. Is this summary accurate? I would like you to separate the claims that correctly capture utilitarian or deontological reasoning from those that misrepresent them—particularly whether deontologists really treat rules as an unstructured checklist with no conflict-resolution mechanism, whether utilitarians genuinely deny any non-instrumental weight to rights, and whether the computational-intractability argument or the dismissal of threshold deontology actually follows from the philosophy rather than from an engineering framing. I am especially interested in whether the colleague has forced a false dichotomy that ignores positions philosophers actually defend in between these two poles.
