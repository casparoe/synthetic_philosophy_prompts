The exam question given to students was: Douglas Hofstadter has argued that statistical machine translation is shallow because it processes language without understanding meaning—specifically, without grasping the semantic content and cultural context that make a translation appropriate rather than merely probable. Evaluate whether the transition from phrase-based statistical models to modern transformer architectures, which use self-attention and large-scale contextual embeddings, resolves, sidesteps, or reproduces his core objection. In your answer, distinguish between syntactic disambiguation (for example, resolving polysemy through surrounding words) and the kind of semantic aboutness—representations being directed at states of affairs in the world—that philosophers in the Brentano tradition call intentionality.

One student submitted the following answer:

Hofstadter's central claim is that statistical machine translation lacks intentionality, the property Brentano defined as mental states being about something. In older phrase-table systems this was obvious: the model matched surface patterns without recognizing that "bank" could denote a river edge or a financial institution. Modern transformers solve this through self-attention, which builds dynamic contextual embeddings that capture meaning relationships across entire sentences. Because these representations are distributed and globally updated, they achieve genuine semantic aboutness that phrase-based models lacked. Therefore Hofstadter's critique is largely historical: it targets the 1990s statistical paradigm, when corpora were too small to support deep contextual inference, but it does not apply to neural networks, which approximate human understanding through large-scale pattern extraction. The symbol-grounding problem that Hofstadter originally identified is thus overcome by architecture rather than by explicit rules. The only remaining valid point is that cultural intent is still hard to capture, but that is now a practical engineering limitation rather than a philosophical deficiency.

I am not asking for your own view on whether machines understand; I am asking whether the student has correctly reconstructed Hofstadter's position and applied it consistently to the neural case. In particular, check whether the student correctly attributes the concept of intentionality to Hofstadter rather than importing philosophical machinery from elsewhere; whether the move from attention mechanisms to semantic aboutness actually follows; and whether treating Hofstadter's objection as historically bounded to 1990s phrase-based systems is consistent with what he actually argued. Note any conflations, misattributions, or unsupported steps, even when the overall intuition sounds reasonable.
