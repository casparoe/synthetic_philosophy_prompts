I'm planning a ninety-minute graduate seminar session—attended by philosophy and cognitive-science students with no prior exposure to evolutionary epistemology—devoted to Daniel Dennett's four-tier taxonomy of minds: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures. Rather than explaining the taxonomy yourself, construct the pedagogical plan. First, lay out the dependency order: which conceptual distinction must be secured before the next tier can be introduced, with particular attention to why the Popperian capacity for offline simulation cannot be taught as merely accelerated Skinnerian conditioning. Second, specify the single carrying example—four agents (biological or artificial) confronting an identical hidden-reward maze—indicating for each agent the precise behavioral signature it must exhibit and the capacity it must conspicuously lack, so that the boundaries between tiers remain non-arbitrary. Third, anticipate the misconceptions this audience will predictably form—such as conflating Darwinian selection with individual stupidity, treating Skinnerian reinforcement as Darwinian evolution compressed in time, reducing Gregorian culture to Popperian reasoning plus vocabulary, and interpreting the hierarchy as normative rather than functional—and map exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be intercepted and dissolved. Fourth, propose one diagnostic question for the end of the session that tests whether students grasp the structural dependencies rather than having memorized the four labels. Finally, identify which elements of Dennett's broader framework—such as his attack on the Cartesian theater or his evolutionary just-so stories—should be explicitly excluded at this level, and justify the omission in terms of protecting the conceptual ladder you have designed.
