I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on philosophy of language, and my study group has been arguing about whether the standard taxonomy of performative utterances actually captures the disagreements that matter once you move past introductory examples like marrying or sentencing. We want an instrument—not a comparison essay, but a diagnostic decision tree—that can sort someone into a position on performative speech by asking them concrete questions about cases, not by asking which philosopher they prefer or what label they like.

Please construct a decision tree rooted in the commitment that some utterances genuinely alter institutional reality. Each internal node should present a specific claim or hypothetical case on which philosophical positions come apart, answerable without prior knowledge of academic labels. I want the tree to branch through at least these four diagnostic splits: first, whether the speaker’s requisite authority is binary (present or absent) or comes in degrees; second, whether a successful performative creates an institutional fact entirely de novo or merely activates a latent normative framework that was already in place; third, whether misfires (like an unauthorized officiant) and abuses (like an insincere appointment) should be explained by the same mechanism or by categorically different failures; and fourth, whether performative force can operate asynchronously or across virtual media—say, a pre-recorded video playing the words “I do” at a ceremony—or whether co-presence is essential. For each leaf, name the position if it has one (for instance, a strict conventionalist view, a constitutive-rules view, an institutional-activation model, or an unnamed combination), summarize its core commitment in one sentence, and note its most awkward implication. Please also map the logical space: identify any combination of answers that no prominent view occupies, briefly diagnosing whether that vacancy is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your tree fails to genuinely separate—positions that agree on all four nodes but diverge only in framing or emphasis—rather than forcing a false distinction.

Throughout, separate what is relatively settled among specialists (for example, that performatives depend on conventional procedures of some kind) from what remains genuinely contested (such as whether those procedures are best understood as collective intentions, formal rules, or social norms), and do not present any contested interpretation as if it were the consensus. Keep every node phrased as a question about what happens in a case, not as “do you hold view X?”
