I'm developing an argument for a workshop on AI governance that now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to overgeneralize, and I'd like a precise diagnosis of where the chain breaks. Here are the steps as I've formalized them. (1) Define the alignment tax T as the expected capability degradation required to make a frontier model satisfy a given safety criterion S. (2) In a competitive equilibrium among firms developing frontier models, the actor with the lowest effective T—meaning the least safety investment—captures the greatest market share, ceteris paribus. (3) Therefore competitive dynamics systematically select against paying T, making S unsustainable without external coordination. (4) Now extend the reasoning: in any technological domain where safety requires a performance tax—pharmaceutical testing, aviation certification, nuclear safeguards—competitive dynamics should erode safety standards in the absence of regulation. (5) But in those domains, regulatory frameworks have successfully sustained safety standards despite competitive pressure, which shows that the competitiveness constraint is not intrinsically fatal to safety investment. (6) Apply this back to AI: if regulation can solve the problem for pharmaceuticals, it can solve it for AI, so the alignment tax is not uniquely fatal. (7) However, I want to preserve a disanalogy: AI development has a recursive self-improvement property—models can be used to design more capable successors—so competitive pressure does not merely erode safety; it accelerates the very capabilities that inflate T. (8) From (7) it follows that the regulatory solution available to pharmaceuticals is unavailable to AI, because by the time a regulator understands the system well enough to regulate it, the system has already improved beyond the regulatory framework's comprehension. (9) Therefore the competitiveness constraint is uniquely fatal for AI alignment, unlike for other technologies. (10) But this proves too much: if (8) holds, then no regulatory framework could govern any exponentially improving technology, which would include not just AI but also synthetic biology, advanced materials science, and any domain with rapid feedback loops. (11) Yet we do not conclude that nuclear energy or gene editing are unregulatable in principle; we conclude that regulation must adapt its tempo and structure. (12) So either (8) is false—there is no unique unregulatability—or (9) collapses, meaning the competitiveness constraint for AI is not different in kind from other domains, and the original argument that it makes alignment impossible is overstated.

Where does this go wrong? I am especially attached to keeping (7) about recursive self-improvement, and I also want to preserve the intuition that AI governance faces a distinctive tempo problem. But I worry there is an equivocation between "competitive dynamics select against safety" and "competitive dynamics make safety impossible," or perhaps a modal slide between "regulation is difficult at this speed" and "regulation is impossible in principle." Can you identify the specific step that fails, explain why it looks plausible, and price the alternatives—particularly what I pay if I reject (7) versus what I pay if I reject (8)? If I have to choose, I would rather preserve the distinctiveness claim in (7) and absorb the cost elsewhere, but I want to know exactly what that cost is.
