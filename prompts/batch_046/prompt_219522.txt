I am a doctoral student in moral and political philosophy working on the ethics of armed conflict, and I have been struck by how the debate over the moral equality of combatants seems to have reached a stable impasse despite decades of precise engagement on both sides. The traditionalist position holds that combatants on opposing sides possess symmetric rights to kill and be killed not because their causes are equally just, but because they occupy a shared institutional role defined by reciprocal risk and because they typically lack both the epistemic access and the freedom required to judge the war’s justice reliably. The revisionist counter-position argues that moral permission to kill in war derives directly from individual liability and just cause, which entails that unjust combatants lack permission to fight and are liable to defensive killing, thereby collapsing the equality thesis entirely. Both camps accept the same descriptive premises—that combatants are often coerced, that states systematically obscure information, and that the laws of war require discrimination—yet they diverge radically on whether these premises generate symmetric permissions or asymmetric liabilities.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not ultimately about missing empirical data, but about a hidden divergence in the model of agency being applied: traditionalists treat combatant status as a relational, corporate role that distributes moral permissions institutionally, whereas revisionists treat it as an aggregate of individual moral judgments that must be justified case by case. I also suspect there is a deeper, largely unacknowledged dispute about what I would call epistemic risk allocation—whether the uncertainty surrounding a war’s justice should be borne by the individual combatant, who must refrain unless certain, or by the institutional framework, which permits action under conditions of reasonable uncertainty—and whether this is genuinely a moral disagreement or rather a disagreement about which epistemic standards are appropriate in conditions of collective violence. Is the stall therefore evidence that the question is ill-posed, or are the sides simply answering different questions—one about legitimate authority and role morality, the other about individual justification—under the shared label of combatant equality? I want an honest diagnosis with reasons, not a literature survey, together with an assessment of whether any conceptual distinction, formal result, or change of question could actually end the impasse, or whether no progress should be expected.

Each item should address a distinct aspect of the diagnosis: the nature of the disagreement, the hidden premises about agency or epistemic risk, whether it is verbal or substantive, what form of progress might resolve or dissolve it, and whether the impasse is likely permanent.
