I'm trying to stabilize the following package of views on harm aggregation, centered on the classic torture-versus-dust-specks comparison, and I want to know whether they form a coherent position or whether I'm relying on an unstated exception. My commitments are:

(1) There is a qualitative discontinuity between torturing one person and distributing barely perceptible irritations across an enormous population—no finite number of dust-speck harms is morally equivalent to the torture case.

(2) At the same time, I accept that aggregation operates across populations: there is some scale at which the cumulative burden of distributed trivial harms outweighs the burden of the single torture case.

(3) I deny any sharp, metaphysically privileged boundary between severe and trivial harms; severity is continuous and context-sensitive.

(4) Consequently, I reject lexical thresholds—there is no magical number N at which harm suddenly becomes incommensurable with smaller harms, and positing one is ad hoc.

(5) I also hold that part of torture's distinctive wrongness lies in a structural violation of dignity or autonomy, not merely in the quantitative intensity of suffering, and that this structural feature is absent in distributed trivial harms.

Audit this set. In particular: is the tension between (1) and (2) genuine once (3) and (4) are in place, or does (5) dissolve it? If (5) does all the work in preserving (1), can (2) still be justified without smuggling back the very cutoff I rejected in (4)? Identify any pairs that only appear to conflict, order the real tensions by severity, say which commitment is cheapest to revise or abandon, and state whether the package can be preserved intact through clarification alone or requires giving something up.
