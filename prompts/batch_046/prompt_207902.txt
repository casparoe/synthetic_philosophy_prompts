I am a retired physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has turned my attention to metaphysics, particularly to the philosophy of time. I am currently working through Jorge Luis Borges's essay "A New Refutation of Time," and I find myself stuck on a specific argument in which Borges moves from the possibility of identical mental moments to a denial of temporal sequence altogether. In the passage, Borges writes:

These tautologies (and others I shall not disclose) are my whole life. Naturally, they recur without design; they are variations of emphasis, temperature, light, general psychological state. I suspect, nonetheless, that the number of circumstantial variants is not infinite: we can postulate, in the mind of an individual (or of two individuals who do not know each other but in whom the same process is operative), two identical moments. Once this identity is postulated, we may ask: Are not these identical moments the same moment? Is not one single repeated terminal point enough to disrupt and confound the series in time [(or) the history of the world, to reveal that there is no such history]? Are the enthusiasts who devote themselves to a line of Shakespeare not literally Shakespeare? ….

What I cannot grasp is the precise logical mechanism by which Borges derives the collapse of temporal series from the mere postulation of two identical mental states. Is he advancing an argument from the identity of indiscernibles applied to moments—such that if two moments share every property they are not two but one, and therefore the linear ordering that connects them dissolves? Or is he making a more radical claim about the constitution of personal identity, analogous in some ways to the puzzles that arise in statistical mechanics when one considers Poincaré recurrence, where a system returns to a state indistinguishable from its initial condition? As someone accustomed to treating identical physical states as distinct occurrences indexed to different temporal coordinates, I am unsure whether Borges intends to reject that indexing altogether, or merely to suggest that in the mental realm such indexing is an illusion. I would be grateful if you could help me parse the inference: specifically, does the identity of the two moments entail that they occupy the same point in a non-successive reality, and if so, how does that undermine the existence of history rather than merely reducing it to a circular repetition? I do not need references to secondary literature; I am interested in the internal logic of the passage itself.
