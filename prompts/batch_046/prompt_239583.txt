Two scholars in my seminar were arguing about whether Benedict Anderson's account of nations as imagined communities and Eric Hobsbawm's analysis of invented traditions converge on the claim that nations are essentially modern. The first argued that both authors agree: pre-modern groups lacked the anonymous, horizontal comradeship Anderson requires, and any tradition reaching back before roughly the late eighteenth century is, in Hobsbawm's terms, genuinely continuous rather than invented, so calling medieval kingdoms nations is anachronistic. The second objected that Anderson's print-capitalism criterion is overly specific: manuscript-based religious networks—certain transregional Christian and Islamic scholarly and devotional communities—already fostered a form of imagined solidarity among people who would never meet, which suggests Anderson's modern cutoff is arbitrary rather than structural. The first responded that this misses Anderson's precise claim: it is not solidarity per se but the modality of anonymity combined with simultaneous, calendrical time that distinguishes the nation, and manuscript elites never achieved mass anonymity. The second then proposed a distinction she called mnemonic continuity versus performative continuity: she claimed Hobsbawm's real insight is that traditions survive through repeated performance, not through intact memory chains, and that many apparently ancient traditions were reconstructed after mnemonic breaks without anyone consciously fabricating them. The first countered that if reconstructed performance alone counts as invention, then Hobsbawm's thesis collapses into the trivial observation that all culture changes, erasing his specific point about rapid, self-conscious fabrication in response to modern social disruption. The second insisted she was not broadening the concept but clarifying it: Hobsbawm shows that narratives of mnemonic continuity are themselves ideological masks for performative discontinuity, regardless of whether the reconstruction was deliberate.

Neither convinced the other, and they turned to me for an adjudication. Please go through their exchange point by point: on Anderson's modernity claim, on whether manuscript communities genuinely challenge his framework, on whether this mnemonic versus performative distinction helps clarify Hobsbawm or instead dissolves his specific thesis, and on what—if anything—both sides are overlooking. In doing so, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists in this area, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
