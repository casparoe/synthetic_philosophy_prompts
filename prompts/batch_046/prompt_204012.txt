I have been discussing with colleagues whether the Kelly criterion can be defended not merely as a heuristic for gamblers but as a normative constraint on rational belief revision when outcomes are sequential and capital—whether monetary or evidential—is multiplicative rather than additive. To help me think through the tension between arithmetic expectation and geometric survival, could you write a villanelle—nineteen lines, five tercets, and a concluding quatrain, with two alternating refrains—about the moment a bettor realizes that maximizing expected value is structurally self-defeating across repeated trials?

Use the first refrain, appearing at lines 1, 6, 12, and 18—"The edge multiplied becomes the growth"—to capture the geometric law of compounding survival. Use the second refrain, appearing at lines 3, 9, 15, and 19—"The mean promises more than time allows"—to capture the arithmetic illusion that collapses under repetition. The opening tercets should establish the naive picture: the bettor computing a single-trial expectation, convinced that risk is merely emotional and that a large edge justifies a large wager. The turn should come with the recognition that ruin is an absorbing boundary, not a remote tail event, and that the optimal fraction is not derived from risk aversion but from the logarithmic shape of growth: wager too little and capital stagnates, wager too much—beyond the Kelly fraction, which equals the edge divided by the odds—and the long-run rate turns negative, making eventual ruin structurally inevitable. In the closing quatrain, both refrains should appear together, ending not on comfort but on the austere recognition that rationality, in this frame, demands accepting lower arithmetic returns in exchange for the only kind of expectation that survives repetition. Please make the rhyme scheme itself do conceptual work—repeating sounds should feel like compounding or erosion—so that the poem is technically accurate enough that someone familiar with the Kelly literature would recognize the argument, and formally disciplined enough to stand as verse rather than a rhymed lecture.
