I'm currently taking an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind and formal methods, and I'm developing a proposal for a short independent research project—perhaps for a departmental fellowship or as the framework for my final seminar paper. The question I want to pursue is whether Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments in the Lucas-Penrose tradition retain any targeted force once we restrict their scope to contemporary "reflective" mechanist theories rather than to mechanism in general. Most discussions treat the argument either as a decisive refutation of all computational theories of mind or as a dead letter because it confuses meta-theoretic reasoning with object-level computation. I think both assessments are too quick, because they overlook a middle path: formal architectures that are designed to be self-modifying or axiom-revising, which remain mechanist in the broad sense but do not map neatly onto the fixed, recursively enumerable formal system that Gödel's theorems directly govern.

My proposal asks precisely this: if we model human mathematical cognition not as derivation within a single, consistent, fixed axiomatic framework, but as a dynamic process of system-transition guided by informal insight, does the Lucas-Penrose argument still generate a genuine contradiction for the mechanist? And if not, does the failure depend on technical features of self-modifying architectures, or on a prior conceptual mistake about what "knowing one's own consistency" requires? The existing positions—at least in the standard philosophy-of-mind surveys I have encountered—tend to settle the matter by declaring that human mathematicians are not formal systems, full stop. That answer leaves open whether there is a more precise, limited version of the argument that targets only those mechanist accounts that do treat cognition as formal derivation within an unchanging framework.

The approach I would take is conceptual analysis paired with informal architectural modeling rather than original formal logic. I would distinguish three mechanist architectures—static formal-system models, self-modifying but consistency-preserving models, and self-modifying models that permit temporary inconsistency—then ask for each whether the Lucas-Penrose premises can be jointly satisfied without contradiction. I choose this framing over the obvious alternatives—either a purely historical exegesis of Lucas and Penrose, or a technical mathematical rebuttal—because the live issue for cognitive science is not whether Gödel's theorems are true, but which mechanist commitments make a theory vulnerable to them. The deliverable would be a typology of mechanist commitments that shows exactly where Gödelian pressure applies and where it dissipates, together with a clear criterion for when a computational model of reasoning avoids the objection not by being non-mechanist, but by being the right kind of mechanist. A concrete result might look like this: the argument succeeds only against theories that combine formal completeness with explicit self-certifying consistency claims, and fails against architectures that separate derivational closure from meta-level belief revision. Failure, conversely, would be shown if every plausible mechanist architecture either evades the premises or collapses into incoherence—indicating that the argument's real value is diagnostic rather than refutational, telling us what a mechanist theory must avoid claiming rather than proving mechanism false. Given a six-month timeline, I would limit the scope to this conceptual typology and one detailed case study drawn from current debates about reflective revision in automated reasoning systems, without attempting original proofs. What adjustments to this framing and scope would make the deliverable concrete and compelling for reviewers who are not specialists in logic?
