For my philosophy of psychology seminar I'm supposed to write out where I stand on virtue ethics after reading all the situationist stuff, and I'm honestly not sure my view holds together. So here's my profile: I think the classic situationist experiments (Milgram, the dime study, etc.) basically kill the idea that people have global character traits like "honesty" or "courage" that predict behavior across contexts—I think those traits have near-zero predictive power. But I don't want to abandon virtue ethics entirely; I think it still describes something real about moral achievement, except virtues have to be reconceived as "executive competences"—basically domain-specific skills of attention and emotional regulation, not internal dispositions. I reject the usual social-psych fallback of "local traits" (like being honest-in-the-office but not honest-at-home) because that makes virtues too fragmentated to do any real normative work. At the same time, I think long-term narrative identity—the story you tell about yourself over years—genuinely shapes behavior across contexts, though I don't think it's a "trait" in the psychological sense; it's more like a self-model that constrains your predicitive processing. I also think moral education should mostly target institutional choise architectures and environment design, not "character building," since these executive competences don't generalize enough for generic habituation to work. But I still think Aristotlean habituation is valid, just only for procedural skills like recognizing moral salience in specific domains, not for building global character. Can you go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which just sound weird? I need the conflicts ordered by severity, what the cheapest fix is for each, and then which single revision would clean up the most mess overall.
