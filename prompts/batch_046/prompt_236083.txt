I have been trying to stabilize a position on the singleton hypothesis and long-horizon coordination, and I would like an audit of whether the following commitments hang together or pull in incompatible directions. Here is the package: (1) I hold that competitive dynamics among sufficiently advanced decision-making systems make the emergence of a singleton—a single agent or tightly integrated system with decisive strategic advantage—structurally likely rather than merely possible, because returns to strategic coherence increase nonlinearly at scale. (2) I hold that value is irreducibly plural in a strong sense: there is no unique correct utility function or objective hierarchy capable of representing everything worth preserving, and any attempt to collapse plural goods into a single optimization target eliminates distinctions that matter. (3) Despite (2), I hold that should a singleton emerge, its operating values ought to be derived from a prior negotiated compact or credible pre-commitment among distributed agents, rather than being whatever values the first system to achieve decisive advantage happens to encode. (4) I hold that the trajectory toward a singleton is likely to be discontinuous and epistemically opaque—governed by contingencies that resist reliable modeling—so that detailed institutional architectures designed to steer the transition are likely to be wasted effort; the appropriate response is to preserve optionality and adaptive capacity rather than to prescribe structures. (5) I also hold that rational agents facing a potential coordination failure of this magnitude ought to bind themselves in advance, through credible commitments, to cooperative equilibria before competitive dynamics foreclose the option of doing so.

Please assess whether this is a coherent package. Specifically: does the combination of (1) and (2) undermine (3), since structural inevitability combined with value pluralism may imply that there is no coherent collective value-set to encode? Does (4) genuinely conflict with (5), given that credible pre-commitment seems to require precisely the kind of architectural confidence and predictive capacity that (4) denies? Is there a further tension between (2) and (5), if irreducible pluralism makes unanimous or binding agreement among prior agents impossible? Additionally, please identify any apparent conflicts—such as between (1) and (4)—that dissolve on inspection, and order all genuine tensions by severity. For each tension, state what would have to be modified or abandoned to restore consistency, and indicate which revision is the least costly in terms of the overall position.
