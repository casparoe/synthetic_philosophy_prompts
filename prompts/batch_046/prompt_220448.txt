I am composing a novel in which an actress returns repeatedly to a tragic role, not for catharsis but for a more disciplined practice: the cultivation of sorrow as a private craft. For a scene in which she explains this to a skeptical director, I require a poem—strictly a sestina, with the end-words fiction, tears, contract, rehearsal, safe, and grief—delivered in her voice. The form itself must perform the paradox it describes: the cyclical return of the end-words should mirror the audience’s voluntary return to suffering they could avoid, and the envoi must bring contract and rehearsal into direct adjacency, proposing that our pleasure in tragic art arises not from purging emotion but from entering a tacit agreement to rehearse grief within a protected frame. The poem should acknowledge, without simplifying, the central contradiction—that we flee real anguish yet actively seek its representation—while allowing the refrain structure to show how the same sorrow can be endured precisely because it is known to be safe. Render it with sufficient philosophical accuracy that a reader familiar with the paradox recognizes the tension between aesthetic distance and genuine feeling, yet with enough lyrical precision that it stands as verse rather than exposition. Do not include annotations, citations, or references to named philosophers; let the idea live entirely in the imagery and in the turning of the lines.
