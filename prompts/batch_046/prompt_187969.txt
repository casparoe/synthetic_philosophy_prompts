For the final assessment in my graduate seminar on philosophy of psychiatry, I need one essay question that forces students to apply a looping-effect framework—where diagnostic categories interact with the people classified—to a case that does not appear in standard textbooks, rather than letting them summarize the theory in the abstract. The scenario should center on a proposed clinical category, chronic digital dissociation, describing sustained perceptual dislocation and altered self-boundary experiences reported by long-term users of immersive virtual environments. Self-identified patient groups have begun organizing online, circulating symptom checklists, and lobbying for formal recognition in diagnostic manuals; clinicians observe that the presenting complaints are shifting in response to these community narratives, with earlier cohorts reporting different phenomenological profiles than those who entered care after the advocacy networks formed. The exam prompt should ask students to analyze whether this dynamic is best understood as a genuine looping effect, as a biologically anchored response to technological exposure that happens to attract social labeling, or as pure social construction with no independent clinical reality, and to specify what conceptual or empirical considerations would decide among these interpretations. Please provide: (1) the exam question, designed for 90 minutes and approximately 1,000 words; (2) a model answer of about 600 words; (3) a marking scheme organized around five must-hit analytical moves, with point allocations totaling 25, and a brief one-line note on the specific failure mode or misconception that costs marks on each move. The question should be unanswerable by reciting definitions of interactive kinds; it should reward precise distinctions between looping effects, epidemiological feedback loops, and performative labeling, and it should require students to address what changes—if anything—in the clinical phenomenon itself versus in its reporting and institutional framing.
