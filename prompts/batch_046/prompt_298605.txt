You serve on a three-person technical review panel for an intergovernmental biodiversity board that evaluates species-level recovery proposals. The board receives roughly a dozen submissions per quarter, each requesting a decade of funding and regulatory protection for a specific population. Your mandate is not ecological merit but ontological eligibility: you must determine whether the target population qualifies as a defensible species unit under at least one established concept—biological, phylogenetic, morphological, or ecological—while excluding demonstrably transient hybrid swarms and domesticated feral lineages that have not achieved evolutionary independence.

Your panel operates under tight constraints: thirty minutes per proposal, one biologist and one legal officer with no philosophy training, joint sign-off required, and escalation to the full board permitted only with a written memo identifying the precise conceptual impasse. Design a reusable decision procedure—an ordered sequence of operational questions, each with a sufficient-answer criterion and an explicit stopping rule—that a non-philosopher can apply consistently across submissions.

The procedure must translate the core philosophical distinctions of the species problem into practical tests without requiring citation of theorists. It must include explicit value hand-off points where judgment about conservation priorities, not conceptual application, takes over. It must address hard cases: reproductive isolation that is geologically recent and potentially reversible; cryptic morphological stasis paired with deep genetic divergence; ecological niche separation without reproductive barriers; and stable, multi-generational hybrid populations that maintain intermediate phenotypes.

Be explicit about which steps are irreducibly judgmental rather than mechanically decisive. Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals:

First, a freshwater fish population isolated in a volcanic lake system formed approximately eight millennia ago. It is morphologically indistinguishable from its marine ancestor but exhibits complete reproductive isolation.

Second, a wetland frog population arising from sustained gene flow between two recognized species. It occupies a distinct ecological niche and displays stable intermediate morphology across at least six generations, with no clear reproductive barrier in either direction.

Third, a canid population domesticated centuries ago, then feral for roughly two hundred years. It now shows wild-type morphology and behavior but retains detectable genetic signatures of recent domestication; the submitting government argues it represents a re-emerging wild species deserving protection.

For each case, show whether the procedure yields approval, rejection, or escalation, and identify the case that produces an unwelcome result for the proposing party—explaining why the procedure demands that outcome despite the political cost.

Conclude by stating what the procedure necessarily cannot do: which borderline configurations will always force escalation regardless of design, and which metaphysical commitments—regarding whether species are discovered natural kinds or constructed explanatory tools—the procedure silently presupposes without being able to justify them.
