I'm trying to articulate the conditions under which a welfare state can legitimately distinguish between "deserving" and "undeserving" claimants—through workfare mandates, fraud investigations, or benefit sanctions—without collapsing into either unconditional universalism or punitive moralism. I'm particularly struck by what we might call the epistemic asymmetry of deservingness: the state demands that claimants prove their moral entitlement to support, yet the criteria are administratively constructed, often opaque, and applied with structural bias, so that the burden of justification is shifted onto the vulnerable party rather than onto the institution that withholds aid. Before I assess particular normative frameworks, I want to fix the standard of evaluation itself.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate normative theory of welfare conditionality and the "undeserving poor" must deliver. For each requirement, identify the motivating case or conceptual tension—such as (a) the false-positive problem in fraud detection, where legitimate claimants are wrongly denied; (b) the dignity constraint, that conditionality must not amount to bureaucratic humiliation or status degradation; (c) the reciprocity demand, that contributors should not be forced to subsidize free-riders if free-riding is genuinely present; (d) the epistemic fairness condition, that the state rather than the claimant should bear the burden of proof when moral entitlement is genuinely in doubt; and (e) the institutional feasibility constraint, that the theory not require omniscient or incorruptible administrators. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—especially whether rigorous fraud-prevention can coexist with epistemic fairness and dignity—and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding inadequate candidates. Then, briefly, score contractualist reciprocity, luck-egalitarian desert, and relational equality against this list, keeping the weight on whether they satisfy the criteria rather than on exegesis of the views themselves.
