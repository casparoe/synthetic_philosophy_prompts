As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the past three years in systematic study of comparative epistemology, I am scheduled to present a seminar paper to the philosophy of science colloquium. My thesis is that Jain anekāntavāda—specifically the saptabhaṅgī schema of seven-fold predication—should be interpreted not as an expression of metaphysical pluralism, cultural tolerance, or soteriological humility, but as a formal constraint on ontological commitment structurally analogous to the principle of complementarity in quantum mechanics. I contend that each predicational mode (syāt asti, syāt nāsti, syāt avaktavya, and the compound forms) functions like a non-commuting observational framework: within any given naya, one descriptive mode is determinately applicable, while the others are not false but inapplicable, thereby blocking the reification of any single perspective into an absolute ontology. I draw the parallel not at the level of physical content but at the level of logical architecture—both systems use a structured multiplicity to prevent the illegitimate extrapolation of local descriptive validity into universal metaphysical claim.

The audience is precisely defined. Dr. K. is a specialist in Jain logic who has worked extensively on syādvāda and will defend the orthodox textual interpretation against what she regards as scientistic appropriation. Professor M. is a mathematical logician who considers analogies between classical Indian philosophy and modern physics to be category errors unless they can be expressed in a shared formalism. The remaining members are analytic philosophers of science familiar with Bohr’s complementarity but generally skeptical of extending quantum structural analogies into metaphysics or epistemology.

Please prepare me for the question period. Order the objections by how likely they are to arise and how damaging each would be if mishandled. For each objection, phrase it in the vocabulary and conceptual framework of the interlocutor who will raise it—Dr. K. in philological and soteriological terms, Professor M. in logical-formal terms, and the philosophers of science in methodological and explanatory terms. For each, indicate what I should concede without collapsing the thesis, what I should hold firm, and where my reply is weakest. Identify the specific weakest point in my own argument—namely, whether the complementarity analogy survives formal scrutiny or collapses into mere metaphor—and advise me on how to defend that point in under two minutes without overclaiming. Include the objection I am most hoping nobody asks, which I anticipate from Dr. K.: whether my reading systematically erases the soteriological and ethical function of anekāntavāda by treating it as a logical technology rather than a path to kevala-jñāna. Provide concise, time-appropriate replies suitable for a seminar Q&A. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or suggestions for further reading; I need only the strategic preparation.
