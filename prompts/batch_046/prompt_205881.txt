I'm preparing a long-form feature on the normative architecture of labor time, and I'm trying to move past the familiar cycle in which Weber's thesis about work as a moral calling is mentioned, France's statutory thirty-five-hour cap is noted as an institutional counterweight, and Japanese karoshi is invoked as a pathological endpoint. Those three nodes are well known, but the conceptual space between them feels under-theorized, particularly around what I would call cognitive off-duty rights: protections not merely against physical presence at work, but against the mental intrusion of work-related obligations during ostensibly free time. The thirty-five-hour framework regulates clock time; karoshi often involves unmeasured, internalized pressure that persists even when the body is technically away from the office; and the Weberian ethos treats mental preoccupation with one's calling as a virtue rather than a violation. Given that framing, I need to decide where reporting energy and institutional capital should actually go.

Specifically, I want a defended agenda rather than a survey. For researchers, policymakers, or funders with limited resources—a single legislative session, a research group, or a foundation grant—which open questions, unresolved tensions, or empirical and philosophical lines of work in this triad matter most? Rank them, but defend the ranking with explicit criteria: near-term tractability, whether resolving one question unlocks progress on the others, how neglected the issue is relative to potential impact, and whether it addresses the quantitative-time dimension, the cognitive-intrusion dimension, or both. Which two or three lines of inquiry would genuinely change how we regulate, measure, or ethically evaluate labor if pursued seriously? Conversely, which widely discussed angles—perhaps debates about whether shorter weeks boost productivity, or whether the Protestant work ethic is culturally unique—are overrated or fail to connect to the problem of cognitive off-duty rights? I also want clarity on what would change your ranking: if new data showed that mental intrusion is largely voluntary rather than structurally coerced, or if a jurisdiction successfully enforced cognitive boundaries without reducing clock hours, how should priorities shift? The deliverable should be a point-by-point audit of where effort belongs and why, not a bibliography or a literature review.
