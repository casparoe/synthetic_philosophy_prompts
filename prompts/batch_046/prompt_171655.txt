I am drafting an essay for a seminar in the history of philosophy of science, and I would value your critical comments on its conceptual architecture and historical framing. The thesis is that Newton’s private theological commitment to a single, undivided divine dominion—his anti-Trinitarian insistence on one supreme being whose authority is not partitioned among co-equal persons—bears a structural analogy to his metaphysical defense of absolute space and absolute motion in the Principia. Specifically, I argue that in the Scholium to the Definitions of Book I, Newton distinguishes absolute motion from relative motion by appealing to a framework that is not itself composed of movable bodies or internal relations. I propose that this non-relational, infinite, unitary framework functions conceptually like Newton’s unitary deity: both serve as the ultimate, indivisible ground within which all particular relations occur. On this reading, absolute space is not merely a mathematical device for dynamics but carries a theological signature, functioning analogously to the idea—familiar from Newton’s theological manuscripts and from the Opticks queries—that space might stand in relation to God not as a part but as an active principle of presence.

The essay addresses three anticipated objections. First, the standard separation thesis holds that Newton’s science and theology are methodologically independent; I respond that methodological independence does not entail metaphysical disconnection, and that the ontology available to Newton may still be constrained by his theological commitments. Second, I consider the charge of genetic fallacy: even if theological belief motivated Newton, this does not validate or invalidate his physics. I reply that my claim is not about motivation but about conceptual continuity—the ontology of absolute space is intelligible within the same unitary, non-partitioned metaphysical schema that governs his theology, which makes the separation thesis harder to maintain as a purely metaphysical claim. Third, I note that scholars disagree over whether Newton was an Arian, a Socinian, or some other form of anti-Trinitarian; I argue that for my purposes the precise Christological taxonomy matters less than the shared commitment to an undivided supreme dominion.

I also identify a potential misfire in my own argument. If absolute space is analogized too closely to divine omnipresence, then Newton’s careful distinction between space as a container and God as an active agent risks collapse, which might undermine rather than support the autonomy of mechanics from theology.

Please comment specifically on the following, without offering bibliographies or reading lists:

- Whether the analogy between unitary divine dominion and the metaphysics of absolute space is philosophically substantive or merely decorative metaphor.
- Whether my handling of the genetic fallacy objection is adequate, or whether I conflate conceptual genealogy with logical justification.
- Whether I mischaracterize the relationship between Newton’s anti-Trinitarian notebooks and the published arguments of the Principia.
- Whether there is a historical inaccuracy in framing his theological manuscripts as sharing an ontology with his physics, given that the manuscripts remained largely unpublished.

Additionally, please indicate where the argument seems to overreach and where it might be strengthened by a distinction I have overlooked.
