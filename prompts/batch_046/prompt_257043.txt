Compare the standard intuition-based reading of Descartes's cogito—according to which the thinker immediately grasps that thinking entails being in a direct, non-inferential act of self-awareness—with the performative self-verification reading, on which the cogito is not an inference but a reflexive speech-act: the very attempt to doubt one's own existence while engaged in thought pragmatically presupposes the existence of a doubter. In particular, assess whether the performative reading genuinely dissolves the classic objection that Descartes relies on an unstated general premise linking thought to existence, a premise his method of doubt has not yet licensed. Then consider how both readings relate to a more restrictive modal-stability interpretation, which holds that the cogito establishes only the co-occurrence of thinking and being at a given moment without committing Descartes to any thesis about temporal persistence, substantial identity, or the conditions under which the same thinker survives a gap in consciousness. Please separate what is textually settled in Descartes's Meditations and related writings from what reflects later interpretive reconstruction, and do not present any single reading as the unanimous view among scholars of early modern philosophy.
