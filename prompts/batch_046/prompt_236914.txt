Construct a biologically realistic thought experiment — presentable in one paragraph and suitable for an undergraduate with basic biology literacy — that drives apart etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role (systemic-capacity) accounts of biological function for a single trait, under the following hard constraints. First, the trait must not be one of the canonical textbook examples (no heart, eye, feather, appendix, flagellum, or kidney). Second, it must have an unambiguous evolutionary history: in the ancestral lineage it was clearly selected for one specific contribution (call it Function A). Third, in the present organism and environment the trait makes no contribution to Function A, but it makes a different, stable contribution (call it Function B) to survival or reproduction. Fourth, the etiological account should deny that the trait currently has Function B (either denying any present function or retaining the now-causally-inert Function A), while the causal-role account should assign Function B. Fifth, the scenario must not invoke intentional design, synthetic engineering metaphors, or "purpose" language that presupposes one account. Sixth, everything except the split between evolutionary origin and current causal contribution must be held fixed, and the verdicts must be generated from the theories' own criteria rather than stipulated. The case is intended for a philosophy-of-biology discussion testing whether the origin/contribution distinction can yield opposite function-assignments without smuggling in design intuitions; do not cheat by making Function B epiphenomenal or Function A ambiguously present.

Deliver the scenario, then run the check: state the exact claim about the trait's function being evaluated; identify the method each theory uses to assign it; specify the relevant conditions or nearby worlds for each account; and show precisely why the etiological account delivers its verdict and why the causal-role account delivers the opposite one.

Then provide an honest diagnostic: identify which feature of the case does the isolating work; describe what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed (for instance, if the trait no longer contributes to Function B, or if the evolutionary history is unknown or contested); and explain how a defender of the account that comes out badly — the one that denies the intuitively present current function — would attack the case, whether by reclassifying the trait as a spandrel, challenging the stability or adaptiveness of Function B, or arguing that reading selection history off present structure involves circularity.

Use American English throughout. Finish with a line beginning exactly "Verdict:" followed by a brief label stating whether the constructed case genuinely separates the two accounts or collapses under the defender's attack.
