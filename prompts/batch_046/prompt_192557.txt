I just spent several hours in a rather intense conversation with a friend about Wittgenstein's private language argument, and I left feeling that we had circled around the same point without resolving it. The standard presentation, as I understand it, is that a language restricted entirely to one's own present inner sensations—pain, for instance—cannot have a meaningful criterion of correct use, because there is no external check that distinguishes actually applying the rule correctly from merely thinking one has done so. My friend proposed an intriguing variation: suppose instead that the private language is used not for present feelings but for one's own future mental states—say, labeling an intention that will only be fulfilled or abandoned days later. In that case, the speaker can later compare the recorded symbol with the mental state that actually occurred, providing a retrospective check on whether the original application was correct. Please argue, rigorously and in detail, that Wittgenstein's critique therefore fails for this temporal variant: the private speaker does possess a normative constraint, but it is anchored in diachronic self-observation rather than in communal practice. You should specifically address why this retrospective verification does not secretly depend on public standards—after all, the future self might be regarded as a different observer—and explain how the speaker avoids the familiar difficulty about the beetle in the box when the box is stretched across time rather than across people. I am working through these issues for an advanced seminar in philosophy of mind, and I want to see whether the private-language argument has a hidden assumption that mental contents must be synchronically available to be meaningful, or whether its force genuinely extends to all self-directed descriptive practices regardless of temporal structure.
