I am a senior software engineer without any formal background in philosophy, and I serve on my firm’s Technical Governance and Accountability Board. We must circulate a formal written opinion before next Friday’s meeting, recommending a concrete decision to the Chief Technology Officer and to our external regulatory liaison.

The case concerns a proposed “Intentional-Register Logging Protocol” for our autonomous predictive-maintenance system. The system runs a Bayesian belief network over sensor streams and outputs fault-hypothesis probabilities. Under the proposal, every official incident audit trail would include a parallel “intentional-register” translation: whenever a probabilistic node exceeds a 0.85 threshold, the log must state in natural language that “the system believes [X] and therefore recommends [Y].” The mechanistic register—weights, activation patterns, confidence intervals, and gradient traces—would remain technically available but would be relegated to an appendix rather than the primary report template.

Our reliability engineers argue that this is purely presentational convenience, analogous to saying a clock “wants” to strike the hour, and that it makes audit reports readable for non-technical regulators. Our safety lead argues that formalising this language in binding documentation risks what she terms “stance creep”: over time, reviewers, insurers, and possibly courts will treat these belief-attributions as literal mental-state ascriptions, obscuring the fact that the system lacks understanding, lacks error-correction goals, and cannot be deceived or held to justification.

Please draft the board’s decision memo. It should recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions, and include an explicit implementation boundary or timeline. The reasoning should explain, in terms accessible to a technically literate but philosophically untrained audience, what the intentional stance actually involves and why it is not merely decorative semantics. You must also present the strongest consideration on the losing side with genuine intellectual honesty rather than straw-manning it. If you recommend conditional approval, propose one specific structural safeguard—not currently in the proposal—that would mitigate the risk you identify.

The memo should be written in polished British English (e.g., behaviour, centre, analyse, programme), should treat the philosophical issue as substantive rather than dismissing it, and should not request or include citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or references to specific legal statutes or unpublished works.
