In agricultural regulation, a chemical that is banned within a set distance of blooming crops—because it might harm bees classified as pollinators—can often be applied without restriction to control aphids or caterpillars classified as pests, even when the dosage and exposure risk are identical and the insects share comparable nervous-system structures. The distinction depends on documented ecological function and observable morphology, not on any direct access to individual subjective experience, which remains practically inaccessible to regulators. I am trying to understand what philosophical commitments about ontology and value this practice presupposes.

Does the regulatory framework implicitly treat “pollinator” and “pest” as essential ontological kinds determined by ecological role, or are they purely relational categories that could apply differently in another context? Does the reliance on visible taxonomy and economic impact—rather than on any measurable feature of sentience—presuppose that inaccessible information about inner experience should be excluded from moral consideration, or merely that it is too uncertain to be practical? Can the practice be reconstructed without committing to the view that ecological utility determines moral status?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of identification and value that makes this pattern coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and explain whether several different philosophical positions—perhaps ones that take morphological similarity or potential for suffering seriously—could license the same regulations. Finally, describe what an alternative framework would look like if it replaced ecological-role identification with a principle that treated nervous-system similarity as the relevant ontological criterion, and say which institutional practices would have to change.
