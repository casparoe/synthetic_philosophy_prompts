I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about standing to blame and hypocrisy, and I'm getting stuck because my gut verdicts don't line up with the usual "clean hands" story I teach in my ethics course. Here are some cases where I've got pretty firm feelings, plus one I keep flip-flopping on; I want to know if there's one principle — maybe something about whether the blamer is currently subject to the same obligation in the same register, or whether they've paid some kind of standing debt — that captures the pattern, or whether I just have to drop a verdict for coherence. Please don't just map out three theories and leave me to pick; take a definite position, defend it, and tell me exactly which of my judgments it can't save.

Case 1: Maria was a serious plagiarist in grad school, went through a long public reckoning, and has been meticulously honest about attribution for fifteen years. Last month she caught a colleague copying a paragraph and went hard on him in a meeting, calling him intellectually dishonest. I think Maria has full standing — her past doesn't nullify her present judgment, and maybe her history even gives her authority.

Case 2: A senator who voted for tax cuts he personally benefits from and hides money offshore gives a fiery speech about "tax fairness" blaming ordinary citizens. No standing, obviously.

Case 3: My friend Tom never lies about serious things, but he tells constant little social lies — "I'm five minutes away" when he's leaving, "this restaurant is great" when he hates it. He recently tore into another friend for being "fake" in a professional networking context. I feel Tom lacks standing here, even though his own dishonesty is minor and in a different domain. So maybe standing requires matching not just the wrong but the register?

Case 4: A nation that committed colonial violence, paid substantial reparations, dismantled the institutions that enabled it, and has had democratic governance for generations now condemns a current authoritarian regime for suppressing dissent. I think they do have standing — the repair matters — but I'm less sure than with Maria.

Case 5: The one I keep going back and forth on. A therapist specializing in addiction recovery secretly relapsed six months ago (no clients harmed, she's in active treatment) finds out her client lied about using. She confronts him firmly. Does she have standing? Part of me says yes because she's actively working the same problem; part says no because she's currently failing at it. I genuinely can't decide.

So: is there a coherent account — maybe about normative proximity, or whether reform creates standing-credit that current failure doesn't fully erase — that gets cases 1 through 4 and tells me what to think about case 5? Or do I have to overturn one of my earlier verdicts? And apply whatever principle you land on to this further case: a parent who was emotionally absent for years, went through therapy, and is now present, but criticizes his brother for being distant with his own kids. I recieve that this might break the principle; I want you to say so if it does.
