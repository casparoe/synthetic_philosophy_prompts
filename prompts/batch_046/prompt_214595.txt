I am working through a problem for my metaphysics seminar on individuation and the identity of indiscernibles, and I have constructed an argument that begins with Max Black’s symmetric two-sphere universe but ends in a conclusion I find intolerable: that every apparently distinct physical object is actually one entity with non-simple location. I want a precise diagnosis of where the reasoning fails, because each premise looks defensible in isolation, and I would strongly prefer to preserve the bundle-theoretic framing in (1) and the application of the identity principle in (6) rather than accept the monistic result in (12).

Here is the argument, with premises numbered:

(1) On a bundle-theoretic ontology, an object is exhaustively constituted by the qualitative properties it instantiates; there is no bare particular or substratum underlying them.
(2) In Black’s symmetric universe, sphere A and sphere B share every intrinsic qualitative property—radius, mass, color, charge, internal structure, and so on.
(3) The only feature that seems to distinguish them is spatial position: A is manifested in region R1 and B in region R2, separated by a fixed distance.
(4) But spatial position relative to a manifold is not itself a qualitative property included within the bundle; it is an external relational determination of where the bundle is manifested, not what the bundle is.
(5) Therefore the qualitative bundles that constitute A and B are identical in every respect.
(6) By Leibniz’s principle of the identity of indiscernibles—restricted here to qualitative bundles—if two bundles are identical in every qualitative respect, they are numerically the same bundle.
(7) So A and B are not two spheres; they are one and the same sphere that is non-simply located in both R1 and R2.
(8) If non-simple location is metaphysically possible for a sphere, there is no principled barrier to its being possible for objects of any kind.
(9) Now consider the actual world: every electron is qualitatively identical to every other electron (same charge, mass, spin magnitude). Any differences are purely relational—positions and quantum-state correlations relative to nuclei and to other particles.
(10) Applying (4) through (7) consistently, all electrons are not many but one electron non-simply located throughout space.
(11) By parallel reasoning, every qualitative duplicate across all physical kinds collapses into a single multi-located entity.
(12) Therefore there is only one physical object in existence, instantiated everywhere its qualitative profile permits manifestation.

This strikes me as clearly false—I am not prepared to deny that Black’s universe contains two spheres, let alone that I am numerically identical with every other person—yet I cannot see which step is illicit. Is there an equivocation between a bundle-type and a bundle-token? Does (4) wrongly treat spatial location as purely external rather than part of the qualitative profile? Or is the slide from (7) to (8) an unjustified modal generalization? Please diagnose the argument step by step, identify the specific premise or inference that is faulty, explain why it looked plausible despite that fault, and price what giving it up costs for someone who wants to retain both bundle theory and some version of the identity of indiscernibles.
