For a philosophy-of-science working group I am drafting my epistemic profile on confirmation, belief dynamics, and scientific reasoning. I want an audit of whether the following commitments jointly hang together: rank any genuine tensions by severity, say what the least costly revision would be for each, identify which conflicts are only apparent and dissolve on inspection, and be explicit about whether revising one commitment undermines others.

My commitments are:

(a) Within a fixed hypothesis space, rational belief revision is governed strictly by diachronic Bayesian conditionalization on a fixed evidential partition; once evidence is incorporated into the background, it should not be re-processed as fresh support for a hypothesis added later.

(b) Old evidence—data already incorporated into background beliefs—can provide substantial, non-derivative confirmatory support for a theory formulated after that data were known.

(c) I reject the logical-learning solution to old evidence: the rational boost in credence is not merely the result of learning that the new theory entails the already-known data.

(d) In actual scientific practice, testing a new hypothesis against large, vetted archival datasets is often epistemically superior to collecting fresh data, precisely because well-curated archives reduce selection bias and increase statistical power.

(e) When the hypothesis space itself expands—when a new theoretical framework is introduced—the prior over its parameters should be assigned by objective symmetry principles and well-defined measures, not carried over subjectively from the agent’s prior belief distribution.

(f) There is no epistemically relevant distinction between directly observing a phenomenon and accessing a fully transparent, well-documented archival record of that observation.

(g) To model collective science correctly, we must distinguish the belief dynamics of an individual inquirer—who may never have held the expanded space—from the collective belief state of a research community that jointly adopts a new framework and computes its standing against shared background evidence.

(h) A theory that was formulated specifically to accommodate a known dataset should suffer an accommodation penalty, whereas a theory developed independently and then tested against that same dataset should not.

Please examine these for consistency. In particular, address whether (a) together with (c) makes (b) impossible without some further mechanism; whether (e) and (g) actually provide that mechanism or merely relocate the problem; whether (d) and (h) conflict or apply to different stages; and whether (f) undermines (g) or instead supports it. Rank the tensions by severity, specify the cheapest revision for each, and indicate which single change would clean up the most conflicts.
