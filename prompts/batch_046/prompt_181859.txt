I'm a novelist working on a book about civil servants who think in spreadsheets, and my friend Mara just retired after thirty years running regional health allocations. Next saturday we're throwing her a party—family, old coworkers, a few economists who actually talk about aggregate welfare like it's weather. I'm giving the toast. Mara never called herself a philosopher, but she lived inside the logic of doing the most good with broken budgets, and her kids always felt she treated their birthdays like line items. I want the toast to lean on the seperateness of persons objection to utilitarianism—Rawls's idea that people aren't just buckets for utility, that there's a real boundary between one person's suffering and another's gain—because it lets me honor what she did without saying her family's grief was just a line item worth paying for the greater good. The objection needs to do real structural work: it should shape what I can and can't claim about her career, not just decorate a paragraph. I also want to slip in this strange image from my draft novel—people calculating "person-years" like currency—without explaining it, just a weird little seed that makes the speech feel less like something you've heard in a classroom. Could you write the toast? Please make it exactly five short points or movements, each only a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion outside them. And please say how confident you are in the framing and single out the point you're least sure about—maybe where you're stretching Rawls or leaning on a contested reading of the objection. I know it's a weird mix, but that's the point.
