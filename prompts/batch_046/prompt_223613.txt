I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about ten minutes—at a retirement symposium honoring a sociologist who spent her career investigating whether birth cohorts constitute genuine social kinds. The audience is deliberately mixed: on one side are philosophers of science and demographers familiar with age-period-cohort identification problems and the metaphysics of grouping; on the other are family members, university administrators, and students who have never considered whether labels such as "Boomer," "Millennial," or "Gen Z" denote anything more substantial than marketing segments. I want the remarks to treat the ontology of generations as a live conceptual problem that structures the honoree's research commitments, rather than as a decorative metaphor.

Specifically, I want you to build the speech around a framing I am developing for a longer essay: generations should be understood neither as natural kinds with enduring essences nor as purely nominal conveniences, but as what might be called "interval kinds"—groupings defined not by intrinsic properties shared across the lifespan, but by a bounded temporal window of shared historical exposure that produces projectible behavioral and attitudinal patterns only within that interval, after which the cohort dissolves into ordinary age-graded variation. The speech should explain why this distinction matters for assessing claims about intergenerational difference, should honor the honoree's empirical work without reducing her subjects to demographic caricatures, and must land for the experts without losing the non-specialists. Please do not provide citations, bibliographies, or suggested readings; the argument should stand on its conceptual architecture alone. Write the remarks in a voice appropriate to a journalist addressing both scholars and strangers.
