Since I began assisting with an archive that documents mid-twentieth-century state assimilation programs, the question of how to name large-scale cultural destruction has become personally urgent. At a recent seminar, two colleagues—Marcus and Elena—disagreed sharply over whether to apply the term genocide to a well-documented policy of systematic child removal, language suppression, and forced religious conversion carried out by a government in the 1950s and 1960s. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander; please adjudicate it without signaling deference to either side.

Marcus opened by insisting that the 1948 Convention's specific-intent standard is conceptually central: genocide requires proof of intent to destroy a protected group physically or biologically, in whole or in part. He argued that assimilation policies aim at incorporation rather than elimination, so calling them genocide is legally inaccurate and morally inflationary—if the term expands to cover every coercive assimilation program, it loses its distinctive force and international law becomes unworkable.

Elena replied that this confuses Lemkin's original 1940s framework with its politically narrowed legal successor. She noted that Lemkin's formulation treated cultural genocide—the destruction of a group's language, religion, and social institutions—as an integral mode of group destruction, not a lesser harm. She added that separating physical from cultural destruction is conceptually artificial, since destroying the conditions for collective life frequently operates as either precursor or functional substitute for killing.

Marcus conceded that cultural harm is grave, but pressed for a principled boundary. He asked whether Elena's view implies that virtually every colonial education or language policy qualifies as genocide, and warned that without a clear line, attribution collapses into subjective judgment about state motives.

Elena proposed a middle criterion she called structural intent: a policy targets genocide-level harm not when it expresses explicit exterminationist rhetoric, but when it is systematically designed to eliminate the conditions necessary for a group's continued existence as a collective entity—through child removal, language bans, and severance of land ties—while leaving individual members alive. She argued that the Convention's drafters excluded this category not to refine moral analysis but to shield colonial and assimilationist states from accountability.

Marcus objected that structural intent is unfalsifiable. Bureaucracies routinely invoke benevolent aims, he said, and inferring destructive intent solely from harmful effects collapses the distinction between genocide and aggressive assimilation. Without direct evidence of intent, Elena's standard risks labeling every harmful policy genocidal.

Elena countered that specific intent is not unfalsifiable but selectively enforced: courts accept circumstantial evidence of intent in ordinary criminal law, yet demand impossible direct proof for genocide. She concluded that clinging to the narrow definition protects perpetrators rather than concepts, and that moral categorization should not be held hostage to a legal text shaped by political compromise.
