I am organizing a colloquium session on urban justice and plan to interview a philosopher who defends a position she calls “proximate democratic tenure”—the claim that residents who have lived in a neighborhood for a substantial continuous period acquire a non-transferable, quasi-proprietary interest in preserving its existing built form and social composition, giving them a legitimate democratic veto over upzoning and densification measures, even when those measures serve citywide or regional housing needs. I want the interview to move past familiar NIMBY talking points and press the view where philosophical literature on the right to the city and distributive justice actually applies pressure. Please draft eight to ten interview questions. At least one should target the boundary problem of who exactly qualifies as a tenure-holder—whether renters, recent immigrants, or displaced former residents count, and on what principled basis the cutoff is drawn. At least one should ask how this veto right can be reconciled with the claim that adequate housing is a fundamental human right that should not be held hostage to the preferences of an incumbent group. At least one should examine whether the argument from historical investment and democratic proximity collapses into a bare status-quo bias that systematically privileges wealthier homeowners over mobile renters and newcomers. For each of these three questions, include a specific follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give: for the boundary problem, anticipate her appeal to a participatory-democratic threshold and ask how that avoids arbitrariness; for the human-right conflict, anticipate her distinction between negative liberties and positive provision and press whether a veto that blocks supply is genuinely negative; and for the status-quo objection, anticipate her claim that long residence creates legitimate expectations and ask whether those expectations survive when they depend on exclusionary zoning that itself was historically unjust.
