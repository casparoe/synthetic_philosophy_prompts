I am an architectural conservator with a background in structural engineering and heritage-law policy, but no formal training in philosophy. Restate Samuel Scheffler's argument—sometimes called the afterlife conjecture—that human extinction would be bad for present people in ways that go well beyond the deaths it involves, because many of our projects and values depend for their meaning on the continued existence of future generations who will inherit, interpret, or complete them. Do this using only the vocabulary of my field: load-bearing dependencies, deferred maintenance cycles, intergenerational stewardship, the distinction between fabric and monument, successive custodianship obligations, and the preservation of function versus form. For each element of his claim, translate precisely: the idea that present goods derive value from an anticipated future reception; the asymmetry he draws between individual death (which leaves the collective and its values intact) and collective extinction (which removes the audience or inheritor for all prior effort); and his suggestion that some values are essentially diachronic, requiring transmission across time rather than static preservation. Then provide the residue report: which parts map cleanly onto conservation practice, which require distortion—for instance, treating collective "meaning" as if it were structural integrity or material authenticity—and which have no counterpart whatsoever in preservation vocabulary. Finally, assess whether any untranslatable residue points to a genuine limitation in heritage-thinking, such as an inability to represent value that lacks a material substrate, or whether it reveals that Scheffler's argument relies on notions of diachronic collective meaning that are less precisely defined than they initially appear.
