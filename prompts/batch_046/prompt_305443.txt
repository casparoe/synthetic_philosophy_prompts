I recently began serving on a committee that approves research funding in sequential rounds with a declining remaining budget, a structure that struck me as structurally analogous to the centipede game. At dinner afterward, two colleagues—both working in philosophy of economics—fell into a sharp disagreement about what the backward induction paradox actually shows regarding rational cooperation in finite interactions. (1) The first argued that the paradox dissolves once we reject logical omniscience: real agents possess only finite reasoning depth, so the inductive chain never actually initiates, and cooperation is therefore perfectly compatible with rationality. (2) The second replied that this misdiagnoses the problem—common knowledge of rationality is a shared normative standard, not a mental computation requiring infinite steps—so abandoning logical omniscience does not rescue cooperation unless we also abandon the idea that the agents are rational in any robust sense. (3) The first then countered that if we genuinely allow bounded rationality, consistency demands we also allow bounded self-interest; people continue cooperating in sequential negotiations not because they miscalculate, but because their preferences are locally other-regarding, which is exactly what we should expect from descriptively accurate agents. (4) The second responded that this conflates descriptive psychology with normative justification: explaining why people deviate from backward induction does not answer whether they are rational to do so, and without that normative bridge the explanation remains inert. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and indicate what—if anything—both parties are missing about the relationship between epistemic bounds and strategic norms in sequential interaction.
