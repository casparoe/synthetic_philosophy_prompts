Analyze the April 1970 Apollo 13 crisis—the service-module oxygen-tank explosion, the lunar module used as a lifeboat, and the improvised CO2 scrubber—through the lens of collective intentionality and joint-action theory. Distinguish Michael Bratman’s account of shared cooperative activity, with its conditions of mutual responsiveness, commitment to the joint activity, and commitment to mutual support, from Margaret Gilbert’s plural-subject model centered on joint commitment. Map the actual coordination onto these frameworks: identify the agents (the three astronauts versus the mission-control team), determine whether the hierarchical command structure permits genuine joint commitment or only institutionally assigned role-performance, and assess whether the emergency improvisation meets Bratman’s conditions despite the absence of prior shared planning. Then specify where the theory breaks down or remains silent—particularly whether frameworks built on interpersonal we-intentions can accommodate coordination driven mainly by technical artifacts, written protocols, and distributed cognition across a large organization rather than by face-to-face mutual responsiveness. Finish with a brief, justified conclusion about whether Apollo 13 is best understood as a case of philosophical joint action or as an aggregation of individually executed, role-bound responses integrated by institutional infrastructure.
