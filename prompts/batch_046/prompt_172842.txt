I’m running a small philosophy workshop next tuesday — about 10 people, mostly grad students plus a couple undergrads, 90 minutes. We’ve been talking about libertarian paternalism and nudging in the usual choice-architecture sense, but I want them to feel something wierd: what happens when the nudge isn’t about picking option A vs B, but about being steered to form a belief — like interpreting a risk statistic in one direction rather than another. I’m calling this epistemic framing, and the tension I want them to hit is that if you make the frame transparent, the subject can’t just “opt out” the way they can with a default option, because belief isn’t voluntary like action.

Design a structured in-class activity (not a lecture) where they actually experience this asymmetry. Split them into “framers” and “interpreters” with hidden rules, or use a staged deliberation with constrained information access; include small stakes (points or social pressure, not money) arranged so they run into the problem themselves before anyone names it. Then include a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the distinction between action-nudges and belief-nudges, tell me what will predicatbly go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity can’t reproduce — probably the long-term habituation or the involuntariness of belief formation. Keep it runnable with just paper and pens, and make sure the idea shows up in what happens, not just in what I say afterwards.
