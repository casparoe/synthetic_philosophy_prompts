Design a runnable 75-minute seminar activity for approximately eighteen philosophy undergraduates, working in one classroom with movable tables, that makes participants feel the force of a verbal dispute in metaontology: the experience of arguing passionately about what exists, only to discover that the disagreement tracks hidden differences in what each party counts as an “object” or “entity” rather than competing descriptions of a shared world. The scenario should mix six ambiguous cases—such as a reconstructed deleted file whose bits are scattered but recoverable, a contractual obligation that binds without physical form, a shadow cast by an object that has since been destroyed, a symphony performed once and never recorded, a corporate board decision, and a hole in a wall—presented as neutral descriptions without ontological labels. Divide participants into six triads; give each triad a secret “cataloging protocol” that defines “inventory item” with different necessary conditions (for example, spatiotemporal continuity and causal efficacy; appearance in the best predictive model regardless of causal status; or being a possible subject of a true ordinary-language statement) but without philosophical terminology. In Phase One (fifteen minutes), each triad applies its hidden protocol to produce an inventory and a brief justification. In Phase Two (twenty-five minutes), pair triads with mismatched protocols and instruct them to negotiate a single joint inventory for an “archive accreditation” report; award ten consensus points if they agree, plus five points for lists that match an undisclosed “expert standard” (in fact there is none, but the competitive framing creates stakes). The winning group earns a small seminar privilege, such as choosing the closing discussion question for the week, so the payoff is social and symbolic rather than monetary. The rules and pairing should be arranged so that deadlock emerges organically—participants should defend incompatible counts with genuine conviction—before any facilitator reveals the hidden protocols. After negotiation collapses or stalls, reveal the protocols, have each triad read its criteria aloud, and ask the room to map the argument onto the idea that the dispute was about classificatory rules embedded in language rather than about reality itself.

The debrief (fifteen minutes) should map each moment precisely: Phase One to private conceptual scheme; Phase Two’s deadlock to the appearance of a substantive metaphysical disagreement; the revelation to the verbal-dispute diagnosis; and any partial reconciliation attempts to the question of whether fixing the language removes all disagreement or leaves a further choice of framework. Predict what will go wrong in the first run: some groups will guess there is a hidden rule and perform cynically rather than arguing sincerely; others will reach false consensus by accident, masking divergence until justification is demanded; and emotional investment in “being right” may resist the debrief’s framing. Handle this by requiring written justifications before negotiation begins and by treating false consensus as data—ask those groups to explain their reasoning aloud, which usually exposes the hidden divergence. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: it stages disagreement as purely definitional, but it cannot by itself establish whether there remains a non-verbal question about which framework to adopt, or whether ontological commitment is ever more than adherence to a linguistic rule. That deeper layer—the distinction between questions internal to a framework and questions about framework choice, and whether the latter are substantive or pragmatic—must be supplied directly by the facilitator after the activity ends. Do not include bibliographies, citation requests, or reading lists in the design; keep all references to theories implicit in the protocol descriptions rather than named. Write the prompt in a formal, polished register.
