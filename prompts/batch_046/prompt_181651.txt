I am a doctoral student in the history of political thought, and next week I must lead a single seventy-five-minute seminar for second-year undergraduates reading both philosophy and theatre studies. They have encountered excerpts from the Social Contract, but they have never read Rousseau’s polemic against theatrical spectacle, and I want them to understand the structural argument rather than the biographical anecdote: that staged drama produces a specific form of moral passivity by separating emotional consumption from civic action, and that his preference for ancient public festivals is meant as an architectural comparison of social relations, not as nostalgic antiquarianism.

Please plan the explanatory arc rather than delivering the lecture itself. First, establish the dependency order: which concept must be secure before the theatre critique becomes intelligible—his distinction between amour-propre and amour de soi, or his account of how civic virtue is formed through direct participation? Next, identify the one carrying example that can bear the full weight of the argument, and explain why using modern cinema or immersive performance art as the primary vehicle would collapse the distinction Rousseau is trying to draw. Then map the misconceptions in the order the students are likely to form them: initially that he is merely a puritan hostile to art; then that he regards all emotional response as politically dangerous; and finally that he wishes to replace theatre with state-directed propaganda. Specify exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be anticipated and defused, so that the correction does not feel like an apology but like a clarification of the structural point. Design one precise question I can pose at the end that will reveal whether they have grasped the spectator’s relation to representation—rather than merely memorising his opposition to the Geneva stage. Finally, state explicitly what should be left out at this level—whether his correspondence with d’Alembert, the specific Genevan constitutional context, or the textual history of the Letter—and say so plainly, so that I do not overburden the session.
