I'm delivering the opening address tomorrow at the dedication of the Chen Adaptive Systems Laboratory, named for my father, who died three months ago after forty years as an industrial control engineer. The audience is split: half are robotics researchers fluent in feedback architecture and deterministic design; half are family members and university administrators with no technical background. Last week, sorting through his office, I found a 1973 journal article he had annotated heavily — the classic observation of the sphex wasp's burrow-checking ritual — with a handwritten margin note I have not been able to stop thinking about: "If the loop is all there is, is the wasp less free than me, or am I less free than the wasp?" I want the address to take sphexishness — mechanical rigidity versus genuine flexibility — as its structural backbone, not as ornament. It must honor a man who believed deterministic systems could be elegant without being dead, and who never had patience for talk of an immaterial will. At the same time, it must not reduce him to a deterministic mechanism in front of his grandchildren. Build the speech around one precise philosophical tension: can a being — wasp, machine, or human — be genuinely flexible if every deviation from routine remains causally determined by prior conditions? Let that tension constrain what I can claim about his legacy, shape the moment where I thank the family for letting us name the lab for him, and give the engineers in the room a live argument for the reception without leaving the non-philosophers behind. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to texts beyond common knowledge; treat the sphex study as familiar to the specialists. I want the speech to sound like it was written by someone who has only just realized that the philosophy he lived by is also the philosophy through which he is now being grieved.
