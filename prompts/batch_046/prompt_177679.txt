I've been thinking about how goverments do these big formal apologies for old wrongs — residential schools, colonial violence, that kind of thing — and they stage it like a real moral event, but then they don't change the policies that still hurt the same people. Sometimes they say "previous adminstrations did this, not us," yet they still speak as if the state itself is the same thing that owes the sorry. What theory of collective resposibility does that practice actually need? Does it presuppose that a country is like a long-lived person that can carry guilt across generations, or is it closer to a corporation declaring moral bankruptcy to wipe its slate clean? And if the apology is supposed to settle something, why is it usually separated from reparations — is the ritual secretly treating the words themselves as a kind of payment? I want to know whether this is consistent with one view, or if it's smuggling in two incompatible ideas about whether dead people can pass on blame and whether saying sorry cancels the debt.
