I'm constructing a taxonomy for a philosophy-of-history workshop that asks: in evaluating whether Nazi Germany—understood specifically as a one-party racial state with aggressive expansionist foreign policy—was counterfactually dependent on Adolf Hitler, which commitments actually separate the competing explanations?

Please design a decision tree with exactly five branching nodes. Each node should present a concrete claim or counterfactual scenario, not a historiographical label, that a respondent could agree or disagree with without knowing school names. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of named positions. The first node should address structural sufficiency: whether the post-Versailles institutional crisis, economic collapse of 1929–33, and existing authoritarian tendencies made a radical-right seizure of power likely regardless of leadership. From there branch by (i) causal substitution—whether Hitler's specific organizational and rhetorical capacities were substitutable by another NSDAP figure or conservative-nationalist coalition leader; (ii) modal evaluation—whether the counterfactual absence of Hitler is assessed by nearest-possible-world similarity (small perturbations do not cascade into different macro-outcomes) or by structural-tendency reasoning (underlying conditions dominate over individual agency); (iii) outcome differentiation—whether Hitler's absence changes only the leader's identity, only the timing of consolidation, or also the ideological content and genocidal scope of the regime; and (iv) explanatory dominance—whether institutional path dependence (Weimar constitutional weaknesses, Article 48, Prussian police structures) or conjunctural crisis (the specific sequence of 1930–33 elections, back-room deals, and the Reichstag fire) is the dominant factor.

The tree should yield six to eight leaves. Each leaf must carry a position name (for example, Strong Great Man, Substitution-Robust Structuralism, Conjunctural Contingency, Institutional Path Dependence, Overdetermined Convergence, Modal Fragility, or Unnamed) and exactly one sentence stating its most awkward counterfactual commitment—the specific claim about 1930s Germany it must defend that sounds least plausible on its face.

Then, separately, identify any combinations of answers that remain unoccupied in the logical space you have mapped. For each empty cell, diagnose briefly whether the gap arises from incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect (for instance, a view that denies both structural conditions and Hitler's uniqueness, or one that affirms structural sufficiency but also claims the outcome was modally fragile in every nearby world).

Finally, flag any two positions that your five questions cannot cleanly separate, explaining precisely which nodes they share answers on and what additional diagnostic case—phrased as a concrete counterfactual claim—would be needed to split them.

Constraints: do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Do not invoke obscure historiographical debates by proper name; keep references to widely known entities—Hitler, Weimar, Versailles, NSDAP, 1929–33—only. The audience is graduate students in philosophy or history with no specialist background in counterfactual logic but with basic competence in causal reasoning. Write in a formal, polished register.
