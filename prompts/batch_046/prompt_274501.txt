I recently joined my school district’s equity committee, and last week we received a consultant’s report that has made the statistical concept of heritability suddenly and uncomfortably concrete for me. The report observes that in our district—where curriculum, testing schedules, and resource allocation are highly standardized—the heritability coefficient for standardized achievement scores is estimated near 0.85. From this figure, the consultant concludes that genetic differences are the dominant cause of the remaining achievement gaps, and that further environmental interventions will therefore face sharply diminishing returns. Before I cast my vote on whether to adopt this framing in our upcoming policy review, I need to understand whether the inference actually holds.

Write a short dialogue between a quantitative behavioral geneticist who defends the consultant’s reasoning and a philosopher of science who rejects it. Their disagreement should turn specifically on whether a heritability coefficient calculated within a deliberately homogenized environment can license causal conclusions about individual students, or whether the suppression of environmental variance renders the coefficient a statistical artifact of institutional design rather than a discovery about fixed human potential. Both speakers should be fully competent, should respond directly to each other’s actual objections rather than talk past them, and should avoid reducing the dispute to a generic nature-versus-nurture cliché. The dialogue should begin immediately with the first speaker’s opening line, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of the question.
