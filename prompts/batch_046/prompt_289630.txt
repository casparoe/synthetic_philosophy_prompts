I am working on a long-form essay that takes Burkean conservatism seriously as a framework for institutional reform rather than mere preservation. In particular, I keep returning to Chesterton's fence—the principle that one ought not remove a fence until one understands why it was erected—but I find it used more as a rhetorical reflex than as a developed philosophical position. I want to map the genuinely open conceptual problems in this area, especially for someone without empirical social-science resources who nonetheless wants the analysis to bear on real decisions about dismantling legacy regulatory structures or redesigning democratic procedures.

Specifically, I see several unresolved lines of work. First, is the fence fundamentally an epistemic claim about hidden functional complexity, a risk-averse decision rule under uncertainty, or a normative thesis about the burden of proof in institutional change? Second, does it apply symmetrically—once we understand a fence's purpose and reject it, is there any corresponding obligation to preserve or rebuild it, or does understanding alone license removal? Third, how should the principle handle what I will call institutional amnesia, where a rule persists not because anyone grasps its original purpose but because authority over it has diffused, which seems to invert the fence's logic? Fourth, which of these conceptual resolutions actually change our evaluation of modern reform cases, as opposed to being philosophical tidying-up with little practical bite?

Given that I have roughly one semester to develop one of these into a rigorous chapter, which should I prioritize? Please rank the open questions—or substitute others you think are more important—by defending an ordering based on tractability for a philosopher working without empirical resources, how many downstream debates depend on resolving this one first, and whether settling it would genuinely alter how we think about institutional reform or is largely overrated. I am especially interested in whether clarifying the fence's logical structure unlocks broader answers within conservative political philosophy, or whether that particular problem is a dead end that looks deep but changes little downstream.
