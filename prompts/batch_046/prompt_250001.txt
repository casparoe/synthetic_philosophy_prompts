I am writing a dissertation on the ethics of public memory, and I have been asked to advise a municipal memorial commission that must decide, twice a year and in a single meeting, whether to preserve, alter, or decommission contested commemorative installations—statues, named buildings, or memorial sites—where the honoree’s legacy includes both significant public achievements and documented serious harms, and where stakeholders disagree sharply about whether the site functions as historical record or as living veneration.

Please construct a reusable decision procedure the commissioners can actually run, translating the conceptual tension between Margalit’s claim that societies bear a duty to remember wrongs and victims and Rieff’s argument that forced collective remembrance can perpetuate harm and that some memories ought to be permitted to fade. To make the distinction operational, treat every site as serving either preservative remembrance (maintaining an accurate, accessible historical record) or honorific remembrance (sustaining public veneration and moral exemplarity), or both simultaneously. The deliverable should be a step-by-step flow in plain language: the ordered questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the clear stopping points (preserve with contextualization; preserve as non-honorific archive only; decommission), and the explicit hand-off points where the issue is irreducibly one of values and must go to a vote rather than being settled by the procedure.

The procedure must be able to handle three hard cases without breaking: (i) a site whose historical record is already well-archived elsewhere, so removal does not erase knowledge; (ii) a site whose retention in current form continues to honor a perpetrator, but whose removal would eliminate the only local material trace of an event; and (iii) a proposal to replace an honorific monument with a counter-memorial that records the harm without veneration—an outcome that commissioners favoring simple retention will find unacceptable. Apply the procedure to all three, showing where it delivers a determinate recommendation and where it forces a judgment call that cannot be proceduralized away. In particular, flag whether the procedure can resolve, without prior moral commitment, the difference between a wrong done to a specific identifiable group and a diffuse public harm.

Keep the answer practical: a checklist of tests, a decision tree rendered in words, and explicit notation of misfire risks and judgment-call zones, rather than an essay on the philosophy of memory. The commissioners are not philosophers; the procedure should work for people who must sign a defensible decision and face the same kind of case again in six months.
