I have recently begun reconstructing the historical presence of a community that was systematically excluded from official census and property registries during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Working through the remaining fragments has made the politics of archival silence personally urgent: I am trying to understand not only what is missing from the repositories I consult, but how the very conditions of being recorded or not recorded constitute a form of power that operates before any document reaches an archive.

In this connection, I have been struggling with a passage from Hilary Jenkinson’s early twentieth-century manual on archive administration. There he argues that the archivist’s primary duty is to preserve the organic integrity of a record group, refusing to destroy or suppress any document because of its content, since the future historian depends on the unmediated completeness of the fonds. At the same time, he treats the generation of records by state agencies as a naturally occurring administrative process, never examining the prior decision about which subjects are granted the bureaucratic form that produces an archive in the first place.

What I find difficult is whether Jenkinson’s principle of organic integrity implicitly legitimizes the silences that precede the archive. If the record group is conceived as a naturally grown body, then the absence of records for people who never entered the bureaucratic system appears not as an exclusion produced by power but as a natural absence of historical significance. Does his framework contain any internal resources for recognizing that the integrity of a state fonds is itself shaped by prior political decisions about who counts as a recordable subject? Or does the concept of organic growth necessarily obscure what I have been calling the pre-archival void—the negative space created when power withholds the bureaucratic form itself?

Please help me understand this passage with careful attention to where Jenkinson’s argument is silent rather than forcing it to speak. In particular, I ask that you say what would change your mind about this interpretation—what specific feature of his argument, or what counter-example from archival practice, would lead you to conclude that his view actually accounts for the politics of record-creation rather than masking it?
