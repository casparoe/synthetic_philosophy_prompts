My friend and I just spent the better part of an evening arguing about Derek Parfit's hitchhiker and whether rational irrationality describes anything real. She insisted that the paradox only arises if you treat a promise as a prediction about future behavior rather than as a present act of self-binding; I countered that this merely relocates the problem, because if binding is instrumentally rational only when it serves your future interests, then defection of the Gollum kind remains rational and the concept of commitment collapses into long-term prudence. We left it unresolved, and I want to force the framework to prove its worth on a case neither of us can dismiss as a toy example.

Read J.R.R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings—specifically Gollum's oath to guide Frodo into Mordor and his betrayal at Shelob's lair—through the lens of Parfit's hitchhiker and the subsequent literature on rational irrationality. Treat Gollum not merely as a corrupted creature but as an agent facing the classic two-stage choice: he secures present benefit (release, proximity to the Ring) by making a credible commitment, then confronts the choice of honoring it when the cost exceeds the remaining benefit. At the same time, read Frodo's decision to spare Gollum at the Black Gate and to continue trusting him as a form of precommitment: Frodo binds himself to a course of action that makes the promise credible, even though he suspects betrayal.

I want you to take a definite position, not a balanced survey. Does Tolkien's narrative ultimately vindicate Parfit's diagnosis—that rational stage-by-stage reasoning produces a collectively ruinous outcome that only a non-instrumental commitment could prevent—or does the novel undermine the framework by showing that Gollum's defection is not rational self-interest but the symptom of a fractured personal identity in which no stable agent persists across the two stages? Focus especially on whether the Smeagol/Gollum split functions as an internal enforcement mechanism that fails, and on whether Frodo's mercy is best understood as a rational precommitment strategy or as a moral stance that the hitchhiker model cannot accommodate without distortion.

Give me your committed answer: if you think the analogy holds, specify exactly what it reveals about Gollum's destruction that moral philosophy alone misses, and say whether Frodo's precommitment succeeds because it changes the payoff structure or because it operates outside instrumental reasoning altogether. If you think the analogy breaks, identify the precise feature of Tolkien's account of will, corruption, or identity that fractures it, and argue whether Parfit's analysis should absorb that feature or reject it as irrelevant to rational choice. End by saying what this reading adds to the philosophical debate about rational irrationality that argument alone does not.
