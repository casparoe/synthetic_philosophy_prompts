I'm trying to figure out what I actually think counts as democratic backsliding, going by my gut on cases. Here's where I land.

Hungary under Orbán: they rewrote the constitution, gerrymandered districts, and captured media, but they still hold elections with opposition parties running. I say that's backsliding, but it's the "structural" kind where the game is rigged without cancelling the match.

Poland's Law and Justice party: they packed the constitutional tribunal and disciplined judges, but elections stayed competitive and they eventually lost in 2023. I still call it backsliding, though it feels different from Hungary—more like "institutional capture" than "electoral manipulation."

Turkey after 2016: mass purges, media shutdowns, and later constitutional changes concentrating power. Elections happen but aren't fair. Definately backsliding, maybe the clearest case.

The US in 2020-2021: state legislatures proposed bills to let themselves overturn election results, and some passed "election integrity" laws that made voting harder for specific groups. But the overturn bills mostly failed, and courts upheld some of the voting laws. I think the failed attempts don't count as backsliding actually happening—more like "backsliding pressure"—while the successful voting restrictions feel like normal partisan policy, not democratic erosion, unless there's a clear intent to prevent turnover.

Then there's this hypothetical I keep coming back to: a democracy where the ruling party doesn't touch election rules at all, but uses regulatory power to slowly squeeze opposition funding and appoint loyalists to every oversight board. No dramatic moment, just death by a thousand cuts. I think that counts too, maybe even more insidiously.

And one more: a country tightens voter-ID rules through normal legislative debate, courts approve it, and it disproportionately affects rural minorities but there's no evidence of intent to suppress opposition—just bureaucratic incompetence and neglect. I don't think that's backsliding.

So my verdicts are all over the place: some structural changes count, some don't; intent seems to matter but I don't want to make it all about hidden motives; and I care a lot about whether peaceful turnover is still genuinely possible.

Is there one principle behind these—maybe something like "the removal of competitive uncertainty"—that captures all of them? Or do I need two principles, one for institutional capture and one for electoral manipulation? And what does my framework say about the case I'm unsure about: a popular leader who keeps winning free elections but gradually replaces career civil servents with party loyalists in every agency, including election adminstration? Is that backsliding if the elections stay technically free?

Also, is "democratic backsliding" even a useful concept here, or is it just a partisan label for "changes I dislike"? I keep going back and forth.

Please tell me what principle, if any, gets close to my gut, which verdict I'd have to drop to keep it simple, and what it predicts for that election-adminstration case.
