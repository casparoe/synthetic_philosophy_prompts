I have been studying Raphael Lemkin’s original concept of genocide and the political disputes over which atrocities receive that legal-moral label, and I want to move past the usual examples. To that end, I am examining a less-discussed edge case that I will call “mnemonic erasure”: the systematic destruction of a group’s archives, oral-history infrastructures, and commemorative practices with the intent of rendering that group historically unrememberable, even when direct physical killing is minimal or absent. Before I can argue whether mnemonic erasure belongs inside, outside, or adjacent to the framework of genocide, I need a rigorous specification of what any adequate taxonomy for naming atrocities must actually deliver.

Please provide the complete list of desiderata—meaning the phenomena it must explain, the distinctions it must preserve, the specific cases it must classify correctly, and the legal, historical, and conceptual constraints it must respect—with the reasoning or motivating conflict behind each requirement. In particular, include the following seven: (1) a requirement that the framework distinguish physical destruction of a group from cultural or epistemic destruction without collapsing the two into a single axis; (2) a requirement that it accommodate Lemkin’s multi-dimensional conception of group destruction (encompassing political, social, economic, cultural, biological, religious, moral, and physical dimensions) rather than reducing the category to biological killing alone; (3) a requirement that it account for politicized naming, in which states or movements apply or withhold the atrocity label for strategic rather than descriptive purposes; (4) a requirement that it remain consistent with the criteria of the 1948 international convention on genocide while still allowing conceptual space for proposed extensions such as mnemonic erasure; (5) a requirement that it separate intent from outcome in a way that captures both deliberately orchestrated campaigns and structurally emergent patterns of group destruction; (6) a requirement that it preserve the distinction between possessing moral-legal status as an atrocity and the comparative severity or priority of international response that status triggers; and (7) a requirement that it be applicable in real time without depending on access to classified state archives or on historiography that will only be available decades later. For each, explain the case or tension that makes it indispensable.

Identify any pair that appears jointly unsatisfiable, paying special attention to the tension between (4) and any framework that incorporates mnemonic erasure. Finally, state which single desideratum, if enforced strictly, would exclude the greatest number of existing popular theories or common political usages of atrocity language. A structured table is acceptable for the tension-analysis and exclusion parts, but the reasoning for each desideratum and its status should remain in prose.
