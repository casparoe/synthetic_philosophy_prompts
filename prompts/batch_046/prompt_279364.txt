I'm trying to understand a particular tension I've noticed in discussions of Buddhist anatta and personal identity, and I'm wondering whether philosophers have given it a specific name or treated it as a distinct problem.

The situation looks like this. Someone accepts the standard Buddhist analysis: there is no permanent, unitary self underlying experience; what we call "I" is just a convenient label for five impermanent processes—body, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—constantly arising and passing away, and clinging to that label is said to be the root of suffering. So far, this sounds like a familiar reductionist view, comparable to some Western accounts that explain personal identity through psychological connections rather than a fixed substance. But then a different kind of worry appears. Even after dissolving the self into these impermanent processes, there remains something like a first-person orientation—an implicit "from-here" quality—that doesn't seem to belong to any particular process. It's not a Cartesian thinking substance; it's not an enduring soul; and it's not just another mental state like a feeling or a perception. Yet without some account of it, the denial of self seems to happen "nowhere"—there's no standpoint from which the dissolution is observed, and the processes themselves appear to be observed without an observer. Some Buddhist texts seem to address this by saying consciousness is aware of itself, but classical Buddhist philosophers reject even that, insisting consciousness is just another dependently arisen factor with no owner. Meanwhile, modern interpreters sometimes try to rescue a "thin" form of subjectivity compatible with anatta, but they risk reintroducing exactly the kind of ego-illusion the tradition meant to eliminate.

I've seen this pattern come up in a few contexts: in classroom debates about whether anatta is compatible with phenomenological accounts of embodied experience; in comparisons between Buddhist psychology and contemporary philosophy of mind; and in discussions of whether meditation practices that investigate "who is experiencing this?" are uncovering a hidden self or just dismantling a grammatical habit. In each case, the tension isn't quite the familiar Western problem about whether someone survives as the same person over time, because the issue isn't about survival across time—it's about whether first-person presence can be fully explained away without leaving an explanatory gap, and whether filling that gap necessarily violates anatta.

Does this specific configuration have an established name—perhaps in Buddhist philosophy, in comparative philosophy of religion, or in the philosophy of mind? How exactly does it relate to debates about reflexive awareness, or to distinctions between a minimal sense of being a subject and a full narrative identity? Or is it a distinct failure mode that arises specifically when Western frameworks for personal identity are mapped onto Buddhist categories of mind? I'd like to know exactly what this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie—particularly whether it can be resolved within orthodox Buddhist metaphysics or whether it forces a choice between accepting some form of irreducible first-person standpoint and abandoning the coherence of anatta altogether. Please answer in American English.
