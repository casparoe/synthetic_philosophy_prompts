For decades, responses to Fitch's paradox of knowability have recycled through the same three maneuvers: restrict the principle that every truth is knowable to domains free of epistemic self-reference; dissolve the contradiction by distinguishing knowing a conjunction from knowing each conjunct separately; or reformulate the modal logic so that the derivation never gets off the ground. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not a failure of ingenuity but a symptom of a deeper misalignment: one camp treats the paradox as exposing a failure of closure conditions for knowledge, another treats it as revealing an ambiguity in how "possibly known" interacts with factivity, and a third treats it as a normative boundary on what should count as an acceptable unknown truth. Is this disagreement verbal, or is there a substantive divergence about the formal properties an epistemic operator must satisfy? Do the sides differ about empirical constraints on human cognitive access, about which formal results should settle the matter, or about whether they are even addressing the same question—metaphysical possibility versus epistemic accessibility—under one name? I want a diagnosis supported by what the restrictionists, the dissolutionists, and the modal reformulators actually say, together with an honest assessment of whether a precise conceptual distinction, a new formal theorem, or a reframing of the modal framework could break the impasse, or whether the paradox functions more like a Rorschach test that makes progress impossible.
