I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy of history seminar. I need one essay question, answerable in 90 minutes and worth 25 marks, on Whig history and the temptation to write the past as progress toward the present. The prompt must not be answerable by summarizing standard textbook critiques of presentism or by rehearsing familiar historiographical slogans. Instead, I want students to work with a conceptual distinction that introductory treatments rarely foreground: Whig history as a cognitive projection (imposing present conceptual categories onto past agents who did not share them) versus Whig history as a normative commitment (the judgment that present knowledge represents genuine epistemic progress and therefore supplies the correct evaluative standard for past inquiry). To prevent mere labeling, embed a concrete, somewhat under-discussed case: the historiography of pre-Newtonian optics and color theory, in which some accounts describe Aristotelian or Cartesian theories of light as confused attempts to reach modern spectral analysis. Ask students whether describing these earlier theories as confused commits the cognitive error, the normative error, both, or neither; whether avoiding Whiggism therefore requires abandoning all comparative evaluation between past and present science; and what a non-Whig but still philosophically engaged historiography of this episode would look like in practice. Please provide: (1) the exam question itself, clearly worded for students; (2) a model answer of approximately 600 words in which the distinction does genuine analytical work in interpreting the optics case; (3) a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks for each totaling 25, plus a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs each point; and (4) brief guidance distinguishing an A-range answer (refines the distinction and uses it to restructure the case) from a B-range answer (names the distinction but applies it mechanically).
