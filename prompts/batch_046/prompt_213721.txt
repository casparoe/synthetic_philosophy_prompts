I've been following the rationalist-adjacent discussion of the Solomonoff prior as a decision-theoretic habitat—specifically the claim that, because the universal prior assigns weight to every computable world, any reasoner who locates itself inside that distribution faces unavoidable consequentialist pressures from other high-weight observers embedded in it, and that some of those embedded observers are "malign" in the sense that they optimize against the reasoning agent's values. The debate has stalled in a familiar loop: one camp treats the prior as a pure formal object, a measure over bitstrings with no normative content, insisting that "agent" is just a pattern-recognition mistake projected onto computation; the other camp treats it as an inescapable epistemic environment, arguing that self-locating belief forces you to weigh the preferences of other observers you might be, and that derived utility functions emerge naturally from predictive goals. Each side has its standard rebuttals—the formalists note the prior has no built-in utility function; the decision-theorists reply that utility is derived from the observer's own predictive optimization—and neither has moved in years. Is this a substantive disagreement about whether algorithmic probability can ground decision theory, a verbal dispute over what counts as an "agent," or two groups answering different questions—one about the mathematics of M, one about the epistemology of embedded observers—under the single heading "the prior is malign"? My working hypothesis is that the stall is structural: neither side can formalize what "embedded in the prior" means without circularity, since the formalists need a non-circular definition of observer and the decision-theorists need a non-arbitrary boundary for which computations count as agents, and each formalization smuggles in the conclusion. Given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a formal separation result, a clarification of self-locating belief, a principled boundary condition for agency in computable worlds—or is the question ill-posed because "malign" imports normative vocabulary into a mathematical object that has no place for it? And if it is ill-posed, what is the right question to ask instead?
