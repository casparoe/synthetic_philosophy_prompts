Design a 75-minute structured deliberation for an advanced undergraduate philosophy seminar of about twelve students, intended to make them feel the nonidentity problem before it is named. The setting is a simulated intergenerational climate council: participants must vote between a high-carbon development path that benefits present members but produces severe climate harms 150 years hence, and a low-carbon austerity path that burdens present members but avoids those harms. To make the logical tension visceral, build in a role-replacement mechanism: after each binding vote, future-representative seats are filled by drawing role cards from path-specific pools, so that the individuals who occupy those seats under Path A (with one set of values, life-plans, and grievances) are necessarily different from those who occupy the same physical seats under Path B. The rules should specify that representatives may register complaints about their conditions, but that a complaint is only valid if the complainant can coherently prefer non-existence to existence in that condition; the payoff structure should use course-credit points and in-class social standing rather than money, arranged so that present members gain status for choosing the high-carbon path while future representatives gain or lose standing based on whether their path-specific identity can sustain a grievance. Include the full voting and replacement rules, the role-card design, the debrief sequence that maps specific moments—especially the point at which a harmed representative attempts to protest—onto the logical parts of the problem (the comparison of existence and non-existence, the failure of the harm principle, the non-complaint), and state frankly which feature of the idea—most likely the metaphysical comparison of a particular life with never having lived at all—the simulation cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally. Also specify what will predictably fail the first time it is run (for instance, students may treat the seat as holding a continuous identity across paths, or they may collapse the dilemma into straightforward utilitarian aggregation) and how to correct it in the moment. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the prompt should stand entirely on its internal logic and the facilitator's explanation.
