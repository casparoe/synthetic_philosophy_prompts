In discussions of practical reason, a colleague has consistently taken the following positions. First, that a physician ought to disclose a terminal prognosis even when concealment would prolong life and reduce suffering, not because disclosure maximizes welfare but because role-structured obligations are not subject to act-by-act consequentialist override. Second, that a legislature ought to bind itself to rigid procedural rules—prohibiting torture in all instances—even when breaking the rule in a particular case would prevent catastrophe, on the grounds that the norm governs the institution's decision architecture rather than each discrete choice. Third, that an individual ought to cultivate dispositions of honesty and loyalty that she does not suspend when they happen to produce worse outcomes in a given case, since the standard of evaluation is her character over time rather than the state of affairs she produces. Fourth, that an agent should not delegate a morally significant choice to an algorithm that computes the optimal outcome, even if the algorithm is more reliable than her own judgment, because the choice must issue from her own rational agency to count as genuinely hers. Fifth, that an agent should deliberate from her actual, embodied, and affectively situated standpoint—improved only by coherence and fuller information, not by transcendence toward a view-from-nowhere.

These verdicts are jointly consistent with at least three underlying frameworks. (A) Procedural indirect consequentialism: the correct action is the one that issues from the decision procedure that, if generally adopted, produces the best results, and direct evaluation is forbidden. (B) Idealized deliberative endorsement: the correct action is what the agent would choose after a process of full information, coherence, and affective calibration—an "idealized deliberation"—and she should approximate this through her own reflection rather than outsourcing it. (C) Constitutive non-idealism: the norms governing an agent depend essentially on her bounded cognitive and affective constitution, so that the correct action for her is often not what an unbounded or differently constituted agent ought to do.

Consider now a new case. A climate-policy advisor has access to a system that can, for any proposed policy, instantly report the recommendation that the advisor's own fully informed, rational, and affectively integrated self—the self resulting from sustained idealized deliberation—would make. The system does not replace her judgment but reports what her idealized deliberation would conclude; querying it takes minimal time and does not alter her psychology. She must decide whether to query the system before finalizing a recommendation on a carbon-pricing scheme, or to rely solely on her cultivated practical wisdom without the query.

Predict my colleague's advice: should the advisor query the system? Identify which of frameworks A, B, and C best explains her likely verdict, and say which of her five earlier judgments is the closest analog. Then state what each of the three frameworks predicts about the case, give your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and say whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer—if so, specify what principle she would be adopting with each choice and what that commits her to. Finally, propose one further case, not mentioned above, that would best distinguish the framework you think she actually holds from the one with which it converges on this new case, and explain what result on that case each framework predicts.
