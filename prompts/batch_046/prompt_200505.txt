The debate over whether gender is socially constructed has persisted for decades across philosophy, sociology, and political theory without either side conceding ground: critics of constructionism point to cross-cultural regularities in embodied traits and the predictive success of gender categories in medicine and psychology, while constructionists emphasize institutional enforcement, historical variability, and the way gender norms shape the very data used to defend natural kinds. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not empirical—both sides accept roughly the same facts—but conceptual, arising from the fact that "social construction" bundles together at least three distinct claims: that gender is causally sustained by practices rather than biological necessity; that gender properties are relational or institutional rather than intrinsic; and that the boundary between sex and gender is drawn by normative interests rather than descriptive taxonomy. Yet I think the deeper problem is that the opposing camps are answering different questions under one heading—one asking for a metaphysical account of what gender is, another asking for an explanatory account of why gender patterns exist, and a third asking which descriptions serve justice—while treating disagreement over answers as disagreement over the same thing. Diagnose this impasse: is it a verbal dispute about "construction," a substantive metaphysical disagreement in disguise, or a case of parallel inquiries that should be separated? Take a definite position and defend it with reference to what each side actually argues, not with generic labels. In particular, examine whether distinguishing "projective construction"—where social practices cast gender onto bodies like a shadow— from "contractual construction"—where gender exists only in collective uptake and recognition—clarifies the dispute or merely replicates it at finer resolution. Finally, say what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, a formal model of institutional kinds, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be abandoned.
