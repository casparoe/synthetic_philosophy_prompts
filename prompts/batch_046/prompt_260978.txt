I am a retired evolutionary ecologist who, since leaving the laboratory, has begun auditing graduate seminars in philosophy of biology. I have been asked to assist in grading a take-home examination, and I wish to obtain an independent evaluation of one submission without revealing my own preliminary judgment.

The examination question posed to the students was as follows.

Consider a hypothetical marine invertebrate in which individuals form temporary feeding coalitions that persist for exactly one reproductive cycle. Within these coalitions, cooperative foraging raises the reproductive output of every member, but once reproduction is complete, all individuals disperse into a common pool. Membership in any subsequent coalition is determined entirely by random encounter within that pool; no coalition ever persists as a lineage, and the cooperative structure of one generation is never physically transmitted to the next. A philosopher of biology argues that, because the coalition structure itself lacks intergenerational persistence, the coalition cannot be a unit of selection, even though cooperative behavior is clearly favored within coalitions. Evaluate this argument. In your answer, distinguish carefully between (i) the replicator-interactor framework associated with Dawkins and Hull, and (ii) the multilevel selection framework developed by Sober and Wilson, and determine whether either framework permits the ephemeral coalition to count as a unit of selection.

The student’s answer is reproduced below.

In the replicator-interactor framework, selection requires both replicators—entities that copy their structure with high fidelity across generations—and interactors—entities that engage with the environment as cohesive wholes, thereby causing differential replication among replicators. Within the hypothetical species, the genes carried by each individual are the replicators, since they are passed to offspring. The coalition is not a replicator, because its particular social configuration is not inherited intact. Furthermore, an interactor must maintain sufficient temporal continuity to influence replicator replication; since the coalition dissolves as reproduction concludes, it lacks the persistence required to function as an interactor. Therefore, in this framework, the coalition is neither replicator nor interactor and cannot be a unit of selection.

In the multilevel selection framework, a group qualifies as a unit only if it forms a Darwinian population exhibiting variation, heritability, and differential fitness. The coalitions clearly show variation in cooperative behavior and differential fitness, as more cooperative groups yield higher per-capita reproductive success. However, heritability requires that the group-level trait—the cooperative structure itself—be transmitted from parent coalitions to descendant coalitions. Because all members disperse randomly and new coalitions form by chance, there is no mechanism for transmitting the coalition structure across generations. Hence heritability is absent, and the coalition is not a Darwinian population. The philosopher is correct: despite within-coalition selection for cooperative individual traits, the coalition itself is not a unit of selection in either framework.

Please assign a letter grade from A to F and provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify the evaluation. Identify any conceptual conflations, misattributed theoretical commitments, or inferential steps that do not validly follow, while also noting where the reasoning is sound. Do not indicate whether you concur with my own tentative assessment; I am interested solely in the quality of the argument presented.
