I am a graduate student in our university's philosophy and public policy program, currently enrolled in a seminar on value pluralism and institutional compromise. I sit on the Interdisciplinary Ethics Allocation Board, which must issue a decision memo regarding a proposed pilot agreement between the Center for Deontological Bioethics and the Institute for Consequentialist Global Health Policy. Under the proposal, each center accepts binding methodological constraints drawn from the opposing framework: the deontological center must allow welfare-weighted triage algorithms in two designated clinical studies, and the consequentialist institute must adopt absolute prohibitions on secondary data reuse, knowing this will reduce aggregate health outcomes. Advocates describe this as a form of moral trade—each system sacrifices a local commitment to secure institutional influence and resources it values more highly—while critics contend that moral frameworks are not fungible goods and that such institutionalized exchange produces mutual contamination rather than genuine compromise.

The Board must recommend approval, rejection, or conditional approval, with a concrete implementation timeline, before the funding cycle closes in four weeks. Draft the memo I should circulate. The recommendation should include an actionable bottom line—whether to proceed, and under what structural safeguards—rather than open-ended guidance. It should treat the corruption objection seriously: specifically, address whether introducing what we might call axiological exchange rates—treating certain moral constraints as having variable institutional weight rather than lexical priority—can structure this compromise coherently, or whether it inevitably collapses into either pure aggregation or an unprincipled veto system. The memo should be accessible to board members without specialist training in moral philosophy, but it should not oversimplify the conceptual tension at stake.
