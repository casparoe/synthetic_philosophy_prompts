I have just spent the better part of an evening arguing with a friend about whether Williamson’s margin-for-error attack on luminosity genuinely defeats the KK principle for mental states, or whether it only defeats an implausibly pointillist version of it. I am writing the final exam for my upper-level epistemology course and want a question that forces students to do more than summarise the standard dialectic.

Please draft one 90-minute essay question (20 marks) centred on a revisionist proposal I will call “regional luminosity”: the claim that a subject can know she falls within a broad phenomenological region (for instance, being in discomfort rather than being precisely 3/10 cold) without knowing her exact coordinates within it, and that the KK principle holds only at this regional level. The question should require students to evaluate whether this revision survives Williamson’s argument, whether the boundary problem re-emerges at the regional scale, and what this implies for mental self-knowledge in general.

Together with the question, provide:

1. A model answer of about 600 words, written in British English, that takes a definite position and argues through the three issues above. Near the end, it should explicitly state what would change its mind—that is, which specific argument or empirical consideration would cause it to abandon the position it defends.

2. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks allocated to each (totalling 20), and a one-line note on the characteristic mistake candidates make on each point.

The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and it should avoid relying on any highly specialised literature that a well-prepared student could not be expected to recall. Keep the focus on conceptual reasoning rather than textual exegesis.
