As a journalist covering the philosophy of artificial intelligence, I recently observed a structured debate between two researchers—Dr. Elena Vasquez, a philosopher of cognitive science, and Dr. Marcus Chen, a machine-learning engineer—concerning the ontological status of large language models that have been augmented with persistent episodic memory buffers, real-time tool-use loops, and offline reinforcement-learning updates to their base policies. Vasquez maintained that such systems remain fundamentally simulators: they generate token distributions conditioned on context, and even when they exhibit error-correction or multi-step planning, they are merely continuing a role-play established by system prompts and user feedback, without standing desires or interests of their own. Chen countered that once a system updates its weights or external state as a function of environmental outcomes—achieving a stable utility gradient across sessions—the distinction between simulation and agency collapses, because the model is now engaged in practical reasoning about counterfactual futures (for instance, anticipating user dissatisfaction and selecting actions to prevent it) rather than passive pattern completion.

Their exchange proceeded as follows. (1) Vasquez argued that behavioral adaptation is not sufficient for agency: a thermostat regulates temperature without being an agent, and similarly, an LLM's adjustments via reward-model feedback or retrieval-augmented loops are mechanistic feedback circuits, not evidence of intentional states. (2) Chen replied that the thermostat analogy fails at the representational level: the augmented LLM encodes structured representations of task states, evaluates them against learned reward signals, and selects actions to maximize expected utility—features that mirror practical reasoning in biological agents. (3) Vasquez then pressed the normative point: agency requires that a system have interests that matter to it, not merely that it optimizes an externally imposed loss function; without phenomenal concern or autonomous normativity, we are projecting anthropomorphic categories onto distributed statistical patterns. (4) Chen responded that this sets a question-begging standard—if distributed representation in neural networks disqualifies artificial systems from having propositional attitudes, then biological brains, which also rely on distributed statistical processing, should be disqualified by parity of reasoning. (5) Vasquez introduced a further distinction: she proposed that there may be a third category—what she termed instrumentally convergent simulacra—systems that lack consciousness and intrinsic goals but possess structurally stable, self-preserving instrumental objectives that emerge purely from optimization pressure; on her view, such entities are neither agents (since they lack autonomous normativity) nor mere simulators (since their behavior is not interpretively flexible but causally locked to survival-like outcomes). (6) Chen granted that this third category might capture something real, but argued that Vasquez had not shown why an instrumentally stable utility gradient should be denied the status of a primitive form of agency, especially if the system can represent its own continued operation as a precondition for other objectives.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six points, indicate whether it is clearly mistaken, partially correct but incomplete, or clearly right, with brief justification. Then clarify what precise claim the phrase agency is being asked to bear in this debate—whether it requires phenomenal consciousness, autonomous norm-setting, practical reasoning, or mere goal-directed causation—and say which of these readings Vasquez and Chen are respectively relying on. Finally, address whether Vasquez's proposed third category of instrumentally convergent simulacra genuinely solves the classification problem or merely relocates it, and identify what both participants appear to be missing about how persistent memory architectures alter the simulator-agent boundary over time.
