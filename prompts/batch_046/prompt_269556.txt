You serve on the advisory board of an institute for the philosophy of computation. Over the past several years, the board has issued rulings on whether proposed computing architectures genuinely threaten the Church-Turing thesis, interpreted not as a mathematical theorem but as an empirical claim about the limits of effective, mechanically realizable computation. Their published track record includes the following four verdicts.

First, they ruled that an idealized analog computer solving the halting problem through continuous physical dynamics—exploiting infinite precision in its initial conditions—does not constitute a counterexample, because infinite precision and perfect noise suppression are not physically effective conditions.

Second, they ruled that a universal quantum computer running algorithms such as Shor's factoring procedure does not threaten the thesis, on the grounds that quantum computation, however physically exotic, remains computable within the bounds of a standard Turing machine given sufficient, though impractical, time.

Third, they ruled that an oracle-augmented Turing machine equipped with a black-box solver for the halting problem should be treated as extending the formal model of computation rather than refuting the thesis, since the oracle is not itself a mechanically constructible component.

Fourth, they ruled that a deep neural network trained to approximate the values of a non-computable function across a large but finite dataset is not a counterexample, because finite approximation on sampled inputs is distinct from computing the function over its entire domain.

The board now faces a new proposal. Its designers describe a discrete digital circuit built from standard electronic components, arranged so that its state transitions depend on a global consistency constraint enforced by a closed timelike curve permitted in certain interpretations of quantum gravity. In this architecture, the circuit attempts to run a program; if the program does not halt within a fixed interval, the future state of the loop sends information backward that prevents that non-halting execution path from being realized. The designers claim that this solves the halting problem exactly and in finite physical time, without invoking infinite precision, pseudorandomness, or an explicit external oracle; they argue that the information travels through a physically permitted spacetime channel, making the device a genuine mechanical counterexample rather than a formal extension. Critics reply that the loop functions precisely as an embedded oracle, and that allowing retrocausal constraints changes the rules of computation rather than revealing a limit in the standard thesis.

The board must issue a recommendation consistent with its own precedents. Identify the plausible rules that jointly accommodate their four prior verdicts. For each rule, state what it predicts about this retrocausal circuit. Which prior ruling is the closest analog, and what is your best guess for the board's decision, with the reason for preferring that rule? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, specify the principle the board would be adopting with each available choice. Finally, describe a further hypothetical case—varying one physical or formal feature—that would best distinguish these competing rules.
