I've been trying to understand the philosophical claim that a person necessarily comes from the specific sperm and egg they actually came from — that different gametes would mean a different person altogether. I keep returning to the well-known Baby M surrogacy case from New Jersey in the 1980s: Mary Beth Whitehead agreed to carry a child conceived with William Stern's sperm and her own egg, then refused to surrender the infant after birth; the court eventually awarded custody to the Sterns while preserving Whitehead's visitation rights. If origin essentialism is right, that child could not have arisen from any other combination of gametes, so both Whitehead and Stern contributed an essential, non-replaceable origin. Yet the legal framework treated Whitehead's genetic and gestational contribution as contractually separable from her parental claim, while treating Stern's genetic contribution as the basis for custodial priority. I want to see whether origin essentialism illuminates this case or collapses under it. Does the court's reasoning depend on a hidden essentialist assumption about paternal origin being the "true" one, or does it implicitly deny that origins matter by making them assignable through agreement? And since modern reproduction can split genetic origin, gestation, and social parenting, does origin essentialism need to divide into different kinds — genetic versus gestational — or does the case reveal that the theory was never meant to handle arrangements where these roles come apart?
