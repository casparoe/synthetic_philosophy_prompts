As a retired physicist who has recently begun working through philosophy of action and behavioral economics, I have arrived at the following views on weakness of will and intertemporal choice, and I want a consistency check before I present them to a reading group.

(1) Akrasia is real: an agent can sincerely judge, at the moment of action, that A is better than B, and yet do B without having revised that judgment.
(2) Hyperbolic discounting is the correct descriptive model of human intertemporal preference; exponential discounting fails empirically.
(3) Rational authority does not belong automatically to either the present or the future self: temporal location alone never makes one preference more rational than another.
(4) Rationality nonetheless requires diachronic consistency—if preferences at t1 and t2 conflict without new information or values, at least one is defective.
(5) In addiction, yielding to present desire is a paradigm of irrational akrasia, not a rational preference reversal by a different temporal self.
(6) The ego-depletion model of willpower is empirically discredited, so explanations of akrasia should avoid appeals to depleted mental resources.

My worry is that (2) together with (3) seems to dissolve the normative ground for condemning present-biased choice, which threatens (5), while (4) demands consistency that hyperbolic curves violate structurally. At the same time, (1) and (6) look compatible, but I fear I am using (1) to smuggle in a normative standard that (3) explicitly rejects. Which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear so? Rank the conflicts by severity, identify the least costly revision, and tell me what I must surrender if I want to preserve the core claim that addictive choice is rational failure rather than mere intertemporal preference change.
