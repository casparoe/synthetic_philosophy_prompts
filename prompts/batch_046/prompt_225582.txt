I am giving the opening remarks at a workshop I co-organized before a three-month medical leave forced me to work from outside my institution; the event, titled "Formal Boundaries, Living Systems," gathers philosophers of logic, cognitive scientists, and scholars from adjacent fields. The leave felt like an attempt at JOOTSing—jumping out of the formal system of my academic practice to see it from outside—yet every paper I drafted and every argument I rehearsed seemed to obey the same internal rules, as if I were still inside Hofstadter’s MIU system trying to derive MU without ever leaving its axioms. I need the speech—no more than 600 words, about four minutes, and please begin with the bottom line stated in a single sentence—to welcome both audiences, introduce the workshop’s theme, and make the MU-puzzle and JOOTSing do real structural work rather than serve as metaphors. Roughly half the room knows these ideas; the other half does not, and I must not lose either group. Specifically, I should not claim that my absence granted me a transcendent outside perspective, because that would violate the logic of the puzzle; but I also must not pretend that my framework remained unchanged. Instead, I want the audience to feel the precise tension that every escape attempt is already anticipated by the system’s meta-rules, yet the attempt still alters what is visible from within. The speech should be formal without being stiff, should avoid any bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations, and should end with the workshop’s central question: whether genuine conceptual escape is possible, or whether we only ever expand the box that contains us. Please write it so that a listener who knows nothing of formal logic still grasps why this matters to someone who has just tried, and failed, to step outside his own system.
