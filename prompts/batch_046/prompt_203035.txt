I'm trying to figure out what makes a state apology actually good, going by my gut on cases. Here are my verdicts.

When a goverment apologizes for a specific massacre that happened 120 years ago, survivors are long dead, but the descendants still live without access to lands taken right after the killings. I think that's a real apology and should come with land restitution.

When the same state apologizes for decades of forced assimilation of children, but the statement says we regret harm that may have occured without naming the policy, delivers it only in the dominant language, and adds no institutional change. That feels hollow, like they're closing a file.

Then there's a personal delivery case: president meets survivors of wartime internment, hands over direct compensation, and then the curriculum gets rewritten. That's the gold standard in my book.

Contrast that with a nearly identical internment where the apology comes as a press release, the leader says we must not let guilt paralyze us, and there's zero compensation. That feels worse than silence.

Here's the pair I can't square with a simple rule. Country X ran a sterilization program that was fully legal and court-approved at the time. Their apology calls the laws themselves illegitimate, repeals them retroactively, and funds healthcare for victims. Country Y apologizes for torture that was clearly illegal even then, but frames it as bad apples breaking an otherwise sound legal framework. I feel X did the real work and Y is just doing damage control, even though X's wrong was legal and Y's was criminal.

Also: a state apologizes for colonial extraction, returns a few artifacts, but keeps all the revenue and offers no structural change. Partial credit, but not enough.

Then there's my flip-flop case. What about an apology delivered by a current democratic goverment for crimes committed by the monarchy that preceeded it? Sometimes I think institutional continuity means they should, other times it feels like costume theatre. I can't settle it.

So is there one principle behind all this? Or do I have to drop one of my verdicts? And if there is a principle, what does it say about the monarchy-to-democracy case?
