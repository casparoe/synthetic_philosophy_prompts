Here is an argument that extends Arendt's analysis of Eichmann in a direction that seems to collapse the very distinctions she wanted to preserve. (1) Arendt claims that Eichmann's wrongdoing was banal: it arose not from deep ideological hatred or demonic intent, but from thoughtlessness, unreflective bureaucratic role-playing, and a failure to take others' perspectives seriously. (2) If moral culpability still attaches to such acts, then the presence or absence of malicious intent cannot be what distinguishes guilty perpetrators from innocent bystanders; rather, culpability must depend on objective participation in harmful institutional processes, regardless of subjective awareness. (3) In contemporary administrative and economic life, most people routinely contribute—through employment, taxation, and consumption—to systems that cause distant or diffuse harm, without malicious intent and often without specific knowledge of the damage. (4) By the reasoning in (2), these ordinary participants therefore share in the same banal evil, differing from Eichmann only in the scale or directness of their causal contribution. (5) Yet Arendt herself insisted on a sharp distinction between active perpetrators who organize harm and ordinary citizens who merely inhabit structures they did not design. (6) So the concept of banal evil either applies so widely that it loses all discriminative force, or it covertly reintroduces the intent-based distinctions that (2) was meant to eliminate. Which step should be rejected? If you want to keep (1) and preserve (5), what revision to (2) or (3) is least costly, and what does it concede about moral responsibility?
