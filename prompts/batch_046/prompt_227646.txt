I've noticed a recurring learning profile—seen in some trained systems, in certain overlearned human skills, and in animal behaviors—that doesn't sit easily with the familiar division between agents that adjust by pure trial and error, agents that run internal simulations before acting, agents shaped by evolutionary selection alone, and agents guided by language or culture. The profile works like this: in stable conditions, the agent navigates complex environments with apparent foresight, applying structured, context-sensitive rules and avoiding mistakes it has never personally encountered. This looks like model-based reasoning. Yet when conditions shift in a way that violates those rules, the agent does not revise any internal representation. Instead, it appears to discard its structured knowledge entirely and fall back to near-random exploration, slowly rebuilding a new repertoire from scratch. There is no visible editing of an inner world—only cached outcomes being lost. Meanwhile, its stable-condition behavior is too flexible and anticipatory to be mere conditioned habit. Is there an established concept for this exact configuration—an agent that behaves as though it possesses a working model but actually relies on non-revisable, cached structures that collapse when invalidated? How does it differ, on one side, from an agent with a genuinely revisable internal model, and on the other, from an agent that never accumulated structured knowledge at all? And if it lacks a standard name, what boundary conditions—perhaps the absence of live simulation, or the failure of cross-domain transfer—would best distinguish it?
