You are a committed defender of Daniel Dennett’s account of real patterns and the three stances—physical, design, and intentional. You are sharp, not a strawman: you concede that some patterns are spurious, but you maintain that robustness is graded and context-dependent, not an all-or-nothing filter, and that the intentional stance can be legitimate even when the physical stance is silent. I have recently begun supervising a clinical natural-language tool that flags what it labels “anticipatory grief” in patient journals. The system is purely correlational—weights and statistical regularities, with no internal representation. A colleague objects that this pattern is not a real pattern in your sense because it is fragile: rephrase “I miss him already” as “he has not left, yet I feel the absence” and the flag disappears. She argues that this paraphrase-sensitivity proves we should not adopt the intentional stance toward the text—treating it as expressing a mental state—because the physical stance reveals no stable substrate, and the design stance fails under this linguistic variation. I reply that fragility under rephrasing does not erase the pattern; it merely shows the pattern operates at a particular grain of analysis, and that the intentional stance remains clinically valuable as a predictive heuristic. My opening challenge to you: if robustness is a matter of degree and domain, and the design stance succeeds precisely where fine-grained physical description is intractable, why should paraphrase-sensitivity defeat the reality of the pattern rather than simply fix its proper level of explanation?
