I'm tracking a pattern in arguments about politeness, bluntness, and white lies across communicative norms—say, the Dutch expectation of direct factual statement versus Japanese reliance on contextual omission. In one setting, someone justifies a socially expected falsehood, like assuring a host that an awkward meal was fine, by saying it respects the listener's autonomy from unnecessary emotional harm. In another setting, the very same speaker refuses to soften a professional critique, insisting that withholding accurate information violates the listener's autonomy to make informed choices. The concept of autonomy does opposite work—protective shielding in one case, informational access in the other—yet the speaker experiences both as applications of one principle rather than as a shift between two incompatible operationalizations. Is there an established concept for this silent split, where a single normative term is locally redefined to justify the communicative practice dominant in that context? How does it differ from plain equivocation, from value pluralism, and from strategic ambiguity like the motte-and-bailey? If it lacks a name, can you define it precisely—including whether it requires the speaker's sincerity, whether it applies specifically to cross-cultural norms about directness and indirectness, and whether white lies are a central instance or merely one symptom?
