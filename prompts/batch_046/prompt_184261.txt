Since my partner and I have begun discussing whether to start a family, the ethics of abortion has shifted from an abstract philosophical exercise to a framework that bears directly on how I understand bodily autonomy and compelled care. I have arrived at a position I want tested without charity. I hold that abortion is permissible not because the foetus lacks full moral status, but because gestation constitutes an uncompensated, sustained form of physiological labour that cannot be compelled — extending the violinist analogy. Crucially, I claim that the wrongness of compelled gestation is specific to direct metabolic integration: the pregnant person is being asked to serve as a life-support system, not merely to refrain from harming an independent being. From this I derive that if safe ectogenetic extraction and ex utero gestation were available, the moral justification for termination would dissolve; one would no longer be withdrawing one's own bodily resources but actively deciding whether to initiate the separate existence of a potential person. At that point, I concede, a modified future-like-ours consideration — that killing deprives a being of a valuable future — gains genuine traction, whereas in standard in utero termination it fails because it conflates killing with allowing to die when the mechanism of preservation is the very body being withdrawn. I therefore reject the standard future-like-ours argument against early abortion on these grounds. What would have to be true for this view to be wrong? I want an honest map of its exposure: which empirical findings, conceptual distinctions, or moral intuitions would be decisive defeaters, and which merely awkward? Specifically, assess whether the killing-versus-letting-die distinction holds up in pregnancy; whether the ectogenetic scenario genuinely transforms the moral logic or only relocates it; whether my position collapses into an unacceptable general libertarianism about bodily obligations; and whether its defenders have qualified it into unfalsifiability. Rank the defeaters from fatal to inconvenient, indicate which ones a committed defender should concede, and tell me whether the view is genuinely at risk or largely insulated.
