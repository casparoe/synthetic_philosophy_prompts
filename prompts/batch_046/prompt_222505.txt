As a retired biologist who has recently turned to philosophy of mind and value theory, I have become preoccupied with a problem that clinical neurology poses but rarely resolves: pain asymbolia. Patients with this condition preserve nociceptive discrimination and can report that a stimulus hurts, yet they display none of the usual emotional aversion or withdrawal behavior, and they often seem genuinely untroubled by injuries that would distress an ordinary person. The philosophical question is whether this dissociation shows that the badness of pain is separable from its sensory presence, or whether the badness remains intact but is merely masked by a disrupted expressive pathway.

I would like you to draft the joint statement that two opposing camps might sign after a sustained, good-faith adversarial collaboration on this precise issue. The camps are:

Camp A — Affective Essentialists: They maintain that the normative badness of pain is constituted by its intrinsic negative affective valence, and that pain asymbolia demonstrates that when this valence is disconnected from nociceptive processing, the remaining sensory state lacks genuine welfare significance.

Camp B — Representational-Functionalists: They maintain that the badness of pain derives primarily from its representational content (bodily damage) and from its functional role in motivation and long-term welfare, and that asymbolia involves a dissociation of behavioral expression or subjective report rather than an absence of badness; the state remains harmful to the subject, though its harmfulness is no longer behaviorally manifest.

Please produce a formal, polished document with exactly the following structure:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — factual, conceptual, and methodological propositions both camps can endorse without reservation. These should cover the clinical and neural profile of pain asymbolia, the shared framing of the philosophical problem, and any common assumptions about the relation between phenomenal experience and welfare.

2. A disagreements table — the precise formulations of residual disagreement, each phrased so that its own camp would accept the wording as an accurate statement of its view, with the opposing camp's parallel formulation presented alongside. The wording should be neutral, not slanted, and should make clear exactly where the camps part ways.

3. The cruxes — the specific sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would move at least one camp. I take it that one crux concerns whether there is a necessary conceptual connection between recognizing bodily damage and experiencing that recognition as bad, and another concerns how to evaluate hypothetical cases of complete dissociation. Please identify the cruxes explicitly and state them in a form both camps recognize.

4. For each crux, specify: (a) a concrete case, argument, or hypothetical scenario both camps agree is genuinely relevant; (b) Camp A's prediction or interpretation; (c) Camp B's prediction or interpretation.

To ensure the exercise remains meaningful without access to specialized archives, please do not include bibliographies, citation lists, requests for further reading, or appeals to specific texts by title or date. You may, however, introduce a precisely defined conceptual distinction or hypothetical clinical profile to sharpen the disagreement — for instance, a condition of complete affective-motivational dissociation in which nociceptive signaling and damage-representation remain fully intact, all avoidance behavior and negative affect are permanently eliminated, and the subject retains full conceptual understanding of the injury. The document should be written in a formal register, using the first-person plural where appropriate for agreed claims, but preserving the distinct philosophical voice of each camp in the disagreements and predictions sections.
