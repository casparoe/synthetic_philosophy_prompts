I am in an upper-level philosophy of science course, and my professor has offered verdicts on five modeling cases. She praised a macroeconomic model that assumes perfect rationality and homogeneous agents because it predicts inflation dynamics well despite its false premises; she called a neuroscience model with linear neuronal integration acceptable only as a provisional isolating device, since its simplification hides known nonlinear dynamics; she rejected a demographic model that held migration rates constant because that unrealistic assumption directly caused its false prediction of urban collapse; she approved a climate model treating ocean currents as a uniform fluid, labeling it a sound isolating idealization; and she criticized a gravitational-wave model that treated black holes as point masses, noting that the unrealistic assumption was harmless only because general relativity—not the idealization—secured the result. For next week we are examining an integrated climate-economy model that defines social welfare as a negative entropy gradient of economic activity, a physically unrealistic thermodynamic value assumption, yet it derives accurate carbon-tax efficacy predictions. Predict her verdict on this model, determine whether she is implicitly applying Friedman-style predictive immunity, a Cartwright-style isolating-mechanism view, or a robustness-dependence criterion, explain what each of those three rules predicts here, and specify the further model case that would discriminate among them. Provide the response in American English as a numbered list of exactly five items, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
