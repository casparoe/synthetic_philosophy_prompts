I was arguing with a friend late into the evening about whether negative utilitarianism is a serious normative framework or merely an unsettling intuition that collapses under its own weight. She kept returning to the classic asymmetry—why the absence of suffering is a decisive reason not to bring someone into existence, while the absence of pleasure is not a reason to do so—but then pressed a sharper point: if suffering-focused ethics is correct, it should not only govern procreative decisions but also reshape longtermist risk assessment, medical triage priorities, and perhaps even the design of artificial agents that might experience harm. I found I could not defend the view’s practical relevance without first knowing which of its conceptual fractures are actually load-bearing and which are merely decorative. So rather than asking for a survey, I want a defended agenda.

If you were advising a small conceptual research group—three philosophers with about three years, no empirical lab, and an interest in suffering-focused ethics—where should their limited effort go? I am less interested in an exhaustive list than in a strict prioritization: which open questions or lines of implication should they tackle first, in what order, and on what criteria? Please make your reasoning explicit—upstream dependence (which answers unlock others), tractability for philosophers working without experimental tools, and practical importance for domains like longtermist risk, bioethics, or institutional design. Among the candidates, consider the threshold problem of whether any amount of happiness can outweigh a given quantity of suffering and whether a non-arbitrary cutoff exists; the interface with population ethics, particularly whether suffering-focused longtermism requires a total or person-affecting framework to avoid implausible conclusions about future generations; and what I will call modal robustness—whether suffering-focused ethics should prioritize eliminating harms that are structurally robust across nearby possible worlds rather than contingent on specific causal paths, and whether that distinction survives analysis. For each, defend its rank, note the trade-offs between difficulty and downstream payoff, and tell me what kind of conceptual result in the next few years would cause you to reorder the list entirely.
