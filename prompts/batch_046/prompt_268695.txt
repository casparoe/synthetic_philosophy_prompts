Take Marian Anderson's 1939 Easter Sunday concert at the Lincoln Memorial — the one after the Daughters of the American Revolution barred her from Constitution Hall — and analyze it through Du Bois's claim that all art is propoganda. I'm thinking of his 1926 Crisis essay where he argues Black artists should make work that serves both racial equality and beauty, not just pretty things for their own sake. Don't just summarize the two; actually map the theory onto the episode: was Anderson's performance propaganda by Du Bois's lights, even though she sang Schubert's Ave Maria and America instead of an explicit protest song? Did the political framing — the monument, the 75,000 people, Eleanor Roosevelt resigning from the DAR — do the propagandistic work that the music itself refused? And what does the case reveal about Du Bois's view: does it show that context can turn any performance into propaganda, or does it show that his framework forces artists into a representative role they never asked for?

One more thing: please seperate what is genuinely settled about Du Bois's position (he did insist art should advance the race, that's not in dispute) from what scholars actually fight over — for instance, whether he meant propaganda in a narrow political sense or a broader cultural-aesthetic one, and whether his view leaves any real room for art that declines to speak for the group.
