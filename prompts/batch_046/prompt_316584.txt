Design a 75-minute structured workshop for an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar of ten students. The aim is to make participants feel, through their own structured choices, the philosophical force of gradual cognitive replacement in mind uploading—specifically, the conflict between functionalist continuity and the intuition that identity depends on something more than behavioral equivalence—before the facilitator names the problem.

Construct a staged deliberation with the following architecture. Scenario: a patient is undergoing sequential replacement of three cognitive modules—episodic memory encoding, affective valence tagging, and narrative self-modeling—with synthetic equivalents. Each replacement is behaviorally indistinguishable in the short term, but one module (unknown to the Board) contains a latent divergence: a different long-term stability trajectory that will only manifest after all replacements are complete. Roles: one Patient (who communicates only through standardized functional reports), one Engineer (who knows which module carries the divergence but is bound by confidentiality rules not to disclose it), four Ethics Board members who must vote unanimously after each round, and one Observer tracking justifications with a point system. Stakes: use a token economy in which unanimous "continue" votes accumulate shared resources for the group, but a single "archive" vote halts the process, forfeits accumulated tokens, and ends the scenario. The Board must therefore weigh the cost of stopping against the risk of continuing through an opaque transformation.

Provide a precise timeline (e.g., 10 minutes setup, three 15-minute rounds with voting, 15 minutes revelation and debrief). Specify the rules governing speech (e.g., the Patient may not appeal to "inner feeling," the Engineer may only describe functional specifications). At the end of round three, reveal that all biological substrates have been replaced and that the latent divergence is now active; ask the Board whether they retroactively endorse round-one, round-two, and round-three votes, and record the inconsistencies.

In the debrief, map each round onto a philosophical position: round-one assent onto naive functionalism, round-two hesitation onto psychological-continuity concerns, and round-three crisis onto biological or substrate-continuity objections. Predict what will go wrong on first run—likely that the Observer's point system distracts from identity questions, or that students fixate on identifying the hidden divergence rather than examining why they wanted a cutoff at all. Be explicit about which feature of the actual mind-uploading debate the exercise cannot reproduce—namely, the first-person phenomenology of gradual change—and state exactly how the facilitator must supply that gap verbally.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke standard thought-experiment names such as "Ship of Theseus." Frame the replacement module-by-module rather than neuron-by-neuron, and embed the latent-divergence twist as the novel element that prevents the activity from being a generic identity-game. Write the deliverable as a fully runnable protocol: role cards, token rules, voting procedure, revelation script, debrief questions, and an honest limitation note.
