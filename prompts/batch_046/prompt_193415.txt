I do not have an academic background in philosophy or ethics, but over the past year I have been thinking seriously on my own about care ethics — the view that moral life is centered on relationships, dependency, vulnerability, and attentive response rather than on abstract rights or universal rules. I have read a little about its origins and its contrast with justice-based frameworks, but I am not trying to reproduce scholarship; I want to understand which directions in this area are actually worth pursuing and which are dead ends.

From my own reading and reflection, I can see several open lines of work. First, whether care ethics can be extended from intimate, face-to-face relationships to large-scale institutions — governments, healthcare systems, international aid structures — without becoming so diluted that it no longer differs from ordinary welfare liberalism. Second, how care ethics handles conflicts between caregivers and care-receivers when both belong to structurally disadvantaged groups, since the framework emphasizes responsiveness but does not obviously provide a decision rule. Third, whether care ethics can ground obligations toward distant strangers, future generations, or non-human nature, given that its usual justification appeals to direct relational encounter. Fourth, a problem I have been trying to formulate myself: care ethics often treats moral attention as something that should be expanded and deepened, yet in practice attention is a scarce cognitive resource; if caring requires sustained attention, and attention is limited, then distributing care becomes an allocation problem that looks structurally similar to the distributive justice problems care ethics was meant to avoid. I do not know whether this is a genuine internal tension or a misunderstanding of what care ethics claims about attention. Fifth, what care ethics practically implies for the design of automated decision-making in social services — for instance, whether algorithmic welfare eligibility or medical triage should be evaluated by criteria other than efficiency and formal fairness.

If I were advising a small interdisciplinary group — perhaps five researchers with training in moral philosophy, social policy, and institutional design, with about three years of funding — which two or three of these lines should they treat as priorities? I am asking for a defended agenda, not a survey. For each line you rank highly, explain why it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable with current conceptual and empirical tools, and what kind of result over the next few years would either confirm its importance or show that it should be deprioritized. For the lines you rank lower, explain whether they depend on resolving the higher-ranked ones first, or whether they are simply less likely to change practice. In particular, address my concern about attention and scarcity: is it a real conceptual crux for care ethics, or does it rest on a false assumption about how the framework treats mental resources?

Please defend your ordering with explicit criteria — for example, how many other questions depend on this one, how feasible it is without breakthroughs in unrelated fields, and whether answering it would alter how real institutions operate. I want to know where limited intellectual effort should go, not just what is unresolved.
