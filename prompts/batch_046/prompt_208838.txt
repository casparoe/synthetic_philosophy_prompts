I am preparing a long-form essay for a general intellectual audience on the ontology of death in early Daoist thought, and I have encountered a conceptual problem I need help resolving. The canonical anecdote—Zhuangzi responding to his wife's death not with lamentation but with drumming on a tub—is usually interpreted as emotional equanimity before cosmic transformation. I want to treat this not as psychology but as metaphysics. Specifically, I am trying to reconstruct Zhuangzi's notion of hua, or transformation, within a framework that denies substantial selfhood: if the person is never more than a temporary pattern of relations and energies rather than a persisting substance, then death is not the destruction of an entity but only the redistribution of that pattern into a broader field. Here is the difficulty: if there is no substantial self to be lost, only a change in relational configuration, can the concept of grief remain coherent? Does mourning presuppose a substantialist error—the belief that a specific, bounded entity has been annihilated—or can it be reinterpreted as a legitimate response to the dissolution of a particular relational structure without committing to substantialism? Please explain the conceptual tension between a process-ontology reading of Zhuangzi and the possibility of rational mourning, and indicate whether resolving the tension requires abandoning the metaphysics, reinterpreting grief, or accepting that the two positions stand in genuine internal contradiction.
