Question:
Consider Rosenthal's centipede game with perfect information and alternating moves, in which the payoff pool expands with each continuation but the share allocated to the player who stops is slightly larger than that received by the other player, and both payoffs grow with each round. The standard backward-induction solution predicts termination at the first decision node. Some theorists have appealed to Selten's trembling-hand refinement to explain why real players continue for several rounds, arguing that a small probability of accidental continuation breaks the chain of rationality. Others have suggested that bounded rationality or altruism could sustain longer play.

Explain precisely why backward induction in this game requires common knowledge of rationality across a nested belief hierarchy, not merely mutual first-order rationality. Then, describe the mechanism by which a tremble at an interior node propagates backward through preceding belief levels. Finally, evaluate the claim that introducing a small, common-knowledge probability that a player is altruistic—rather than error-prone—resolves the backward-induction paradox without relocating the problem elsewhere.

Student answer:
The centipede game involves two players who take turns choosing whether to Stop or Continue. If a player stops, they receive a slightly larger share of the current pot, while the other receives a smaller share; if they continue, the pot grows but the payoff structure shifts unfavorably for the player who will eventually stop. Because the game has perfect information, we solve it by backward induction: at the final node, the player prefers to Stop; knowing this, the previous player prefers to Stop one step earlier, and so on, all the way back to the first node. Therefore rational players should stop immediately.

This result seems paradoxical because experimental subjects rarely stop at the first node. One explanation is the trembling-hand perfect equilibrium proposed by Selten specifically for this game. If each player has a tiny chance of accidentally pressing Continue instead of Stop, then no player can be certain that the opponent will stop at any given node. This uncertainty breaks the backward-induction chain because the belief that the opponent is rational at the next node is no longer guaranteed with probability one. Specifically, a tremble at node n means that player 1 at node n-1 must assign some positive probability to reaching node n+1, which means the rationality belief at node n-1 is only approximate, not absolute. Importantly, this disturbance is local: because the tremble is independent at each node, the belief hierarchy at nodes far from n remains intact, so the backward propagation is blocked and only adjacent nodes are affected.

However, trembles are just errors. If instead we assume that players are rational but may be altruistic with a small probability epsilon that is common knowledge, then continuing can become rational. At each node, a player calculates the expected payoff from continuing, weighting the altruistic branch where the opponent continues out of kindness. If epsilon is large enough relative to the payoff gap between stopping and continuing, the expected value of continuing exceeds that of stopping at every node, so backward induction now predicts continuing all the way to the end. This genuinely resolves the paradox because it gives rational players a reason to continue without relying on error. The problem is not relocated; it is solved by enriching the utility function. Common knowledge is still required, but now it applies to the altruism parameter rather than to pure rationality, so the paradox is dissolved.
