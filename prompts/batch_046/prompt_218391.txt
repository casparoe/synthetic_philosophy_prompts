Below are working notes for an essay extending Simone Weil's critique of political parties. I distinguish between closure-parties, which treat institutional survival as a non-negotiable background constraint on judgment, and permeability-parties, which permit dissent without expulsion, and I suggest Weil's argument applies mainly to the former. Even in permeability-parties, I propose there remains a residual gravity: a downward emotional pull, not enforced by explicit rules but sustained by the social cost of dissent, that erodes the capacity for unforced moral attention she prized. I am uncertain whether this residual effect is genuinely distinct from ordinary peer pressure or whether any organizational form could eliminate it without reducing the group to an unstructured discussion club. Please comment on whether the distinction is coherent, whether residual gravity adds explanatory value beyond standard social psychology, and whether I overgeneralize from Weil's historical examples of interwar French parties. Say clearly what argument or empirical finding would change your view on whether any party structure can fully preserve individual moral judgment. Respond in exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
