I have reconstructed an argument about the phenomenal-judgment paradox that seems to collapse into self-refutation, and I suspect it proves too much. Here are the steps:

(1) If phenomenal properties are epiphenomenal, then no token judgment of the form 'I am experiencing E' is caused, even in part, by the phenomenal property E itself; the judgment's entire neural causal chain is physically closed.

(2) If a judgment's complete causal ancestry contains no reference to P, then P does not determine the judgment's intentional content; the content is fixed solely by the physical states that do cause it.

(3) Therefore, on epiphenomenalism, judgments that appear to be about phenomenal properties are in fact about the underlying neural configurations that cause them.

(4) But if all apparent phenomenal judgments are actually physical judgments misdirected onto consciousness, then there is no genuine first-person category of phenomenal judgment—only systematically mislabeled neural self-monitoring.

(5) Yet phenomenal judgments occur, and they carry distinctive correctness conditions: I can be wrong about my cortical state but correct about my pain, which suggests the judgment is genuinely about the phenomenal state and not about the brain.

(6) So phenomenal properties must possess at least enough causal efficacy to determine the content of judgments about them; hence epiphenomenalism is false.

The problem is that the pattern applies everywhere. Mathematical properties are causally inert, yet we form mathematical judgments with their own correctness conditions; by (1)-(3) those should all be about neural states, and by (4)-(5) they should be misdirected physical judgments, yet they are not. By (6) numbers must be causally efficacious. That proves far too much—causal efficacy for every object of judgment.

I want to preserve (1), since physical causal closure seems hard to surrender, and I would prefer to preserve (5), because the phenomenology of first-person error and correction feels real. So I am inclined to attack (2). But if I reject the principle that causal connection is necessary for intentional content, do I owe a general non-causal theory of mental content? Would that save mathematics without saving epiphenomenalism about consciousness? Or is the real flaw in (4), where I slide from 'content is physically determined' to 'there is no phenomenal category at all'?

Tell me which step is weakest, explain why it looked plausible, and price the alternatives: what do I pay if I reject (2) versus (4) versus (5)? And please say what would change your mind—specifically, whether a convincing counterexample to the causal theory of mental content, or an empirical demonstration that phenomenal judgments are systematically unreliable in exactly the ways neural self-reports are, would shift your diagnosis.
