I am writing a novel in which a character is subjected to an iterative awakening protocol: each morning she is roused, presented with identical surroundings, and asked to wager on the outcome of a fair coin that was flipped before the protocol began. If the coin landed tails, she is awakened every day of the week; if heads, only on Monday. She retains no memory of prior awakenings and possesses no clock. From the perspective of narrative logic, I need you to explain the conceptual issue at the heart of the Sleeping Beauty problem—specifically the disagreement over whether her credence in heads should be one-half or one-third upon awakening—but I want you to do so without naming specific philosophers, papers, or canonical formulations, because I am working in isolation and need the conceptual machinery itself rather than a literature review. In particular, clarify why treating her temporal location (Monday versus Tuesday) as an indexical that partitions the possibility space yields a different updating rule than treating it as mere ignorance about a static world. Then, extend this to a narrative design problem: if my protagonist must choose an action whose rational justification depends on whether she is in the single-awakening world or the double-awakening world, but she cannot distinguish them, how should her reasoning be structured so that she neither double-counts epistemic possibilities nor suppresses the self-locating information? Please keep the analysis rigorous, avoid citations or reading lists, and address it as though explaining the tension to a fellow writer who understands probability but has not encountered this particular paradox in its standard form.
