I am preparing the 90-minute final essay exam for a graduate seminar in philosophy of religion. I need one question focused on Gödel's modal ontological argument that students cannot answer by merely reciting Gödel's axioms and deriving the necessary existence of a God-like being; instead, it must force them to do original structural work on the interaction between modal logic and the definition of positive properties.

Specifically, construct the question around the following injected problem. An interlocutor accepts Gödel's definition of a God-like being as one that possesses every positive property, and she accepts that necessary existence is positive. However, she targets Axiom 2—closure of positive properties under entailment—with a precise, localized denial she calls modal rigidity. She grants that being omnipotent is positive, and she grants that in standard S5 modal logic omnipotence entails necessary omnipotence, but she denies that being necessarily omnipotent is itself a positive property. Her argument is not a general rejection of modal logic, but a claim that modal strengthening transforms an active, world-responsive power into a rigid, world-independent state, thereby violating the intuitive flexibility that makes omnipotence a perfection rather than a limitation. Consequently, she allows that God is omnipotent in the actual world, but she denies that omnipotence belongs to God's essence, which blocks the inference that God is necessarily omnipotent and, she claims, undermines the derivation of necessary existence.

Write the exam question that asks students to evaluate whether Gödel's proof can survive this targeted denial. The students should be required to determine (a) whether blocking modal closure for this single property collapses the derivation of necessary existence or can be isolated, (b) whether the interlocutor's move forces her into a broader rejection of S5 or can be contained as an ad hoc restriction on the closure axiom, and (c) whether the notion of a positive property can be independently motivated—perhaps through considerations of pure limitation or value—in a way that either accommodates or rules out modal rigidity.

Then provide:
1. A model answer of approximately 700 words.
2. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks assigned so that the total is 20. For each point, include a one-line note describing the specific mistake candidates typically make (for example, treating the objection as generic modal skepticism rather than a targeted failure of closure, or listing Gödel's axioms without applying them to the omnipotence case).

The question should be suitable for students who have studied Gödel's argument and standard criticisms, including well-known parodies and consistency concerns, but it should not depend on knowledge of any specific secondary literature beyond what is standard for the seminar. Avoid asking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
