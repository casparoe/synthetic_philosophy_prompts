I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been working systematically through philosophy of mind and the philosophy of science. I now serve on my university hospital's Joint Clinical Ethics and Research Oversight Committee, and we must render a formal decision by Thursday.

A neuroscience group proposes to enroll adult patients with complete corpus callosotomy for intractable epilepsy in a study they call "bilateral preference elicitation." The protocol presents identical economic-choice gambles—small-certain rewards versus larger-risky ones—separately to the left and right visual hemifields; the patient responds with the ipsilateral hand, left for right-hemisphere stimuli and right for left-hemisphere stimuli. The investigators claim that if the two hemispheres display systematically divergent risk profiles, this demonstrates not merely divided information processing but two distinct utility functions, which they take as an operationalization of whether there are two centers of consciousness in one body. They seek approval under a single informed-consent process, treating the patient as one subject, but they also want the committee to endorse language in the consent document stating that participants may possess two independent decision-making systems and that either hemisphere may withdraw on behalf of the other.

Half the committee regards this as an elegant within-subject behavioral design with no special ethical burden beyond standard disclosure; the other half argues that if the investigators' framing is correct, enrolling these patients violates the principle that every research subject must possess a unified capacity to consent or withdraw, and that treating one body as potentially containing two subjects without separate consent processes is impermissible regardless of scientific value.

Please draft the committee's written opinion for circulation before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete course—approve, approve with binding conditions, or reject—together with any necessary time frame. The reasoning must engage seriously with the investigators' claim that divergent revealed preferences can serve as a proxy for divided consciousness, rather than dismissing it as mere metaphor. At the same time, it must present honestly the strongest opposing consideration: that institutional ethics and legal coherence require a unitary subject of consent, and that introducing bilateral withdrawal rights might confuse or undermine patient protection rather than enhance it. Write in a formal, precise tone appropriate for clinicians, scientists, and philosophers—an actionable institutional document, not personal guidance to me.
