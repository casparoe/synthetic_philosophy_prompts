I sit on the advisory board for a municipal heritage trust that must decide the fate of an endowment sufficient either to restore a deteriorating nineteenth-century public market now or to be placed in a restricted covenant that releases the capital in 175 years for preservation purposes then unknown. The board’s economist insists that standard intertemporal reasoning applies: future welfare should be discounted at a modest rate, which still favors immediate repair because present needs are certain and distant ones are speculative. Our ethicist counters that temporal location is morally arbitrary, so discounting is unjust discrimination against people who happen to live later. Into this familiar deadlock, a retired civil engineer—with no philosophical training but considerable authority on long-range infrastructure—has introduced a claim I find genuinely troubling. She argues that the problem is not how much future people matter, but how little right we have to bind them: because we cannot predict their values, technologies, or even whether they will regard our market as worthy of preservation, committing resources now imposes our aesthetic and practical judgments across centuries. She proposes what she terms a “binding-risk premium”: the moral cost of constraining future autonomy should compound with distance, meaning that long-range preservation obligations weaken not because the beneficiaries are less valuable, but because our entitlement to decide for them erodes. This feels different from both pure discounting and from equality across generations, and I am unsure whether it is a profound correction or a sophisticated rationalization for present consumption.

I want your assessment: is this “binding-risk premium” a coherent moral consideration that genuinely undermines long-term preservation commitments, or does it collapse into a disguised form of present bias? Take a committed position, with explicit reasons, rather than laying out the standard options and leaving me to choose; tell me precisely where the reasoning succeeds or fails, especially with respect to the analogy she draws to trusteeship in private law, and be willing to conclude that her argument either exposes a real gap in conventional intergenerational ethics or is ultimately unsustainable. Do not offer a bibliography or suggest further readings; I need a direct philosophical verdict on whether her reasoning holds.
