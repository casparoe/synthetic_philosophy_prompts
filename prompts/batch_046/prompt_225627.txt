I serve on a working group that issues advisory rulings on whether particular agents possess moral standing to blame others. Our published verdicts to date are these. First, we ruled that a public advocate who condemns carbon-intensive lifestyles while maintaining an equivalent high-emission personal lifestyle lacks standing, on the ground that the fault is of the same kind and remains active in the blamer. Second, we ruled that a former judge who once imposed a sentencing practice she now repudiates retains standing to blame current prosecutors for using that practice, but only on the condition that she explicitly acknowledges her own past participation; we treated genuine reform combined with acknowledgment as cleansing prior personal fault. Third, we ruled that a citizen whose taxes fund military actions she actively protests retains standing to blame soldiers for specific war crimes, on the ground that structural financial contribution paired with sustained dissent does not amount to the kind of personal complicity that undermines standing. Fourth, we treat the innocent bystander with no causal or institutional connection to a wrong as retaining full standing, which we use as our baseline.

We now face an unruly case. A legal scholar never personally engaged in corruption. However, she designed and continues to defend the overall value of a regulatory framework that, by her own admission, creates predictable and systematic incentives for the corruption she now condemns. She publicly blames the officials who exploited those incentives, but she does not repudiate the framework itself and in fact argues that its benefits outweigh these costs.

We must issue a recommendation that is consistent with our own track record. Identify the closest analog among our four precedents. State whether our precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome. Then, giving the recommendation that best coheres with our prior rulings, explain what principle we would be adopting if we rule that she retains standing, and what different principle we would be adopting if we rule that she lacks it. In particular, address whether institutional design—creating conditions that make a wrong rational—without personal execution of that wrong can constitute the kind of complicity that defeats standing in our framework.
