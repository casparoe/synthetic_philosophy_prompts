Right, I'm writing a feature on whether 'collapse' is even a useful word for historians, and I need to check my own gut before I commit to a line. Here are my verdicts on specific cases — tell me if there's a coherent principle behind them, or if I'm just being inconsistent.

The Bronze Age systems collapse around 1177 BCE: I'd call that a real collapse, not mere change, because writing and long-distance trade basically vanish for centuries and the political centres don't come back in anything like the same form.

The Classic Maya 'collapse' in the southern lowlands: transformation, not failure. Populations stick around, they just move north, and the whole idea of one civilisation snapping feels like we're projecting our love for dramatic endings onto them.

Easter Island: closer to collapse, but not because of the old ecological-suicide story. More that the social contract around the moai and the birdman cult seems to dissolve and isn't replaced by anything with comparable intergration, leaving a smaller, fragemented society.

The Western Roman Empire in the 5th century: definitely transformation. The western state machinery breaks up, but culture, religion and economic networks adapt; calling it collapse feels like taking the imperial bureaucracy's point of view too seriously.

But then there's this case I'm toying with — the Indus Valley decline around 1900 BCE, where we see de-urbanisation and script loss, but no clear invasion or climate trigger. Sometimes it feels like collapse, sometimes like a deliberate dispersal into smaller communities that are just invisible to archeologists now. I keep flip-flopping.

And a hypothetical I'm using to test the concept: if a modern nation-state lost ninety percent of its people to a pandemic but kept its legal code, digital backbone and language intact, is that collapse or just extreme change?

So — is there one principle that sorts all these? Or do I have to drop one of my verdicts? What does your principle say about the Indus case and the pandemic hypothetical?
