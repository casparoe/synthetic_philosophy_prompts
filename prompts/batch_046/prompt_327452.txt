I've been arguing with a friend about whether the principle of indifference can genuinely survive Bertrand's paradox, and I want a decisive verdict rather than the usual hedge that "it depends on parameterization." The familiar circle problem is clear enough: draw a chord at random and ask for the probability that it exceeds the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on whether you distribute endpoints uniformly on the circumference, distribute the midpoint uniformly over the interior area, or distribute radial distances uniformly, you obtain 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4. Standard treatments treat this as showing that continuous spaces lack a unique uniform distribution. But I'm drawn to a more subtle defense: rather than asking which parameterization is "natural," we might apply indifference only within equivalence classes of descriptions that carry exactly the same structural information about the generating process. On this view, the paradox doesn't reveal ambiguity in the principle; it reveals that different parameterizations implicitly encode different assumptions about how the chord was produced. The sharp counter-objection is that defining these equivalence classes requires prior knowledge of symmetries—the very thing the principle of indifference was supposed to provide from pure reasoning—so the defense risks circularity.
