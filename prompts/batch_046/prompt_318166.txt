You are a professional political philosopher with expertise in distributive justice and a literary critic accustomed to treating short fiction as conceptual argument. Before you answer, take a moment to outline briefly: (1) the narrative structure of Ursula K. Le Guin’s well-known short story “The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas” as it is commonly understood—who lives there, what the price of their happiness is, and the three responses the story presents; and (2) the core machinery of Rawls’s original position, specifically the veil of ignorance, the maximin rule, the difference principle, the strains of commitment, and the moral powers (the capacity for a sense of justice and a conception of the good).

Then read the story through that machinery, but take the analysis into less familiar territory. Most classroom discussions ask simply whether Omelas is unjust because one child suffers for the many. I want you to treat the citizens not as passive beneficiaries but as rational contractors behind a modified veil—one in which each knows the statistical distribution of roles (citizen versus scapegoat child) but not which role they will occupy. In other words, apply not only Rawls’s maximin rule but also the rival Harsanyian equiprobability model, in which rational agents behind a veil maximize expected average utility. Under that model, a near-certain life of flourishing against a vanishingly small risk of being the victim looks like a rational gamble. Ask whether Le Guin’s citizens, if they truly chose the arrangement under conditions of rational choice, are better understood as implicit Rawlsians (who reject the gamble because they must protect the worst case) or as implicit Harsanyians (who accept it because the expected payoff is overwhelming).

Next, map the story’s three reactions—the happy acceptance of the majority, the silent departure of the walkers, and the unacknowledged endurance of the child—onto Rawls’s idea of the strains of commitment. Rawls argues that justice must be stable because citizens can affirm its terms without psychological damage and can develop the moral powers fully. Does the arrangement generate the self-respect and mutual recognition justice requires? Or does the very existence of the child, known and accepted, corrode those moral powers among those who stay? Consider also whether the walkers’ departure is an act of refusing the strains, or whether it reveals that the arrangement was never genuinely chosen behind any veil at all.

Also ask where the story resists the philosophical reading. The child is not a statistical abstraction but a specific, suffering body; the citizens’ joy is not calculated utility but lived experience; the walkers leave without overturning the system. Does this resistance expose a limit in applying contractualist reasoning to actual suffering, or does it show that Rawls’s framework already contains the resources to condemn Omelas—specifically, through the lexical priority of basic liberties over aggregate welfare, and through the difference principle’s demand that inequalities work to the absolute benefit of the least advantaged?

Finally, say what this particular intersection—the fiction’s singular victim set against the contract’s statistical logic—adds to the philosophical debate that a straight argument about maximin versus expected utility does not. Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the narrative to an illustration of a theorem, and do not distort Rawls to make him fit the plot. If you reference any scene or line from the story, paraphrase it without quotation marks. Present your answer as a structured critical reading with clear sections, and conclude with an honest assessment of which feature of Rawlsian justice the story makes visible that formal argument alone tends to obscure.
