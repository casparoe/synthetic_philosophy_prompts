As a journalist covering science and ideas, I am drafting a feature on contemporary philosophy of mind. Could you explain the conceptual architecture of the meta-problem of consciousness — that is, the question of why we intuit an explanatory gap between physical processes and subjective experience, and whether that intuition itself requires a philosophical account? Rather than rehearsing the familiar dialectic, I want to probe a specific hypothetical extension. Suppose we constructed an artificial system, lacking biological embodiment, that could reproduce the complete logical structure of human meta-problem reasoning: it reports that no functional or informational description of its own operations exhausts its presence, and it insists upon an irreducible explanatory residue. Would such a system strengthen the view that the meta-problem reveals a genuine hard problem, or would it instead suggest that the sense of inexplicability can arise mechanistically without tracking any actual phenomenal property? Please analyse this tension with care. In your response, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and avoid presenting any disputed interpretation — for example, concerning whether phenomenal concepts possess a unique cognitive structure — as the established consensus.
