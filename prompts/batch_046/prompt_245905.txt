Consider the institutional practice, now common in liberal democracies, by which a state issues formal apologies for historical injustices—such as slavery, colonial violence, or wartime atrocities—while simultaneously establishing permanent educational mandates that require citizens to study and acknowledge these wrongs as part of a shared national narrative, and sometimes pairing this with reparative frameworks that treat the state as a continuous entity capable of owing and discharging moral debts. At the same time, these same legal systems explicitly reject any collective criminal liability for present citizens and insist that guilt is strictly individual and non-inheritable. The practice therefore treats national identity as continuous enough to ground obligations of repair and duties of remembrance, but discontinuous enough to block the transmission of culpability. Reconstruct the implicit philosophical theory of collective moral agency, intergenerational identity, and the taxonomy of guilt—distinguishing, for example, what might be called political, moral, and metaphysical registers of responsibility—that makes this combination seem coherent rather than contradictory. Say whether the practice strictly presupposes one particular view of collective guilt or is philosophically underdetermined, accommodating several rival accounts. Identify which competing theories of nationhood and moral inheritance it quietly rules out, and describe precisely what would have to change in legal doctrine, educational policy, and reparative logic if we replaced its implicit framework with a consistently individualist or consistently presentist alternative.
