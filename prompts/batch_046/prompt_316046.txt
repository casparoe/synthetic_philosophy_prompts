I sit on the review board for the Westside Behavioral Health Partnership, which usually just means signing off on data-sharing agreements I barely read, but last week a proposal landed that I've been turning over constantly. A research group wants to pilot an adaptive care-coordination tool in two community clinics, using inverse reinforcement learning—specifically a Boltzmann-rational reward model—to infer each patient's "true" treatment preferences from behavorial traces: missed appointments, portal logins, prescription pickup rates, even how long they sit in the waiting room. The system then nudges clinicians to align care plans with these inferred preferences rather then going by the intake interview alone.

I'm genuinely stuck on whether this makes philosophical sense. In IRL you assume behavior is noisy-optimal with respect to some hidden reward function, right? But in mental-health contexts, missed sessions might reflect a bus line getting cut, avoidance due to stigma, or adaptive preference formation—where people lower their expectations because the system's already failed them—rather then authentic choice. There's also a feedback loop: once clinicians nudge based on inferred preferences, patients adapt their behavior to the nudge, and the system starts learning its own shadow. I don't think I'm qualified to solve this, but the board needs to decide whether approval requires proof that the model can actually seperate revealed preference from revealed constraint.

My co-chair wants to reject outright; I lean toward approve-with-conditions if there's a validation framework, but I'm not sure. Can you draft our decision memo for Thursday—recommend approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, with reasoning both sides can actually respond to? Please take seriously the strongest objection: that the Boltzmann assumption treats structural deprivation and psychological avoidance as if they were just noisy preferences, and that without that separation the system risks encoding poverty and stigma as patient choice. Also please be honest about what's settled versus genuinely contested in the IRL and philosophy-of-preference literature—don't present a contested view, like that behavioral coherence equals preference authenticity in clinical populations, as if it's the consensus. And since I'm writing this without library access right now, don't include bibliographies or citation lists—just reason through it concretely enough that the researchers could write a direct response.
