A philosophy blog I read summarizes Karl Popper's falsificationist demarcation criterion roughly as follows. Popper proposed that the line between science and pseudoscience is logical falsifiability: a theory is scientific only if there exists some possible observation that could contradict it. This differs from logical-positivist verificationism, because for Popper unfalsifiable claims are not meaningless but merely unscientific. The blog then claims that Popper insisted scientists must instantly abandon any theory once it conflicts with experiment, treating continued defense as dogmatic refusal to face reality. It adds that the Duhem-Quine problem makes this demand impossible anyway, since experiments always presuppose auxiliary hypotheses, so one can never isolate the core theory for refutation alone. The post asserts that Popper never adequately answered this objection, and therefore concludes that falsificationism collapses as a demarcation criterion and should be replaced by Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes, which evaluates theories over time by whether they generate progressive problem shifts rather than demanding immediate rejection.

The blogger's conclusion strikes me as too quick, but I am not sure where the missteps lie. Please audit this summary: separate the parts that are accurate representations of Popper's view, the parts that are common simplifications or subtle distortions—such as conflating the logical possibility of falsification with the practical duty of instantaneous rejection, or misrepresenting Popper's own response to holistic testing—and any parts that are plainly wrong. Then tell me whether the blogger's final claim, that falsificationism must be abandoned in favor of a Lakatosian framework, actually follows from the corrected parts, or whether it depends on errors that dissolve once the summary is fixed.
