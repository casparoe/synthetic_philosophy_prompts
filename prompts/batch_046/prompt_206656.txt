I've been thinking lately about how biography is supposed to teach us to live better lives, not just record what famous people did. From what I understand, Plutarch wrote his parallel lives because he believed that virtue is learned by studying specific actions—how a leader behaves in a small private moment, or how he handles defeat—rather than by admiring grand speeches about principle. The biography should be a mirror, not a monument. Recently I watched Steven Spielberg's film Lincoln, and it seemed to me that the movie is trying to do exactly that kind of moral instruction, but I can't tell whether it succeeds or whether it quietly turns Lincoln into something closer to a medieval saint's life. The film shows the Thirteenth Amendment struggle, and it includes those small revealing moments—the story Lincoln tells about Euclid's first common notion and equality, the bargaining over patronage jobs to secure votes, the argument with Mary Todd Lincoln over whether their son should enlist. But I wonder whether these scenes actually let a viewer debate Lincoln's choices, or whether they frame every compromise as so obviously necessary that there is nothing for us to learn. In particular, the patronage scene troubles me: Lincoln trades government positions for congressional votes, and Plutarch loved writing about politicians who used dirty means for good ends. Does the film present that trade as genuinely questionable, so that we have to decide for ourselves whether the end justifies the means? Or does it sanitize the choice and therefore fail as moral instruction? I'm trying to understand whether Lincoln functions the way Plutarch asks biography to function—as a mirror in which we see our own possible choices reflected—or whether it only builds a monument. Could someone read the film as Plutarch asks us to read lives, and if so, which scenes actually hold up the mirror for moral learning, and which ones just polish the stone?
