hey, i'm putting together a handout for my ethics workshop (grad students, not specialists) about whether acting for the 'right reason' is a seperate kind of moral achievement. people keep mixing up 'did the right thing' with 'deserves praise for how they thought about it.' i want a decision tree — like a flowchart in words — that sorts someone into a position based on how they answer concrete case questions, not on which philosopher they claim to like. about 6 to 8 leaves.

start with one root question: is there any real gap between 'the act was right' and 'the agent is morally worthy for doing it'?

then branch using these concrete diagnostic cases (each node should be a yes/no or pick-one that a student can answer without knowing the labels):

- case A: a doctor saves a patient purely because she enjoys feeling needed.
- case B: a doctor saves a patient because she recognizes suffering and wants to end it, but she thinks morality is just social convention with no real authority.
- case C: a doctor saves a patient because she believes God commands saving lives, and she never asks whether God has good reasons.
- case D: a doctor saves a patient because she believes God commands it specificaly because it respects human dignity.

then inject this niche scenario i just made up: an AI system (or a human with severe brain damage) consistently reduces harm in ways that align with compassion, but its motivating state is only a compressed statistical correlation — no propositional belief about suffering, no concept of 'good', just opaque pattern-matching. call this the opaque-tracking case. use it as a branch point: does this kind of subpersonal, non-conceptual tracking confer any moral worth at all?

build the tree from the logical space first: list the combinations of answers the branches allow, then locate the actual positions in those slots. for any empty combination, say whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, don't paper over them.

each leaf gets a name (or 'unnamed') and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. don't include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists — this should work for someone with no web access. don't quote any real philosophers verbatim; paraphrase if you need to mention anyone. keep the node language casual and direct, and the handout is definatly not polished so a typo or two in your framing is fine.
