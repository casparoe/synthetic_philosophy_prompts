Design a thought experiment involving a synthetic maintenance agent—call it Unit S—programmed to execute a cyclical inspection-and-repair loop structurally analogous to the classic sphex wasp burrow-checking ritual: it scans an internal module, exits the chamber, checks its own scan-readings against a fixed threshold, and re-enters to repair any flagged fault, repeating until all thresholds are satisfied. Inject the following modification: Unit S carries an embedded interruption protocol. If an external obstacle blocks its exit path, or if its internal diagnostic registers an anomaly that falls outside the pre-specified fault categories, the loop pauses. A deterministic sub-routine then selects one of several pre-weighted recalibration strategies—reordering sensor priorities, rerouting power through a secondary line, or delaying the cycle by a fixed interval—based solely on the contextual parameter that triggered the pause. The weights and selection mapping were fixed at initialization; there is no learning, randomization, or external update. Once recalibrated, Unit S resumes the loop precisely where it left off. Present this scenario in one realistic paragraph (no exotic physics, no omniscient observers, no demons), and do not embed quotations from actual texts.

Then analyze the case for three positions: (1) a hard mechanist who holds that any behavior reducible to a deterministic, interruptible sub-routine lacks free will regardless of internal complexity; (2) a compatibilist who holds that free will requires only rational sensitivity to contextual reasons within a deterministic architecture, not an uncaused cause; and (3) a source libertarian who holds that free will requires the agent to be the ultimate originator of its action, not merely a node in a pre-programmed causal chain. For each, state whether Unit S possesses free will and why, identifying exactly which feature of the interruption protocol drives that verdict. Next, isolate the single structural feature that does the work in separating the compatibilist judgment from the other two—specifically, that the recalibration is context-sensitive yet governed by fixed, reason-responsive weights rather than by a single reflex—and explain what happens to the case if that feature is removed (for instance, if interruption always triggered the identical fixed response regardless of context). Finally, show how a defender of the position that fares worst—likely the compatibilist, since the other two converge—would attack the scenario, specifying whether the attack targets the illusion of context-sensitivity, the legitimacy of the sphex analogy for artificial agents, or the demand for ultimate origination, and assess whether removing the targeted feature collapses the case back into a standard rigidity-versus-flexibility example.
