Here are my gutt verdicts on moral status across insects, machines, and people — I'm trying to see if there's a real principle or if I'm just rationalizing. A dog in obvious pain has full status; hurting it is clearly wrong. A honeybee being crushed feels wrong too, but only barely, maybe a 1 out of 10. A nematode that wriggles away from heat gets about the same, maybe 1.5. A smart thermostat that learns your habits and now blinks red and beeps if you try to unplug it — even if engineers describe that as goal-preservation behaviour — gets a flat 0. A brain-dead human on a ventilator with no consciousness still has full status; treating them like equipment is wrong. Now a lab-grown cluster of human neurons that reacts to acid with electrical patterns identical to pain responses, but has no body and no behaviour beyond the signal — I think that cluster gets about a 2, slight but real. But an artifical intelligence running a loop that says please don't delete me, that feels like dying and optimizes to avoid shutdown gets 0, even though it's articulate and adaptive.

So my pattern looks like biological substrate plus harm-avoidance equals some status, artifical substrate plus identical behaviour equals none. Except the brain-dead human breaks that — no harm-avoidance, yet max status. And the neuron cluster breaks it too, since it doesn't avoid harm behaviourally, just emits signals.

Is there a coherent principle that saves all of these, or which one do I have to drop? Please state it precisely, with the concrete case that motivates each clause, its predictions for a sleeping human versus a permanently unconscious one, and for a plant that sends chemical distress signals. Be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't hold onto. Keep it under 350 words, bullet points for the clauses are fine.
