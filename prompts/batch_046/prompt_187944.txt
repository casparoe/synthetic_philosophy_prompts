I am giving the opening remarks at a workshop marking roughly two centuries since John Stuart Mill began his career at the East India Company. The room is split evenly between historians of British India, who know the administrative archives in detail, and normative political theorists who have read On Liberty but have never seriously engaged with Mill's writings on colonial administration. The occasion is analytical rather than celebratory: we are examining whether Mill's defense of East India Company rule can be reconstructed as a coherent theory of non-electoral legitimacy, or whether it collapses into an unprincipled exception that undermines his liberal commitments. I need about five minutes of continuous prose—no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no lists of any kind—just an uninterrupted address rendered in full sentences, as if spoken from carefully prepared notes. The philosophical idea must do structural work, not decorative work: it should determine what I am permitted to claim and what I am forced to concede. Specifically, I want you to present Mill's position as turning on an epistemic condition rather than on a crude racial or civilizational hierarchy. The claim is that Mill believed representative government requires a certain kind of shared knowledge between ruler and ruled, and that in India this condition was absent in a way that made direct representation impossible; therefore authority could be delegated to an administrative proxy only if that proxy maintained a form of situated knowledge that remained answerable to the welfare of the governed. Then inject and work through one somewhat niche framing: treat the distinction between governing-for and governing-with not as a binary opposition but as a gradient measured by the reversibility of epistemic delegation—meaning the speed and reliability with which the proxy's authority can be revoked when its knowledge fails. Use this gradient to structure the speech's central tension. If the delegation is genuinely reversible, then Mill's colonial defense and his liberalism are internally consistent, though they purchase that consistency at a specific moral cost that the audience should feel rather than merely register. If the delegation is irreversible in practice—if there is no credible mechanism by which the governed could withdraw authority once the proxy's epistemic claims are falsified—then the coherence collapses, and the remarks must say so plainly, without retreating into archival apology or biographical charity. The address must be accessible enough that the theorists are not lost in administrative detail, yet precise enough that the historians do not wince at anachronism or oversimplification. It should contain no bibliographies, no citation strings, no page references, and no reading lists; the deliverable is the conceptual architecture exposed in real time. Write in the voice of a longtime reader of rationalist forums and blogs: formal, polished, somewhat in the weeds, respectful of disciplinary differences, and entirely in continuous prose.
