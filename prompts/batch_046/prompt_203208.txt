I serve on a parliamentary defense review board for a small democratic state that has maintained a purely professional volunteer force for three decades but is now weighing proposals to reintroduce selective conscription while formally codifying armed neutrality commitments partly informed by Swiss, Swedish, and Finnish precedents. In our recent sessions, colleagues keep treating conscription and neutrality as purely budgetary or strategic choices, but I am convinced they involve deeper conceptual commitments about the relationship between citizenship, coercive state power, and non-participation in collective security frameworks; I was discussing this with a constitutional scholar last week and realized we lack any operational translation of these commitments. I therefore need a reusable decision procedure—essentially a structured checklist in sequential stages with explicit stopping rules—that converts the philosophical and institutional logic of armed neutrality and conscription legitimacy into practical evaluation steps that a non-philosopher staffer could run on any proposed restructuring bill within an hour, with clear sign-off points indicating whether the recommendation is staff-level or must be returned to elected members as a value judgment.

The procedure should begin with definitional filters distinguishing neutrality-preserving from neutrality-eroding force structures, proceed through legitimacy conditions assessing whether any draft mechanism respects civic equality and avoids selective burden-shifting, and conclude with operational coherence tests examining whether the manpower model actually sustains the neutrality posture rather than undermining it through hidden alliance dependencies or training interdependencies. At each stage you must specify what counts as a sufficient answer, mark where the question is irreducibly normative and must be handed back to the committee rather than resolved technically, and include a hard stop that rejects proposals failing structural neutrality regardless of strategic merit. Please construct this with no more than six ordered stages, then apply it to three concrete proposals: a Swiss-style universal militia with minimal standing forces; a Swedish-style selective service with rapid professionalization tracks; and a Finnish-style broad reserve model with formal neutrality maintained despite extensive cooperative training arrangements that stop just short of treaty obligations. I want the procedure to yield an unwelcome answer for at least one proposal—showing where conceptual consistency forces rejection despite clear strategic appeal—and to state explicitly what kinds of hybrid manpower models fall outside the framework’s adjudicative capacity without further normative commitments about the proper scope of citizenship obligation.
