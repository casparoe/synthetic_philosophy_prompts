I'm taking a course on intuitionistic logic and constructivism and my study grp got stuck on this argument we cooked up over coffee. It feels like it proves way too much but I can't see the leak.

(1) In intuitionistic logic, asserting A ∨ B requires that I actually have a proof of A or a proof of B — there's no hidden middle ground I'm just ignoring. (2) Negation works the same way: ¬A just means I've shown A leads to contradiction, not that A is "false" in some classical realm. (3) Now apply this to ordinary empirical claims. Take "there is microbial life on Mars right now" (call it L). I don't have a constructive proof of L, and I also don't have a proof that L leads to contradiction. (4) So by (1), I can't assert L ∨ ¬L. I have to stay silent in a stronger sense — not just "I don't know," but "the disjunction literally isn't available to me." (5) But the exact same thing applies to "my friend is in the other room" or "the sun will rise tomorrow." For almost every claim outside immediate direct experience, I'm in the same boat. (6) So if intuitionism isn't just a math trick but a serious logic, it forces me into radical silence about the external world. (7) That can't be right — constructivists talk about physics and other people all the time — so one step must be wrong.

But which one? Is the mistake extending (1) beyond mathematics? Or is there a hidden way intuitionists assert disjunctions about the world without full proofs? My friend thinks the error is in (4) — that intuitionistic "silence" isn't skepticism, so there's no problem — but I feel like that just renames the issue. I'd rather keep the idea that intuitionists genuinely reject excluded middle, but I don't want to end up saying they can't talk about Mars. Where does the argument slip, and what does fixing it cost?
