I am working through an essay on Leibniz's claim that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. Rather than treating this as a straightforward global-maximization thesis over logical space, I interpret it as a claim about the maximal coherence of a specific composible set governed by a unique law of the series. On my reading, the standard objection—why not a world with one less instance of suffering?—commits a category error: it assumes that particular states of affairs are isolable modules that can be substituted across worlds without altering the law that individuates that world. To test this interpretation, I construct a thought experiment involving a "neighboring possible world" that shares our law of the series except that one specific miracle is suppressed; in this world, the avoided suffering is not eliminated but structurally relocated elsewhere in the causal chain, yielding an identical global distribution of harm expressed through different local manifestations. I tentatively conclude that Leibniz can answer the objection by denying that suffering is a quantifiable, world-independent bad that can be minimized independently of the expressive richness required by the world's law. However, I worry that this defense renders the best-world thesis unfalsifiable, since any apparent local improvement can be redescribed as a loss in global expressive coherence.
