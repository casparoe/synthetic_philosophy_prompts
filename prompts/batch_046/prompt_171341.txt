You are an expert political philosopher and conceptual analyst with experience in well-being economics. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the key conceptual distinctions involved — especially between life evaluation and experienced well-being, between subjective satisfaction and objective-list theories, and between measuring conditions versus measuring mental states. Then reason step by step through the arguments.

In my school's Model UN club, two members — Priya and Marcus — got stuck arguing about whether countries should try to top the World Happiness Report. Priya said the ranking is built mainly around the Cantril ladder, which asks people to rate their lives on a scale; she argued this captures life evaluation shaped by adaptation and local reference groups rather than actual quality of life. She added that because the ladder prompts a narrative, whole-life judgment, it can reward societies that encourage optimistic storytelling about one's life rather than societies where people genuinely flourish day to day.

Marcus replied that the Report combines that ladder with data on social support, freedom, and corruption, so Finland and Denmark rank high for institutional reasons, not statistical artifacts. He said that even if adaptation exists, persistent cross-national gaps suggest real differences, and that for a government to ignore citizens' own reported satisfaction — substituting philosophers' theories for people's judgments — is paternalistic.

Priya answered that combining metrics doesn't fix the deeper conceptual problem: the index implicitly assumes subjective preference satisfaction is what matters. She pointed out that on an objective-list view — where well-being includes meaningful achievement and practical reasoning regardless of reported satisfaction — a country could score highly while failing citizens in dimensions the ranking cannot detect. Marcus countered that no aggregate metric is perfect, and that imposing an external objective list risks authoritarianism; democratic legitimacy, he said, requires weighing citizens' own evaluations.

Neither would concede. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: where is Priya right, where is Marcus right, and what are both missing? Specifically, address whether these rankings measure happiness in any philosophically defensible sense, and whether a state should treat climbing them as a policy objective. Distinguish empirical disputes from conceptual ones, and say what a policy genuinely aimed at well-being — rather than at the metric — would have to do differently.
