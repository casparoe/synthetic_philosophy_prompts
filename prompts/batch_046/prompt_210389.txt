I am writing from a position of recent personal urgency: over the past months I have been advising a close relative on a medical choice where the clinical literature offers not a point estimate but a spread—success probabilities reported across studies running from roughly thirty percent to sixty percent, with no consensus on how to weight them. Watching the consultations unfold, I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern that I cannot name and that does not quite match the standard discussions of ambiguity aversion I have encountered. The pattern has two phases that are kept rigorously separate. In the first phase, a physician or advocate cites the full spread to defeat any charge of overconfidence: if an interlocutor warns that the treatment might fail, the response is that the range explicitly contains lower outcomes, so no single prediction has been asserted. In the second phase, however, once the defensive work is done, the same speaker applies an unstated resolution rule—often a worst-case minimization, sometimes an unstated averaging—to select one specific course of action, presenting it as if it followed naturally from the range rather than from an independent criterion. What makes the move distinctive is not the use of a range, nor the use of a decision rule, but the way the range is used to immunize the conclusion against criticism that should apply equally to the resolution rule itself. I have seen an analogous structure in climate-risk assessments, where model ensembles generate spread predictions that are invoked to show humility, but then a specific damage function is applied without defending the choice of that function against the other possibilities inside the ensemble. Does this two-phase structure—interval-as-shield followed by unstated-precision—have an established name in the literature on imprecise probabilities or decision theory? I am not asking about the sure-thing principle, about E-admissibility, or about ordinary equivocation between a broad and a narrow claim; I am asking whether there is a recognized concept for the strategic separation of a belief-interval's defensive function from its resolution function. If not, I would welcome a precise definition and boundary conditions that distinguish it from straightforward maximin reasoning on one side and from mere rhetorical bait-and-switch on the other.
