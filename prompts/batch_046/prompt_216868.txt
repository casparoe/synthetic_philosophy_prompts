Two colleagues—one in ethics, one in classics—were debating whether Plutarch’s Parallel Lives remain useful for moral education once we treat them as partly hagiographic. (1) The ethicist argued that smoothing inconsistencies and magnifying virtues is pedagogically necessary: moral learning through biography works by aspirational identification, not psychological accuracy. You learn courage not from a forensic portrait of Alcibiades but from seeing his best moments crystallized into an imitable pattern. (2) The classicist replied that this mistakes moral theater for moral formation. If the portrait is smoothed, the reader practices admiration rather than judgment, risking performance of virtue rather than its integration. She said modern biography’s inclusion of failure—think of how recent accounts treat Lincoln’s melancholy or Churchill’s strategic errors—teaches moral resilience because the exemplar stays within reach. (3) The ethicist countered that modern “warts-and-all” biography collapses exemplarity into mere relatability: the figure becomes too human to be exemplary, turning moral learning into therapeutic self-acceptance. He noted that Plutarch’s Greek-Roman pairing already builds in comparison and judgment, so the smoothing isn’t naive but a deliberate framing device. (4) The classicist said pairing only doubles the problem, creating paired polished mirrors rather than critical distance. She introduced the idea of “exemplarity traps”: copying the surface traits of heroes without grasping the situational demands of virtue, which hagiographic omission makes more likely by hiding the costs and failures that shaped the virtue.

Please adjudicate move by move—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with brief justification—and identify what both miss about how Plutarch’s anachronistic cross-cultural pairing might alter whether smoothing aids or undermines moral learning.
