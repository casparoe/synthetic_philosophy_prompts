Here are my judgments about when individual sovereignty over a personal choice should override collective or interpersonal preferences, and I want to know whether they can be unified.

Case 1: A wishes to read a controversial philosophical text in private. B, A's partner, regards it as morally corrupting and prefers that A not read it. I judge that A's choice should prevail; B's disapproval, however sincere, does not confer authority over A's private mental life.

Case 2: A wishes to practice percussion instruments at 3 a.m. in a shared residential building. The other residents prefer quiet sleep. Here I judge that the collective preference should prevail; A's artistic autonomy does not extend to imposing direct sensory interference on neighbors.

Case 3: A wears a provocative political symbol in a shared workplace. B finds it deeply offensive and asks A to remove it. I judge that A should remain free to wear it; B's psychological discomfort, absent direct coercion or physical harm, is insufficient to override A's expressive choice, parallel to Case 1.

Case 4: A adopts a diet and lifestyle that B, A's long-term partner, regards as a betrayal of shared commitments they explicitly undertook together. B asks A to revert. Here I feel, against my inclination in Cases 1 and 3, that B has genuine standing and A owes accommodation—not because B is physically harmed, but because the choice erodes a jointly constituted practical identity. This verdict sits uneasily with the others.

Case 5: Consider two individuals, Prude and Lewd, and a single controversial book. Prude's ranking is: neither reads best; if one reads, he himself reads rather than Lewd; both read worst. Lewd's ranking is: both read best; if one reads, he himself reads rather than Prude; neither worst. I judge that each should have sovereign authority over whether he personally reads it, even though this minimal liberal rule can produce an outcome—say, both read—that Prude ranks worst, and even though there exists an allocation both might agree on if they could bargain. The individual right seems to block Pareto-improving coordination.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps distinguishing kinds of reasons others may invoke, or defining when a domain is genuinely personal—that accommodates all five? Or must I abandon Case 4 to preserve consistency with 1, 3, and 5? If the principle saves Case 4, does it implausibly license partners to control each other's private reading and dress? And what should it say about a case I have not considered: A refuses a life-saving medical intervention, and B, as A's legal guardian, demands it be administered?
