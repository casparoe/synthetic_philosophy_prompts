Write the full list of desiderata that any adequate theory of commemorative legitimacy for defeated national causes must satisfy. I am not asking for a literature review; I want the specification itself, oriented toward guiding institutional practice and public justification.

The theory must handle at least these four cases without collapsing them into one another: the American Confederacy, commemorated in parts of the United States as regional heritage despite being a slaveholding secession now widely repudiated; Masada, commemorated in Israeli national narrative as heroic resistance despite being a collective suicide that ended a failed revolt; the Kosovo Field narrative, commemorated in Serbian national memory as sacred martyrdom despite being a decisive military defeat; and Dunkirk, commemorated in British public memory as a moral victory despite being a tactical retreat and evacuation.

For each desideratum, state the phenomenon or distinction it protects and why it is required. In particular, the list must include: (i) an explanation of why some defeated causes become politically toxic while others become foundational, without reducing the difference to who won the war or to which group controls textbooks and monuments; (ii) a firm distinction between honoring the suffering or courage of the defenders and endorsing the political aims of the cause, together with a mechanism that prevents the two from collapsing in practice; (iii) a resolution of what I will call intertemporal constituency — the fact that commemorative rituals often treat present citizens as heirs to the defeated cause, even when those citizens reject its aims and bear no causal or moral responsibility for its actions — and whether this inheritance relation can be dissolved without destroying the commemorative value altogether; (iv) consistency with the unambiguous historical fact that the defeat was real, even when the narrative transforms it into victory, sacred loss, or moral triumph; (v) an account of why societies invest institutional resources in preserving some lost-cause monuments and suppressing others, addressing questions of tractability and democratic legitimacy rather than symbolic coherence alone.

Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps — for example, whether the theory must yield a clear decision procedure for monument removal, or whether it must remain neutral between republican and liberal conceptions of citizenship. Identify any subset of requirements that appears jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, if demanding that commemorative value strictly track the moral status of the original cause conflicts with demanding that present citizens honor defenders without endorsing aims — and say so explicitly; that tension is the part I most want to see.

Then, briefly, score three broad approaches against the list: a moral-clarity view that ties commemorative legitimacy to the justice of the cause; a functionalist view that ties it to present social cohesion; and an inheritance view that treats commemoration as an intergenerational moral obligation. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
