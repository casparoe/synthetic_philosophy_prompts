I'm giving the retirement toast for my old PhD supervisor, Dr. Elena Voss, next Friday — she's spent thirty-odd years in metaphysics and the philosophy of physics, mostly tearing apart relationalist accounts of space with symmetric-world cases. Roughly half the room will be philosophers who know her work inside out; the other half are family, neighbours, and old friends who mostly think she studies "clouds or something." I want the speech to actually use a philosophical idea as its backbone, not just decoration. Specifically, I want to centre it on Black's spheres — but on Elena's own variant, where she imagines three perfectly identical spheres arranged in an equilateral triangle in an otherwise empty universe, and argues that even total symmetry doesn't let you collapse them into a single object without losing real relational differences. The point should shape what I can and can't say: I mustn't claim she "solved" the identity of indiscernibles once and for all, but I can use the triangle image to talk about how she always insisted that being indistinguishable isn't the same as being replaceable — which, if I'm honest, is also why her students and family all feel irreplaceable to her in slightly different ways.

Please write about five minutes of material, casual but not sloppy, and make sure it works for both audiences without lecturing either. Oh, and please write it in British English — I definatly need that, and I don't want any bibliography or citation list, just the speech itself.
