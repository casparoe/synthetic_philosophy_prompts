I am drafting a novel in which a librarian named Mary has spent her entire life inside a grayscale archive, having memorized every spectroscopic analysis of crimson, every linguistic taxonomy of redness, and every poem ever composed about sunset, yet never once having encountered chromatic light. In conversation with a fellow writer, I invoked her case to probe a maxim that seems to circulate in our workshops like an unexamined axiom: "What you haven't lived through, you haven't truly known." My interlocutor seized upon it to insist that no archival study, however exhaustive, could bridge the gap between Mary's descriptions and the actual feeling of seeing scarlet for the first time, treating the matter as philosophically settled. Before I allow this line to drive the architecture of my plot, I need to understand precisely what labor it might be performing. Could you lay out the distinct, precise theses hiding behind that slogan—separating modest epistemic claims about skill or justification from metaphysical claims about phenomenal properties, distinguishing versions compatible with physicalism from versions that require irreducible acquaintance, and identifying which readings actually entail that Mary lacks a specific form of knowledge rather than merely a practical familiarity? For each interpretation, indicate whether it genuinely supports the judgment that my archival Mary is missing something essential, whether it applies exclusively to qualitative experience or extends to abstract knowledge more broadly, and which version my fellow writer was likely invoking when he used the phrase to close debate. I want the distinctions arranged as a clear menu I can bring back to my writing desk, so that the line in my story functions as precise philosophical machinery rather than atmospheric ornament.
