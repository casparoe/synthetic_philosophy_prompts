everyone in the climate policy debates i follow keeps using this analogy: discounting future welfare is like running compound interest backwards. a dollar of wellbeing in 2100 is just smaller in present-value terms, not because we hate our grandkids but because capital grows and opportunities accumulate. they use it to defend putting a 4-5% pure rate of time preference into the social cost of carbon, which makes long-term climate damage look cheap today.

i want to know how far that analogy actually holds. can you map the correspondance -- present value, interest rate, principal, maturity date -- onto welfare, generations, and time? then tell me where it breaks: does welfare actually "grow" like capital? is there an analog to reinvestment? what about the fact that people don't actually experience utility with compound returns?

also, there's this other idea i came across about treating generations like members of an intertemporal risk pool, where the right discount factor depends on covariance with global growth rather than pure impatience. does injecting that idea break the analogy further, or does it actually rescue it? and if the compound-interest picture collapses once you separate pure time preference from the growth-rate component, does the policy conclusion -- that we should discount distant climate damage heavily -- survive, or was the analogy doing all the work?
