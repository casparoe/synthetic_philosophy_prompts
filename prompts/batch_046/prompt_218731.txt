I am a novelist constructing a narrative centered on a municipality divided into four districts defined not by geography but by trade and economic priority. In my plot, residents of each district are polled separately about two proposed reforms, Proposal X and Proposal Y, and within every district a clear majority favors X. Yet when the town council aggregates the votes across all districts, Y prevails. I understand that disparities in district size can produce such reversals, but my scenario includes an additional complication: the districts are constituted by a preliminary ballot in which citizens rank their economic priorities, and those priorities shift depending on which reform is expected to pass. Consequently, if Y appears likely, more citizens adopt the economic profile that places them in District C, thereby altering both the district's composition and the aggregate tally in a circular fashion. This means the categories used for aggregation are endogenous to the outcome being measured, not fixed background conditions.

I am aware of the well-known statistical phenomenon in which group-level trends reverse in the aggregate, yet the configuration I have invented couples that reversal with outcome-dependent categorization. Does this specific arrangement—aggregate preference reversal driven by self-selecting, preference-contingent groups—have an established name in statistics, social-choice theory, or philosophy of collective decision-making? How does it differ structurally from the standard reversal case, and what conceptual resources exist for analyzing collective preferences when the grouping of agents is itself responsive to the preference being expressed? Please provide your answer in American English, and feel free to coin a precise term with clear boundary conditions if no established one exists.
