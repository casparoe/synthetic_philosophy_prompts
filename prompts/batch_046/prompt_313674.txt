I am a senior in a joint philosophy and cognitive science program, and for my seminar on conceptual architectures of mind I have been assigned to draft opening remarks for a real-world occasion in which one of our course frameworks must do genuine structural work rather than serve as decorative vocabulary. The occasion is the opening session of a two-day interdisciplinary workshop, “Collaborative Cognition and Its Limits,” hosted jointly by our philosophy department and the university’s AI research institute; the audience is deliberately mixed—graduate students in philosophy and neuroscience, computer scientists building multi-agent systems, and invited faculty from fields with no background in philosophy of mind. The framework I want to center is the taxonomy of cognitive architectures known as Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures—not as an evolutionary narrative about species, but as a typology of how information is generated, tested, retained, and revised inside any functioning system, whether biological, artificial, or institutional.

Please write the opening remarks, approximately eight hundred words. The taxonomy should genuinely constrain what can and cannot be said: for example, I should not be able to celebrate individual “Gregorian” theoretical breakthroughs without simultaneously acknowledging the “Darwinian” baseline of institutional knowledge and peer review that makes them possible; I should not be able to treat collaborative AI systems as purely “Skinnerian” optimizers without confronting whether they can ever become “Popperian” or “Gregorian”; and I should not be able to reduce scientific collaboration to any single kind without misrepresenting the workshop’s purpose. The remarks need to welcome participants from disparate fields without losing the philosophers, set a serious but not solemn tone, and avoid presenting the framework as the only valid lens—while still letting it shape the agenda and the questions we pursue over the next two days. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the speech should stand entirely on its own for listeners who may not have direct access to the original texts.
