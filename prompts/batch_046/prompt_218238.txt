I've been writing a scene where a charcter needs to hide someone from a murderer knocking at the door, and I kept running into this pattern in how people talk about Kant's view. They all agree lying is wrong — even to save a life — but then they immediately say you'd just mislead the murderer with a technically true statement, or stay silent, and they treat that as morally totally different, even though the guy still finds the victim. It's like there's this hard shell around literal falsehood: any deception that doesn't contain an explicit false proposition gets classified as 'not lying' and therefore not violating the duty, no matter how much harm it causes. I've seen the same move in arguments about everyday relationships — someone witholds crucial information, or frames a true fact so it gives the wrong impression, and when called out they say 'well at least I didn't lie.' Is there an established name for this specific asymmetry, where the prohibition on lying functions as a kind of moral pressure valve that redirects all deception into technically truthful forms? I know about mental reservation and Kant's own distinction between lying and withholding, but those feel like parts of the mechanism rather than the name for the pattern itself. If there's no term, can you name and define it precisely, and say where its edges are — for instance, does it cover cases where the speaker genuinely believes the literal content but knows it will be misinterpreted?
