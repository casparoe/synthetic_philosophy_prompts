I'm currently taking a philosophy of mind module that examines whether emotions are best understood as judgments, value-laden perceptions, or bodily feelings. For my essay, I'd like to read Virginia Woolf's Mrs Dalloway through this debate, focussing on Clarissa Dalloway's preparations for her party and Septimus Warren Smith's traumatic flashbacks. Rather than mapping each character onto a single theory, I want to explore whether the novel supports a more distributed picture: that emotional states can be partly 'delegated' to social rituals and material arrangements—Clarissa's flower-buying and room-arranging functioning as an affective scaffold that suppresses explicit judgment without removing feeling altogether. Where does Woolf's modernist technique resist reducing emotion to judgment, perception, or raw feeling? And does the text suggest something that standard philosophical arguments miss—perhaps that the boundary between having an emotion and performing one is itself unstable? I'd appreciate a reading that takes both the philosophical positions (Nussbaum, Solomon, Prinz, the James-Lange tradition) and the novel's major scenes seriously, without reducing either to an illustration of the other.
