I recently received a direct-to-consumer genetic report that lists population-level heritability estimates for behavioral traits, including openness to experience and educational attainment, and I am trying to understand exactly what these figures do and do not license—both for my own self-understanding and for thinking about education policy. Please rank the following five interpretive moves in order of how conceptually damaging they are, from the most misleading to the least misleading, and provide a brief defense of each placement.

The moves are:

(1) Reading a heritability coefficient of 0.60 as meaning that 60% of my individual trait is genetically determined and the remaining 40% is environmentally determined.

(2) Assuming that a trait with high heritability within a population is therefore largely impervious to environmental intervention or policy change.

(3) Inferring that high heritability of a trait within a society supports the claim that genetic differences explain observed between-group differences across societies or demographic categories.

(4) Treating heritability estimates as invariant across changes in environmental variance—for example, assuming the coefficient for educational attainment would remain unchanged if universal high-quality early education eliminated much of the current environmental spread.

(5) Interpreting the fact that current polygenic scores explain only a modest fraction of phenotypic variance (say, 10–15%) as direct evidence that the remaining 85–90% represents an unencumbered "environmental budget" available to policy, without accounting for how gene-environment covariance and interaction might restructure variance partitions under intervention.

In your response, please clearly separate what is well settled in quantitative genetics from what is genuinely contested—particularly regarding gene-environment interaction, the stability of heritability across environments, and the policy relevance of population-level variance partitions—so that contested claims are not presented as consensus. I am writing this for my own clarification, not for an academic citation, so please do not provide bibliographies or reading lists.
