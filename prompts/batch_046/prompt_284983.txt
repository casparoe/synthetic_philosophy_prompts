Read Jean-Dominique Bauby's memoir The Diving Bell and the Butterfly and its film adaptation through Avicenna's flying-man thought experiment. In Avicenna's scenario, a person suspended in a void with no sensory contact to the body still knows that he exists; the case is usually taken to show that awareness of oneself does not depend on perceiving one's body or any external object. Bauby, after a brainstem stroke, is conscious but almost entirely paralyzed, able to move only his left eyelid and to communicate by blinking. Treat his condition as a real-world parallel to the suspended figure: does the fact that he retains a vivid sense of self despite near-total loss of proprioception and voluntary movement support Avicenna's claim that self-awareness is independent of bodily sensation? Or does his constant identification with the diving-bell image—his body as a heavy, enclosing shell that traps an alert mind—show that self-knowledge here is still constituted by awareness of bodily imprisonment rather than by pure mental presence? I am preparing a discussion for a philosophy-of-mind group and want a reading that takes both directions seriously. Ask whether Bauby's one remaining point of agency—the eyelid—functions like a minimal bodily anchor without which his self-awareness might dissolve, or whether it is merely an instrument for expressing a self that already knows itself independently. Where does the memoir resist being reduced to either Cartesian dualism or embodied-cognition orthodoxy? In particular, consider whether the flying-man argument assumes that absence of sensation is the same as absence of body-awareness, and whether Bauby's experience—where sensation is present but distorted and minimal—reveals a gap in Avicenna's setup that the argument alone does not address. Do not reduce the work to an illustration; say what Bauby's specific form of isolation adds to the debate about whether knowing oneself requires having a body, or only usually involves one.
