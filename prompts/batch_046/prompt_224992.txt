My colleague, who writes on Buddhist metaphysics and personal identity, has defended the following verdicts across earlier cases: in Nāgasena’s chariot dialogue she denies any self over and above the aggregates; in the candle-flame analogy for rebirth she holds that the new flame is neither identical to nor wholly other than the old, allowing causal continuity without identity; when assessing reports of an arhat recalling past lives with markedly different traits, she treats them as transmission of mental formations rather than proof that one person persisted; on the death of an arhat entering parinirvāṇa she insists there is neither a surviving stream of consciousness nor an annihilated self, only complete cessation; and in severe dementia where no psychological continuity remains, she denies that the same conventional person is present, despite bodily persistence.

Consider now a case drawn from the literature on the mind-made body (manomayakāya): an accomplished meditator generates a fully functioning mind-made form at a distance while her physical body remains in deep meditation; the projected form carries all her memories and psychological traits, and upon reabsorption she retains unified memories of both locations. Would my colleague judge the mind-made body to be the same conventional person, a separate stream analogous to rebirth, or something else? I am less interested in the bare answer than in identifying the rule she appears to be running, at least one rival rule that accommodates her earlier verdicts equally well, and the precise further case that would discriminate between them. If her track record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, set out what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice.
