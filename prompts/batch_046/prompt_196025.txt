I've been thinking about whether an economy could rationally allocate resources without using market prices to signal scarcity, especially now that computing power can process enormous amounts of local data almost instantly. Rather than asking which side won a historical debate, I want to know what any serious theory of non-market calculation actually has to deliver. Write the desiderata: which practical allocation problems it must solve correctly—such as distributing limited lithium and cobalt between battery manufacturers and other industries during a supply disruption, or adjusting factory output when consumer preferences shift faster than production cycles—without relying on profit signals. What conceptual distinctions must it maintain, for example between knowledge that can be gathered centrally and knowledge that only exists in dispersed form, or between computing in physical quantities versus in unified value terms? Note any pair of these demands that appear incompatible—perhaps the speed required for local adaptation undermines the coherence needed for national-scale planning. Then briefly evaluate three proposals against the list: the view that dispersed local knowledge makes central planning impossible regardless of computing power; the trial-and-error adjustment model that simulates price signals administratively; and the proposal that modern distributed algorithms could aggregate preferences directly without either markets or central planners. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on attributing positions to specific thinkers.
