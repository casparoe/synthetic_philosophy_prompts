I'm taking a seminar on moral agency and have reconstructed Strawson's Basic Argument with an epistemic twist that seems to render it self-undermining, but I cannot locate the flaw. Here is the chain:

(1) Moral responsibility for an action requires responsibility for the mental states that produce it.
(2) Responsibility for any mental state requires that it was produced by a prior mental state for which the agent is already responsible.
(3) This yields an infinite regress with no first, self-originating mental state.
(4) Therefore no one is responsible for any mental state, and hence for no action.
(5) But the argument applies reflexively: if sound, I am not responsible for believing (1)-(4) or for judging that moral responsibility is impossible.
(6) Rational acceptance of an argument, however, requires that I can take ownership of the belief it produces; otherwise there is no normative distinction between my accepting it and being causally compelled to utter it.
(7) So if the Basic Argument is valid, it eliminates the conditions for its own rational endorsement.
(8) Thus it proves too much: it undermines not only moral responsibility but the possibility of being answerable for one's judgments.

My instructor suggested the problem might lie in how (2) treats mental-state causation, or in the inference from (4) to (5), but I am unsure. Which step should I reject to block the reflexive collapse without reinstating the kind of causa sui that Strawson rejects? And if I must keep the intuition that we are not self-creating, what does abandoning the other premises cost?
