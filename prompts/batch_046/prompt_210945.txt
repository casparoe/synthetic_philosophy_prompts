I cover conceptual research and philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently drafting a feature on how philosophers adjudicate the reliability of intuition pumps—those compressed scenarios designed to elicit immediate judgments about identity, consciousness, or moral value. In the course of my reporting I have been tracking the published verdicts of Professor Renata Cole, who directs a small working group on conceptual stability at an independent research institute. Over the past eighteen months she has evaluated eight well-known pumps. Her rulings, in order of publication, are as follows: she treats the Ship of Theseus as legitimate, arguing that it isolates a genuine ambiguity in persistence conditions without importing confounding variables; she judges Searle’s Chinese Room to be misleading, on the grounds that the person-in-the-room framing bundles syntax with an unstated anthropomorphic intuition that collapses once distributed processing is made explicit; she accepts the standard switch-version of the Trolley Problem as legitimate but rejects the footbridge variant as misleading, because the latter ties moral wrongness to physical proximity rather than to the structural feature of agency; she regards Jackson’s Mary as legitimate; she accepts Nozick’s Experience Machine as legitimate; she dismisses the Utility Monster as misleading, since it bundles utility aggregation with an unrealistic scaling assumption; and she accepts Rawls’s Veil of Ignorance as a legitimate procedural pump, provided it is presented without biographical framing.

These verdicts are jointly consistent with at least two plausible implicit theories. One is a Conceptual Boundary Preservation view: a pump is legitimate exactly when it isolates the target conceptual boundary and does not smuggle in an irrelevant structural feature—proximity, infinite scale, hidden syntax—while it is misleading when such a feature is present. The other is a Narrative Transmission Stability view: a pump is legitimate when its core intuition survives re-description, paraphrase, or cross-contextual transmission; if the judgment depends on a fragile framing that dissolves under translation or retelling, the pump is artifactual.

Next week Cole is scheduled to evaluate a new proposal I have been asked to report on: the Cascading Framing Pump, devised by a cognitive ethnographer who wants to test whether moral intuitions are stable across testimony chains. In this scenario, a baseline moral case—saving five strangers at the cost of one—is presented to Observer One, who then verbally describes the case to Observer Two without seeing the original text; Observer Two describes it to Observer Three, and so on through ten links. By the tenth link, narrative drift has subtly altered the scenario: the number of lives shifts, the method of harm is left unspecified, and emotional valence is amplified or attenuated according to each reteller’s emphasis. The pump then asks whether the moral judgment delivered at the end of the chain reveals anything about the original case, or whether it is purely an artifact of transmission noise.

Predict Cole’s verdict—legitimate or misleading—identify which of the two theories above (or something else) she is implicitly running, and explain your reasoning in detail. Then tell me which further case would most cleanly distinguish the Conceptual Boundary Preservation theory from the Narrative Transmission Stability theory, so that my feature can note what evidence would actually settle the taxonomy. I was discussing this with colleagues over coffee and realized that without knowing the decisive case, I cannot frame the profile properly.
