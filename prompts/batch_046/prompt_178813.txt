Consider a philosopher of cognitive science who holds that real patterns—predictive regularities that justify a particular explanatory stance—are stance-relative, and who distinguishes the physical stance (laws and forces), the design stance (functional architecture and purpose without mental attribution), and the intentional stance (attributing beliefs, desires, and rational agency). She has offered the following verdicts across cases:

- A domestic thermostat regulating temperature: the design stance captures all real patterns; treating it as wanting 72°F adds no predictive compression and is not a real pattern.

- A leafcutter ant colony maintaining a fungal garden: the intentional stance is genuinely warranted at the collective level; attributing goals like preserving monoculture succeeds predictively in ways that neither biochemical physics nor individual-ant design rules can match.

- A large language model trained on scientific corpora that proposes novel experimental hypotheses: only the design stance applies; attributing belief in truth or curiosity yields no predictive gain over tracking weights, training distribution, and sampling algorithms.

- A patient with Capgras delusion who claims his spouse is an impostor: the intentional stance remains appropriate for predicting his everyday speech and navigation, but the specific delusion requires a localized design-stance explanation (disrupted face-recognition-to-emotion pathways); pure physical-stance neuroscience is insufficient for either.

- A decade-long stock-market index: statistical-physical mechanics captures the patterns; design stance applies to individual algorithmic traders, but intentional-stance talk about the market fearing recession is purely metaphorical with no real predictive pattern.

Now consider a new system: a synthetic biological computer composed of engineered, non-evolved DNA strands organized in a gel matrix. The strands have no individual goals, but the collective exhibits adaptive problem-solving—reconfiguring its molecular layout in response to novel chemical inputs to reach stable states—where the collective behavior is not derivable from the biochemical rules of any single strand or from the initial engineered blueprint.

What would this philosopher likely conclude about which stance applies to the collective's adaptive behavior, and what are the two or three most plausible rules that could consistently generate all five of her earlier verdicts? For each rule, state what it predicts about the DNA-gel system. Which rule do you think best fits her overall pattern, and why? Finally, specify one further hypothetical case—different from both the DNA-gel system and the five listed—that would most sharply distinguish the leading rule from its closest competitor.
