I have recently begun consulting for an interdisciplinary group that models causal dependencies in complex adaptive systems, and the parallel has forced me back to a debate I had assumed was settled in outline: Al-Ghazālī's occasionalist denial that created things possess inherent causal powers, versus the Aristotelian and Avicennian defense of secondary causation in nature. The dispute, as it plays out in both medieval reconstructions and modern philosophy-of-religion discussions, seems to have reached a peculiar stasis. The occasionalist side insists that fire does not burn cotton by any necessity intrinsic to fire; rather, God creates the burning directly and could just as well create burning without fire, or fire without burning. The defenders of secondary causation reply that this dissolves the intelligibility of science, since prediction and explanation depend on stable creaturely natures. Yet in every reconstruction I encounter, the arguments are already familiar, the examples—fire, the sun, medicine—are identical, and neither camp moves. I want a diagnosis, not a survey.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is partly verbal—the term "cause" is being used for ontological production on one side and for explanatory regularity on the other—and partly a hidden dispute about what counts as a satisfactory explanation of observed regularity, masked as metaphysics. But I am unsure whether there is also a genuine, unresolved formal or empirical question buried underneath: for instance, whether an occasionalist ontology can underwrite the same predictive and interventionist practices as a powers-based ontology, or whether the two frameworks are empirically equivalent but theologically divergent.

Please diagnose the stall. Use what each side actually says about causation, prediction, miracles, and the scope of natural science—not generic characterizations—to determine whether the disagreement is verbal, substantive but masked, bedrock and intuitive, or ill-posed. If it is substantive, identify the precise point at which the arguments fail to connect: is it about the criteria for attributing powers to creatures, about the conditions under which an explanation terminates, or about whether observed regularity requires an ontological ground? And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal demonstration that occasionalism is empirically indistinguishable from secondary causation in its predictive outputs; a conceptual distinction that separates causal production from explanatory dependence; an argument that one side's conception of divine omnipotence is internally incoherent; or a reason to think the question should be abandoned as badly formed. I am particularly interested in whether injecting a modern distinction—between vertical (divine-to-world) and horizontal (creature-to-creature) causation—actually resolves anything, or whether it merely relocates the same stall into a different vocabulary.
