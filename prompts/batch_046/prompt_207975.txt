I serve on the review board of an international technology-ethics fund, and we increasingly receive funding proposals justified by expected-value reasoning that pairs an astronomically large payoff with a probability so small that no frequency data or mechanistic model can support it—for example, a claimed 10⁻¹² chance that a particular protocol will prevent a civilizational collapse, or a speculative “tail risk” of an unknown unknown that is assigned no quantitative basis at all. These are instances of the decision structure commonly called Pascal’s mugging. Write a reusable decision procedure that a board member without philosophical training can run in roughly fifteen minutes during a meeting. The procedure should ask ordered questions, specify what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and produce one of three outputs: reject as mugging; proceed to substantive evaluation on non-expected-value grounds; or stop and hand the decision to human judgment because the question is one of values rather than calculable risk. Be explicit about which steps are genuine checks and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. The procedure must distinguish between risks that are quantified but extreme and risks that are epistemically ungrounded—where there is neither statistical base rate nor causal pathway, only bare logical possibility. Apply it to three proposals: (1) a request for major funding justified solely by a 0.000000000001 probability of curing all age-related diseases within one year; (2) a safety-monitoring system justified only by an unquantified “unknown unknown” tail risk with no proposed mechanism or detection protocol; and (3) a well-quantified engineering proposal in which a catastrophic failure mode is assigned a rigorously modeled 10⁻⁹ annual probability, where the procedure should yield an answer the board is likely to find counterintuitive—specifically, treating it differently from cases (1) and (2) despite the extreme odds. Finally, state precisely what this procedure cannot do, especially regarding the boundary between rational risk-aversion and arbitrary value assignment, and flag any case where the output depends on a prior commitment the board has not explicitly endorsed.
