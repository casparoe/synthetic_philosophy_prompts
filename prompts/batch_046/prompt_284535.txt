As a retired physicist who has recently turned to moral philosophy, I have been studying the claim that Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic populations are psychological outliers in folk morality, placing far greater weight on harm and fairness than on loyalty, authority, or sanctity. I am trying to understand what this means for moral objectivity, but I keep returning to Hume's account of sentiment and sympathy.

Consider a conceptual inversion of the usual cross-cultural pattern. In several documented small-scale societies, judgments about interpersonal harm appear highly context-sensitive: the same injury is evaluated differently depending on kinship distance, prior reciprocal obligations, and ritual status, with no agent-independent principle governing the verdict. By contrast, judgments about ritual purity in these societies are remarkably uniform and resistant to contextual variation. This reverses the WEIRD profile, in which harm is treated as universal and purity as culturally plastic.

If David Hume were alive today and confronted with this inverted architecture—where sympathy is not a universal calibrator but is structured by local relational matrices, and where the domain we regard as most objective is actually the most context-dependent—how would he respond? Would he be driven toward moral relativism, or could he reconstruct objectivity as the consistency and intersubjective communicability of sentiments within a given relational framework? More specifically, would this cross-cultural inversion undermine his separation of descriptive and normative claims, or would it merely complicate the psychology that supports that separation? I seek a philosophical reconstruction of his likely position, not a literature review.
