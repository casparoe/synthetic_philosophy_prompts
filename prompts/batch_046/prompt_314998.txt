In my graduate seminar on normative ethics, discussions of Bernard Williams's integrity objection—the view that an agent who determines whether to save a loved one or strangers solely by weighing outcomes has entertained one consideration too many—reliably stall in a pattern that feels structurally different from ordinary disagreement. The utilitarian interlocutor grants that psychological coherence matters but denies it carries independent normative weight; the defender of integrity insists that ground projects are not mere preferences but conditions of meaningful agency, yet struggles to articulate why these conditions generate agent-relative reasons without circular appeal to the very attachments they are meant to justify. Both sides command the standard literature and understand each other precisely; neither expects empirical psychology or a novel case to settle the matter. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is neither verbal nor empirical, but conceals a deeper disagreement about the proper architecture of moral justification—specifically, whether integrity functions as a deontic constraint on right action or as an aretaic condition on the worth of an agent's life. I want a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says about ground projects, impartial calculation, and the status of agent-relative reasons: are the two sides answering different questions—one about which acts are right, the other about which acts are worthy of being performed by this particular agent? Is there a hidden disagreement about whether moral reasons must be universally justifiable, or about whether evaluation of actions can be separated from evaluation of agents? Given your diagnosis, is progress possible through a formal distinction, a reframed thought experiment, or an argument that the debate should be dissolved rather than resolved?
