Read Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016) as a study in Bongard-style concept induction: Louise Banks is presented with only a sparse, ambiguous set of heptapod logograms and must derive from them not merely a lexicon but an entirely different ontology of time. Treat her successive attempts at decipherment as attempts to solve a minimal-example induction problem — distinguishing structural invariants from accidental surface features, avoiding overfitting to the first few patterns, and revising her background ontology when the examples resist her initial hypotheses. Where does the film dramatise the genuine difficulty of this kind of learning, and where does it rely on a sudden, almost mystical insight that bypasses the inductive labour? In particular, consider the moment she grasps that the language reshapes her cognition of causality and sequence: is this best understood as the successful induction of a new concept, or as something closer to direct acquaintance with a pre-existing temporal structure? And if the latter, does the film unintentionally suggest that concept induction from few examples is impossible without some prior conceptual framework that is not itself induced? I want a reading that respects both the film's narrative architecture and the philosophical literature on induction and concept acquisition, and that asks what Arrival reveals — if anything — about the limits of learning from minimal evidence that formal accounts of Bongard problems do not already capture.
