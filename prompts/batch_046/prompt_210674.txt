You are grading a take-home exam for an upper-division philosophy of art course. The exam asks students to address the following question.

Question: In debates about immoral art, philosophers distinguish the biographical fallacy—judging a work by the artist’s character—from the ethical evaluation of representational content within the work itself. Two positions structure this second debate. First, aesthetic cognitivism about moral value holds that a work’s moral perspective is part of what we assess aesthetically, but only through a specific mechanism: we evaluate whether the work’s moral vision is coherent, not whether it is morally correct. Second, robust autonomism holds that moral properties are never aesthetically relevant to a work, even when they shape the emotional responses the work solicits. With these positions in mind, discuss whether immoral art can be good art. In particular, address whether a work that solicits sympathy for an immoral character can be aesthetically excellent if that solicitation is structurally necessary for a larger moral insight the work achieves, and distinguish this case from one in which the audience’s moral response is mediated by receptive framing—the internal structural cues that determine whether an emotional response is endorsed, held in suspension, or distanced.

Below is one student’s answer. Provide a letter grade (A through F) and a specific, point-by-point assessment that justifies it. Identify precise strengths (e.g., accurate distinctions, clear applications) and precise errors (e.g., mischaracterized positions, steps that do not follow, conflations). Do not offer only a general impression; comment on individual claims. You are grading another person’s work—do not signal your own philosophical agreement or disagreement with the student’s conclusions.

Student answer:

The biographical fallacy is indeed the error of judging a work by the artist’s moral character, which is separate from evaluating the moral content represented inside the work. Aesthetic cognitivism about moral value holds that a work’s moral perspective belongs to aesthetic evaluation, but specifically we assess the coherence of its moral vision rather than its moral correctness. By contrast, robust autonomism maintains that moral properties are never aesthetically relevant, even when they structure the emotional responses solicited by the work; in fact, on this view moral properties are not representational properties of artworks at all, so they cannot enter aesthetic assessment.

Applying this to immoral art: a work can be aesthetically excellent despite representing immoral acts if its representational strategy achieves coherence. For instance, if a novel solicits sympathy for an immoral protagonist, this need not diminish aesthetic value provided the sympathy is part of a larger, structurally necessary moral insight—such as exposing the seductive logic of corruption. Here the moral defect (sympathy for vice) is transformed into an aesthetic virtue because it serves an overarching cognitive purpose elsewhere in the narrative.

Robust autonomism rejects this transformation. Since moral properties are not representational, the solicitation of sympathy is purely an emotional mechanism operating independently of moral evaluation, and receptive framing is therefore just a formal device with no moral content. Thus, autonomism treats the emotional response as entirely non-cognitive.

This suggests that immoral art can be good art only under cognitivism, and only when the immoral representation is redeemed by compensatory moral insight elsewhere in the narrative structure. If no such redemption exists, the work remains aesthetically defective regardless of its formal sophistication.
