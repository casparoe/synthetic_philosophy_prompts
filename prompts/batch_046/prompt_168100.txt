Read Milos Forman's One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest (1975) through Ian Hacking's account of looping effects in psychiatric classification, but with a specific twist: rather than treating the patients as merely reactive to the labels imposed upon them, examine whether Randle McMurphy's performance of sanity functions as what we might call a counter-loop — an attempt to destabilise the institutional taxonomy by exaggerating the very behaviours that the ward uses to diagnose non-conformity. I am preparing a seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry, and I want a reading that does not reduce the film to an illustration of Hacking's interactive kinds, but asks precisely where the idea illuminates the narrative and where the narrative exposes a gap.

Consider in particular the group-therapy scenes, the fishing expedition, and Chief Bromden's final act: do these moments show individuals being reshaped by the category 'insane', or do they reveal a kind of strategic self-fashioning that Hacking's model of looping does not fully capture? Does the institution's classification system produce only passive internalisation, or can it generate a form of resistance in which patients learn to weaponise the very diagnoses meant to contain them? I would like you to address whether McMurphy's rebellion breaks the loop or inadvertently reinforces it, and whether Chief Bromden's escape represents genuine liberation from an interactive kind or merely a different mode of being defined by it. Finally, say what the film adds to the philosophical debate — perhaps about agency, institutional power, or the limits of taxonomy — that a purely theoretical account of psychiatric looping effects tends to miss, without quoting dialogue verbatim or relying on details that only appear in the novel rather than the widely known film.
