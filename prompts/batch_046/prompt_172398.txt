I am a journalist covering the intersection of science, technology, and political philosophy, and I am preparing a feature on how pre-modern texts illuminate governance in decentralized digital systems. I have been working through a difficult passage in Pascal’s Pensées. In the passage, Pascal argues that justice without power is impotent, because it will always be resisted by those who reject it; at the same time, power without justice is arbitrary and tyrannical. He concludes that justice and force must therefore be united, so that what is just becomes strong and what is strong becomes just, yet he laments that human societies rarely manage this union, sliding instead into either anarchy or despotism.

I want to apply this framework to a problem that is not usually discussed in the standard commentaries on Pascal: the governance of autonomous protocols in which enforcement is not executed by a state apparatus but by immutable smart-contract logic—what some theorists describe as structural, distributed coercion. In this setting, justice might be analogized to the procedural fairness of token-weighted deliberation, while force might be analogized to the automatic execution of protocol rules regardless of individual human intent.

Could you help me understand, strictly from the logic of the passage itself, whether Pascal’s argument permits a stable separation between justice and this kind of non-human, distributed force? Or does the text imply that whenever justice and enforcement are decoupled—whether in a monarchy or in code—instability is unavoidable? I am especially interested in whether the passage contains a hidden third possibility that is usually overlooked: a conception of justice whose authority derives not from union with force, but solely from the internal coherence of the reasoning that justifies it. Please develop your answer without reference to secondary scholarship or bibliographic recommendations, and ground your interpretation in the structure of the argument as presented above.
