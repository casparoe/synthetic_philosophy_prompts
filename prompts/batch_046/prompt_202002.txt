I defend a particular reading of Leibniz's claim that this world is the best of all possible worlds—one that departs from both the standard global-maximization interpretation and the more recent decision-theoretic reconstructions. On this view, "best" means locally optimal with respect to compossible variety under a constraint of metaphysical economy: rather than surveying an infinite array of worlds and ranking them by aggregate perfection, selection occurs through a modal filter that eliminates worlds containing contradictory essences before any comparative evaluation takes place, leaving only those worlds with maximally dense but non-conflicting possibilities. Among these, our world is distinguished not by having the least suffering in absolute terms, but by exhibiting the greatest internal variety of substances compatible with a single unified law of the series—so that apparent evils function as structural conditions for that particular density rather than being outweighed by compensating goods elsewhere. This predicts that in any nearby possible world with slightly less suffering, the total variety of compossible essences would necessarily contract, and that there is no single cross-world metric of global perfection capable of ranking worlds that operate at different density levels.
