In the AI safety literature on adversarial debate—roughly the framework in which two agents argue before a weaker judge to surface honest reasoning—there is a disagreement I have watched stall for years without either side conceding ground. One camp holds that the protocol is undermined by what I will call structural obfuscation: a sufficiently capable debater can construct technically correct lines of reasoning whose logical dependencies are nested so deeply that verifying any single step requires first solving the original problem, meaning the judge can never achieve independent mechanistic understanding, only correlational confidence across decomposed sub-claims. The opposing camp replies that this mistakes cognitive difficulty for deception; if an argument genuinely resists decomposition into verifiable steps that preserve causal understanding, then by the protocol's rules it should be rejected, and what looks like obfuscation is merely weak argumentation that the judge is right to distrust. The same examples—long-horizon strategic reasoning, nested counterfactuals, cryptographic-style proof chains—are recycled endlessly, and both sides now seem to treat the disagreement as a matter of taste about how much the judge must understand.

My working hypothesis is that this is not an empirical dispute about scaling capabilities, but a hidden normative disagreement about what verification must preserve: whether the judge needs causal-mechanistic comprehension of each step, or whether distributed correlational confidence across decomposed checks is sufficient for alignment purposes. Yet I am unsure whether that diagnosis is correct, or whether there is a deeper formal question—perhaps about whether verification complexity can scale differently from generation complexity in a way that makes structural obfuscation inevitable at certain capability thresholds—that neither side has formalized.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? Are the sides talking past each other about the definition of understanding, about the distribution of cognitive labor between debater and judge, or about a bedrock intuition that some truths are inherently non-decomposable? Is there a hidden empirical claim about how verification difficulty scales with argument length or conceptual nesting, or is the stall a sign that the question is ill-posed because "judge strength" is not a stable parameter across domains? Given your diagnosis, what would actually move the needle—a formal result separating verification complexity from generation complexity, a conceptual distinction between epistemic and structural obfuscation that clarifies the normative split, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why? I want the diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, together with an honest assessment of whether anything—a distinction, a theorem, a change of question—could end it, not a survey of debate protocols.
