I came across a summary on a philosophy blog that tries to use the sphex wasp to settle debates about free will, and I'm trying to figure out which parts are trustworthy for a paper I'm writing. The blogger describes Sphex ichneumoneus dragging a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, checking inside, and then pulling the cricket in; if the cricket is moved during the check, the wasp repeats the check-loop rather than adapting. The blogger claims philosophers use this not merely as an oddity but as proof that apparent flexibility always hides deeper mechanical rigidity. Specifically, the post says Douglas Hofstadter coined sphexishness to argue that human cognition differs only in loop-complexity: our brains run longer branching scripts with nested conditionals, but never escape deterministic execution. The blogger then connects this to predictive-processing models of the brain, arguing that since cognition minimizes prediction error through fixed hierarchical updates, human decision-making is structurally identical to the wasp's loop—only with more layers of error correction. From this, the blogger concludes that free will is an illusion of scale, and that any viable account of agency must accept we are sphexish at higher resolution. I suspect the summary collapses Hofstadter's actual point into hard determinism, and that it misuses predictive processing—treating error-correction loops as proof of rigidity rather than as mechanisms that can generate flexible adaptation. Is this summary fair? Please distinguish what is accurate about the wasp's behavior and Hofstadter's actual use of the example, what is a misleading but plausible extension, and whether the conclusion about free will survives once the summary's errors are corrected.
