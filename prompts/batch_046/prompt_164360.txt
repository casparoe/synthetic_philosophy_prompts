I'm drafting a position statement on historiographical ethics for a seminar and want a consistency check before I defend it. Here is where I stand:

(1) The contextualist stricture: historians should reconstruct past practices using only the normative and descriptive categories that were live for the participants; applying modern ethical vocabularies retroactively is a form of interpretive violence I call taxonomic presentism.

(2) The constructivist acknowledgment: archives, sources, and narratives are always assembled under present conditions of power and interest; there is no "view from nowhere" that would let a historian recover the past untouched by her own time.

(3) The duty of verdict: when facing past atrocities—systematic enslavement, colonial extraction, gendered exclusion—historians must issue explicit moral judgments by contemporary standards, since neutrality reproduces harm under the guise of objectivity.

(4) The separation thesis: explaining why agents acted (hermeneutic recovery) and evaluating whether they acted rightly (normative assessment) are methodologically distinct tasks, and evaluation can follow explanation without contaminating it.

(5) The invisibility thesis: if a present conceptual scheme renders a past form of life structurally unrepresentable—say, obligations that do not map onto modern family, contract, or citizenship categories—then the historian has not taken a partial view but has failed to understand the phenomenon altogether.

Please audit this package. Which commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the severity ranking? For each real tension, say what the least costly revision would be and what it costs. I am especially unsure whether (3) and (1) can survive together if (4) holds, and whether (5) undermines (2) or is actually entailed by it. Do not treat all conflicts as equally serious—some should dissolve on closer inspection.
