I am preparing a short argumentative essay for my philosophy of mind and ethics seminar on the gradation of moral status across beings that vary in sentience, and I would appreciate critical comments on the outline and reasoning below. My central claim is that moral considerability should be treated as scalar rather than binary, indexed not to phenomenal consciousness alone but to what I call "integrated homeostatic depth"—the degree to which a system combines valenced representation, cross-modal sensory integration, and temporally persistent goal-state regulation. On this view, mammals occupy the high end of the scale because they possess thalamocortical loops that unify nociceptive signals with affective and cognitive processing; insects occupy a genuinely intermediate position because their decentralized ganglia support nociception and adaptive avoidance without global workspace integration, yielding a low but non-zero grade of moral status; and thermostats, even sophisticated predictive ones with distributed sensors and feedback loops, sit near the zero point because they lack both valenced representation and any form of unified sensory integration, despite having negative-feedback homeostasis. I worry that this framework risks two opposing errors: on one side, a functionalist slide that would inflate the status of engineered climate-control networks if we weight feedback complexity too heavily; on the other, a vertebrate-chauvinist slide that discounts insect suffering because it lacks mammalian neuroanatomy. To avoid both, I propose weighting the three axes—sensory integration, affective valence, and temporal persistence—such that a being must score above threshold on at least two to claim non-negligible status. I then apply this to a borderline case: a smart building's thermal-management system that predicts occupancy patterns and adjusts heating preemptively. By my criteria it should remain below the insect threshold, but I am unsure whether my distinction between "predictive feedback" and "valenced anticipation" holds up under scrutiny. Specifically, I would welcome comments on whether the three-axis model is conceptually coherent or merely ad hoc, whether the comparison between decentralized insect ganglia and bimetallic or algorithmic thermostat loops genuinely illuminates the boundary or instead confuses metabolic homeostasis with moral considerability, and whether there is a stronger reply to the functionalist objection that avoids begging the question against panpsychist or purely behaviorist intuitions. I am also concerned that my middle section anthropomorphizes insect nociception; if you see that risk, please indicate where the reasoning overreaches and how I might reframe the insect case in terms of observable adaptive architecture rather than implied subjective experience. Finally, I would value suggestions for sharpening the transition from the theoretical framework to the normative conclusion—specifically, whether grading moral status this finely actually changes practical obligations, or whether it collapses back into a binary "protect versus ignore" distinction once policy is involved. I am writing this without reference to any specific published taxonomy of sentience, so I am especially interested in whether the conceptual architecture sounds internally consistent rather than whether it matches a particular literature.
