Please assume you are grading a final examination in the philosophy of social science for advanced undergraduates. The examination asks students to engage with Max Weber’s claim that the ascetic ethos of early modern Protestantism contributed causally to the development of rational capitalism, and with the broader problem of whether values can serve as explanatory causes rather than as mere rationalizations of material change.

Here is the examination question exactly as it was presented to the student:

Weber argued that the spirit of capitalism emerged partly from the worldly asceticism of Calvinist and related Protestant sects, and that this cultural orientation carried causal weight in the development of modern rational capitalism—not merely reflecting underlying economic transformations. In your answer, do three things: (1) Explain Weber’s concept of elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft) between the Protestant ethic and capitalist economic structures, and clarify how it differs both from claiming that religious values were necessary conditions and from claiming that they were sufficient conditions for capitalism’s rise; (2) Address the causal-exclusion objection: if material conditions—capital accumulation, contract-law frameworks, and colonial resource extraction—already provide a complete explanation for the emergence of capitalism, in what sense, if any, can a value-orientation such as the calling (Beruf) be said to cause rather than accompany economic change? In doing so, apply the counterfactual stability condition introduced in the course: a cultural factor counts as genuinely explanatory only if, holding material conditions constant, its absence would have produced a measurably different economic trajectory within a historically reasonable window; (3) Assess whether Weber’s framework successfully answers this objection, or whether it ultimately collapses into an epiphenomenalist account in which values merely rationalize behavior that is fully determined by material interests.

Below is the student’s answer, reproduced verbatim. Grade it on a standard A-F scale, and then provide specific, point-by-point comments that justify the grade. Do not offer a general impression without tying it to particular sentences or claims in the answer; identify both what the student gets right and where the reasoning goes wrong, especially where an error is subtle enough that a hasty reader might miss it. Do not ask for outside sources or suggest further reading.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Weber’s main point is that Protestantism caused capitalism to happen in Europe. He uses elective affinity to describe this, which basically means that Protestant values and capitalism are like two puzzle pieces that fit together—functionally interchangeable, so that one can substitute for the other in explaining the same outcome. This is not the same as saying religion was necessary or sufficient, because Weber admits capitalism could exist without Protestantism, just with lower efficiency.

On the causal exclusion problem: if economics already explains everything, culture can’t also be a cause. But Weber avoids this by showing that Calvinists worked harder and saved more because they believed hard work was a sign they were saved. The counterfactual stability condition asks what would happen if we removed religion but kept the economy the same. Since capitalism eventually spread to Japan and other non-Protestant places, removing Protestantism from England wouldn’t have changed the long-run trajectory, so the condition actually fails. That means values don’t really cause anything; they are just epiphenomenal. Weber tries to save his view by retreating to hermeneutics—he says values don’t cause behavior but only help us interpret it. So in the end his theory collapses into epiphenomenalism, though he doesn’t want to admit it.

Weber also distinguishes worldly asceticism from other-worldly asceticism. The Calvinists practiced other-worldly asceticism because they focused entirely on salvation in the next life rather than on worldly success. This mental orientation made them deny themselves luxury and reinvest profits, which accidentally created capitalism. Even though values are internal mental states that are hard to observe, Weber gets around this by using statistical correlations between Protestant regions and capitalist development, which proves the causal link.
