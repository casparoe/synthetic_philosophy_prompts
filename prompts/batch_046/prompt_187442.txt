As a retired physicist recently turned to political philosophy, I am studying Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (Book IV, Chapter 7) and wish to understand a dense passage on colonial monopoly. Here is the text:

"The exclusive trade of the mother countries tends to diminish, or, at least, to keep down below what they would otherwise rise to, both the enjoyments and industry of all those nations in general, and of the American colonies in particular. It is a dead weight upon the action of one of the great springs which puts into motion a great part of the business of mankind."

Smith contends that exclusive trade misdirects capital away from its "natural" course, yet he never specifies the governing principle. I am inclined to interpret his "dead weight" and "springs" metaphors through the lens of non-equilibrium thermodynamics—capital as energy flowing through open channels toward states of lower potential—yet I fear this is an anachronistic projection of modern physics onto an eighteenth-century moral argument.

Could you help me parse the reasoning? Specifically: (1) By what mechanism—price, labor allocation, or otherwise—does Smith think monopoly "cramps" industry? (2) Do the mechanical metaphors carry explanatory weight, or are they merely rhetorical ornament? (3) Is the thermodynamic analogy useful, misleading, or both?
