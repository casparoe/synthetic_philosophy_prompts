I'd like a consistency audit on a cluster of commitments I've adopted for work in comparative moral history. Here is where I stand:

(1) There are mind-independent facts about the severity of mass atrocities, including differences in scale, intentional design, and the suffering inflicted.
(2) Comparing atrocities for structural analysis—mapping bureaucratic mechanisms, escalation patterns, or institutional architectures—is permissible and valuable, provided the comparison is not weaponized to minimize or excuse any particular case.
(3) Constructing a single linear ranking of historical evils is both morally inappropriate and epistemically dubious, since dimensions like industrial efficiency, ideological motivation, and lived victim experience are not fully commensurable.
(4) Each major atrocity possesses an irreducible moral particularity: the Holocaust, the Rwandan genocide, the Atlantic slave trade, and the Holodomor each embed specific historical configurations that resist full absorption into a generic category of mass violence.
(5) Despite (4), cross-case comparison is indispensable for genocide prevention and for testing theories of state violence; refusing all comparison risks isolating cases into incomparable singularities that block practical learning.
(6) I reject evolutionary or cultural debunking of comparative moral judgment: our capacity to recognize gradations of evil is not merely a tribal heuristic, even if our particular rankings are often biased.
(7) The legitimate unit of comparison should be institutional mechanisms rather than the event as a moral whole—so we can compare division-of-labor structures or propaganda architectures without comparing overall moral weights.

Do these hang together? Specifically: does (1) combined with (3) implicitly commit me to some metric of severity that I officially reject? Is the tension between (4) and (5) genuine, or does (7) dissolve it by relocating comparison from moral wholes to sub-institutional parts—if so, does that rescue (4) or undermine its claim to irreducibility? Does (6) overcommit me to confidence in comparative judgment in ways that conflict with the epistemic humility demanded by (3)? And is there a hidden contradiction between endorsing (2) and (7) while maintaining that particularity in (4) is truly irreducible?

Please rank the tensions by severity, distinguish real conflicts from apparent ones, and identify the least costly revision—without assuming I am willing to abandon moral realism, drop comparative history, or accept that all comparison is obscene. Be precise about which commitments pull against each other and what exactly would have to give.
