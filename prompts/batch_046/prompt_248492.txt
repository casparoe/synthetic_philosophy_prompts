I'm delivering a paper next month at the Society for Applied Philosophy's annual session on moral psychology and institutional ethics. My central claim is that Bernard Williams's concern about having one thought too many — the idea that certain moral calculations violate the practical identity of the agent — undermines rather than supports the standard defense of advance directives in medical ethics. Specifically, I argue that when a patient drafts a directive refusing future treatment to avoid becoming a burden, and her later demented self appears genuinely content, honoring the directive forces clinicians and family members to treat the patient's earlier life-project as an external constraint upon the later self. This reproduces exactly the structure Williams identified in intimate relationships: the agent must accommodate a consideration that ought to be internal to her practical identity, except here the accommodation is institutionalized through legal-medical authority. The audience will consist mainly of applied ethicists and philosophers of medicine committed to relational autonomy and skeptical of psychological-continuity theories of personal identity, but also deeply invested in patient self-determination; they will likely charge that I am misapplying a thesis about face-to-face intimacy to bureaucratic institutional contexts where role-based reasoning is not only appropriate but required.

Please construct my Q&A preparation: list the objections I will actually face, ordered by how severely they damage my position, phrased precisely as a relational-autonomy theorist in bioethics would phrase them — not as a metaphysician or decision theorist would. For each objection, provide a concise reply that takes a definite stand, explains why the reply holds, and explicitly marks its weakest point. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises — the one that presses me on whether the contented demented patient possesses a practical identity capable of overriding the directive, since I regard this as the most vulnerable juncture in my argument. I want a committed strategic assessment rather than a neutral survey of options: defend the thesis I have outlined, tell me where I must concede ground to preserve credibility, and avoid any bibliography, citation list, or request for further reading. Write with the conviction of someone advising a colleague before a high-stakes defense.
