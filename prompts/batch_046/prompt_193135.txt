As a novelist accustomed to constructing consciousness out of nothing more than syntax and shadow, I find the paradox of fiction to be not merely an academic puzzle but a problem of craft. Write a formal essay examining whether it is genuinely irrational to feel profound pity, grief, or moral outrage toward characters one knows with certainty do not exist. Weigh the familiar cognitive and affective accounts—those that treat fictional emotion as either make-believe or as a misfired real-world response—against a more unconventional framing introduced here: that emotional engagement with fiction produces what might be called an asymmetrical emotional debt, a one-sided ethical pull toward beings who can never reciprocate, be aided, or released from their suffering, and that this very unresolvability is not a cognitive failure but the defining mechanism by which fiction exercises moral imagination without exhausting it. Reach and defend a clear conclusion: does recognizing this asymmetry dissolve the paradox by placing fictional feeling in its own category of experience, or does it deepen the paradox by showing that we willingly sustain obligations we can never discharge? Justify your verdict with conceptual precision, avoiding appeals to bibliography or external authority.
