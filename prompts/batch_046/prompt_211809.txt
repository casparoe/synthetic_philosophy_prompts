I've been working through the standard opposition between Ranke's well-known aim of showing how things actually happened and the later scepticism of Carr and Hayden White. The usual story is that Ranke believed in a recoverable past independent of the historian, while Carr and White showed that narrative form and present interests inevitably shape the account. Re-reading Ranke's methodological remarks, though, I think there's a neglected intermediate layer that makes the dichotomy look too neat.

Ranke insists on primary sources and warns against moralizing judgments, yet he also organizes narratives around the ideas he claims drive states and peoples. These ideas are not directly observable in archives; they are inferred as organizing principles. This looks less like naive empiricism and more like a form of structural realism in which the historian posits unobservable entities—the interests or guiding ideas of states—to explain observable diplomatic and military events.

If that reading holds, then Carr's critique that all history involves interpretation doesn't quite land on Ranke, because Ranke already admits interpretation at the level of causal structure; he merely restricts it to structural explanation and excludes moral evaluation. Meanwhile, White's claim that historical narratives are essentially literary genres might be addressed by noting that Ranke's ideas function somewhat like theoretical terms in science: they constrain which narratives are admissible without uniquely determining them.

My tentative conclusion is that Ranke occupies a position closer to what philosophers of science call model-dependent realism—the past is real, but accessible only through models that select and weight causes—rather than the naive correspondence theory he's usually assigned. This would imply that Carr and White attack a straw man, though not for the reasons usually given in defenses of Ranke.

I'd welcome comments on three points. First, is this reading of Ranke's ideas as theoretical terms defensible, or am I projecting an anachronistic philosophy-of-science framework onto nineteenth-century historiography? Second, does the model-dependent framing actually distinguish Ranke from Carr in a meaningful way, or does it collapse into Carr's position that interpretation is unavoidable? Third, are there specific features of Ranke's practice—perhaps his treatment of religious or cultural factors—that resist this structural-realist reading? I'm particularly interested in whether this framing survives once we stop treating Ranke's anti-moralism as a guarantee of objectivity and start treating it as a constraint on which models are permissible.
