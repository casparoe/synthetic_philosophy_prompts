I work in software, where I usually distinguish between tracking correlations and modeling underlying mechanisms. In reading about Al-Ghazali's occasionalism — specifically the claim that God produces each event directly and that the pairing of fire with burning is only a habitual correlation — I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern across a few discussions. First, defenders respond to the objection that occasionalism undermines science by saying that science needs only reliable correlations, not real causal powers; they compare scientific prediction to a statistical model that produces accurate outputs without internal mechanism representation. This is offered as a defensive shield: if correlation alone is sufficient, occasionalism does not damage scientific practice. Second, the same defenders turn around and argue that the very success of correlation-based science gives positive reason to reject metaphysical causation — since we can predict everything without postulating hidden necessary connections, such connections are redundant. Here the claim that correlations are sufficient is used offensively, to attack rival metaphysics. I have seen this two-phase maneuver in discussions of Ash'arite theology and occasionalism, but I do not know whether it has a specific name. Is there an established term for using the sufficiency of a correlation-only framework first to insulate a no-causation view from practical objections and then to deploy that sufficiency as an argument against causation itself? If so, what is it and how exactly does it apply? If not, can you define it precisely — including its boundary conditions and how it differs from circular reasoning, from an inference to the best explanation, and from simple equivocation?
