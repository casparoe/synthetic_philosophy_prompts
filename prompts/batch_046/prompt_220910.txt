Draft the joint statement that a constitutional-veto camp and a functional-allocation camp on federalism and subsidiarity could both sign after a sustained adversarial collaboration focused on the institutional architectures of the United States, Germany, Switzerland, and the European Union. The constitutional-veto camp holds that subsidiarity operates as a constitutional presumption against centralization: authority should rest at the lowest democratically accountable level capable of addressing a given problem, and upward delegation requires explicit justification bearing a high burden of proof, because the proximity of government to citizens is a primary democratic good that layered jurisdiction tends to erode. The functional-allocation camp holds that subsidiarity is an optimization principle for multilevel governance: authority should be assigned to the level—local, national, or supranational—that can best achieve legitimate collective goals, taking into account cross-border externalities, economies of scale, rights-protection failures at lower levels, and the democratic legitimacy that can be generated through deliberative and representative mechanisms at larger scales; on this view, there is no inherent democratic superiority of proximity, and centralization can be subsidiarity-compliant when lower levels are genuinely incapable.

Produce the document in two parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims—statements both camps accept about the formal division of competences in the four systems (including the German Basic Law’s allocation of legislative powers and the Bundesrat’s role, Swiss cantonal autonomy coupled with direct-democratic instruments, the U.S. anti-commandeering doctrine and conditional-spending framework, and the EU Treaty’s explicit subsidiarity clause together with its practical application through the Commission’s impact assessments). Include agreed empirical observations about patterns of centralization and decentralization over recent decades, and agreed normative conditions that any adequate theory must satisfy: it must account for democratic accountability, policy effectiveness, and the protection of minority rights across levels. Also include an agreed conceptual distinction that neither camp invented but both find indispensable: the difference between formal subsidiarity (the legal assignment of competences in constitutional or treaty texts) and effective subsidiarity (where actual decision-making power resides once conditional funding, regulatory preemption, administrative implementation, and judicial interpretation are taken into account). Within that agreed framework, both camps should acknowledge the reverse-delegation paradox—cases in which subnational governments actively seek central rules or funding conditions to avoid competitive downward pressure—which complicates any simple equation of lower-level authority with democratic preference.

Second, present a disagreements table. Each row should contain: (a) the precise formulation of the disagreement in wording that does not presuppose either camp’s framing; (b) the constitutional-veto camp’s own formulation of its position, phrased so that its members would accept it; (c) the functional-allocation camp’s own formulation of its position, phrased so that its members would accept it; (d) the crux—the specific sub-question such that resolving it in one direction would move at least one camp; (e) a concrete institutional feature, empirical pattern, or case that both camps agree bears on the crux (for example, the EU’s differential treatment of environmental standards versus social-policy harmonization under the subsidiarity protocol, or the interaction between U.S. state autonomy and federal conditional grants); and (f) for that feature or case, the prediction or interpretation each camp would advance, recorded in advance.

The cruxes must include, but need not be limited to: (1) whether democratic proximity is an intrinsic value or solely an instrumental one; (2) whether the reverse-delegation paradox undermines the veto camp’s core argument or can be fully accommodated within it without collapsing the distinction between voluntary cooperation and imposed centralization; (3) whether effective subsidiarity can ever be secured without formal constitutional entrenchment of lower-level competences; and (4) whether cross-border externalities in compound republics are best addressed by upward assignment of authority or by inter-jurisdictional cooperation with central enforcement reserved strictly as a fallback.

The statement should be drafted so that neither camp could fairly claim the other side’s wording is a strawman. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citation requests, or appeals to authority outside the argument itself. The prompt should be self-contained: a model without web access should be able to construct the statement using general knowledge of these four federal systems together with the conceptual distinctions introduced above.
