You chair the clinical-ethics review panel for a regional hospital network. Every week you receive proposals for "surge-reallocation protocols" — policies that redirect staff, beds, or equipment away from some patients during capacity crises in order to treat others with a higher probability of survival. Your panel has forty-five minutes per proposal; your recommendation is advisory but must be documented for accreditation review and communicated to families who will understand the outcome as "protocol, not choice." You need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the doctrine of double effect, that translates its four conditions into operational steps a clinician or administrator without philosophical training can apply using only a proposal document and standard medical records.

Write the procedure as an ordered checklist of questions. For each step, specify: the exact distinction being tested (e.g., whether the harmful effect is intended as an end, intended as a means, or merely foreseen); what counts as a sufficient answer based on available documentation; the precise point at which the procedure stops with "do not recommend"; and the explicit points where judgment about values — not facts — is unavoidable and must be flagged for human deliberation rather than automated resolution.

The procedure must be able to process three structurally different hard cases: (a) delaying elective surgeries for thirty days, knowing that some delayed patients will experience clinical deterioration and severe anxiety, but not using that deterioration as the mechanism that frees resources; (b) automatically downgrading ICU monitoring for patients whose calculated survival probability falls below a set threshold, where the reduction in nursing attention is itself the mechanism that frees staff, and missed clinical deterioration is a foreseen but undesired consequence; (c) temporarily withholding from families the fact that a rationing scheme is active, on the grounds that disclosure would trigger panic-driven demands that collapse the life-saving protocol, making the deception an intended instrument of preservation.

Run the procedure on all three cases. For at least one — ideally case (b) or (c) — the procedure should produce a recommendation that conflicts with the intuitive judgment that many panel members are likely to hold; state exactly what the procedure outputs and identify the precise step at which intuition and procedure diverge. Finally, articulate the limits of the procedure: describe the institutional conditions under which double-effect reasoning risks collapsing into straightforward consequentialist weighing, and specify which safeguards (outside the procedure itself) are necessary to prevent that collapse.
