I am a high school senior currently enrolled in an introductory philosophy course, and I have become somewhat preoccupied with a problem we discussed last week regarding Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. Our instructor introduced Max Black's well-known thought experiment—two spheres that share every intrinsic qualitative feature, suspended in an otherwise empty universe that exhibits perfect rotational and reflective symmetry—as a purported counterexample to the claim that no two distinct objects can share all properties. I have been discussing this with a few classmates, and I think I may be importing an assumption that the scenario is specifically designed to exclude.

Specifically, it seems to me that even in such a sparse universe, sphere A bears the relational property of being situated three miles to the left of sphere B, whereas sphere B bears the distinct relational property of being situated three miles to the right of sphere A. If relational properties genuinely count as properties of the objects that instantiate them, then the two spheres do differ in at least one respect, which would mean that Leibniz's principle is not violated after all but is instead satisfied. Given this, I am unsure why philosophers treat Black's example as undermining the principle rather than confirming it. Is my error the implicit reliance on an absolute spatial framework—one that assigns an objective left and right—within a universe that is supposed to lack any such orientation? Or is there a deeper reason why these spatial relations fail to count as the kind of properties Leibniz had in mind?

I would appreciate a careful explanation of where this reasoning missteps, particularly regarding whether perfect symmetry eliminates the very relational distinctions I have described. I want to resolve this confusion before I contribute to our seminar discussion next week, as I do not wish to build my argument on a misconception about how spatial relations function in metaphysically minimal scenarios.
