I find the standard Davidsonian response somewhat unsatisfying because it often stops at saying that self-knowledge is just more direct, not that it's structurally different. So I'd like you to argue for a stronger claim: that within Davidson's framework, first-person authority is not an independent epistemic privilege at all, but rather a pragmatic artifact that emerges when an agent applies the principle of charity to herself through an internalized interpretive stance.

More specifically, please develop the argument that if mental content is constitutively fixed by the conditions of radical interpretation—where belief attribution requires triangulation between speaker, interpreter, and shared world—then there is no coherent space for a purely private, non-interpretive access to one's own beliefs. You should engage directly with the intuition that I don't need to observe my own behavior to know that I believe it is raining; explain how a Davidsonian can accommodate that intuition without granting it foundational status. I also want you to consider the objection that this collapses self-knowledge into a kind of self-interpretation that sounds suspiciously like confabulation, and respond to it using Davidson's resources rather than importing external theories. Don't provide a bibliography or cite specific page numbers—just build the reasoning out clearly, keeping the discussion at the conceptual level rather than expository.
