I'm preparing a 75-minute seminar for first-year philosophy undergraduates who have just read a popular essay arguing that factory farming is wrong if pigs are conscious. They enter with a binary mental model: consciousness is an on-off switch, and ethics only needs to locate the cutoff. I want them to understand that sentience and moral status are genuinely gradated—running meaningfully from insects through decorticated mammals to homeostatic devices like thermostats—without collapsing into "everything matters equally" or "only full-blown consciousness counts."

Plan the explanatory arc, not the lecture itself. First, state the conceptual prerequisite that must be in place before the gradation claim can sound coherent rather than contradictory: what binary assumption has to be dismantled, and why must it come before any example is introduced? Second, specify the single carrying comparison—choose one insect case, one decorticated-mammal case, and one thermostat-level feedback system—and for each state exactly which capacity it must exhibit and which it must lack, so the triad does structural work rather than trading on intuition. Third, list the three misconceptions they will raise in predictable order—the anthropomorphic projection objection, the "gradated means all harm is equally wrong" slippery slope, and the "thermostats have interests" reductio—and map each to the precise point in the sequence where it should be preempted, explaining how the material immediately preceding that point is designed to absorb it. Fourth, design an in-room check for understanding that reveals whether a student has adopted the gradation framework or is merely repeating that "it's complicated"; it should not be an essay or quiz, but a task or exchange they perform on the spot. Fifth, identify one standard body of material—such as detailed thalamocortical-loop neuroanatomy or canonical literature references—that should be explicitly left out at this level, with a one-sentence justification. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests.
