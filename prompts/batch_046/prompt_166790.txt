I serve as a senior ethics reviewer for an interdisciplinary research funding body, and our board has adopted a principle we are calling integrative provenance: a conceptual framework may be treated as proprietary to its originators only if it contains at least one binding node—an explicit inferential commitment that connects two otherwise autonomous domains and that, if adopted, necessarily restructures reasoning within both domains simultaneously. Simple aggregation of existing concepts across fields, or the addition of a new application domain without such a restructuring commitment, does not establish proprietary status, regardless of the labor involved. I need a reusable decision procedure my review panel can apply to framework-ownership disputes. The deliverable should be a sequence of ordered questions, with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer to each; it should specify where the procedure terminates with a denial of ownership, where it terminates with an acknowledgment of ownership, and where it must defer to human judgment because the issue is normative rather than analytical. The procedure should include a small diagnostic toolkit—specific sentence patterns or logical tests reviewers can apply—to distinguish true binding nodes from implicit aggregation, and it should flag cases in which a binding node is present but unstated. Please apply the procedure to two proposals: first, a research team that fed outputs from existing climate models directly into existing epidemiological models without altering the internal logic of either; second, a team that argued the concept of noise in information theory must be reconceptualized through the lens of biological repair mechanisms, with explicit revisions to the classification rules in both fields. One of these applications should yield a result that the originators will find unwelcome. Finally, please indicate how confident you are in the analysis as a whole, and identify the specific step in the procedure about which you are least certain.
