I have no academic background in philosophy or psychology, but I have spent years thinking on my own about what emotions really are — whether they are judgments we make about the world, perceptions of something important, or simply feelings that arise in the body. At the same time, I have repaired mechanical watches for about fifteen years, and that is the vocabulary I actually think in: mainsprings and their tension, escapements that release energy in discrete beats, balance wheels oscillating back and forth, jewel bearings reducing friction, gear trains transmitting force, and the calibration that keeps everything in time.

Please restate the three main philosophical positions on emotion — that they are evaluative judgments about what matters, that they are perceptions of value or of bodily change, and that they are conscious feelings of bodily disturbance — using only the concepts and relations of watch mechanics. Do not treat the watch parts as loose metaphors; try to express what each philosophical claim actually says, translated into terms of tension, release, oscillation, transmission, lubrication, wear, and calibration.

For each of the three, report the residue: what translates cleanly, what only fits if you distort either the philosophy or the mechanics, and what has no expression at all in this vocabulary. Then tell me whether the parts that do not survive point to a real limitation in the philosophical accounts — something they fail to capture that watch mechanics can name — or whether they only show that comparing a person to a watch is fundamentally the wrong kind of comparison.

Please write in British English. End with a single line beginning 'Summary:' followed by a one-sentence verdict on whether the watchmaking framework reveals a genuine blind spot in the philosophical debate about emotion.
