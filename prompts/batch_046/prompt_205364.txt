I take the defensible core of the “right to the city” to be neither mobility nor housing supply, but what I will call spatial continuity: the interest in maintaining one’s practical and social identity across urban change without being forced to relocate or adopt an alien mode of life. From this, zoning that protects existing residents’ continuity is permissible, while zoning that prevents newcomers from establishing their own is not. The American attitude captures continuity but conflates it with property-value stasis and excludes newcomers; the European attitude captures openness but risks dissolving the specific attachments that make continuity meaningful. What would genuinely count against this framing? Identify the empirical findings about displacement, the normative principles (e.g., equality of opportunity, efficiency, strong property rights), and the institutional realities of zoning practice that would damage it; distinguish fatal defeaters from merely awkward ones; say which objections a defender of spatial continuity should concede and which can be absorbed by refining the concept; and assess whether the view is actually exposed or largely insulated by its qualifications. Respond in one paragraph, at most 250 words.
