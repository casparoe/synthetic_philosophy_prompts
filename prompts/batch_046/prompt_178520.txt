In my philosophy of medicine working group, the debate over whether killing and letting die differ in moral weight has stalled despite agreement on the clinical facts: both sides accept that withholding ventilation and administering a lethal dose can produce identical outcomes with identical intentions. One side insists the distinction tracks a real difference in the agent's causal contribution—doing versus allowing—while the other insists it collapses into a culturally variable norm about directness, with no independent metaphysical basis. My hypothesis is that the deadlock is not ethical but metaphysical: the two sides presuppose incompatible accounts of whether causation can be decomposed into "doing" and "allowing" independently of a normative baseline. Is that right, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about physician intuition, or a conceptual disagreement about what counts as a baseline, actually driving the impasse? I want a diagnosis that engages what each side actually says about agency and baseline-dependency, not a survey of positions. Given your diagnosis, what—if anything—would count as progress: a formal model of causal contribution, a conceptual clarification of baseline, or should we expect this particular disagreement never to resolve, and why?
