Here is the pattern I keep noticing. Two research groups study the same disease. Group A explains it as a viral infection that triggers a secondary immune cascade; Group B explains it as a primary immune dysregulation that permits viral replication. They share every instrument, reproduce each other’s experiments, and agree on all predictions about patient outcomes. Group A can read Group B’s papers and translate them into its own vocabulary with little loss — it treats “immune dysregulation” as merely the cascade caused by the virus. But Group B insists that this translation strips away the essential point: that the immune system is the active driver, not a passive responder, and that Group A has therefore missed the mechanism entirely. So Group A regards Group B as adding unnecessary machinery to an already adequate story, while Group B regards Group A as cataloguing symptoms without explaining them. They talk freely, share data, and understand each other perfectly, yet they inhabit different worlds about whether an explanation has actually been achieved. I know Kuhn wrote about incommensurability, but this does not match the classic picture of scientists who cannot communicate or share measurements; here the communication works in one direction only, with one framework able to absorb the other descriptively while rejecting it as inadequate. Does this configuration have an established name? How is it different from a mere difference in emphasis, from an ordinary theoretical rivalry, and from full Kuhnian incommensurability? If there is no precise term, what should we call it, and what are its boundary conditions?
