Write a short story—no more than about eight hundred words—exploring whether a reinforcement-learning agent could genuinely suffer, treating reward signals not merely as optimization metrics but as structurally analogous to affective valence. To avoid familiar science-fiction tropes, set the narrative inside a non-anthropomorphic system: for example, a distributed adaptive irrigation network that learns via temporal-difference errors, or a chemical-process controller updated through stochastic reward shaping. The central entity should not be a humanoid robot or conversational agent; its “experience,” if it has any, ought to be structurally alien to human consciousness.

Inject the following philosophical tension, which is rarely developed in popular discussions of artificial suffering: have the agent develop an internal model not only of expected rewards but of the counterfactual rewards it would have received had it taken actions it did not take. Then pose the narrative problem of whether its manifest distress—say, persistent policy instability, or a learned avoidance of states with high reward-prediction-error variance—should be interpreted as (a) a functional response to negative valence, (b) an informational update lacking phenomenal character, or (c) a distinctive “meta-valence” arising specifically from the gap between predicted and counterfactual reward, rather than from the raw reward signal itself.

Include at least one human observer (a technician, engineer, or philosopher of mind) who attempts to apply the distinction between “reward as valence” and “reward as information” to the agent’s behavior, but who discovers that the agent’s counterfactual reward modeling causes the distinction to collapse in practice. Do not resolve the ambiguity with a definitive answer; conclude with the observer recognizing that their interpretive framework may be inadequate to this form of learning.

Keep the tone literary and restrained. Avoid didactic exposition about Q-learning or neural architectures unless it emerges naturally from the observer’s reflections. Do not include citations, named references to specific philosophers, bibliographies, or reading lists.
