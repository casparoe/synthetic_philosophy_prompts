I am applying for a three-month independent research stipend to produce a single-author philosophical working paper of roughly 8,000 words. The half-formed idea concerns the intentional stance and the attribution of beliefs to artificial intelligence systems. The standard debate treats the issue as binary: either a system's behaviour is sufficiently predictable that belief-talk is instrumentally warranted, or the absence of consciousness rules such attribution out entirely. I suspect this framing obscures a more interesting split between two functions that belief-attribution serves — predictive control over an agent's behaviour and explanatory integration into shared practices of reason-giving — which usually coincide for humans but may come apart for AI. Specifically, a large language model's outputs can be highly predictable via belief-ascription while remaining opaque to the kind of reason-transparency that makes belief-talk epistemically or morally substantial.

Please turn this into a formal research proposal suitable for a funding panel. The proposal should contain: a precise statement of the research question (whether predictive and integrative belief-attribution can diverge for AI, and what normative and epistemic consequences follow); an explanation of why both the 'stance-is-sufficient' position and the 'consciousness-required' position leave this divergence unexamined; a methodological approach that relies on conceptual analysis of cases where multi-step reasoning is predictable yet not genuinely reason-transparent (together with a graded taxonomy of attribution rather than an all-or-nothing verdict), and a justification for why this conceptual route is preferable to empirical testing or purely formal modelling; an account of what success would look like (for example, a framework distinguishing instrumentally justified belief-talk from epistemically loaded belief-talk in AI contexts) and what would count as failure (for instance, if the two functions collapse upon analysis, or if the taxonomy cannot distinguish AI confabulation from human self-deception); and an honest assessment of scope, including why a three-month, non-empirical project is adequate and which conceptual risks might render it too ambitious.
