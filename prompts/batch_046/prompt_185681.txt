I recently read a summary of populist political theory in a popular essay that struck me as both insightful and suspiciously tidy. The author wrote that populism rests on three interconnected claims: first, that the people form a homogeneous moral community with a single, pre-political will that exists independently of any institutional process; second, that this will is epistemically transparent, meaning that ordinary citizens collectively and immediately grasp what serves their common good without the mediation of deliberation, expertise, or representative procedures; and third, that any institutional filter—whether parliamentary debate, constitutional review, or bureaucratic administration—is therefore not merely inefficient but actively illegitimate, because it substitutes an artificial construction for an authentic, already-given truth. The summary then argued that serious populist thinkers implicitly treat the people as a corporate agent analogous to an individual person, possessing intentions and beliefs in a literal rather than metaphorical sense. From this, the author concluded that once one rejects the metaphysical possibility of a collective corporate agent, populism collapses into a bare majoritarianism that offers no principled protection for minorities, and that this internal contradiction is sufficient to refute the view.

I suspect this account may conflate distinct strands of populist thought—perhaps a normative claim about sovereignty with an empirical claim about political knowledge, or a critique of representation with a denial of pluralism altogether. I also wonder whether it fairly represents the position of thinkers who treat populism as a thin-centred ideology that can be attached to various substantive programmes, rather than as a full metaphysical doctrine about collective minds. Could you audit this summary for me? Please distinguish what is an accurate characterisation of positions found in serious populist theory, what is a subtle misrepresentation or an overstatement of a more modest claim, and whether the criticism about corporate agency genuinely undermines the view or attacks a straw position that populists need not hold. In particular, I would like you to examine whether there is a coherent intermediate position—one that denies epistemic transparency and rejects the corporate-agent metaphor, yet still maintains a normative priority of popular sovereignty over institutional mediation—that the summary has overlooked entirely.
