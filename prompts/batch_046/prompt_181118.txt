As a retired biophysicist who spent decades studying non-equilibrium thermodynamics in living systems, I have recently turned to philosophy and find discussions of death either too detached from biological reality or too quick to invoke standard arguments without specifying what they must actually explain. Before I study the classic Epicurean claim that death is not harmful to the dead, or deprivation and time-relative interest theories, I want a rigorous specification of the desiderata any adequate account of whether death is bad for the one who dies must satisfy. I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of named texts; I want the criteria themselves, with the case or scientific consideration behind each.

Please write the full list of requirements. For each, state the phenomenon, boundary case, or conceptual distinction it must preserve, indicate whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and flag any pair or subset that you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable given ordinary metaphysical and logical constraints.

I want particular weight on the following considerations, which I take to be where genuine confusion lies.

First, the theory must address the asymmetry between pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence. It must explain why, if death is a harm, symmetrical reasoning does not render never having been born an equally grave harm, and it must do so without reducing the asymmetry to a mere psychological bias about temporal orientation.

Second, it must address the no-subject problem. If biological death terminates the psychological subject, the theory must either identify a persisting bearer of harm or provide a principled argument that harm in this case does not require one. It should distinguish harm to a biological organism, harm to a narrative or autobiographical self, and harm understood as a counterfactual comparison of life-trajectories in state-space.

Third, it must remain consistent with the biological reality that death is not an instantaneous event but a distributed process—anoxic cascade, neuronal depolarization, and organ-level failure occurring over different timescales. The theory should specify at which point in this process, if any, the harm is located, and whether psychological death—irreversible loss of consciousness and autobiographical continuity—can occur separately from biological death, with different normative implications.

Fourth, it must preserve the distinction between harm to the deceased and harm to survivors or dependents. It must not conflate the badness of death for the dead person with the grief of the living, and it must explain whether posthumous interests, such as the interest in one's projects continuing, can be frustrated when there is no subject present to experience that frustration.

Fifth, it must be consistent with the view that non-existence is not an experiential state: one cannot suffer being dead. At the same time, it must account for the strong intuition that death can be a misfortune for the dead, not merely for those who outlive them.

Sixth, it must handle boundary and technological cases: reversible clinical death, cryopreservation with potential future revival, and gradual neurodegeneration that erases psychological identity before biological function ceases. The theory should say whether harm is tied to irreversibility, to permanent loss of consciousness, or to the termination of a specific low-entropy trajectory, and whether a revived or reconstructed individual can retroactively bear or nullify the harm.

Seventh, it must clarify whether the badness of death is intrinsic—death itself is an evil state—or purely deprivation-based—death is bad only because it prevents future goods. It should also specify the reference class for comparison: is death harmful relative to the actual life lived, relative to an optimal continuation, or relative to some counterfactual trajectory?

Then, identify any subset of these desiderata that is jointly unsatisfiable, particularly combinations that require both a persisting subject and the absence of experiential suffering, or combinations that require deprivation-based harm without a counterfactual bearer. Note which single requirement, in your judgment, eliminates the largest number of existing theoretical candidates.

Finally, score briefly—without relying on detailed knowledge of specific named works—how the classic Epicurean no-harm position, a straightforward deprivation account, and a time-relative interest account fare against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard; my purpose is to have the specification firmly in hand before I read further in the literature.
