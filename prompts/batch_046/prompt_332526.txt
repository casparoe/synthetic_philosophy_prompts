I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on philosophy of time and relativity, and we've been working through McTaggart's 1908 essay The Unreality of Time together with the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe. There's a passage near the middle of the essay where McTaggart claims that the B-series—relations of earlier and later—cannot by itself constitute change, since an event that is earlier than another never ceases to stand in that relation; genuine change requires something like the progressive determination of an event as present, then past, then further past, which he calls the A-series. He then argues that every event must possess all three A-determinations and that this is contradictory.

I'm struggling to see exactly how this argument interacts with the relativistic denial of absolute simultaneity. If the Rietdijk–Putnam reasoning shows that for any event E there are other events simultaneous with E in one inertial frame but not in another, then whether E counts as present seems frame-relative. Yet McTaggart treats presentness as an absolute, non-relational property. My specific difficulty is whether one might rescue a modified A-series by making presentness worldline-relative rather than frame-relative—so that each worldline carries its own continuous present that is not reducible to B-relations, yet avoids contradiction because no single event is assigned incompatible A-determinations along the same worldline. Or does McTaggart's contradiction still arise internally for each worldline?

Could you help me understand whether the passage is best read as ruling out any such localization, including a fibered version of the A-series indexed to worldlines rather than to global hyperplanes? I want to know whether the contradiction he identifies depends on treating the A-series as globally unified, or whether it already undermines any attempt to distribute presentness continuously along individual trajectories through Minkowski spacetime.
