As a journalist covering artificial intelligence and philosophy of mind, I have been observing a recurring failure mode in language models that sits adjacent to, but does not seem identical with, the classic symbol-grounding problem. The pattern runs like this: a system can deploy a concept—gravity, say, or thermal equilibrium—with remarkable symbolic sophistication. It generates accurate explanations, draws analogies across domains, corrects subtle misconceptions, and teaches the idea to others in fluent prose. Yet place that same system in a direct perceptual-motor loop—ask it to identify which of two falling objects will strike the ground first from raw visual input, with no linguistic mediation—and the concept appears to vanish entirely. The symbol is not merely unanchored; it is hyper-integrated within a self-sealing web of other symbols while being completely decoupled from sensory experience. This feels less like a uniform grounding deficit and more like a sharp split between symbolic richness and perceptual nullity. Is there an established name for this specific asymmetry—explanatory fluency paired with zero sensory-motor anchoring—or should it be folded into standard symbol-grounding failures? If it is genuinely distinct, how precisely does it differ from plain ungroundedness or from the absence of embodiment, and does recognizing it as its own phenomenon change how we ought to evaluate whether a system understands anything? Give me a committed answer: either identify the nearest established concept with clear boundary conditions, or coin and defend a term, specify what it excludes, and state plainly whether this distinction carries any real weight for assessing artificial cognition.
