As a journalist covering philosophy and public life, I'm preparing to moderate a ninety-minute seminar for an interdisciplinary reading circle—mostly attorneys, epidemiologists, and urban planners—who have never taken an ethics course but are fascinated by the boundary between duty and heroism. The topic is supererogation: actions that are morally exemplary yet not morally required, and the deeper puzzle of why moral systems seem to have floors without ceilings. I need you, acting as both a professional moral philosopher and an experienced pedagogue, to construct a teaching plan rather than deliver the explanation yourself. Before outlining the sequence, please begin with brief internal brainstorming: identify the conceptual dependencies—what must be secured before an audience can understand that morality can be satisfied without being maximized?

Then present the pedagogical architecture. First, specify the exact order in which the pieces must arrive: the distinction between the impermissible, the obligatory, and the supererogatory, and why this particular ordering prevents the audience from collapsing the third category into an especially demanding version of the second. Second, propose one concrete carrying example—drawn from institutional or civic life rather than the standard saint or battlefield narratives—explaining exactly what features it must possess and what emotional baggage it must lack to serve as a genuine analytical anchor rather than an admiration trigger. Third, map the predictable misconceptions: I anticipate they will confuse supererogation with "super-obligation," or assume that recognizing a moral ceiling implies indifference above it; tell me precisely where in the sequence each error should be intercepted. Fourth, design a single check-for-understanding question that reveals whether they have grasped the structural point about moral ceilings rather than merely memorized the vocabulary. Fifth, state explicitly what you will leave out—perhaps the formal debate over whether supererogation is compatible with act-utilitarianism, or the theological origins of the concept—so that the session does not overreach. The audience needs a framework they can apply to policy and professional ethics, not a bibliography. Please think carefully, avoid suggesting readings, and calibrate the plan for intelligent non-specialists.
