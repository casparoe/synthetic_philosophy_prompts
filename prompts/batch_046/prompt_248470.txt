I sit on the university's joint philosophy/math curriculum board and we have to vote on a weird proposal. A poli-sci grad program wants to make a required module called Constructive Sovereignty: Hobbes's Geometry and the Limits of Demonstrative Politics. The idea is that Hobbes's attempt to square the circle — and Wallis tearing it apart — isn't just a math fail but reveals his whole nominalist-constructivist view: geometric figures only exist because we construct them through motion, so the commonwealth is legitimate only if it's literally constructed by covenant, not discovered in nature. The proposal wants students to read Hobbes's geometric writings alongside his main political work to see why he thought politics could be as rigorous as geometry, and also why it kept failing.

Half the board (mostly philosophers) thinks this is brilliant — it shows that Hobbes's political authority depends on a very specifc, and actually wrong, philosophy of mathematics. The math side thinks it's embarrassing: we're teaching a failed quadrature proof as if it were a conceptual breakthrough, and they say Hobbes was just bad at geometry, full stop, and linking it to sovereignty is post-hoc rationalization.

We need a decision memo — approve the module, reject it, or approve with conditions — with actual reasoning the grad program can respond to. Include the strongest argument for the side that loses. Don't ask for a bibliography or citations, the board doesn't care about that. Also, the memo should be writen like we'd actually circulate it, not an essay.

One more thing: there's a niche interpretation floating around that Hobbes rejected infinitesimals in geometry not just for mathematical reasons but because he associated infinite divisibility with civil dissolution — like, if the body politic can be divided infinitely there's no sovereign unit. The proposal leans on this but it's not standard textbook Hobbes. I want the memo to address whether that interpretive move is defensable enough to build a required course around, or if it's too speculative.

Please draft it. And sorry if this is a bit rambling — I'm writing between meetings, definatly not my best prose.
