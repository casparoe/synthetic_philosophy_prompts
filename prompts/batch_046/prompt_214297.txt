I'm currently preparing a long-form feature on the boundaries of utilitarian aggregation, and I've been revisiting the classic challenge posed by the utility monster—the hypothetical being that derives such immense satisfaction from resources that standard utilitarian calculus demands we sacrifice the welfare of countless ordinary people to feed it. For my piece, I'm interested in pushing this thought experiment into territory that hasn't been exhausted by decades of undergraduate debate, so I'm exploring a conceptual variant in which the monstrous utility is not localized in a single consciousness but emerges from a distributed network of interconnected agents, each node experiencing only modest well-being, yet the system as a whole generating astronomical aggregate utility through complex feedback loops between nodes.

Given that framing, could you give me an example of a conceptual issue that specifically arises when we try to apply traditional anti-utilitarian objections—such as concerns about distributive fairness, individual rights, or the separateness of persons—to this networked or emergent version of the utility monster? In particular, I'm trying to understand whether the moral problem lies in the magnitude of pleasure produced, in the architecture of its production, or in the fact that no single identifiable subject can claim the surplus welfare as its own, and I would value an example that isolates one of these tensions with some precision.
