I had a long and somewhat heated discussion last night with a close friend about whether incommensurable values can ever be rationally adjudicated in institutional settings, and I tried to sketch an essay arguing that the standard philosophical taxonomy is missing a distinct layer. The essay distinguishes three forms of value conflict: metric incommensurability, where no common quantitative scale applies; ordinal incommensurability, where we lack a rational ranking of the options; and what I am provisionally calling translation incommensurability, where the very attempt to render two values comparable within a single decision framework corrupts the internal logic of at least one value. I apply this to a concrete planning dilemma: a municipal board must choose between preserving an informal community garden that embodies relational equality and situated local knowledge, versus constructing a climate-adaptive housing block that serves distributive justice and intergenerational security. My claim is that framing this as a trade-off, or even as a case of Ruth Chang’s hard choices between values on a par, still presupposes a value-currency that can travel across domains without distortion. But here, quantifying social cohesion through contingent-valuation methods or social-capital metrics does not merely fail to capture it; it actively transforms the kind of good that cohesion is, producing what I call metric corruption. I want the essay to defend the view that some conflicts are not just hard but structurally resistant to comparison, without collapsing into simple pluralism or decisionist arbitrariness.
