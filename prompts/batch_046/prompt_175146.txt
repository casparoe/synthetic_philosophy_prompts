I've been thinking on my own about why human extinction seems worse than just the sum of all the deaths it would cause. I recently came across the philosopher Samuel Scheffler's argument—what he calls the afterlife conjecture—that a lot of what we care about, from raising children to pursuing long-term projects, only makes sense because we assume human life will continue after we're gone. That forward-looking point resonates with me. But I've also been wondering about a different, maybe stranger reason: it seems like past generations made enormous sacrifices—enduring wars, labor, and suffering—partly with the implicit expectation that eventually, far in the future, there would be people who could understand, honor, and complete the story of what they went through. If humanity were to go extinct, it wouldn't just be that those future people are missing; it would be that there's no one left to serve as the final audience or interpreter for everything that came before. The dead wouldn't be harmed in any direct sense, but the arc they were building toward gets permanently cut off.

Could you please argue that this backward-looking reason—the breaking of an implicit covenant that human achievement and suffering deserve some eventual witness or justification—is a real and independent reason why extinction is bad, separate from both the immediate deaths involved and from Scheffler's forward-looking point about our present values? I'd like you to defend this as genuinely important, not just poetic, and address the obvious objection that meaning shouldn't depend on having a future audience to recognize it. Please make the case without relying on standard arguments about lost future lives or potential, and try to show why this particular kind of loss is unique to human extinction rather than something that applies to any species dying out.
