I have recently been appointed to the University Library’s Committee on Digital Preservation and Curatorial Identity, and the assignment has forced me to take seriously philosophical problems I had previously treated only in the abstract. Over the past fifteen years our Special Collections division has gradually replaced every physical page of the philosopher Helena Voss’s working notebooks with digitized reconstructions; the originals were destroyed in a climate-controlled storage failure in 2011, and conservators rebuilt the corpus page by page from microfilm, spectral imaging, and, in the final phase, predictive linguistic models that infer illegible text from surrounding contextual patterns. The committee must now decide, before the research portal opens next quarter, whether to catalog the reconstructed corpus as the authoritative Voss Archive—entitled to the same citation status and institutional weight as the lost originals—or to classify it as a derivative work, Archive Voss-R, thereby signaling to scholars that its contents are mediated and possibly contain non-authorial material. Because my own research concerns metaphysical persistence and identity, I feel a particular obligation to think this through carefully, but I want the recommendation grounded in what is genuinely settled in conservation practice and what remains philosophically contested, rather than presented as if there were a consensus where none exists.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—either full authoritative designation with specified access conditions, or derivative classification with an explicit protocol for marking algorithmically inferred passages—and include a clear implementation time frame, since the portal launches in six weeks. In your reasoning, treat the strongest consideration for the opposing view with real seriousness: if you recommend authoritative status, you must engage the worry that predictive reconstruction has introduced textual content with no original authorial presence; if you recommend derivative status, you must engage the worry that functional, historical, and institutional continuity have already made this reconstructed corpus the only living vessel of Voss’s thought. Write the entire response in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would write a formal letter or memo. And please separate clearly between points about archival preservation that are well established among conservators and points about identity and persistence that remain genuinely disputed among philosophers, without presenting any contested philosophical position as though it were the settled consensus.
