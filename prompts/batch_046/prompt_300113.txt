I'm working through some old notes on why people reject aggregating happiness across different lives, and I keep running into a pattern that doesn't quite match the standard objections I read about. Usually people talk about the seperate-ness thing — you can't hurt Alice to help Bob because they're different centers of consciousness. But there's this other move people make in population cases: they imagine a choice where you either preserve one existing person with an okay life, or you let that person fade out and create two new people with slightly better lives. The objector acts like you've violated some boundary not because anyone was harmed, but because you've treated the "slot" that Alice occupied as something that can be liquidated and replaced by multiple slots with higher combined value. It's not the non-identity problem, since nobody's comparing lives within the same identity; it's more like an intuition that personal boundaries shouldn't be fungible across generations. Does this have a specific name in the literature — maybe a variant of the separateness objection, or something from person-affecting ethics? If not, what's the closest established concept, and how exactly does this differ? I just want the carving, not a bare label. Please keep it tight: max 180 words, one paragraph if you can.
