I'm giving the opening remarks at a workshop next Tuesday — it's called Language Games and Life Forms, held in a church hall that usually hosts chess clubs. Half the room are analitic philosophers of religion, half are local clergy who volenteered the space and probably think we're a bit odd. I want the talk to turn on Hilary Putnam's idea that religious belief isn't a botched scientific explanation but a practical commitment that shapes what counts as real for you, a bit like the way a musician treats a score not as a description of sounds but as rules for making them. But I also want you to weave in this comparison I just cooked up — call it the broken compass idea: a religious framework can be wrong about directions in one empirical sense, it doesn't point to an objective north, and still be the only thing that tells you where you're actually standing. Write me about eight minutes of remarks that don't patronise either side, avoid anything Putnam would have called supernaturalist nonsense, and don't treat the clergy like case studies. Keep it casual, like chatting over coffee, but it needs to do real work — the audience should leave feeling that the idea actually constrains what you can say about belief, not just decorates it.
