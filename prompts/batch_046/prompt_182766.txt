I am a doctoral student in philosophy of law serving as the graduate representative on the State Department of Corrections’ Pilot Facility Oversight Board. The board must issue a formal recommendation to the Commissioner regarding the proposed Meridian Unit, a 36-month pilot at a 40-bed rural facility. The proposal explicitly adopts Norwegian-style normalization—open architecture, ununiformed staff-inmate interaction, vocational programming, and minimal security barriers—yet it rejects both the Scandinavian rehabilitative rationale and the American retributive logic that underwrites mass incarceration. Instead, it operates under a novel “restricted-agency” framework: incarceration is defined not as the infliction of suffering for its own sake, nor as a therapeutic intervention to correct character, but as a temporary, proportionate suspension of specific civic capacities (electoral participation, unrestricted movement, full contractual liberty). The facility’s sole institutional obligation is to protect the inmate’s remaining moral agency and to construct conditions for the gradual restoration of those suspended capacities. Success is to be evaluated not by recidivism rates, but by an independent ethics panel’s structured assessment of practical-reasoning recovery—specifically, the inmate’s demonstrated ability to navigate complex normative choices without coercive scaffolding. A further feature requires each participant to co-author an individualized capacity-restoration plan, which raises immediate concerns about whether meaningful consent is possible within a coercive carceral context. The board is split: one faction contends that the model evades the hard moral accounting that retributivism demands, substituting a philosophical abstraction for justified punishment and masking state coercion behind the language of autonomy; the opposing faction contends that it covertly imports rehabilitative norms—treating inmates as deficient agents in need of restoration—while imposing a contested metaphysics of moral agency on a captive population. Please draft the board’s formal decision memo—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—addressed to the Commissioner. The memo should present a clear, actionable recommendation; provide reasoning that the proposal’s architects and state administrators could genuinely respond to, avoiding purely abstract theoretical dismissal; and include an honest, substantive account of the strongest consideration on the losing side. If you recommend conditional approval, specify the institutional safeguards, conceptual clarifications, or evaluation protocols that must be in place before implementation. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; the board requires deliberative reasoning it can act upon directly.
