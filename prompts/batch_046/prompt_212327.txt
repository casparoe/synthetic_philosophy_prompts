I'm presenting at the history dept colloquium next thursday — audience is mostly imperial historians and early americanists who think presentism is bad scholarship but also that the man of his time defense is intellectually lazy. My thesis is what I call moral trajectory analysis: dont ask if Jefferson, Churchill, or Columbus was good by modern standards, and dont excuse them with context alone; instead ask whether their specific choices expanded or contracted the moral options available in their societies relative to real alternatives they knew about. So Jefferson isnt just a slaveholder of his era — he actively suppressed abolitionist petitions that were circulating in Virginia. Churchill resisted fascism when other elites appeased, but also ordered the Bengal famine policies that had alternatives. Columbus isnt just of his time — there were models of encounter that didnt involve enslavement, and he chose the worst one.

The audience will object that I'm doing philosophy not history, that I'm inventing counterfactuals without archival backing, and that this is just culture-war framing in academic drag. Also what should I concede up front — probably that this requires counterfactual reasoning historians hate? And whats the one reply that saves the talk? Plus the objection I'm hoping nobody asks — maybe about figures where we genuinely dont know the alternatives, or whether this framework secretly assumes a progressive teleology I'm claiming to avoid. Write it casual, like I'm talking to a friend who knows the field.
