Two grad students in my seminar got stuck arguing about Newton/Leibniz and then Darwin/Wallace. (1) He said simultaneous invention proves discoveries are inevitable: if calculus hadn't shown up in 1700, it would've by 1720, so fighting over priority is mostly ego. (2) She said that misses the point -- Newton's fluxions were tied to physical motion in ways that shaped later physics differently than Leibniz's notation, and Darwin bundled natural selection with specific commitments about gradual change and common descent that Wallace framed somewhat differently. So they didn't discover "the same thing" twice; they built overlapping but distinct theoretical packages. (3) He replied that's just definitional gerrymandering: define the core narrowly enough and of course it looks contingent, but the functional heart -- differential calculus, selection pressure -- was sitting there waiting to be found, and the rest is presentation. (4) She came back that if packaging doesn't matter, why do we still teach Leibniz's notation and Darwin's specific formulation? History settled on particular versions, which suggests contingency runs all the way down, and inevitability is just hindsight bias wearing a lab coat. Please adjudicate move by move -- rate each from clearly wrong to clearly right, briefly justify, and tell me whether the inevitability debate actually affects how we should assign scientific credit, or if it's metaphysics with no practical bite.
