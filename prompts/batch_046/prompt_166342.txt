In my reporting on moral philosophy over the past several years, I have observed the debate over Scanlonian contractualism and the aggregation problem settle into what appears to be a permanent stalemate. Contractualists hold that an act is wrong if it is governed by a principle that at least one person could reasonably reject, and they have consistently argued that this framework blocks the aggregation of small harms to many into justification for imposing a large harm on one. Critics counter that this resistance to aggregation makes contractualism implausibly rigid—unable to justify saving five strangers at the cost of one limb, for instance—unless the framework smuggles in consequentialist weighing through the back door of "reasonable rejection" conditions. Despite extensive work formalizing rejection conditions, comparing pairwise versus group-level reasoning, and testing boundary cases involving heterogeneous moral patients, neither side has shifted. My hypothesis is that the disagreement is not hidden-empirical—no psychological or sociological finding seems likely to break the deadlock—nor is it purely verbal, since both camps share a common understanding of what counts as a principle and what counts as reasonable. Instead, I suspect the impasse is bedrock and structural: the contractualists begin with a bilateral, pairwise moral relation that treats aggregation as a category mistake, while the critics begin with a collective standpoint that treats failure to aggregate as a failure of moral arithmetic, and neither side has independent, non-circular arguments for its foundational framing. Is this the right diagnosis? Could there instead be a concealed formal issue—say, about whether rejection relations are transitive across groups with non-comparable complaints, or about whether contractualism requires a single representative agent whose reasonableness is being assessed—that has been masked by the standard trolley-style formulations? And given whatever diagnosis you offer, what would actually constitute progress: a distinction between institutional and interpersonal morality, a formal result about the consistency conditions on pairwise rejection, or an argument that the question is ill-posed because it conflates two different moral tasks?
