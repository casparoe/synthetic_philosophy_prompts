As a journalist covering cognitive science and philosophy of mind, I have been trying to formalize my own philosophical commitments for an upcoming piece on whether the self can be distributed across brains. Here is the profile I have reached, and I want to know whether it holds together or whether I am borrowing incompatible motivations from different traditions.

(1) I accept a Hofstadter-style patternist view: the I is a strange loop of self-reference rather than a Cartesian substance, and in principle it could be instantiated in a different substrate without losing its essential character.

(2) I believe this pattern can genuinely persist, in an attenuated form, in other people. When close friends or long-term collaborators deeply internalize my values, reasoning habits, and emotional responses, part of my self-pattern continues running in their brains. This is literal persistence, not metaphor.

(3) Within one life, I hold that survival requires causal-psychological continuity of the right kind. Gradual replacement of my neurons with functionally equivalent synthetic ones preserves me, but a sudden scan-and-instantiate procedure that lacks a continuous causal chain linking the original process does not yield my continuation.

(4) So if two perfect digital copies of my brain-state were activated simultaneously, neither is the true continuation of me. Both are new instantiations of my pattern; the original stream is either extinguished or arbitrarily assigned only if there is a unique causal trajectory, which there is not.

(5) I am a physicalist about phenomenal consciousness: there is no non-physical ego. A philosophical zombie with my exact neural structure but no inner experience would behave identically yet lack a self entirely, because the strange loop requires actual subjective realization to be more than a hollow simulation.

(6) Finally, I maintain that if two living brains were linked by a sustained, high-bandwidth interface that merged decision-making processes, this would not produce a single collective we. Each brain would still host a distinct I with overlapping contents, because first-person perspective is tied to a singular locus of integration rather than to the distribution of information itself.

Do these hang together? I am especially concerned about the relation between (2) and (3): (2) allows my pattern to survive in others without direct causal linkage from my brain to theirs, while (3) demands causal continuity for identity across time. Is there a principled distinction—say, between replication and continuation—that saves both, or am I applying a double standard? Does (4) sit uneasily with (1)'s substrate independence, given that substrate independence seems to license copying? Is (6) consistent with (2), or does allowing patterns to be shared across brains without dissolving the self commit me to rejecting merged consciousness on arbitrary grounds?

Please audit the set. Rank the tensions by severity, and distinguish real conflicts from merely apparent ones—particularly whether (5) creates tension with (1) that dissolves once phenomenal realization is treated as a constraint on pattern instantiation rather than an extra ingredient. For each genuine tension, identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and say exactly what I would have to give up conceptually. I am not asking for a general survey of personal identity theory; I want to know whether this particular package is jointly tenable and, if not, which single change cleans up the most problems.
