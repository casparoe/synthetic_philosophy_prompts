Consider a government retirement-enrollment program that operates with two distinct arms. In the first arm, the choice architecture—defaults, framing, and option ordering—is left entirely untouched, but citizens are required to complete a brief, interactive training module in probabilistic reasoning and compound-interest computation before they may select a plan. The agency defends this arm not as steering behavior but as improving the quality of autonomous choice, insisting that it respects preferences more thoroughly than classic nudging does because it exploits no cognitive bias. In the second arm, the agency uses standard opt-out defaults and framing adjustments in other domains, justifying those by the same appeal to autonomy-preserving influence. What philosophical commitments about autonomy, rational agency, and the boundary between educating preferences and forming them does this bifurcated institutional practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the person and of state legitimacy embedded in the contrast between untouched architecture plus mandatory cognitive training, on the one hand, and altered architecture without training, on the other. Is the practice philosophically consistent, or do its two arms presuppose rival conceptions—perhaps one treating autonomy as competence-dependent and the other treating it as purely procedural? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate what would have to change in the institutional design if the underlying conception of autonomy were replaced by an account that treats preference-formation as entirely private and competence-independent.
