I'm a doctoral student working at the intersection of ancient philosophy and contemporary metaphysics, with a particular focus on how normative properties depend on non-normative bases. I've been returning to Plato's Euthyphro, specifically the exchange in which Socrates presses Euthyphro on whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or whether it is pious because it is loved—what we now call the Euthyphro dilemma. Socrates seems to argue that if being loved is only a passive affection that befalls the pious, then the gods' love cannot constitute piety's essence; yet if piety is defined solely by being loved, then it becomes arbitrary, contingent on divine whim rather than anchored in anything independent.

I'm trying to understand whether this passage can be read as anticipating modern debates about metaphysical grounding: in particular, whether Socrates is implicitly distinguishing explanatory dependence (the gods recognize and love piety because of its prior value) from constitutive dependence (piety exists only in virtue of being loved). Does the logical structure of the exchange genuinely support attributing to Socrates a concern with the direction of grounding, or is that an anachronistic imposition? I would appreciate help unpacking how the distinction between suffering an affection and being produced by it functions in the argument, whether Plato's framing risks the kind of circularity that contemporary grounding theorists warn against when a property is explained by its own manifestation, and where the analogy between divine love and metaphysical dependence breaks down.
