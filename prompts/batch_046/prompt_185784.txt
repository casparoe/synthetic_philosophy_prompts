I just spent several hours arguing with a friend about whether sentience is genuinely continuous or whether "degrees of moral status" is only a polite fiction that collapses into hidden binary cutoffs the moment anyone has to write a rule. She insists that once you operationalize gradation for actual governance—approve a protocol, restrict it, or reject it—you have implicitly drawn a line, and that pretending the line is "fuzzy" is worse than admitting it is arbitrary. I serve on an interdisciplinary research-ethics board that reviews protocols involving entities with uncertain experiential status: nociceptive insects, decapod crustaceans, mammalian brain organoids with sensory afferents, and synthetic architectures that replicate homeostatic feedback loops. We meet biweekly, have ninety minutes per case, no consciousness scientist present, and the chair—a molecular biologist—must sign off. I need a reusable decision procedure, grounded in whatever philosophy of mind and ethics actually knows about graded phenomenal experience and proportional moral weight, that takes the continuity view seriously rather than treating it as a rhetorical comfort. The deliverable should be a sequence of ordered tests or questions in words, specifying for each stage what counts as sufficient evidence, clear stopping points where the board issues a clear "no special restriction" or "full protection" verdict, and explicit handoff points where the procedure stops and human judgment must take over because the issue is one of values rather than assessment. The procedure must solve what my friend calls the thermostat problem: it needs criteria that can distinguish entities with minimal but real affective experience from entities—such as a synthetic climate-control system with integrated sensors and feedback adjustments—that meet functional analogues of those criteria but should clearly receive no protection, without simply defining the problem away by fiat. It must also handle the hard intermediate cases: an entity with high behavioral flexibility but ambiguous nociceptive architecture, and an entity with global information integration but no clear valence-related behavior or output. Apply the procedure to three cases: a decapod crustacean in a nociception study (where protection is expected, but show the exact steps), a cortical brain organoid with thalamic inputs but no motor or behavioral output (where I expect an uncomfortable, likely partial or ambiguous recommendation that my board will dislike), and the synthetic homeostatic climate-regulation system (where protection should be denied, and I want to see precisely which filter excludes it). The procedure should say explicitly what it cannot do—for instance, whether two entities with identical assessed scores can be treated differently for resource or feasibility reasons, whether temporal integration can be assessed without behavioral proxies, and whether the framework genuinely avoids the collapse-into-binary objection or merely disguises it with extra steps. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; the board needs something it can run without a philosophy library.
