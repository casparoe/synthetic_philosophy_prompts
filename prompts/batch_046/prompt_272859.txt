Compare Thomas Nagel's notion of resultant moral luck with the concept of moral risk—namely, the ethical evaluation of an agent for generating a hazardous condition irrespective of whether the harm actually materializes. Does treating risk-imposition as carrying independent moral weight eliminate resultant luck altogether, or does it only displace the contingency onto a different feature of the agent's situation? In addressing this, consider how the comparison bears on Bernard Williams's thesis that constitutive luck is more fundamental than causal luck, and specify where in the analysis the control principle does its heaviest work.
