I'm preparing questions for a podcast interview with a philosopher of mind—let's call her Dr. Vance—who has spent the last decade defending a version of Dennett's multiple-drafts model. Her specific twist, developed in recent talks and essays, is to treat the Cartesian theater not merely as a spatial fallacy (the homunculus sitting somewhere in the brain) but as a temporal-binding fallacy: the assumption that consciousness requires a single, globally synchronized present at which all sensory inputs are presented to a central audience. She argues that what we call the present is actually a draft coalition, a temporarily stable alliance among competing narrative streams rather than a synchronized broadcast, and that predictive-processing architectures support this because prediction errors are resolved locally and asynchronously, with no need for a global workspace clock.

I'd like a sequence of six to eight interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate Dennett. The questions should turn on the actual mechanics of her position: how draft coalitions form and dissolve without a central arbiter; whether her rejection of temporal binding undermines the phenomenology of a unified now; and how her view handles temporal anomalies such as postdictive effects like the color phi phenomenon, or Libet-style readiness potentials that seem to show neural precursors of conscious decisions. For each major line of questioning, include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give—especially her tendency to dismiss objections by saying they smuggle the theater back in through some unnoticed assumption.

In particular, include at least one question that presses her on the boundary between conscious and unconscious drafts in predictive-processing terms: if drafts are just high-level predictions being updated, what distinguishes a conscious draft from an unconscious prediction error? The follow-up should ask whether her answer risks making consciousness either ubiquitous across all predictive systems or epiphenomenal with respect to behavior. I also want one question about artificial systems: if a large language model produces coherent narrative streams through asynchronous token prediction without any global workspace, does that mean it instantiates draft coalitions, or is there a principled reason biological brains do something categorically different?

Finally, end with a question about what kind of empirical finding—specifically in the study of temporal integration, neural synchronization, or postdictive perception—would actually threaten her view rather than being reinterpreted as just another competing draft. The questions should be rigorous enough for a rationalist-leaning audience familiar with philosophy of mind and cognitive science, but they should not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographic recommendations.
