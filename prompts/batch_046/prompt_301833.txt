As a physicist who came to philosophy late in life, I have been trying to formalize a worry about the Löwenheim-Skolem result and its bearing on scientific realism. Below is an argument I have constructed; it seems to prove that if we take first-order formalization seriously as the ontology of physics, then the distinction between discrete and continuous structures collapses in a way that undermines our best theories. I want to know exactly where it fails.

(1) If a physical theory is consistent and expressible in first-order logic with identity, then by the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem it has a countable model.

(2) Our best current physics—approximated by first-order axiomatizations of quantum field theory and general relativity—is consistent, or at least not known to be inconsistent.

(3) So there exists a countable model M in which every physical entity—spacetime point, field value, particle state—belongs to a countable domain.

(4) Within M, there are sets (for instance, the set of real-valued field configurations over a region) that M proves are uncountable: no function inside M bijects them with the natural numbers.

(5) From an external, metatheoretic standpoint, however, the domain of M is countable, and every subset of it—including those that M calls uncountable—is also countable.

(6) Scientific realism holds that the ontology of our best theory describes reality. If the theory is true in M, then reality is M.

(7) Therefore reality contains structures that are genuinely uncountable from the only perspective available inside reality, yet genuinely countable from an impossible external viewpoint.

(8) But scientific reasoning can only appeal to internal resources; there is no outside observer to privilege the external countability.

(9) Hence, within scientific practice we are committed both to the continuity of spacetime and fields (uncountability internal) and to the claim that everything is ultimately enumerable (countability external), with no non-arbitrary way to choose.

(10) This makes the continuous versus discrete debate empirically empty and undermines the realist claim that our theories describe an objective structure independent of formalization choices.

(11) Yet that debate is clearly not empty—experiments distinguish discrete spectra from continuous ones—so the argument must go wrong somewhere.

Which premise should I abandon, and what does abandoning it cost? I am especially reluctant to surrender (6) or (8), since they seem to capture the core commitments of scientific realism and internalism. If I keep both, must I reject (1) by denying that first-order logic is the right framework for physical ontology? Or is there a hidden equivocation between being countable in the model and being countable in reality?
