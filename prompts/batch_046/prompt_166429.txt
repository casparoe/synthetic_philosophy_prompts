I've been arguing with my roommate about whether morality is just a bunch of context-sensitive gut checks or if there's actually a hidden rulebook behind them — he's taking a course on particularism and thinks I'm a walking example of it, but I'm definately not convinced. The whole thing became personal for me last week because I'm helping my sister navigate some messy family obligations, and I realized I keep giving totally different answers about what's okay depending on who I'm talking to and what mood I'm in, so I want to pressure-test my own gut.

Here are my verdicts. One: breaking a promise to feed my neighbor's cat while they're away feels wrong to me even if the cat is fine and nobody finds out — it's the broken promise itself, not the consequence. Two: but if I promised my friend I'd never reveal her old bankruptcy, and now her fiancee is about to cosign on a loan without knowing, breaking that promise to warn him feels not just okay but like the promise never really had weight in that context. Three: telling my kid her drawing is beautiful when it's a scribble seems totally fine, almost kind; telling my grad-student friend that her incoherent thesis chapter is brilliant feels like a real betrayal, even though both are false praise about competence. Four: slowly ghosting someone over months feels like a deeper moral failure to me than suddenly cutting them off right after you discover they've lied — the gradual fade feels cowardly and deceptive, while the sudden break feels like a clean defense. Five: but gradually telling a patient with a bad prognosis the truth over several conversations feels right, way better than dumping everything on them at once. Six: taking the last bus seat when you're able-bodied but running a fever feels okay; taking the last food packet at a shelter when you're just a little hungry and others are starving feels wrong, even though in both cases you're technically able to do without.

I'm tempted by something like "the moral weight of any feature depends on its relational role — protection, deception, care — within the specific bond," but that might just be particularism with extra steps. Is there actually a generalist principle that saves all six without turning into an ad-hoc laundry list? If not, which of my verdicts should I drop? Also, please say how confident you are in whatever you come up with, and point out the one part you're least sure about — I have a feeling the ghosting vs. gradual-truth cases are going to break whatever framework you propose. And what does your principle predict for the case I keep going back and forth on: gradually reducing a chronic-pain patient's placebo dosage over weeks without telling them, where the deception itself is spread out over time?
