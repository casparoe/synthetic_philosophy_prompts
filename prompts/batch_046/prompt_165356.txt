I serve on the Continuity and Endowment Review Board of the Meridian Research Foundation. Our charter obliges us to determine whether a beneficiary named in a 1950 perpetual endowment agreement still exists as “the same entity,” or whether complete turnover of its constituent parts has dissolved the original recipient and triggered reversion of the funds to the university.

The concrete case: The Institute for Analog Computation (IAC) was founded in 1950 with an endowed chair, a dedicated physical plant, and a specific research mission. By 2020 every original researcher had retired or died; the original building was demolished and replaced with a quantum-information laboratory in 2008; the current staff, board, and scientific agenda share no overlap with the 1950 cohort. Only the legal charter, tax-exempt identifier, and an unbroken chain of administrative records link the present organization to the original. The present leadership argues they are the same institution—merely evolved—so the endowment should remain intact. The university trustees counter that if institutional identity requires the endurance of some core substrate (personnel, physical plant, or substantive mission), then the IAC has ceased to exist and the funds revert.

The board must issue a formal written opinion recommending one of the following: (1) affirm substantive continuity and retain the endowment; (2) declare dissolution and revert the funds; or (3) approve retention subject to binding conditions (for example, a formal continuity covenant that fixes essential institutional properties going forward). The memo should present the recommendation with reasons the researchers and trustees could actually respond to, should treat seriously the strongest metaphysical argument on the losing side—specifically, whether a perdurantist analysis (treating the institute as a four-dimensional worm of causally and intentionally linked temporal parts) can ground identity without an enduring substrate, and whether an endurantist analysis can accommodate total part-replacement if some essential property (such as legal charter or genealogical record) is preserved—and should avoid bibliographic references, reading lists, or citation demands. Do not simply restate the legal definition of institutional continuity; engage the philosophical structure of persistence that the case embodies.

Please draft the board’s decision memo with a clear, actionable bottom line. End with a line beginning exactly “Verdict:” followed by your recommendation—retain, revert, or retain with conditions.
