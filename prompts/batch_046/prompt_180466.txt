I am a retired physicist who has recently begun attending a philosophy reading group composed mainly of former natural scientists. Next month I am to lead a single session on Avicenna's flying man argument, and I wish to avoid the conventional classroom presentation. Instead of opening with the familiar suspended-in-air scenario, I propose to frame the argument through a modified case: a scientist placed in total sensory deprivation who retains only interoceptive awareness of her heartbeat and respiration, having never experienced external sensation or bodily position. I need a teaching plan rather than the lecture itself. Please set out the order in which I must introduce the conceptual dependencies—specifically, the distinction between self-awareness and sensory knowledge, the irrelevance of bodily position, and the metaphysical claim that the soul is non-physical—so that each layer rests securely on the previous one. What single carrying example should anchor the discussion, and why ought I to avoid using the standard formulation in which eyes are covered and limbs are spread as the primary illustration? Which misconceptions should I anticipate from an audience trained in empirical verification—particularly the error of treating Avicenna as advancing a neuroscientific hypothesis about brain states rather than a metaphysical argument about the soul—and at exactly which stage in the sequence should I head off each one? Provide one precise question I can ask at the close to determine whether the group has understood the difference between affirming one's own existence and affirming any bodily property. Finally, indicate which parts of Avicenna's broader psychology should be left out at this level, and present your answer in British English.
