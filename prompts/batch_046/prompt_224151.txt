I am preparing for a philosophy of language seminar in which we are comparing epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness, and I have constructed an argument that seems to show the former collapses into the latter. I should like you to diagnose precisely where the reasoning fails, then price the alternatives.

Consider the following chain. (1) Epistemicism maintains that every vague predicate — take "bald" — has a sharp, unknown boundary: there is an exact number n of hairs such that a person is bald if and only if they possess at most n, and our ignorance of n is purely epistemic. (2) For any proposed boundary, there exist adjacent pairs of individuals who differ by only one hair, one falling on either side. (3) A tolerance principle governs our rational engagement with vagueness: if two cases differ only minimally in the dimension relevant to the predicate, we ought not assign them different statuses. (4) Consequently, for every pair near the unknown boundary, rational belief requires us to treat them alike — either both bald or both not bald. (5) Now apply a margin-for-error principle to belief-formation: if we rationally believe that a particular individual is not bald, then in all sufficiently nearby worlds (including those in which the unknown boundary shifts by one hair) that belief ought not be false. (6) Because the boundary's location is unknown, any belief about a specific near-boundary case risks being false in a nearby world where n is slightly different. (7) To avoid being rationally compelled into falsehood, we must therefore withhold belief about every case within the margin of error surrounding all candidate boundaries. (8) This universal withholding across the borderline region produces exactly the semantic profile supervaluationism describes: there is no fact of the matter about these cases. (9) Hence epistemicism, when combined with plausible constraints on rational belief, entails the absence of sharp boundaries — contradicting its defining commitment.

Which step ought to be surrendered? I am particularly reluctant to abandon (1) or (3), since giving up the sharp-boundary thesis abandons epistemicism altogether, whilst abandoning tolerance seems to licence wildly counter-intuitive distinctions between near-identical cases. If the flaw lies elsewhere — perhaps in applying the margin-for-error principle to beliefs about properties whose boundaries we are ignorant of, or in an equivocation between epistemic caution and semantic indeterminacy — please explain why that move looked tempting and what philosophical cost attends its rejection.
