The debate between counterfactual and interventionist theories of causation—roughly the Lewis-Rubin lineage of counterfactual dependence against Pearl’s structural-causal-model framework—has been in a holding pattern since at least the early 2000s. Every standard objection is catalogued: preemption breaks simple counterfactual analyses; interventionist accounts appear to presuppose the very causal structure they claim to ground; absence causation and symmetric overdetermination remain awkward for both sides. Yet neither camp treats these as fatal, and the literature mostly repeats the same moves with finer formalism rather than convergence.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not about causation itself, but about what an adequate causal explanation must deliver. Interventionists seem implicitly committed to the idea that a causal claim must license a specific decision-theoretic intervention—its value lies in control—while counterfactualists require that it survive a specific range of modal perturbations—its value lies in explanatory stability across worlds. So the two sides may be optimizing for different explanatory currencies without any shared metric for comparing them, which makes every objection from one side miss the point that matters to the other. Is that right, or is there a hidden formal disagreement—say, about the representation of exogenous variables, the semantics of nested counterfactuals, or the admissibility of probabilistic dependencies in causal graphs—that actually drives the split? Or is it a verbal dispute about whether the word causation should cover only manipulable relations?

I want a diagnosis, not a survey of interpretations. Use what each side actually says about concrete problem cases—preemption, redundant overdetermination, causal absence—to show whether they are answering different questions, using different standards for what counts as data, or genuinely disagreeing about formal representation. Tell me whether this is a substantive disagreement, a methodological divergence about the purpose of causal modeling, or an ill-posed question masked as one debate. And be honest about progress: would a formal result—such as a proof that one framework can represent every model in the other without loss of predictive or interventional content—actually resolve the dispute, or is the gap normative, about what we want causal knowledge for in science and policy? If it is normative, should we expect resolution at all, or should we treat the two frameworks as domain-specific tools with no obligation to unify them?
