While preparing my qualifying exam in philosophy of mind and cross-cultural cognition, I have become stuck on a conceptual tension that keeps resurfacing in my committee discussions. The tension concerns recent experimental findings showing that judgments about intentional action—specifically the asymmetry between harmful and beneficial side effects—diverge systematically between Western and East Asian respondent groups, and that these divergences persist even when vignettes are subjected to rigorous back-translation and presented under culturally neutral framing conditions. What troubles me is not merely the descriptive variability itself, but an interpretive claim I have encountered in the literature I am reviewing: that these differences are structurally anchored in grammatical differences in how agentive causation is encoded, rather than being superficial performance variations. From this, I find myself drawn to what feels like a destabilizing inference. If the intuitive core of "intentional action" is not cross-culturally invariant but instead fragments into locally indexed family-resemblance clusters shaped by linguistic architecture, then does this not undermine the very possibility of universal normative ethics? Specifically, if there is no shared conceptual substrate for intentional action across linguistic communities, on what basis can a theory of moral responsibility claim universal applicability rather than being merely indexed to particular grammatical worldviews? Is the project of universal moral responsibility therefore resting on a false presupposition of conceptual uniformity that descriptive cross-cultural psychology has now dissolved?
