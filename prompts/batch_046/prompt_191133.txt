Commentators often map Nietzsche's three modes of history — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — onto the departments of a museum: monumental history is the gallery of exemplary lives, antiquarian history is the conservation archive, and critical history is the deaccession process that removes forgeries and dead weight. The analogy is used to argue that a culture, like a well-run museum, needs all three in balanced proportion. Audit the mapping precisely: what plays the role of the curator, the physical collection, the visitor, and the funding authority? Identify where the analogy imports features Nietzsche rejects — for example, whether it treats the past as an inert possession to be displayed rather than a force that must either serve or poison present life. Then consider a disanalogy case: a society that practices pure critical history by continuously erasing and rewriting its archives in real time, leaving no stable antiquarian substrate. Does Nietzsche's framework predict this is liberating self-overcoming or a collapse into rootless presentism? Does the museum analogy illuminate or obscure that verdict? Finally, state whether the normative conclusion — that balance is required — survives the audit, or whether the analogy was smuggling it in.
