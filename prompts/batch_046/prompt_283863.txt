I'm working through a reconstructed version of Hume's argument in his essay on suicide, and I have come to accept a hybrid position that I think captures his spirit but sharpens the boundaries: the traditional objections all fail—there is no credible offense to God, no binding social contract that prohibits self-destruction, and no robust duty to preserve one's own existence—so suicide is permissible when continued life becomes genuinely burdensome, but only if the suffering is intrinsic to the agent's own embodied experience rather than extrinsic, arising primarily from another's projected grief or from social expectation. To make this precise, I treat intrinsic suffering as belonging to the agent's first-person phenomenology of continued existence, while extrinsic suffering is a second-order burden carried largely for the emotional stability of others; on this framing, only the former can justify the act without leaving what I call a moral remainder.

Play it straight: what would have to be true for this conclusion to be wrong? I want an honest assessment of its exposure—whether the intrinsic-extrinsic distinction collapses under conceptual pressure, whether Hume's broader commitment to social embeddedness undermines the moral isolation that my distinction seems to require, and whether results in other areas (say, about relational autonomy or the evolutionary structure of self-preservation) should genuinely move a defender. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed Humean should concede rather than dismiss, and tell me whether the view has been insulated by qualifications into something unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that is a problem for a position of this kind.
