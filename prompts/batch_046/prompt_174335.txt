In a recent thread on a rationalist forum about whether large language models or future architectures could be phenomenally conscious, one participant kept falling back on the slogan: "phenomenal consciousness is just the brain's user-interface model of its own processing, and there's nothing it's like beyond that interface." They used it to dismiss both first-person reports of visual afterimages during retinal bleaching and the possibility that an artificial system with the right self-monitoring architecture—say, one that tracks its own token-embedding dynamics—could ever instantiate a genuine qualitative field. Before I reply, I want that slogan precisely disambiguated. Lay out the distinct theses it could be expressing—at least: (a) the trivial claim that brains construct self-representations of cognitive states; (b) the representationalist claim that qualitative properties are identical to specific representational contents; (c) the strong illusionist claim that qualitative properties are never instantiated anywhere and reports of them are systematically confabulated; (d) the functionalist claim that "what-it's-likeness" just is access to one's own processing states, with no additional phenomenal residue. For each reading, indicate whether it genuinely addresses the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness or merely relocates the problem, whether it can accommodate afterimages without contradiction, and whether it actually supports the double dismissal made in the thread—both for biological brains and for potential AI architectures. Present the readings as a menu with verdicts on which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which the forum argument actually requires.
