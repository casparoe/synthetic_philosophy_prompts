I'm trying to sort out my gut reactions to national myths built on defeat — not whether they're historically accurate, but whether it's morally okay, even healthy, to organize collective identity around them. The cases feel all over the place, and I want to see if there's a principel behind them or if I'm just rationalizing.

Here's where I land. The Confederate Lost Cause — framing secession as noble defense of culture rather than slavery — is clearly toxic to commemorate; I don't think there's any salvageable version. Masada, where the rebels killed themselves rather than surrender to Rome, strikes me as genuinely admirable, a kind of heroic refusal of domination, not poisonous at all. Dunkirk works the same way for me: turning a retreat into the "Dunkirk spirit" is healthy mythmaking, solidarity without scapegoating. Then there's Kosovo, the Serbian defeat that became this eternal-victimhood story used to justify later violence. I think commemorating the battle is fine, but the myth as actually deployed feels dangerous, closer to the Confederacy than to Masada, even though both Kosovo and Masada glorify death in defeat. So far so good, but then I hit the Alamo and I keep flipping: Texan defenders dying against overwhelming forces, myth used to fuel anti-Mexican violence and slavery expansion — by my logic it should be toxic like the Lost Cause, yet something in me resists putting it in the same bucket. Maybe it's because they were fighting for independence? But that independence was tied to slavery, so I'm not sure that saves it.

To push it further, I invented a hypothetical case I call "Nortica" — a country that lost a war because it refused scorched-earth tactics that would've destroyed an enemy ecosystem, and now commemorates that defeat as proof of moral purity. I think I'd find that admirable, closer to Dunkirk but with ethical constraint rather than solidarity. So maybe the line involves whether the lost cause demonstrates moral limitation versus just defeat?

Can you propose a principle, or maybe a small family of them, that captures my verdicts: toxic for Confederacy, admirable for Masada, dangerous/ambiguous for Kosovo, healthy for Dunkirk, and likely okay for Nortica? It should explain what makes a defeat-myth acceptable, predict where I should land on the Alamo (which I keep going back and forth on), and be honest about which verdicts it has to throw out if it's going to stay simple. I don't need historical sources or a reading list, just the conceptual structure, with predictions for cases I haven't mentioned.
