Dr. Elena Vasquez is retiring after thirty years chairing the clinical ethics committee at St. Ambrose Hospital. You are her former student, now a philosopher, delivering the retirement toast at a dinner attended by nurses, administrators, board members, family, and a few former patients. The room is not philosophical, though several guests know your field. You want to honor Vasquez’s governing conviction: that the committee’s task is to recognize constraints on clinical practice that are already binding, not to invent them by vote or administrative fiat—so that a decision is not made right merely by being approved. At the same time, you must acknowledge that without the committee’s institutional voice those same constraints lack practical force, and you must do this without resolving, dissolving, or ignoring the old Euthyphro-style tension between prior rightness and authoritative recognition. Let that tension do real structural work: it should determine exactly which praises are open to you (you may celebrate her resistance to bureaucratic or majoritarian pressure, but you may not praise her as a mere rule-enforcer, nor as a value-creator ex nihilo) and should leave the audience with the sense that the unresolved pull between discovery and authorization is itself part of her legacy. Write the toast so that clinicians feel precisely seen, family members grasp the personal tribute, and anyone familiar with the dilemma recognizes that you have walked both horns without collapsing them. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or appeals to texts the audience could not be expected to know.
