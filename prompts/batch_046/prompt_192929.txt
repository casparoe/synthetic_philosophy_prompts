I have been working through some philosophy of action alone, with no formal training, and I have tried to build my own variation on the Frankfurt-style challenge to the principle of alternative possibilities. I should like an independent assessment of the answer I composed.

Question: Consider Jones, who is about to sign a petition. A hidden monitor, Dr Black, can detect only whether Jones forms a specific second-order desire to hesitate. Should that mental state occur, Black will intervene and cause Jones to sign regardless; should it fail to occur, Black does nothing. In fact Jones never forms the desire and signs willingly. Is Jones morally responsible for signing, despite being unable to do otherwise? And might the fact that he could have formed that second-order desire supply a “flicker of freedom” that preserves the principle?

My answer:

Frankfurt cases are meant to show that you can be morally responsible even when you could not have acted differently. Here, Dr Black never actually intervenes, because Jones never forms the second-order desire to hesitate. Since Jones signs the petition through his own decision-making, he is responsible for the outcome, and the principle of alternative possibilities is undermined.

Some critics talk about a flicker of freedom: Jones might have formed the desire to hesitate, and then Black would have acted. But this is not a real alternative for the action of signing itself; it is only an alternative mental state. The principle of alternative possibilities requires that the agent could have performed a different action, not merely that they could have thought differently. So the flicker objection does not save the principle.

That said, Frankfurt’s own hierarchical theory says that responsibility requires one’s first-order desires to align with one’s second-order volitions. Jones never forms any second-order volition about signing—he does not identify with the desire to sign at a higher level. Because this identification is absent, he lacks the internal structure required for genuine moral responsibility, despite causing the action himself.
