I'm trying to name a particular pattern I keep seeing in political deception, something in the neighborhood of Arendt's idea that modern lying dosen't just hide facts but replaces the whole ground they stand on. The pattern has two steps. First, authorities push a claim that flatly contradicts widely known events—not sneaking it past anyone, but putting it out there openly, sometimes with official ceremonies that pretend to verify it. Second, when people point out the contradiction, the response isn't a correction or a better cover-up, but an attack on the critics for being obsessed with consistency at all, as if caring about the mismatch is itself a partisan move.

What strikes me is that the people running this don't actually need anyone to believe the original claim. They seem to need the audience to accept that belief is the wrong frame—that the whole point is to show that fact-checking is naive. I've noticed it in regimes where everyone privately knows an election was rigged but the state still holds elaborate vote-counting rituals, and in some recent campaigns where debunking the false story is treated as proof you're out of touch.

Is there an established concept for this two-step, where the lie is designed to be caught and that catching is part of how it works? I know Arendt writes about liars eventually trapping themselves in their own fictions, but here the liar seems immune precisely because he never intended to be believed—he intended to be exposed, and that exposure fuels the machinery. Does this fit her category of the modern lie, or is it a seperate thing? And how does it sit with her view that political action necessarily involves changing reality—does this version collapse the difference between changing reality and just fabricating it, or is there still a distinction I'm missing?
