I am currently taking a graduate seminar on historiography and microhistorical method, and I am trying to formalize my working commitments before I submit my prospectus. I want an internal consistency check rather than a bibliography or reading list, because I suspect I am borrowing motivations from frameworks that do not actually cohere. Here is where I stand:

(1) I take microhistory's primary value to be epistemic rather than representational: a closely read local episode should destabilize macro-categories such as the state or the peasantry by exposing their internal heterogeneity, not serve as a colorful illustration of them.

(2) I also endorse the archival-turn critique that repositories are not neutral containers but active producers of legibility; I further think there is an epistemic asymmetry between negative and positive traces, so that archival silence is structurally meaningful yet never transparently readable as absence of practice. Consequently, treating court transcripts, parish registers, or inquisitorial records as direct windows onto subaltern experience is methodologically reckless, since they encode the bureaucratic gaze of the institutions being studied.

(3) At the same time, I reject pure antiquarianism: I believe the historian has an explanatory obligation to show how a local case connects to larger structural transformations — for instance, the jurisdictional expansion of centralizing authorities or the confessional regulation of bodily practice — otherwise the case remains causally inert.

(4) I hold that the ethical center of history from below is anti-ventriloquism. We should not impute to non-literate actors intentions, cosmologies, or forms of political consciousness that exceed what the fragmentary record can independently support, particularly when those attributions mirror the categorical language of their literate observers.

(5) For my own project I intend to reconstruct the practices of an accused village midwife in the 1630s almost entirely through trial transcripts generated by a centralizing judicial apparatus. I want to frame her healing rituals as a form of tacit resistance to that apparatus's growing jurisdictional claims, and I want to weave this local episode into a broader narrative about legal centralization — despite recognizing that the transcripts are structurally biased, that they record her mainly through her accusers' categories, and that resistance is not a term she herself could have articulated.

My question is whether this package is jointly tenable, or whether I am helping myself to incompatible premises. Specifically, which pairs are genuinely in tension rather than merely appearing so? I am especially unsure whether (3) and (4) can be satisfied together, and whether (2) undermines (5) in a way that makes the whole project self-defeating. Please audit these commitments, rank the tensions by severity, identify the least costly revision in each case, and specify exactly what I would have to give up. I do not want a list of sources; I want a conceptual audit of the architecture itself.
