I am working through the pragmatist literature for a graduate seminar on Peirce and James, and I keep encountering the compressed claim that truth is what works. In my reading group last week, the phrase was deployed in at least four different registers: one person used it to mean that scientific theories are merely instruments with no representational content; another treated it as a claim about verification conditions being reducible to practical success in an individual's experience; a third seemed to be saying something closer to Peirce's idea that truth is the limit of inquiry, where working means surviving indefinitely in the long run of investigation; and a fourth invoked it to reject correspondence altogether as a metaphysical fantasy. I find the slogan useful for ending arguments but useless for starting them, because it collapses distinctions that matter for whether pragmatism is a theory of meaning, a theory of truth, or both.

Can you separate the precise theses that could be hiding inside the claim that truth is what works, labeling each as either a platitude, a substantive but defensible pragmatist claim, a misreading of Peirce or James, or an outright falsehood? In particular, I want to know which of these readings actually supports the argument it was being used for in my group—the anti-realist about scientific models, the verificationist about individual experience, the Peircean about the destined agreement of an indefinite community of inquirers, and the global correspondence-rejecter. For each, indicate whether Peirce's account of truth as the opinion that inquiry is fated to reach, or James's account of truth as what proves practically beneficial and experientially consequential in particular contexts, actually licenses the move being made. Also tell me which reading is the one people reach for when they want to shut down a debate about representational fidelity, and whether that reading is the one either philosopher endorsed. Keep the mapping disciplined: say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and do not treat the slogan as having one correct interpretation.
