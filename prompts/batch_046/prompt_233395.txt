I host a small podcast about how groups make up their minds—juries, committees, boards—though I have no formal training in philosophy or logic. Lately I have been preoccupied with a puzzle I understand is called the discursive dilemma: a group can agree on every premise that should lead to a conclusion and yet, when they vote on the conclusion directly, reject it, or the reverse can happen just as easily. I want to interview a philosopher who has recently defended a particular resolution. She is not a well-known public figure, and I will treat her position exactly as she presents it: she rejects both standard premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation. Instead, she proposes what she terms sequential dependency aggregation, according to which premises must be ordered by logical dependency—if one premise is a necessary condition for another, the group aggregates the dependent premise first, then conditions the aggregation of the prior one on that intermediate result. She claims this dissolves the classic jury paradox, but introduces what she calls reversal asymmetry, a situation in which reversing the logical ordering of premises can flip the collective conclusion even when every individual member’s judgments remain completely unchanged.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for me. I want at least one that asks whether sequential dependency aggregation genuinely solves the discursive dilemma or merely relocates the conflict into hidden disputes about logical ordering; for that one, include a follow-up anticipating her reply that the correct ordering is always objectively fixed by the domain’s logic. I also want a question on what happens when group members disagree not about the conclusion or about the premises themselves, but about which premise depends on which—how her method handles contested logical architecture among the voters. Another should press her on reversal asymmetry: ask what normative principle justifies privileging one ordering over its reverse when both sequences seem logically permissible, and include a follow-up keyed to her likely answer that practical context or institutional role determines the correct sequence. Finally, end with a question about what empirical pattern in actual jury or committee deliberation—something observable without asking members to self-report—would convince her that sequential dependency aggregation fails. Keep the tone respectful but intellectually sharp; avoid generic biographical openings, and do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or further reading.
