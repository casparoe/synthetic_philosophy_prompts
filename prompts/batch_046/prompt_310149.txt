Here are my gut verdicts about inventing or altering details of real, dead people in historical drama and biopics, and I want to know whether they point to one coherent principle or force me to abandon some.

Case A: A biopic shows Marie Curie in private expressing imposter syndrome about her Nobel Prize. No record of this exists, but her letters reveal exhaustion and self-doubt. I think this is permissible — it illuminates her documented psychology without distorting it.

Case B: A drama invents a deathbed reconciliation between Alan Turing and a real colleague who betrayed him; in reality Turing died alone. The scene is included solely to give the audience emotional closure. I think this is impermissible.

Case C: A novel depicts the inner thoughts of an unnamed, composite soldier in the First World War, invented entirely, but the text explicitly frames him as fictional and not tied to any named individual. I think this is permissible.

Case D: A film keeps every documented action and direct quotation of a real Roma resistance fighter intact, but silently changes her ethnicity to East Asian for casting reasons, never acknowledging the alteration. I think this is impermissible.

Case E: A playwright stitches together phrases from a dead philosopher’s verified, unpublished notebook fragments into a speech she never delivered. Every word is genuinely hers, but the stitching creates an argument she never made; the stitching is made transparent to the audience. I think this is permissible.

Case F: A documentary-style drama uses a dead patient’s actual, published medical records to depict her illness accurately, then invents a scene in which she comforts her frightened doctor, reversing the real power dynamic, in order to criticise modern healthcare dehumanisation. I think this is impermissible.

My tentative distinction is between “counterfactual dignity” — an invention consistent with the dead person’s documented values and serving understanding of them — and “memorial instrumentalisation” — using a real dead person’s actual suffering or identity as raw material for an emotional or ideological effect they never intended or experienced. Does this distinction, or some repair of it, capture all six verdicts? If not, which verdict should I abandon?

Please also say what your principle predicts for two cases I have not listed: first, a biopic that accurately depicts a dead public figure’s documented racist remarks but frames them with modern commentary that makes the figure appear to have secretly rejected those views; second, a novel that writes in the first person from the perspective of a named, real child victim of a well-documented massacre, inventing her inner monologue for educational purposes.
