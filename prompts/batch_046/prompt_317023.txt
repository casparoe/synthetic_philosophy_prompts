I've been working through some value-theory material with a study group, and we keep circling back to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—not the familiar version about whether all pleasures feel the same, but a more structural worry. Most defenses distinguish sensory pleasure from attitudinal pleasure and claim both instantiate a uniform "feels-good" quality. But suppose we take seriously the possibility that bodily pleasures and cognitive pleasures share no qualitative character whatsoever; their only commonality is that the subject endorses them as good for her. At that point, hedonism risks collapsing into a pro-attitude view disguised in phenomenal language. I'm trying to understand whether there is a defensible middle ground: a position that accepts deep phenomenal heterogeneity among pleasures—denying that there is one "feel" they all share—while still maintaining that pleasures form a natural kind unified by something non-phenomenal but also not merely attitudinal. For instance, might they share a distinctive presentational immediacy, or a specific direction of fit that distinguishes them from beliefs or desires about the good?

Please explain the conceptual architecture of such a middle position, clarifying exactly what unifies the class if not a shared felt quality, and specify whether this unification is robust enough to do the work hedonism requires—namely, explaining why pleasure is the sole intrinsic good. Along the way, please identify the weakest point in your reasoning: what kind of counterexample—perhaps a mental state that has the proposed structural feature but is intuitively not a pleasure, or a pleasure that lacks it—would lead you to reject the middle ground in favor of either a pure phenomenalist or a pure functionalist account? I'm not asking for a literature review or citations; I just want the argument laid out cleanly enough that I can see where it lives or dies.
