I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has been studying philosophy of mind with particular interest in Douglas Hofstadter’s idea that the self is a strange loop—a pattern capable of persisting in minds other than the one in which it originated. In a discussion group I attend, a dispute about whether an I can genuinely be distributed across brains has reached an impasse that feels more structural than substantive.

One side holds that if one brain replicates the causal and informational structure of another’s self-pattern with sufficient fidelity, there is no principled reason to deny that the same self now has two loci; they treat selfhood as analogous to a reproducible state of organization. The opposing side grants that memory, personality, and even suffering can be transmitted, yet insists that indexical self-reference is anchored to a singular embodied trajectory—replication produces sympathy or simulation, not shared identity. For over a year, both sides have agreed on the neuroscience of pattern recognition and on the normative significance of consciousness, but neither has conceded an inch.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden empirical question about whether isomorphic brain patterns preserve phenomenal content across substrates? A conceptual dispute about whether I operates like a proper name tied to one token or like a type that can have multiple instances? Or does the stall reflect a deeper divergence between those who regard identity as an information-theoretic relation and those who regard it as an irreducibly first-personal anchor—a divergence that neither side can justify without circularity?

Please diagnose the structure of the impasse precisely: do the sides answer different questions under one label; is there a formal distinction, a revised framing, or an empirical criterion that could resolve or dissolve it; or is the question ill-posed because it assumes there must be a single answer to where the self is located? Given your diagnosis, specify what would count as progress—whether a new conceptual boundary, a change of question, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why.
