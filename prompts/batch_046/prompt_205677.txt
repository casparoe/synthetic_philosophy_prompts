Having retired from a career in experimental genetics, I have recently turned to philosophy with the enthusiasm of a late convert, and I now attend a small reading circle composed largely of former laboratory colleagues who, like me, are trying to understand contemporary bioethics without formal philosophical training. We have spent several months working through the arguments surrounding procreative beneficence — the idea that parents have a moral reason to select, among possible children, the one expected to live the best life — and the related defence of liberal eugenics, which holds that state-coerced enhancement is wrong but that parental choice in reproductive technology should be maximally permitted. By now, however, our discussions feel circular: the familiar objections about the non-identity problem, the slippery slope to coercion, and the ambiguity of what counts as best have all been met with equally familiar replies, and nobody in the group has shifted their view since our first meeting. I am therefore wondering whether this stagnation indicates a genuine substantive deadlock, or whether the dispute is partly verbal — perhaps beneficence and eugenics are being used with different implicit criteria — or whether the two sides are in fact addressing different questions, one about individual parental duty and the other about the social architecture of reproductive freedom.

What makes our stalemate particularly puzzling is that, coming from the life sciences, we keep noticing an assumption that both camps seem to share: they treat genetic or embryonic selection as if it deterministically produces the phenotype being selected for. In genetics we have long recognised that phenotypic expression depends on developmental plasticity, environmental interaction, and reaction norms, so selecting for a particular genotype does not straightforwardly select for a predictable trait. This leads me to suspect that part of the philosophical impasse may rest on a hidden empirical premise about genotype–phenotype mapping that is now questionable. If that premise is false, then arguments about whether parents ought to select the best child may be addressing a technically incoherent target, and the disagreement might dissolve — or shift — once the biological complexity is acknowledged. So my question is this: diagnose the structure of the disagreement in this area. Is it a case of verbal confusion about key terms, a substantive moral conflict that survives even after the biology is corrected, or a situation in which two distinct questions — one normative, one empirical — have been conflated under a single heading? And if the hidden biological premise is indeed doing work, what would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction that separates the normative from the empirical, a revised formulation of procreative duty that incorporates phenotypic uncertainty, or an argument that the entire framing should be abandoned? I should be grateful for an answer in British English, written with the care one expects in serious philosophical discussion, and I ask that you avoid offering reading lists or bibliographic suggestions, since I am working without access to a university library and must rely on reasoning alone.
