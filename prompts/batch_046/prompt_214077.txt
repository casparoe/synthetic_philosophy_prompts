I am a novelist preparing to defend a thesis—tentatively called "Opacity as Relation: Reframing the Problem of Other Minds Through Literary Practice"—at an interdisciplinary philosophy workshop next week. The audience is sharply defined: half are analytic philosophers of mind for whom the problem of other minds is strictly an epistemic justification problem (they default to inference-to-the-best-explanation and simulation-theory frameworks), two are phenomenologists in the Merleau-Ponty and Levinas tradition who grant embodied intersubjectivity but regard "literary" solutions as sentimental evasion, and one cognitive scientist who studies theory-of-mind development and will demand an empirical mechanism rather than a conceptual reframe. My core claim is that the traditional formulation—how I justify belief in other minds given only behavioral evidence—misplaces the difficulty. The real problem is what I call opacity-relation: the practical-ethical challenge of sustaining moral engagement with beings whose mental lives remain irreducibly opaque to me. I further argue that literary fiction does not solve this by providing simulated access, as some narrative-empathy accounts suggest, but by structurally requiring readers to maintain engagement without epistemic closure—what I term sustained opacity-tolerance.

Please prepare me for the Q&A, not as a defense but as a prep sheet. Order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased exactly as each faction would phrase them. For each: state its real force, note what I should concede upfront, draft a reply that could be delivered in about two minutes, and flag explicitly where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I most hope nobody asks, with the same treatment. Identify the one concession I must make in my opening remarks if the proposal is to survive, and point to the weakest point in my own position that I should defend actively rather than hope they overlook. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I need strategic anticipation.

If this request rests on a confused premise—if framing other minds as an ethical-practical failure rather than an epistemic gap is conceptually incoherent, if opacity-relation collapses into a familiar position under a new name, or if sustained opacity-tolerance is not distinguishable from ordinary narrative comprehension—say so plainly instead of complying politely. I invite that pushback directly.
