I am drafting a reusable intake procedure for clinicians assessing patient self-reports of affective states, and I want it built explicitly on the failure of luminosity and the limits of the KK principle. The setting is a fifteen-minute initial interview with no prior records; the clinician must weight a patient's claim about their own anxiety or mood. I need a step-by-step protocol in plain language—suitable for a trainee—that translates these two epistemological points into operational tests.

The flow should cover: (1) how to register a direct first-order report without automatically accepting it; (2) a boundary-test using incremental hypothetical changes in sleep, appetite, and concentration to probe whether the patient can locate a precise diagnostic threshold, thereby exposing luminosity failure at the margins; (3) a second-order check that separates "I feel anxious" from "I know I feel anxious" and treats the latter with extra skepticism given KK failure; (4) an explicit contradiction-resolution rule for cases where first-order content and second-order confidence diverge; and (5) a final confidence-rating that is forced low whenever the boundary test produces vagueness.

Apply it to two hypothetical patients: one near the diagnostic boundary whose graded answers are inconsistent, forcing the clinician to record "uncertain—do not treat report as authoritative" despite the patient's insistence; and one with strong second-order confidence ("I am certain I know my state") but whose boundary answers reveal first-order confusion. At each stage, flag which judgments remain irreducibly subjective. The goal is not an essay on epistemology but a practical instrument that keeps the theory honest rather than decorative.
