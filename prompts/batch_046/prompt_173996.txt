I have no formal training in philosophy, but after years of travel I have become fixated on a practical question that feels deeper than etiquette: when I am briefly in a country where people queue differently, tip differently, or cross streets differently, am I actually doing something morally wrong if I follow my home habits? Some fellow travelers treat these differences as mere custom; others treat them as moral failures, like breaking an unspoken promise to the people around you. I want to see whether two people who take these opposite views seriously could still produce a joint document—not a compromise, but an adversarial collaboration—that separates what they genuinely agree on from what they genuinely do not.

Please draft that document. Treat the two camps as follows:

Camp One—the Tacit-Entry Conventionalists: they hold that physically entering a functioning social space (a restaurant, a sidewalk, a bus line) creates an immediate, if minimal, obligation to observe the local conventions that make cooperation possible there. The obligation is not about law; it is about not free-riding on a coordination scheme that others maintain, and about avoiding the specific harm of social friction and disrespect that arises when one visibly opts out.

Camp Two—the Universal-Harm Skeptics: they hold that moral obligations do not attach to arbitrary local patterns. A visitor is bound only by universal prohibitions against direct harm and by explicit contracts they have entered. Unwritten customs like tipping percentages, queue spacing, or jaywalking norms are not promises; they are local equilibria that may be wise to follow for practical reasons, but violating them is not a moral wrong unless it causes identifiable harm to a specific person. In fact, if a local convention is itself harmful, a visitor might have reason to disregard it.

The document should be structured exactly as follows:

First, a numbered section of agreed claims—propositions about human cooperation, social signaling, harm, consent, and cross-cultural interaction that both camps could sign without reservation. These should not be empty truisms; they should include specific points about what happens when conventions clash, about the difference between legal enforcement and moral expectation, and about the limits of our ability to read local norms correctly.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one precise point of divergence, with Camp One's formulation written in wording Camp One accepts, and Camp Two's formulation in wording Camp Two accepts. The disagreement should be substantive, not verbal.

Third, identify the cruxes—the narrower sub-questions such that resolving them in one direction would genuinely move at least one camp. I suspect two important ones are (a) whether mere participation in a local practice without protest counts as a form of tacit consent to its rules, and (b) whether the disruption caused by violating an arbitrary convention constitutes a real harm or merely an offense to expectation. You should include at least one crux I have not mentioned, and for each crux you should propose a concrete scenario—a real-world situation both sides agree is relevant—together with the specific prediction or judgment each camp makes about that scenario.

Please keep the reasoning accessible: no citations to academic texts, no requests for reading lists, and no reliance on highly specialized terminology that a general reader would not recognize. At the same time, please inject at least one precise but slightly unconventional conceptual tool—for instance, something like "normative viscosity" (the resistance of a social norm to being carried across borders) or "tacit contractual entry"—that helps make the disagreement sharper and less clichéd than the usual "when in Rome" argument.
