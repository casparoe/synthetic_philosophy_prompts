I am a doctoral student in comparative literature, presently coordinating the peer-review pipeline for a small facing-page translation series—provisionally titled Formal Echoes—that publishes English versions of sixteenth-century French lyric poetry. Following a lengthy discussion with my supervisor about whether our reviewers have been too permissive with drafts that preserve semantic fluency at the expense of rhyme architecture, I wish to operationalise the central argument of Douglas Hofstadter’s 1997 study Le Ton beau de Marot: that formal constraints are not mere obstacles to be overcome but creative sparks which reshape what a translation can legitimately say. I need a reusable evaluation protocol—essentially a checklist or flowchart in words—that my co-reviewers, whose expertise ranges from senior philology to MA-level general literature, can apply consistently in roughly twenty minutes per submission. Each run must yield a signed recommendation—accept, revise with a specified trade-off, or reject—without requiring anyone to consult secondary sources or to recall precise prosodic rules from memory.

The procedure should require the reviewer first to identify the source poem’s non-negotiable formal skeleton—refrain patterns, rhyme scheme, syllabic count, tonal register, and any architectonic feature such as a rondeau’s circularity—then to map every corresponding element in the submitted English draft against that skeleton. At each point of comparison the protocol must distinguish mechanical checks (does the refrain recur? does the syllable count match?) from unavoidable judgment calls, and it must include a constraint hierarchy matrix that the reviewer completes for the specific poem rather than importing a universal ranking: in one sentence the reviewer must justify why, for this particular formal design, rhyme architecture takes precedence over lexical fidelity, or vice versa. The deliverable must handle three worked cases: one draft that passes cleanly; one where the protocol forces an unwelcome revision—say, a translation that is poetically powerful in English but structurally unrecognisable as the source form—together with the explicit misfire this reveals; and one case where two formal dimensions conflict irreducibly and the reviewer must escalate rather than adjudicate alone. The protocol must also state clearly what it cannot do—where it will reliably produce false negatives, which distinctions it collapses, and when a reviewer’s lack of expertise should trigger escalation to the series editor—given that we process approximately forty submissions per term, the translators are unpaid but career-sensitive, and no reviewer has web access during evaluation sessions.

Please frame the role precisely—peer reviewer for an academic translation series with a countersigning editor—fix the frequency and stakes, and ensure the checklist remains honest about which steps are dressed-up judgement calls. Do not ask for bibliographies, citation of theoretical passages, or verification of historical editions; the procedure must be defensible in writing by appealing only to the poem’s visible form and the draft’s observable features. I was also wondering, somewhat tangentially, whether a protocol built around constraint-driven creativity can ever be neutral about whether the translator’s inventive departure counts as a failure of fidelity or as the very spark Hofstadter describes—so please build that tension into the decision nodes rather than resolving it in advance.
