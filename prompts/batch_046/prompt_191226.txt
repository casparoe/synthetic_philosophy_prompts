At a recent dinner with friends, I found myself caught between Elena and David, who were arguing about whether the French Revolution really proves that “revolutions devour their children.” I have been turning the question over since then, but I lack formal training in history or philosophy, and I would prefer a direct adjudication of their actual exchange rather than a general lecture.

Elena began by saying the familiar phrase is misleading. She noted that Robespierre, Danton, and the Hébertists were not destroyed by one impersonal revolutionary force; they fell at different times, for different reasons, and often at the hands of colleagues with concrete aims—stopping the Terror, changing economic policy, or escaping personal blame. To treat all of them as victims of a single cannibalistic logic, she said, is like blaming gravity for every fall. She also pointed out that if revolutions truly consume their makers, we should expect a steady, inevitable consumption of leaders, but in fact the violence was tightly clustered around 1793–94 and then halted abruptly after Thermidor, which looks more like a contingent crisis than a structural destiny.

David replied that Elena was mistaking the mechanism for the pattern. He granted that Tallien and others had personal motives for bringing Robespierre down, but he insisted those motives only turned lethal because the Revolution had invented a new kind of legitimacy: authority derived not from law or birth but from the claim to embody the general will. Once legitimacy works that way, anyone else who claims to represent the people becomes an existential threat that must be eliminated. The flight to Varennes, the war, and sans-culotte pressure did not accidentally cause the Terror; they activated a feature already built into the idea of popular sovereignty. David said that examining individual executions misses the forest: the Revolution did not eat its children randomly, but because they were made of the same substance—claims to speak for the nation—that made the Revolution possible.

Elena then offered a comparison she had developed on her own. She said the Revolution suffered from something like a constitutional autoimmune disorder: the body politic kept redefining who counted as “the people,” and each redefinition caused it to attack its own leading cells. In 1789 the people meant the Third Estate; by 1793 it meant the virtuous and the sans-culottes; after Thermidor it meant property-owning citizens again. Because the boundary kept shifting, yesterday’s legitimate representative became today’s foreign body. She argued that this explains both the clustering of violence and its sudden stop: once the Convention settled on a narrower, stable definition of citizenship after Thermidor, the “immune response” shut down. For her, this means the purges were not inevitable; if the constitutional definition had stabilized earlier—say, after 1791—the Terror might never have occurred.

David countered that Elena's medical metaphor actually strengthens his position. Autoimmune disorders occur precisely when an immune system is overactive and cannot distinguish self from non-self, just as the Revolution’s constant need to purify itself came from an ideology that refused to tolerate particular embodiments of the general will. He also said her counterfactual is unrealistic: the war and economic crisis made early stabilization impossible, so the disorder was not a contingent accident but the necessary working-out of revolutionary politics under extreme pressure.

They turned to me and asked for a verdict. I honestly could not provide one. So I ask you: who is right about which specific point? Is Elena correct that individual motives and contingent events undermine the “devouring children” narrative? Is David correct that popular sovereignty contains a structural logic of purgation? Does Elena's autoimmune metaphor illuminate something both are missing, or does it obscure the real dynamics? And more broadly, is the phrase “revolutions devour their children” a useful summary or a misleading myth?

Please adjudicate point by point: say where each party has the stronger argument, where both are partly right, and what—if anything—each is failing to see. I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific works; I want a direct philosophical judgment of the disagreement exactly as it unfolded.
