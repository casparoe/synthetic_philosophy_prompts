Please describe the most important implications of G. E. Moore's organic-unity account of intrinsic value—particularly as it applies to friendship and to the appreciation of beauty—for cases in which two close friends come to hold radically incompatible, and perhaps mutually undermining, aesthetic judgments about works or traditions they once valued together. In particular, address: (1) whether, on Moore's framework, shared aesthetic appreciation functions as an essential part of the organic whole that is the friendship, or only as one replaceable component; (2) whether a severe breakdown in aesthetic agreement can diminish the intrinsic value of the friendship itself, or whether the value of the friendship and the value of each friend's mental states remain independently intact; (3) whether this kind of case exposes a tension between Moore's value holism and his restriction of intrinsic value to certain mental states and personal relations; and (4) what this suggests more generally about whether organic unities require harmony among their constituent parts. Write your response in American English, in a formal and polished style, and do not include bibliographies, citations, or references to specific editions or page numbers.
