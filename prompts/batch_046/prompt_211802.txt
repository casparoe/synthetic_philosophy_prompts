I'm preparing a seminar paper on rationality and self-knowledge, and I've become committed to a specific position on epistemic akrasia that I want to stress-test before defending it. The view—call it calibrated level-splitting—holds that when higher-order evidence takes the form of domain-specific meta-cognitive tracking (for instance, knowing that one's statistical reasoning is unreliable in high-stakes forecasting but intact in perceptual judgment), rationality does not require revising the first-order belief downward. Instead, it demands maintaining the first-order belief on its original evidence, adopting a second-order judgment that the belief is likely erroneous, and then calibrating one's action-guiding confidence to match the estimated domain error rate without altering doxastic content. On this account, epistemic akrasia is not a failure of rationality but a structurally necessary posture: the first-order belief and the second-order assessment serve different functional roles, and forcing them into alignment by revising the belief would actually discard intact first-order evidence.

Play it straight: what would have to be true for calibrated level-splitting to be wrong? Which core commitments—functional separation of belief and confidence, the claim that meta-cognitive tracking provides no direct counter-evidence to the belief's content, or the idea that action-guidance should track estimated error rather than belief—are most vulnerable? Are there empirical findings about belief-behavior coupling, formal results in decision theory, or concrete cases where maintaining a belief one judges unreliable produces practical paralysis or self-deception? I want to know what defenders of this view would themselves concede as genuinely damaging: for example, if doxastic revision turns out to be psychologically unavoidable once second-order unreliability is acknowledged, or if calibration collapses into a hidden form of belief revision. Distinguish evidence that would be decisive—say, a general argument that belief and confidence are not functionally separable—from evidence that would merely be awkward, like a single unstable case. Is this position well insulated, and if so, is that insulation a virtue or a sign that it has protected itself by definitional maneuver?
