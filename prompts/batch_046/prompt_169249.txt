Consider four arguments used to justify restricting democratic participation on performance grounds: (i) the updated Mandate of Heaven, in which sustained economic growth and social stability replace dynastic virtue as the basis of political legitimacy; (ii) the Singaporean expert-managerial claim, that a meritocratic elite can limit democratic contestation without reducing welfare because policy is guided by competence rather than popular preference; (iii) the prosperity-threshold thesis — that once a society crosses a basic-development line, demands for procedural democracy become destabilising luxury goods rather than fundamental rights; and (iv) the authoritarian-bargain defence, which treats delivered prosperity and order as implicit compensation for the absence of electoral accountability.

Rank these four from strongest to weakest as justifications for authoritarian or semi-authoritarian rule in modern, industrialised societies. Assign each a score out of ten for internal coherence and a separate score out of ten for empirical plausibility in the Chinese and Singaporean contexts. For each argument, identify in one sentence the single counter-example or disanalogy that most undermines it. Finally, state whether your overall ranking changes if the evaluation is restricted to societies that have already achieved high-income status. Please answer in British English.
