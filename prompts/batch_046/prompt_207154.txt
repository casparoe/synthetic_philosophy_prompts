Here is an argument I have been working through since returning to Hofstadter's Ant Fugue. It seems to show that if Aunt Hillary—the anthill—can genuinely think, then any layered computational system whose parts are individually meaningless must also think, which strikes me as absurd. I want to preserve the possibility that collective entities can have mental lives, but I do not want to be forced into attributing beliefs to image-classifiers.

(1) For a collective system to possess mental content, it is sufficient that its subsystems produce outputs that are individually representationally empty but jointly fix a unified state about some feature of the environment.

(2) In Aunt Hillary, the pheromone gradients deposited by foragers, the defensive signaling of soldiers, and the brood-care rhythms of nurses are each, taken alone, bare chemical or motor events with no intentional content; only through their sustained counterpoint do they determine a single colony-level state about, for example, the direction of a food source.

(3) Therefore Aunt Hillary satisfies the condition in (1) and genuinely possesses mental content.

(4) Consider now a deep convolutional network trained on image classification: the activation patterns in its early hidden layers are, in isolation, meaningless vectors; only through the full layered computation do they jointly determine a state that tracks whether the input contains a cat.

(5) By direct parity with (1)-(3), this network also possesses mental content—specifically, a belief-like state directed at feline presence.

(6) But attributing beliefs to such a network is conceptually inappropriate: it lacks the normative, phenomenal, and agential dimensions that govern belief attribution.

(7) So (1) must be rejected: the mere interaction of representationally empty parts is not sufficient for mental content.

(8) Yet if (1) fails, then Aunt Hillary's internal counterpoint provides no special ground for attributing thoughts to her; we are left without any account of collective mentality.

(9) Hence either widespread artificial consciousness follows, or ant colonies are not minds.

Which step should I give up? I am most reluctant to surrender (2) or (3), since the counterpoint structure of the colony seems real and its mental significance plausible. If the flaw is not in (1) but elsewhere—perhaps an equivocation between causal interdependence and intentional integration, or a scope shift in "jointly determine"—I want to know exactly where it enters and what preserving (2) and (3) then costs me.
