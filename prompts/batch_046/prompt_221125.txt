I retired from biology about five years ago—spent three decades studying population dynamics in the Pacific Northwest—and lately I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars at the local university mostly to keep my brain from turning to mush. One thing we've been arguing about in our study group is democratic backsliding, specifically whether it's a real analytical category or just a partisan label people slap on governments they don't like. We've been trying to stay consistent by sorting actual cases instead of going by gut feeling.

So far we've said yes, that's backsliding, for Hungary (Orbán's court packing and state media capture), Poland (the judicial discipline chamber and public broadcasting takeover), and Turkey (the post-coup purges and consolidation into presidentialism). We've said no for the US after 2020—sure, there were state-level voting restrictions and that Jan 6 mess, but federal courts held, the military stayed in its lane, and power eventually transferred, so we filed it under severe polarization rather than backsliding. We also said no for Singapore's tightening of opposition space, since the courts and electoral rules never actually changed; it's just that running against the PAP gets expensive and risky, and that was true ten years ago too.

But I'm not convinced we're running a single rule. One interpretation is did they capture the referees?—independent courts, electoral management bodies, media regulators. Another is did electoral competitiveness actually drop?—the opposition might still sit in parliament but can't realistically win a national election anymore. A third is whether the leaders explicitly reject democratic norms—Orbán's talk about illiberal democracy, for instance—versus framing every change as democratic improvement or meritocracy.

Here's the case I want to test consistency on. There's a small democracy—not in the EU, call it Ruritania—where the courts are untouched, election law hasn't changed, and there's no explicit anti-democratic rhetoric. The leader, in fact, talks about quality democracy and cites meritocratic traditions. But they've introduced a mandatory civic literacy exam for voters, administered by an independant board of retired educators, ecologists, and physicists—people like me, actually—who claim it's nonpartisan. Roughly 60% of opposition supporters fail, versus 10% in government strongholds. Opposition parties still win local seats but can't take national power, and there's no sign of direct election fraud.

If we want to stay consistent with our four earlier verdicts, should we call Ruritania backsliding? Which precedent is the closest analog—Poland because institutional pressure is real, or Singapore because formal rules stay intact? And if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle are we adopting if we say yes versus no? Finally, what further case—maybe one involving algorithmic filtering of campaign speech by an AI commission, or a constitutional clause giving retired scientists veto power over candidates—would best tell whether we're actually running the referee-capture rule, the competitiveness-drop rule, or the normative-breach rule?

I'm less interested in a simple yes/no than in seeing the candidate rules laid out, what each predicts for Ruritania, and where they'd split on the next case.
