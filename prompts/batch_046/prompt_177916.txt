I'm preparing a graduate seminar on whether biography can function as a serious epistemic genre for practical reasoning, and a colleague has pressed me to treat it not as ornament but as a distinct mode of moral learning. To test that claim, I want you to rank the following five biographical modes specifically by their capacity to produce what I will call "revisable moral knowledge"—understanding that can genuinely alter how a reader weighs competing reasons in action, rather than merely inspiring admiration or emotional identification.

Please rank them from 1 (least capable) to 5 (most capable), assigning each a score, and present the bottom line in the very first sentence: the ordered list with scores. Follow immediately with reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. The modes are: (1) Plutarchan parallel lives (synkrisis), pairing Greek and Roman figures to extract virtues through structured comparative contrast; (2) medieval hagiography (vitae sanctorum), framing lives around imitable holiness, ascetic discipline, and miraculous validation; (3) Renaissance mirror-for-princes biography, embedding virtue in narratives of political contingency and statecraft; (4) modern psychobiography in the Eriksonian mode, interpreting moral development as the resolution of psychosocial crises across the life cycle; and (5) a synthetic mode I am calling "antinomian-mirror" biography: a deliberately fractured narrative that presents the same figure exercising structurally incompatible virtues—say, unconditional loyalty and calculated betrayal—without narrative resolution, forcing the reader to adjudicate the conflict rather than absorb a unified exemplar. For each placement, say briefly why the mode lands there, with special attention to whether it permits the reader to correct or complicate prior practical judgments rather than simply confirming them.
