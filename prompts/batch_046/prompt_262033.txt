hey im drafting a short essay for a phil seminar and want feedback on the idea rather than the prose. my core claim is that the universals problem is usually stuck in an all-or-nothing framing: either redness is one shared thing across all tomatoes, or each tomato just has its own private red-trope. i want to defend something i call gradient universalism—the view that a universal can be present to different degrees in different bearers, not just fully present or absent. for instance, in a chemical pathway the property of being-a-catalyst might be distributed across intermediate states where no single molecule fully instantiates it but the system partly does; or in language, grammaticalization where a word gradually acquires grammatical function over centuries rather than suddenly instantiating 'grammatical-ness'.

i think this dodges the usual realist problem of mysterious perfect entities and the nominalist problem of exact resemblance classes, but im worried it just collapses into trope theory arranged on a scale. also im unsure whether the chemistry and linguistics cases are actually doing philosophical work or just serving as loose analogies. does gradient universalism remain distinct from both realism and trope nominalism, or does it secretly depend on one of them? and is there an obvious conceptual objection im missing—like whether partial presence violates the law of non-contradiction if an object both partly has and partly lacks a property?

please comment on the coherence of the view, point out the weakest link in my reasoning, and say whether the examples help or just muddy the waters. no need for a bibliography or reading list, just your conceptual take. also, do you think im just reinventing an old position under a new name?
