I'm prepping a grad seminar on phil of emotion and I keep coming back to this idea I'm calling "shadow evaluation" — the notion that every emotional judgment carries not just a value-claim but an implicit modal claim about whether the evaluated state is repairable or permanently fixed. So grief isn't just "this loss is bad"; it's also "the world is now irreversably deficient in a way that can't be fixed." I think this might distinguish judgmentalist views from pure feeling theories, but I might just be making up jargon.

But here's where I'm actually stuck. If emotions are literally judgments — the Nussbaum/Solomon kind — then feeling deep sadness for a fictional character's death seems to involve judging that an actual person died, which is literally false. Perception theorists can say you're tracking harm inside the fiction without ontologcal commitment, but judgmentalists seem forced to call fiction-directed grief either false or irrational. So doesn't judgmentalism collapse into an error theory for aesthetic emotion? Or is there a standard move I'm missing that lets judgmentalists handle fictional objects without biting that bullet? I need the response to clearly separate what's settled in the literature from what's genuinely contested, and please don't treat the perception view — or any view — as the obvious consensus.
