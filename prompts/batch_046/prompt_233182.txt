Assume the role of an expert philosopher of action and a literary fiction writer working at the intersection of metaphysics and narrative. Before you begin drafting, take a moment to think carefully and brainstorm in private: identify the precise logical structure of Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument—the claim that true moral responsibility requires being causa sui, which launches an infinite regress of prior causes that no agent can escape, since any act of self-creation depends on a nature one did not create. Once you have mapped that regress, design a concrete dramatic scenario that embodies it rather than explaining it didactically.

Your task is to write a polished short story—approximately 600 to 800 words, composed entirely in American English—related to this concept. To keep the idea from being a generic retelling, ground the narrative in a niche speculative premise I will call the Autogenesis Chamber: a hypothetical technology that promises to let a user completely redesign her own fundamental character, motivations, and values from scratch, thereby breaking Strawson’s regress and achieving true self-creation. The protagonist should be the Chamber’s lead architect, a brilliant engineer who is genuinely convinced she has solved the problem of moral responsibility by giving people the power to become the ultimate cause of who they are.

Over the course of the story, she must undergo a concrete dramatic reversal—triggered by a specific event involving a test subject or her own entry into the Chamber—that reveals the technology’s hidden dependency chain. She should discover that every “new” trait the Chamber installs is selected by algorithms, biological substrates, cultural inputs, or prior design choices that she herself did not author, so that even the redesigned self remains the product of prior causes for which she is not ultimately responsible. The emotional arc should move from intellectual hubris through the illusion of liberation to a quiet, precise recognition that true causa sui is a mirage.

Do not cite philosophers, quote texts, or include a bibliography, reading list, or suggestions for further study. Avoid direct didactic exposition; let the logic of the regress emerge through action, dialogue, and the protagonist’s internal realization. Make sure the story feels conceptually rigorous—readers should feel the regress closing in, not merely hear it described. After your brief brainstorming, present the story in clear, polished American English prose.
