You are an expert in classical political philosophy and modern deliberative systems design. Before constructing the deliverable, spend several minutes brainstorming: isolate the precise causal mechanisms—not merely the outcomes—that distinguish the Mytilene reversal, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates as three separate failure modes of collective democratic judgment; decide which conceptual distinctions (e.g., emotional reversal versus strategic overreach versus epistemic closure) must survive operationalization and which can be collapsed into observable behavior; and anticipate where a rigid checklist might generate false positives that suppress legitimate democratic disagreement.

Your practical role: you serve as the senior risk officer for a national office that oversees citizens’ assemblies and participatory governance bodies. Your audit team needs a reusable, real-time procedure—ordered questions, decision nodes, explicit stop conditions, and clearly marked judgment calls—to detect when a live deliberation is replicating one of three Athenian democratic pathologies:

1. The Mytilene reversal: a punitive collective decision made in heightened emotional arousal is reversed within a short window not because of new evidence or changed circumstances, but because a rhetorical reframing exploits deliberative fatigue and redirects anger toward procedural mercy, leaving the underlying harm unremedied and the decision process discredited.

2. The Sicilian overreach: a strategic or resource commitment expands because dissenting risk assessments are treated as indicators of disloyalty or defeatism rather than as information to be integrated; collective enthusiasm and charismatic framing substitute for institutional checks, and the assembly conflates boldness with wisdom.

3. The Socratic closure: an internal critic is expelled, silenced, or delegitimized through procedural manipulation in which the charge is vague (corruption, disloyalty, erosion of trust) and broad enough to absorb substantive disagreement; the assembly confuses punishing dissent with defending collective identity, and the debate duration is disproportionately short relative to the severity of the sanction.

Write the audit procedure your team can run in 30 minutes or less, using only observable behavior in transcripts, votes, and session recordings. For each of the three pathologies, provide:
- The sequence of diagnostic questions in the order they should be asked;
- What counts as a sufficient answer at each step, expressed as concrete behavioral indicators (not abstract intentions or mental states);
- The hard-stop points where the procedure returns either CLEAR or INTERVENE;
- The nodes explicitly labeled JUDGMENT CALL, where the procedure cannot settle the matter and a human auditor must decide, with a brief note on what values are in tension;
- The false-positive and false-negative risks specific to that pathway.

Then apply the full procedure to three live cases:

A. An assembly of 80 members votes 62% to expel a long-serving delegate who has repeatedly challenged the scientific premises of the assembly’s climate-adaptation plan. The motion cites “erosion of collective trust” rather than any specific rule violation. The expulsion debate lasts 20 minutes, followed immediately by the vote.

B. The assembly approves—without independent risk review or dissent recording—a partnership with a foreign municipality to co-manage a strategic water reserve. Approval follows a single charismatic presentation that frames any opposition as “defeatism in the face of crisis.” No risk-assessment document is circulated.

C. The assembly initially votes to impose severe operational sanctions on a corporation for documented pollution violations. Eighteen hours later, after one speaker reframes the sanctions as “vengeance that harms workers,” the assembly reverses itself by a narrow margin, despite no change in the pollution data and no new mitigation proposal.

In at least one application—choose the case where a risk-averse auditor will find the procedure’s verdict least welcome—show the procedure reaching a conclusion that conflicts with intuitive alarm. State exactly what the procedure says, why the indicators point that way, and what the auditor is required to do with that tension: follow the procedure, override it with documented justification, or escalate.

Finally, specify the boundary conditions: under what organizational conditions (assembly size, time pressure, information asymmetry, facilitator neutrality) does the procedure become unreliable? Explicitly state what the tool is not: it is not a theory of democratic legitimacy, not a substitute for institutional design, and not a historical argument about Athens.

Write in a formal, polished register suitable for an internal government manual. Do not provide a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations. Do not quote ancient texts verbatim; describe the dynamics of the ancient cases in your own terms. Do not invent specific modern scholarly attributions.
