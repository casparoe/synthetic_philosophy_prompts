Recently, in working through how an agent should reason about commitments to predictive mechanisms, I have become preoccupied with the precise conceptual relationship between counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. Both are standardly framed as pressures on causal decision theory: the mugger predicts your counterfactual choice and rewards or punishes accordingly, while the XOR blackmailer threatens harm that is executed only if you pay, creating a loop in which payment appears causally self-defeating. Most discussions treat them as structurally analogous. But I suspect there is an underappreciated divergence in how the agent's reasoning about belief updates operates across the two cases. In counterfactual mugging, the predictor's forecast concerns a single counterfactual branch of the agent's decision; in XOR blackmail, the execution condition depends on a correlation between the agent's actual choice and the predictor's prior model of that choice, which seems to embed an epistemic dependency rather than a pure counterfactual one. Could you explain, without relying on formal notation, whether this distinction means that one puzzle genuinely attacks causal reasoning while the other is better understood as a self-referential consistency constraint? In particular, does resolving XOR blackmail by refusing to pay rest on the same counterfactual independence assumption that dissolves counterfactual mugging, or does it require a separate argument about evidential correlation and the predictor's belief state? I am interested in a conceptual unpacking of where the two puzzles diverge at the level of reasoning about causation and belief, not in references or further reading.
