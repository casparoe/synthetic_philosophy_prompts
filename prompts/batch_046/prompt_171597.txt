You direct the intake assessment unit at a state psychiatric facility that conducts mandatory capacity evaluations for involuntary treatment and end-of-life decision-making. You face eight to ten evaluations weekly, each constrained to forty-five minutes, with a junior clinician present who must co-sign any determination, and you have no access to external records during the interview. Your team needs a reusable decision procedure for treating a patient’s introspective report as authoritative clinical data, because first-person authority is not globally valid: it varies by what we will call the access mode—phenomenal transparency (direct registration of qualitative feel), causal narrative attribution (assigning origins or explanations to one’s mental states), or comparative intensity calibration (ranking one’s own states against others or against one’s past self)—and it also varies by temporal proximity to the experience, by current emotional arousal, and by whether the report relies on interpretive scaffolding, meaning pre-existing theories or popular frameworks the patient uses to organize and articulate experience.

Please construct the procedure as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words: the specific questions to ask about a report in sequence, what answer to each counts as sufficient to proceed, where the procedure terminates with a determination that the report must not be treated as authoritative, and where it must hand the decision to human judgment because the issue involves values, irreducible clinical wisdom, or legal risk rather than fact. Be explicit about the hard cases the procedure must handle: reports that mix access modes within a single sentence, reports made under high arousal about distant events, reports that appear transparent but occur in conditions known to distort phenomenology, and reports saturated with interpretive scaffolding.

Then apply the procedure to three cases.

Case one: A patient states, “I feel absolutely no anxiety about the surgery tomorrow,” delivered in flat affect with elevated heart rate and visible avoidance of eye contact. Identify the access mode being claimed, run the sequence of questions, state the procedural outcome, and note exactly where judgment might still be required.

Case two: A patient states, “My chronic pain is worse than anyone else’s suffering because my nervous system is uniquely sensitive,” explicitly referencing a neuroscience podcast. Identify where interpretive scaffolding enters, classify the access mode, and determine whether the comparative intensity claim can be treated as authoritative for medication or disability determinations.

Case three: A patient refusing life-saving chemotherapy states, with calm immediacy and no visible arousal, “I feel a deep, clear peace that this is right for me; my mind is not clouded.” The procedure, treating this as a high-proximity phenomenal transparency report with low scaffolding and low arousal, may classify it as authoritative—an outcome that supports honoring the refusal. State that outcome explicitly, note why it conflicts with clinical intuition about depressive realism or anosognosia, and specify precisely where in the flowchart a human sign-off must intervene despite the procedural result.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: what categories of error it will systematically miss, under what conditions a clinician should override its output regardless, and which distinctions within the access-mode framework must be preserved if the procedure is simplified for faster use.
