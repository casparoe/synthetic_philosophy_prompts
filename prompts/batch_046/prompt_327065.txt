You are an expert philosopher of social choice, formal decision theory, and democratic theory. Before drafting anything, think carefully and systematically: identify the core commitments of this audience, locate the weakest structural joints in the speaker's argument, and anticipate which objections carry the most reputational damage versus which are merely annoying.

The speaker is defending the following thesis. The strategy-stealing assumption (SSA)—the principle that possessing an extra available move can never be strategically disadvantageous—holds only inside perfect-information, zero-sum, monotonic games such as Hex or Chomp. When normative political philosophers and social-choice theorists invoke SSA analogically to argue that first-mover agenda control necessarily produces strategic dominance in collective decision-making, they commit a category error. In real institutional procedures, amendments are not monotonic (adding an option can split coalitions or backfire on the proposer), information about other actors' preferences is incomplete, and payoffs are multidimensional rather than binary. Therefore SSA provides no general support for claims that agenda-setters possess unavoidable strategic dominance, and attempts to use it in normative arguments about democratic procedural fairness collapse.

The audience is a philosophy-of-politics and formal-methods workshop. Most attendees are normative democratic theorists and proceduralists with passing familiarity with game theory but no expertise in combinatorial games. Several are deeply committed to the view that agenda-setting power is the central threat to democratic legitimacy. Two are quantitative social-choice theorists who are comfortable with formal modeling and will recognize a sloppy analogy immediately. The group is skeptical of importing mathematical results into normative argument, especially analogies that abstract away institutional complexity. They value conceptual clarity over technical display, and they will press hard on whether the speaker's claim has any practical implication for institutional design.

The speaker's argument has three steps, which the objections should target precisely. First, a formal reconstruction showing that SSA depends on monotonicity of the game space and on binary win/lose payoffs. Second, an identification of the implicit analogical move made by proceduralists who treat legislative amendments or agenda items as monotonic moves in a similar space. Third, a critique arguing that legislative procedures violate monotonicity, involve incomplete information, and generate non-zero-sum multidimensional outcomes, so the analogy fails and SSA cannot ground normative conclusions about procedural dominance.
