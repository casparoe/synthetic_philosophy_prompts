Having retired from experimental physics after thirty years in condensed-matter research, I have lately taken up philosophy in earnest, enrolling in informal seminars on rational choice and ethical theory. In a recent discussion with colleagues from my former department—none of whom have read any philosophy since undergraduate logic—someone raised the case of counterfactual mugging: an almost perfectly accurate predictor offers one thousand dollars if you take a single box, having already placed the money there only if it forecast that you would take one box, while taking both boxes yields one thousand dollars solely when the predictor expected you to take only one. Another participant immediately proposed the structurally analogous XOR blackmail scenario, in which a malicious agent threatens to destroy a valued possession unless you pay a modest sum, but executes the threat only if it predicts, through some counterfactual mechanism, that you would refuse; should you pay, the threat was never made, yet should you refuse, the damage is already done.

I would like you to write an essay that reaches and defends a clear verdict on whether one should submit to the predictor's terms in the mugging case and whether one should pay in the blackmail case. Weigh the evidential argument—that compliance improves the counterfactual conditions that determine the payoff—against the causal argument that one's present decision cannot retroactively fill the boxes or alter the blackmailer's prior forecast. Crucially, do not treat the two cases as automatically symmetric: address whether the cooperative nature of prediction versus the hostile intent of blackmail creates a rational or moral distinction that justifies different responses. To prevent the answer from being a mere recitation of standard decision-theoretic doctrine, evaluate both scenarios under the specific condition that the predictor's or adversary's accuracy is fixed at precisely 0.85 rather than near-perfect, and that the agent knows this figure exactly. Does this imperfection dissolve the paradox by lowering the stakes below a rational threshold, or does it preserve the conflict in scaled form? Conclude with a unified principle, if one exists, that governs counterfactual rationality across cooperative and adversarial contexts, or explain why no such principle should be expected.
