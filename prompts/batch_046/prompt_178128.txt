I am a retired physicist who has recently begun serious study of philosophy of history, and I am preparing a single ninety-minute session for a small discussion group composed mainly of fellow retired scientists with no formal training in historiography. I want to guide them through the debate over historical objectivity, specifically Leopold von Ranke's aspiration to present the past exactly as it occurred, E. H. Carr's argument that the historian's present concerns and unavoidable selection construct the account, and Hayden White's further claim that historical narratives are fundamentally shaped by linguistic and rhetorical structures rather than by the past itself. Rather than delivering the explanation myself, I need a pedagogical plan: the precise order in which these positions and the supporting distinctions must be introduced so that each layer depends on the previous; the one concrete historical case that should carry the entire discussion, together with the properties it must possess and the properties it must lack; the misconceptions this scientifically trained audience will predictably form—including, I anticipate, the belief that history ought to function like an experimental logbook, that Carr denies the occurrence of events, and that White reduces history to fiction—and exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be anticipated and defused; a single closing question I can pose to determine whether they have grasped the conceptual distinction rather than merely adopted a reflexive relativism; and an explicit statement of what should be deliberately omitted at this level, particularly any technical apparatus from narrative theory that requires prior familiarity with tropological analysis. Because my audience lives in a world of calibrated instruments and theory-laden observation, the plan should also incorporate, at the appropriate moment, an explicit analogy to the philosophy of scientific measurement—demonstrating that recognizing the constructedness of historical narrative does not require abandoning the distinction between evidence and invention any more than recognizing calibration dependence requires abandoning the distinction between signal and noise.
