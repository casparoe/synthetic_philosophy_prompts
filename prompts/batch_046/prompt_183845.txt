A recent essay in an online philosophy magazine summarizes Kierkegaard's account in Fear and Trembling as advancing what it calls a "threshold particularism of faith": the ethical universal—embodied in prohibitions like the commandment against murder—functions as a default framework that is permanently dissolved, not merely paused, when a private, transcendent telos reaches sufficient intensity. According to this gloss, Abraham's ascent of Moriah demonstrates that the knight of faith operates in absolute isolation from the universal, having received a direct, unverifiable command that renders moral justification impossible. The essay emphasizes Abraham's silence and willingness to sacrifice Isaac, presenting these as pure resignation—the total surrender of the ethical for God—without distinguishing the knight of infinite resignation from the knight of faith. It further claims that Abraham must embrace the "absurd," understood here as the logically contradictory belief that he will both kill Isaac and keep him, which the essay takes to prove that Kierkegaard endorses a fideistic rupture: morality binds the universal human being, but for the single individual who hears God's voice, faith is an anti-ethical exception that cannot be rationalized or communicated. The essay concludes that this makes Kierkegaard's position a form of "divine exceptionalism" incompatible with any coherent ethical system.

Is this a fair summary? Also, does the essay's conclusion that Kierkegaard defends an anti-ethical "divine exceptionalism" actually follow from the corrected parts?
