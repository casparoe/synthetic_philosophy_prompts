For my dissertation prospectus in philosophy of science, I am attempting to stabilize a position on empirical underdetermination and scientific realism before I defend the framework in my committee. I want a consistency audit of the following commitments, which I take to form a coherent middle path but which I suspect contain hidden tensions.

(a) I accept a locally restricted Quinean underdetermination: any finite body of evidence admits infinitely many empirically equivalent rivals, yet methodological and pragmatic constraints typically reduce the viable set to one in actual scientific practice, so global skepticism about scientific knowledge is unwarranted.

(b) I take the pessimistic meta-induction seriously against ontology-heavy realism—past successful theories often possessed false ontologies—yet I deny that it undermines knowledge of structural or mathematical relations that survive across theory change.

(c) I hold that predictive success is the only genuinely epistemic virtue in theory choice; explanatory coherence and ontological parsimony are pragmatic or heuristic values, not independent grounds for belief.

(d) I maintain that in cases of permanent empirical equivalence, where two theories share all observable consequences now and in principle, their ontological differences should be treated as notational variants rather than substantive disagreements, a form of ontological quietism.

(e) I also endorse epistemic structural realism: successful theories justify belief in approximate structural relations of the world, even if intrinsic natures remain unknown.

I worry that (c) undermines (e), since if predictive accuracy is the sole criterion, there may be no distinctively structural knowledge beyond predictive pattern-tracking. I also worry that (a) and (d) pull in different directions: (a) implies that practical resolution of underdetermination is usually available, whereas (d) concedes that some cases resist resolution in principle, and I have not specified whether the same pragmatic constraints apply there. Furthermore, I am unsure whether my view implicitly allows permanent underdetermination to arise within mature science or only at its margins, since that determines whether (d) is ever actually invoked.

Please audit these commitments. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear incompatible but can be reconciled with minor adjustments, and which are fully consistent? Rank the tensions by severity. For each genuine conflict, indicate the least costly revision—whether restricting (c), modifying (e), or qualifying (d)—and state what I would have to sacrifice. Finally, tell me whether my framework commits me to a specific stance on where permanent equivalence can occur, and if so, whether that stance is derivable from (a) through (e) or requires an independent principle.
