I recently regained full awareness after several days of medically induced unconsciousness, and the experience has made the meta-problem of consciousness feel less like an abstract puzzle and more like a lived discrepancy. I am drafting a short essay with the following thesis and structure, and I would welcome critical comments.

The essay accepts Chalmers's standard framing: physical explanations of consciousness appear to leave an explanatory residue, and the meta-problem asks why we feel this residue so strongly. Rather than treating the gap as ontological, I propose that it arises from a modal scope error in phenomenal judgment. Phenomenal concepts appear to function like rigid designators in modal reasoning: we treat 'what it is like to be conscious' as necessarily co-extensive with the experience itself across possible worlds, whereas physical descriptions present only contingent correlations. This asymmetry generates the illusion of an explanatory deficit, analogous to the mistake of demanding conceptual transparency for necessary a posteriori identities. My recent absence of consciousness supports this framing: neural activity continued during the unresponsive period without producing any first-person datum, which suggests that the explanatory failure we report is indexed to the presence or absence of a particular representational format rather than to an unexplained ontology. I then address the objection that this collapses the meta-problem into the hard problem, replying that explaining the stability conditions of phenomenal concepts is a distinct task from explaining consciousness itself.

Please evaluate whether this line of reasoning genuinely distinguishes the meta-problem from the hard problem, whether the analogy with modal rigidity holds, and where the argument is weakest. In your response, please say how confident you are in your assessment and explicitly identify the specific point about which you are least certain.
