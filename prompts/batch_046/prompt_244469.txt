I've been thinking about a claim that circulates in recent policy sociology but rarely gets examined on its own terms: that the machinery of workfare and benefit fraud detection does not merely filter out an independently existing class of "undeserving poor" but actually constructs that category through its own operations. The argument, as I understand it, runs like this. Conditionality regimes—mandatory work requirements, recurring eligibility audits, digital tracking of appointments, and complex documentation rules—generate procedural failures such as missed signatures, expired forms, or scheduling conflicts. These failures are then interpreted not as artifacts of bureaucratic friction but as moral failures: evidence of irresponsibility, dependency culture, or fraud. Rather than revealing pre-existing undeservingness, the administrative apparatus produces the very data that justifies its own expansion and stricter conditionality. If this is right, then debates about welfare reform have been systematically miscast, treating "undeservingness" as a behavioral problem to be detected when it may largely be an institutional output.

Play it straight. What would have to be true for this constructionist account to be wrong? Which of its premises could fail—empirically, conceptually, or normatively? What observations would decisively undermine it—for instance, evidence that procedural violations genuinely predict independent measures of work avoidance across diverse populations, or that fraud rates and sanction rates remain stable across administrative regimes with very different levels of surveillance? What would merely be awkward rather than fatal? And what risks does the view take on—does it collapse into a broader skepticism about all state categorization, or can it be quarantined to welfare administration? I want an honest map of its exposure: where it is genuinely vulnerable, where it has been insulated by qualification, and whether defenders of the view would themselves concede any of these points as counting against it.
