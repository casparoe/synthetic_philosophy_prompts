In discussions about whether rational agents should ever accept actions with near-certain catastrophic personal outcomes in exchange for astronomically large but highly improbable benefits—what critics in decision theory call expected-value fanaticism—defenders sometimes invoke an analogy to portfolio management across parallel branches of possibility. They say that a rational investment fund should be willing to liquidate even its own headquarters subsidiary if doing so maximizes the expected value of the total portfolio, since the manager cares about the aggregate balance sheet, not about the survival of any particular entry. By extension, the argument runs, a rational agent should treat her own annihilation as just one subsidiary being written off: if the cosmic ledger—spread across counterfactual worlds or distant future beings—improves enough, self-preservation carries no special rational weight. The analogy is used specifically to license the conclusion that identity over time and subjective continuity are irrelevant to rational choice, and that only the integral of value across all possibilities matters.

Audit this analogy with precision. First, lay out the explicit correspondence table: what in the decision-theoretic case plays the role of the portfolio manager, the subsidiaries, the headquarters, the balance sheet, and the diversification mechanism? Then identify exactly where the mapping fractures—particularly regarding the non-fungibility of conscious experience, the fact that the "other branches" in the expected-value case are not co-owned entities but unactualized possibilities, whether the manager herself survives liquidation, and whether value is registered from a particular point of view or merely summed impersonally. Assess whether the analogy quietly imports the unjustified premise that the agent is merely one line item in a larger ledger rather than the necessary site from which all evaluation occurs. Finally, propose whether a different structural comparison—perhaps not corporate finance but something that better captures accepting near-certain self-destruction for diffuse, non-reciprocal gains—would serve the defenders more honestly, and state clearly whether the conclusion that rationality requires no priority for self-preservation survives once these disanalogies are fully exposed.
