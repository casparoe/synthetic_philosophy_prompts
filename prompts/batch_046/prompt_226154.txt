I have no formal philosophical training, but over the past two years I have been thinking independently about dispositions—properties like fragility, solubility, or conductivity—and the puzzles that arise when an object seems to possess such a property yet is prevented from manifesting it. I have focused especially on the familiar distinction between finks, in which an internal mechanism removes the disposition precisely when the triggering conditions occur, and masks, in which an external blocker prevents the manifestation. From this study I have developed a thesis I intend to defend next Thursday before a philosophy of science reading group at a nearby university.

The audience will be seven people: four graduate students in metaphysics who are well acquainted with the standard counterexamples to simple conditional analyses of dispositions; one senior researcher whose published work concerns the ontology of powers and who is deeply skeptical of any reduction of dispositions to categorical bases; one visiting scholar from institutional design who applies metaphysical language to organizational rules and safety protocols; and a moderator trained in philosophy of physics who is agnostic about non-Humean causation but insists on conceptual precision. They are not hostile, but they are exacting.

My thesis is that the distinction between fink and mask is not ontologically fundamental. Every mask—say, protective packaging surrounding a fragile vase—depends for its blocking power on dispositions of the masking material itself, such as the foam’s compressibility or the box’s rigidity. Every fink—say, a wire that is live but contains a circuit that breaks the instant it is touched—depends on an external trigger condition that operates exactly like a mask. I call this relation “recursive interdependence.” From it I argue that dispositions are never properties of isolated objects; they are properties of distributed systems that include both the object and whatever interfering or enabling mechanisms are present. I want to extend this to institutional behavior: a safety regulation should not be understood as an external mask placed upon an organization’s risk-taking disposition, but as a constitutive part of the system that defines that very disposition.

Please prepare me for the objections. I need them arranged in the order they are likely to arise, each phrased in the vocabulary that particular listener would actually use. For each objection, tell me whether it carries real force, what I should concede immediately, what my best reply is, and—crucially—where that reply is weakest. I also need to know which single reply has to land cleanly for the proposal to survive the session at all. Include the objection I am secretly hoping nobody raises: that if all dispositions are system-relative in this way, they become empirically undetectable and my view collapses into a form of structural realism I do not wish to endorse. Finally, identify the weakest point in my own argument—the point I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook—and suggest a concise response, deliverable in about forty-five seconds, that addresses it without overreaching.
