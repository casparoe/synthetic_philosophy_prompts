As a retired biologist who has recently begun working through philosophy of science and medicine, I have become preoccupied with the difference between classifying organisms and classifying people. In particular, I am trying to understand Ian Hacking's claim that psychiatric kinds are interactive—that being classified changes the people classified, which in turn changes the classification—whereas natural kinds are indifferent. I want to apply this framework to a well-known institutional episode: the American Psychiatric Association's 1973 decision to remove homosexuality per se from the DSM-II, and then the introduction of ego-dystonic homosexuality in the DSM-III in 1980. Please analyze this episode through the lens of looping effects, with discipline about the mapping. First, identify the relevant actors—APA committee members, activist groups, clinicians, and the individuals being classified—and specify what interactive dynamics each faced. How did being labeled, or suddenly un-labeled, alter self-conception, clinical practice, or political mobilization? Second, say what the looping framework explains well here—perhaps the activist argument that the diagnosis was a mutable social fact rather than a biological constant—and what it misses or misdescribes. For instance, the category did not vanish but migrated into an adjacent diagnostic niche; I am inclined to call this taxonomic ghosting, by analogy to removing a species name from biological taxonomy without eliminating the underlying phenotype. Does Hacking's model anticipate such displacement, or does it implicitly assume that removing an interactive kind dissolves its behavioral effects? Third, examine whether there is a temporal asymmetry the standard loop narrative overlooks: the removal occurred in 1973, but the adjacent category did not appear until 1980. Does this seven-year latency suggest an institutional inertia—something like diagnostic drag—that pure interaction models fail to capture? Fourth, tell me what the episode demonstrates about the theory itself. Is it a straightforward confirmation, an unanticipated anomaly, or evidence of a structural limitation—perhaps that psychiatric classifications behave more like ecological niches that can be emptied and then recolonized rather than like simple feedback loops? Be precise about who counts as the classified population at each stage, what counts as the behavioral change, and where the loop is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list; I want the conceptual analysis itself.
