I retired two years ago from a long career in experimental physics, and since then I have been working through philosophy of mind and epistemology in a local reading group; one of my new friends there, a specialist in self-knowledge, suggested I might speak at an interdisciplinary workshop our old university is hosting next month, titled "Limits of Introspection in Scientific Practice." I have agreed to deliver the opening remarks—roughly fifteen minutes—to a mixed audience of philosophers of mind and practicing scientists and graduate students who will not share the philosophers' technical vocabulary. I want the remarks to take luminosity and the KK principle not as decorative allusions but as structural constraints on what I can honestly claim: if mental states are not luminous—if one can feel cold without being in a position to know that one feels cold—then a parallel opacity infects collective scientific belief, so that a research group can hold a theoretical commitment without being in a position to know that it holds it, and the KK principle, even if it survives for individual knowledge, fails to transmit across the distributed cognition of a laboratory. This must shape the rhetoric of the speech: I must avoid any phrase that presupposes transparent collective self-knowledge, such as "we all know that we know our own assumptions," and I want the tone to land as methodological humility rather than as skeptical dismissal.

Please structure the remarks as exactly five brief thematic movements, numbered 1 through 5, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no separate introduction or conclusion outside those five items. The language should be precise enough that philosophers recognize the technical stakes—particularly the distinction between knowing p and knowing that one knows p—while remaining accessible to the scientists in the room, and the closing movement should leave the audience with a sense of epistemic caution rather than triumphal certainty. I do not want bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; I want the argument to stand on its own, with the failure of luminosity in individual minds serving as the direct analog for the failure of second-order knowledge in collaborative research.
