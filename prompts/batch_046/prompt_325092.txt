In my philosophy of history seminar, we have been examining the conventional triad of Leopold von Ranke's methodological prescription that the historian should reconstruct the past as it actually occurred, E.H. Carr's argument that every historical fact is selected and interpreted through the present concerns of the historian, and Hayden White's thesis that historical narratives are necessarily structured by prefigured literary tropes—irony, tragedy, comedy, and romance—that operate prior to any empirical verification. I have come to believe that both Carr and White are attacking a misinterpretation of Ranke. Rather than demanding a psychologically neutral, mirror-like reproduction of past events, Ranke is advancing a procedural norm about the critical evaluation of primary sources: what might be called fidelity to the documentary trace rather than to a reconstructed mental image of the past. From this, I wonder whether Carr's emphasis on selection and White's emphasis on narrative form are not fundamental conceptual obstacles but merely practical or stylistic limitations that could, in principle, be overcome.

Specifically, if we possessed complete digitized archives—giving us access to every documentary remainder without gaps—and applied a formal model of counterfactual dependency that mapped only those causal relations holding between events regardless of how they are narratively configured, could we not achieve Ranke's ideal precisely by eliminating the interpretive framing that Carr and White identify? In other words, doesn't the possibility of algorithmically assisted archival science, combined with a logic of historical causation that is independent of emplotment, show that scepticism about historical objectivity is merely a contingent limitation of our current methods rather than a necessary feature of historical knowledge?
