Read John Milton's Paradise Lost as a philosophical dramatization of the problem of evil, concentrating on what theologians have called prelapsarian evil: Satan's rebellion and the war in Heaven occur before the Fall of Adam and Eve, which means cosmic evil originates not in human disobedience but in angelic free choice. I want a reading that takes both the poem and the philosophy seriously. First, does Milton's depiction of Satan's fall support, complicate, or undermine Plantinga's free will defense—especially the idea of transworld depravity applied to rational creatures other than humans? Consider specific moments: Satan's claim in Book V that he is self-begot and self-raised and therefore owes no obedience; the heavenly council in Book III where God foreknows but does not prevent the rebellion; and the poem's repeated insistence that God permits but does not author evil. Second, where does the poem resist reduction to a theodicy? Does Milton's aesthetic glorification of Satan's rhetoric, energy, and political organization in Pandemonium make evil more attractive or intelligible than any philosophical defense allows, and does that aesthetic power undermine the claim that evil is purely a privation of good? Third, does the work add something to the debate that analytical arguments miss—perhaps about the phenomenology of rebellion, or about how foreknowledge and permission relate to moral responsibility in non-human agents? Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the poem to an illustration of Plantinga or Mackie, and do not distort the philosophical concepts to fit the verse. You need not quote verbatim, but ground your claims in well-known scenes and speeches, and conclude by saying what Paradise Lost contributes to the philosophy of evil that argument alone cannot capture.
