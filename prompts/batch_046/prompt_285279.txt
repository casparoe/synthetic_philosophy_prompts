I'm drafting a methodological chapter for a graduate seminar on historiography, and I keep running into a framing that strikes me as too shallow: my colleagues treat the divide between microhistory and "history from below" on one side, and state-centered or elite-focused macrohistory on the other, as primarily a moral or political choice—whose voices deserve recovery versus whose actions explain structural change. I think this misses a cluster of deeper conceptual dependencies that determine whether the two approaches can even be integrated, but I am unsure which of the underlying problems is genuinely upstream and worth settling first.

In particular, there seem to be at least three unresolved tensions that shape everything else. First, there is what I will call the problem of scalar recursion: the categories through which subaltern experience is reconstructed—terms like debt, honor, kin obligation, or territorial belonging—are often already mediated by the very bureaucratic, legal, or census frameworks produced by states and elites. If that is correct, then "pure" recovery from below may be conceptually unstable, and any integration with macro-level explanation requires resolving how micro-level descriptions avoid silently importing elite taxonomies. Second, there is an evidentiary asymmetry: archives are state artifacts, so the evidential base for microhistory is structurally thinner and more reconstructive than that for elite history, which raises the question of whether the methodological standards applied to both can be the same without begging the question. Third, there is a normative-explanatory ambiguity: does writing history from below presuppose that individual or local experience has intrinsic explanatory priority, or can it remain agnostic about whether such experience actually drives large-scale outcomes?

Given that I have perhaps two years of focused research time and no special access to undiscovered archives, I want a committed, dependency-aware prioritization among these open lines of work—along with any others you think belong in the same cluster. Which problem is upstream of the others in the sense that its resolution would change how we approach the rest? Which is most tractable with existing conceptual tools rather than requiring new empirical discoveries? And which should be deprioritized because resolving it would not substantially alter historiographical practice? Please defend the ordering explicitly: state the criteria you are using—whether upstream dependency, tractability, or impact on integration between scales—and indicate what kind of conceptual result over the next few years would cause you to revise the ranking. I am not asking for a survey or a list of readings; I want a definite agenda with reasons, not a menu of options left for me to choose from.
