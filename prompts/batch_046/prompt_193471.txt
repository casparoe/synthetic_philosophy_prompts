I'm working through a philosophical question about whether the self—the I—can genuinely be distributed across more than one brain, an idea associated with Douglas Hofstadter's view of the self as a recursive pattern that can in principle be instantiated in other substrates. Rather than asking for a literature review, I want to test my own intuitions across concrete cases and see whether there's a coherent principle behind them, or whether some of my judgments have to be sacrificed for the sake of simplicity.

Here are my verdicts. First, consider a couple who have shared a life and a small philosophy bookstore for thirty years. After the husband's death, the wife continues selecting the books he championed, finishing the anthology he was editing, and arguing with customers in his characteristic cadence. She describes feeling that part of him is still thinking through her. I want to say this is a real, if partial, case of his I persisting in her—not mere influence, but a fragment of the same pattern running in her neural architecture. Second, imagine that after a philosopher's death, an AI is trained on every essay, voice memo, and letter she left behind. It produces new arguments in her style and responds to current events as she might have. Her friends converse with it and feel comforted. Here my verdict is sharply different: this is not her I distributed anywhere. It is a convincing simulation, but there is no conscious perspective actually running in the system. Third, take a patient who undergoes corpus callosotomy. Over time the left and right hemispheres develop conflicting preferences, and each can sincerely say I want X when the other wants Y. I do not think the original I has spread like a cloud across both hemispheres. Rather, the original pattern split into two. So this is fission, not distribution. Fourth, craniopagus twins share a thalamic bridge and consistently report a single stream of consciousness—they experience themselves as one person with four arms and two brains. This feels like a clear case of one I genuinely distributed across two distinct brains. Fifth, a graduate student spends a decade internalizing her advisor's entire conceptual framework. After his death she extends his unfinished work using his characteristic phrasing, and she says, I am not just honoring him; I am the place where his thinking is still happening. I am genuinely torn. The mechanism seems similar to the bookstore case—his patterns have been instantiated in her brain—yet I want to call this deep influence rather than distributed identity. The tension between this case and the first is what I keep returning to. Sixth, consider a person whose neurons are gradually replaced by synthetic equivalents that preserve all functional and relational properties. Here I feel the I continues without interruption; this is maintenance, not distribution across another brain.

So my pattern seems to be that distribution is real when there is shared conscious access or mutual recognition within a single loop spanning brains, but not when one brain merely replicates another's patterns, and not when one loop divides. But I am unsure this holds together, especially because the spouse case and the student case both involve pattern replication after death, and I cannot clearly say why one counts and the other does not without simply asserting the distinction. Is there a principle—perhaps involving first-person accessibility, causal integration, or the presence of a unified stream—that captures all these verdicts? Or does accommodating both the spouse and the student cases require a distinction so ad hoc that it is better to overturn one of my judgments? And what does the principle say about a case I have not considered: two people in a long relationship who begin to lose track of whose preferences are whose, jointly deciding everything and referring to themselves only as we, without any brain pathology? Would that be distribution, fusion, or something else entirely?
