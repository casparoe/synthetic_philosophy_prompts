I am a retired physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science, and I now serve on the advisory board of an international scientific forecasting council. Our board must issue a formal recommendation on a concrete case: whether Malthusian dynamics—the claim that population tends to grow geometrically while subsistence resources expand only arithmetically or linearly, eventually producing positive checks—should be included in the council’s official long-range demographic framework (projections extending two centuries or more), or whether they should be excluded on the grounds that the demographic transition and sustained agricultural innovation have already empirically falsified them.

I defend inclusion, framing the theory as “right but early”: the technological rescues observed since the nineteenth century represent temporary perturbations within a much longer arc rather than permanent escapes, and the underlying mechanism remains operative unless an indefinite, sustained decoupling of population growth from resource limits can be demonstrated. The board needs a decision memo—recommend inclusion, exclusion, or inclusion with strict conditions—that members trained in empirical science can actually respond to, with clear reasoning and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.

In your memo, please address the following with formal institutional precision:

- State the actionable recommendation clearly at the outset, then justify it.
- Present the strongest version of the “right but early” argument, including the distinction between mechanism and short-run outcome, and draw an explicit analogy to long-range dynamics in physics or ecology where delayed feedback is standard rather than exceptional.
- Confront the central objection: that allowing predictions extending beyond observable horizons risks rendering the theory unfalsifiable, and assess whether such unfalsifiability is a philosophical vice for a forecasting framework or a tolerable feature of genuinely asymptotic models.
- Identify which empirical patterns over the coming centuries would count as fatal defeaters (genuinely undermining the asymptotic claim) versus merely awkward complications (delaying but not preventing the mechanism), and say whether defenders of inclusion can concede the awkward cases without abandoning the view.
- Assess whether the “right but early” framing is well-insulated from refutation, and conclude with an explicit judgment: is that insulation a philosophical virtue or a vice for an institutional forecasting body?
