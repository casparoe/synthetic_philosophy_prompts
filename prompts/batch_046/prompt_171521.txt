I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy working on the ethics of public memory, and I'm trying to determine what principle governs when commemorative objects—statues, street names, institutional names—ought to be altered, contextualized, or left intact.

Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. A town-square equestrian statue honors a 19th-century governor who founded the region's first public university and also authorized the forced displacement of an indigenous community; it should be relocated to a museum with full historical documentation, not melted down or concealed. A street named after a factory owner who introduced modern sanitation infrastructure but violently suppressed labor organizing should be renamed after a worker killed in that suppression. A museum should retain a portrait bust of a physician who developed a widely adopted surgical technique but performed it repeatedly on non-consenting enslaved subjects without anesthesia, provided the display carries a detailed critical label. A university should keep the name of its founding benefactor, a philosopher whose ethical writings influenced democratic theory but who also published scientific racism, and should instead establish an annual critical lecture series rather than rename buildings.

The pair that resists reconciliation involves a fifth case: a public statue of a scientist who developed a life-saving vaccine but conducted lethal, non-consensual human experiments. Here I judge that the statue should be removed entirely rather than relocated. Both the governor and the scientist caused direct bodily harm alongside lasting institutional contributions; yet I want preservation-with-context for one and erasure for the other.

The case I keep revisiting is ambiguous: a bridge still in daily use bears the name of the engineer who designed it, and he employed forced labor in its construction. Should the name be changed, should a plaque be added, or does functional infrastructure carry a different commemorative weight?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps turning on whether harm is structurally embedded in the legacy being honored, whether the commemorative form functions as honorific endorsement rather than historical indexing, or whether physical interaction with the object is required for the wrong—that delivers all four firm verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn? And what does the best available account predict for the bridge?
