I am presenting at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-science seminar the thesis that Nietzsche’s three modes of history—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—diagnose a structural misalignment in modern research universities: the humanities have collapsed antiquarian preservation and critical demolition into a single “heritage management” mode that can do neither well, while the natural sciences deploy a selectively monumental history that venerates technological progress but suppresses the critical examination of its ethical foundations. I argue that interdisciplinary institutes should adopt a “critical-monumental hybrid,” using monumental narrative not to celebrate past scientists but to expose the contingent moral choices embedded in scientific development, thereby reviving critical history within scientific self-understanding.

The audience will be about fifteen people: historians of science who treat archival recovery as an autonomous good; analytic philosophers of history who regard Nietzsche’s normative taxonomy as psychologically reductive; science-studies scholars who see all “great man” narratives as ideological; and graduate students who will press practical implementation questions. Prepare me for the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each faction would phrase them, indicating which points I should concede up front, which I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I must defend rather than hope they miss. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks, with a concise reply I could deliver in under ninety seconds. Please write your answer in British English.
