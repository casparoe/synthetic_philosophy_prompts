I'm currently enrolled in an upper-division seminar on moral exemplarity and ancient biography, and we've been working through Plutarch's paired biographies alongside contemporary discussions of how narrative shapes virtue. In our conversations, I've kept returning to a particular rhetorical pattern that I can't quite place within the vocabulary we've used—terms like humanization, hagiographic inflation, or the Aristotelian tragic flaw all seem adjacent, yet they miss the specific mechanism I'm observing.

The pattern works as follows. A biographer includes, without excusing or rationalizing, an episode in which the subject commits an act that seems directly antithetical to the central virtue being celebrated: a lawgiver renowned for justice who, in a documented moment of private rage, enacts a brutally disproportionate punishment; a philosopher-ruler who destroys a long friendship through what appears to be pure vanity; a leader celebrated for clemency who, in a single well-attested instance, orders the execution of a prisoner he had just promised to spare. Crucially, the narrative does not treat these episodes as minor blemishes that make the hero relatable, nor does it present them as exceptions that prove the rule. Instead, the negative act is rendered in its full moral weight—sometimes even magnified by the biographer's refusal to diminish it. The result is that the reader's sense of the subject's virtue seems to intensify precisely because of the surrounding darkness, as if the vice functions not as a counter-example but as a kind of moral shadow that gives the virtue dimensionality and gravity. I have noticed this in several of the Lives, and I suspect it operates in later hagiographic traditions as well, though I am unsure whether it has been analyzed as a distinct technique.

Does this configuration have an established name in the literature on biography, moral education, or narrative ethics? I am especially interested in how it differs from the familiar idea of "humanizing" a saint through trivial imperfections, and from the tragic-flaw structure in which a single error drives an inevitable downfall. If there is no standard term, I want to understand exactly where its boundaries lie: for instance, does the effect depend on the vice being genuinely severe rather than petty, and does it collapse if the biographer offers an explicit apology or psychological rationalization? A bare label will not satisfy me; I want to know whether the carving is already recognized, what distinguishes it from neighboring concepts, and whether there are clear conditions under which it fails.
