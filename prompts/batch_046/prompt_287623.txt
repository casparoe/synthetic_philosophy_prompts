I work as a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and after discussing this with colleagues I've been trying to make sense of how scientific thought experiments can yield real knowledge when they never involve an actual lab bench. I've come to a specific view that I'd like honestly stress-tested rather than defended.

The position is this: thought experiments in science do not work primarily by running mental simulations of physical processes, nor by pure logical deduction in isolation, but by functioning structurally like property-based tests in software engineering—where random or extremal inputs are fed to a system to verify that invariants hold. On this account, a scientific thought experiment takes a theoretical framework, constructs an edge-case scenario that should preserve certain core invariants (for instance, the equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass, or the consistency conditions on quantum measurement), and checks whether the framework violates those invariants in that scenario. The knowledge gained is therefore diagnostic: it exposes hidden inconsistencies, unstated boundary conditions, or unintended commitments embedded in the formal structure, rather than delivering synthetic empirical facts about nature. Galileo's falling bodies, Einstein's elevator, and Schrödinger's cat are best understood not as miniature mental observations but as stress-tests that reveal where a theory breaks or what it implicitly assumes.

If this view is wrong, what exactly would have to be true? Which commitments—perhaps that the knowledge is purely diagnostic, or that the software-testing parallel captures something essential rather than being merely metaphorical—could fail, and what would scientific practice or particular canonical experiments have to look like for each failure? I want you to distinguish evidence or arguments that would be decisive from those that would merely be awkward, and to say what defenders of this position would themselves concede as a genuine vulnerability—say, cases where thought experiments seem to generate positive new predictions rather than just expose inconsistencies. Is there any risk that the view has been insulated by qualifications, so that every success gets redescribed as a hidden stress-test and every failure gets dismissed as a poorly constructed input? Finally, if my framing imports software concepts where they genuinely don't belong, or if it rests on a misunderstanding of what philosophers of science actually claim about thought experiments, please say so plainly instead of accommodating the error politely. I want an honest map of how exposed this position actually is.
