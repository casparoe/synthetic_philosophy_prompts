After a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether societies ever truly “collapse” or merely reorganize, I tried to formalize her commitments. She holds that the Late Bronze Age crisis around 1177 BCE was a collapse: palaces burned, Linear B and related administrative scripts were interrupted, long-distance trade networks shattered, and core settlement areas in the Aegean and Levant saw sharp demographic contraction. She insists the Classic Maya lowlands around 800–900 CE were not a collapse but a transformation: lowland cities were abandoned, yet populations persisted, hieroglyphic writing and ritual practice continued in the highlands and Yucatán, and the change looks more like political decentralization than societal death. She counts Rapa Nui around 1600–1800 as a collapse: deforestation eliminated the resource base for large-scale cooperative labor, moai construction halted permanently, population crashed severely, and no functionally equivalent socio-technical system replaced the statue-building polity. Finally, she treats the Chacoan system around 1130–1300 as not a collapse: the great houses were abandoned, but people relocated to the Rio Grande pueblos, maintained kiva-based social organization, and preserved agricultural and ceremonial continuity; the people survived with their basic institutions intact.

These four verdicts seem jointly consistent with at least two rules. One rule defines collapse as severe demographic contraction in the core territory combined with the abandonment of settlement, regardless of whether people or cultures survive elsewhere. Another defines it as the irreversible disappearance of a society’s defining institutional-technical framework—state-level resource redistribution, monumental construction programs, administrative writing—without a functionally equivalent successor in the same geographic region, regardless of population levels.

The case that now divides them, I think, is the Khmer hydraulic state centered on Angkor in the early fifteenth century. The baray reservoir and temple-mountain system was left unmaintained; the capital shifted southward to riverine centers near Phnom Penh; the hydraulic rice economy that sustained massive state labor was replaced by smaller-scale river trade and dispersed agriculture. Yet the Khmer population did not vanish—the kingdom persisted for centuries, and Khmer language, religion, and social structure continued with clear continuity.

Given her track record, should she classify Angkor as a collapse or as a transformation? Which of her four earlier judgments is the closest analog, and does it genuinely determine the answer? If her precedents underdetermine the case, what principle would she be adopting by calling it a collapse, and what different principle by calling it a transformation? And if we wanted one further historical case that would most cleanly distinguish the demographic-contraction rule from the institutional-irreversibility rule, which should it be?
