Consider the long-standing tension in philosophy of mind between epiphenomenalism — the view that phenomenal properties have no causal powers — and the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if phenomenal properties play no causal role, then the mental states we take to be judgments about them appear to arise and be justified without any causal interaction with the properties they concern. This raises not a single problem but a cluster of interrelated open questions.

Assume you are advising a researcher who has roughly two years and only conceptual, logical, and argumentative resources — no access to empirical neuroscience or to literature searches — and who wants to work on this cluster. The researcher can pursue at most two or three lines in depth. Here are four live but differently oriented lines of work:

(i) The causal-grounding line: whether acquaintance, constitution, or non-causal informational correlation can bridge the gap between physically realized judgment-states and causally inert phenomenal properties, and whether any such bridge avoids violating the causal closure of the physical.

(ii) The functional-surrogate line: whether phenomenal judgments are best reconstructed as judgments about functional or representational roles that are fully physically implemented, so that phenomenal properties remain epiphenomenal but our reports remain causally explicable.

(iii) The transparency-modulation line: whether taking phenomenal experience to be transparent — in the sense that introspective awareness reveals only the experienced quality and not the act of awareness itself — strengthens the paradox by removing representational intermediaries, or weakens it by reconceiving what a phenomenal judgment targets.

(iv) The social-epistemic line: whether first-person phenomenal authority can be sustained at the communal level even if individual judgments lack direct causal contact with phenomenal properties, and whether this collective justification undermines or preserves the epistemic motivation for epiphenomenalism.

Your task is to produce a defended, dependency-aware prioritization. Which two or three of these lines are most worth the researcher's limited time? For each, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others: does resolving it dissolve, relocate, or leave intact the core paradox? Assess tractability given only conceptual tools — which lines depend on empirical findings that are currently unavailable, and which can be advanced by pure argument? Finally, identify which line, if settled in either direction, would change the least for physicalism, for the methodology of consciousness studies, and for the coherence of first-person report. Rank them, defend the criteria you use (e.g., upstream dependence, tractability without empirical data, breadth of downstream impact), and explain what kind of result — conceptual, logical, or structural — would cause you to reorder the priorities.
