I have recently been asked to help design a pairwise voting procedure for a small housing cooperative, so I returned to Condorcet’s 1785 essay on majority decision. In the familiar passage he describes three candidates—A, B, and C—and three voter groups whose individual transitive rankings are arranged cyclically: one group orders A over B over C, another B over C over A, and the third C over A over B. Applying pairwise majority comparison then yields A defeating B, B defeating C, and yet C defeating A. I am trying to understand exactly what Condorcet takes to be the root failure here. Is he diagnosing a purely logical defect—namely, that majority aggregation violates transitivity—or is he pointing to something closer to an epistemic problem, an underdetermination of the collective will by the individual judgments, where the very same preference profile supports incompatible social orderings? I also wonder whether his concern is essentially about rational consistency in social choice, or whether it already anticipates strategic vulnerabilities—situations in which voters might misrepresent preferences—even though Condorcet never discusses strategic behavior. Please help me understand the passage and its underlying diagnosis, separating what is settled about Condorcet’s intended argument from what remains genuinely contested among historians of social choice and political philosophy. In particular, do not present a disputed interpretive claim—such as the view that he was primarily worried about epistemic reliability rather than logical consistency—as if it were the consensus.
