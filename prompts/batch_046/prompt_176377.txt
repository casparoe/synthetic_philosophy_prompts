Exam question:
In mechanistic interpretability, some researchers defend a "binding thesis" for representational realism: an internal feature state S in a neural network genuinely represents property P if and only if (i) S correlates with P across training and test distributions, (ii) S plays a causal role in outputs that depend on P, and (iii) S can be systematically decoupled from P—activated in counterfactual or adversarial contexts where P is absent. Critics object that condition (iii) is redundant because artificial networks lack evolutionary history, so they cannot "misrepresent" in any normatively loaded sense; correlation plus causal efficacy should suffice.

In your answer, evaluate whether condition (iii) is necessary for representation in artificial neural networks. Address specifically whether the absence of biological selection history undermines the applicability of misrepresentation conditions, and whether adversarial decoupling demonstrates representational error or merely a robustness failure.

Student answer:
The misrepresentation condition is necessary because pure correlation plus causation describes only a tracking mechanism, not representation—like a thermometer tracking temperature without representing it. In neural networks, adversarial decoupling shows that a feature detector can be triggered by inputs lacking the target property, which proves the state is not rigidly coupled to the world and therefore carries representational content. Without the possibility of such decoupling, we could not distinguish a representation from a direct causal link. The evolutionary objection is weak: although artificial networks lack biological reproduction, gradient descent and loss minimization constitute a functional selection process that establishes proper norms for internal states. Thus the teleosemantic framework associated with Ruth Millikan actually supports applying misrepresentation conditions to AI, since selection—whether evolutionary or algorithmic—fixes representational content. Therefore, conditions (i) through (iii) together are sufficient, and the binding thesis correctly captures representational status in these systems.
