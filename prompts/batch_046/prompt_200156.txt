hey, I've been trying to square Hobbes's geometry obsession with his politics and I'm getting a weird reductio. Maybe you can find the leak?

(1) In De Corpore and those Wallis fights, Hobbes insists real science needs resolution into discreet, constructible parts—he hates infinite processes that never bottom out in an actual figure. Geometry works because you build the line from points you actually move through.

(2) He then tries to do civil science the same way: start with definitions of motion, fear, contract, and "compose" the commonwealth like a geometer constructs a triangle. No infintesimals, no hidden essences—just clear deductions.

(3) But his sovereign isn't discrete like that. Authority has to survive across generations, bind people who never signed anything (tacit consent), and act through representatives whose will is "present" even when they're asleep or dead. That's continuous, not made of countable authorization-acts.

(4) So if we take the geometrical standard seriously, every binding political obligation has to trace back to a specific, named, discrete act of authorization—otherwise we're smuggling in an "infintesimal" accumulation of consent that Hobbes should reject as absurd, just like he rejects Wallis's infinite quadrature.

(5) But then the sovereign only binds the explicit contractors at the founding moment, and collapses the moment they die or dissent. No lasting state, no inherited obligation.

(6) Hobbes clearly wants both the geometrical method (premise 1-2) and the continuous, inherited sovereign (premise 3). But 4-5 show they can't coexist.

So where's the slip? Is it that I'm treating temporal continuity as a mathematical infintesimal (equivocation)? Or that Hobbes's "resolution" applies to definitions, not to historical persistence? I really want to keep (1) and (3) together if I can—there's something appealing about treating the state as literally constructed—so I'd rather not just ditch the geometrical analogy entirely. What's the least painful exit?
