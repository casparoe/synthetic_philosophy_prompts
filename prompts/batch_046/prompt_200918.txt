I'm reconstructing Bernard Williams's scattered aesthetic judgments about opera—particularly his responses to the charge that opera is inherently ridiculous, his defense of Wagner's mythological narratives despite their dramatic implausibility, his tolerance for stripped-down concert performances that discard spectacle, his resistance to modern updates that destroy a work's symbolic architecture without illuminating its dynamics, and his insistence that live bodily presence and vocal vulnerability are essential even when staging is minimal. The new case concerns a proposed premiere of a recently reconstructed opera from 1680, attributed to an obscure Venetian composer, for which scholars agree the score is genuinely exceptional—dense contrapuntal writing and emotionally complex arias—while the pastoral libretto is conventional and symbolically thin.
