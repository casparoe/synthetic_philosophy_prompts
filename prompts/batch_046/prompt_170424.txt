I was argueing with a friend last night about why promises actually bind — not just legally but in that weird normative sense where you feel like you've done something wrong even if no one finds out. I think every theory I've seen (expectation-based ones, practice-based ones, contractualist-style accounts) handles the obvious cases but falls apart on edge cases nobody talks about enough. So I want the full spec: what would an adequate theory of promissory obligation actually have to deliver?

Here are the cases and distinctions I think it can't collapse: first, the basic ones — promises where the promisee never learns of the promise, promises made under false beliefs about what you're committing to, promises broken in ways that actually make everyone better off. But I also want it to handle weirder stuff I'm inventing but that feels conceptually real: promises made in dreams that you remember upon waking, promises where the content is distributed across a group and no individual understands the whole thing (like a collective promise to maintain some infrastructure no one fully grasps), and echo promises where an AI or recording repeats your exact words in a new context — does the original speaker stay bound? For each, say why it's a genuine desideratum vs just a prejudice of one camp.

Also it has to respect the distinction between being bound (having the obligation) and being blameworthy for breaking it, and between moral bindingness and rational bindingness — sometimes it's irrational to keep a promise but not wrong. And it can't flat-out contradict results from evolutionary psychology about reciprocity and cooperation without explaining why, since if promises are just weird social magic that conflicts with how reciprocity evolved, that's a serious strike. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular theories — expectation theories? Practice theories? The ones treating promises as generating reasons of special weight? I don't need a bibliography or reading list, just the requirements list with the reasoning behind each item, which ones are negotiable, and an honest call on which does the damage. A table is fine.
