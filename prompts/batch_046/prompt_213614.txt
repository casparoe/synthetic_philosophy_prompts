After a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend about Quine’s indeterminacy of translation, I find myself unable to shake the summary he insisted was the real upshot of the gavagai thought experiment. He put it like this: Quine demonstrated that for any foreign language there will always be multiple translation manuals equally compatible with every observable behavioral disposition of native speakers, so that gavagai could mean rabbit, undetached rabbit part, or temporal stage of a rabbit with no empirical fact to settle the matter. From this, he said, Quine draws a far stronger conclusion: not merely that translation is underdetermined, but that there is literally no fact of the matter about what anyone means by any word, even in their own language, because all reference is fixed only relative to a background theoretical framework we choose arbitrarily. He added that this makes Quine’s position a form of radical semantic externalism—mental states and intentions play no role whatsoever in determining reference—which is why modern internalist theories of mental content are fundamentally incompatible with Quinean ontology. He concluded that the gavagai problem therefore dissolves the analytic-synthetic distinction not just for epistemology, but for the very possibility of shared meaning, leaving us with ontological relativity as the only coherent stance.

I am skeptical of several moves in this summary—particularly the slide from translation underdetermination to a global denial of shared meaning, and the characterization of Quine as an extreme externalist who excludes mental content entirely—but I am not well-versed enough in Quine scholarship to say exactly where the distortion lies.
