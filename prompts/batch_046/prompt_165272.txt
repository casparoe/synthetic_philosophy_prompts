I'm writing a feature on the resurgence of Leibnizian optimism in computational philosophy, and I've noticed a recurring analogy in recent essays: the selection of the best of all possible worlds is being compared to a global constrained optimization routine. In this framing, God functions as a solver maximizing perfection subject to the hard constraint of compossibility—filtering out incompatible entities like infeasible solutions—while evil and suffering are interpreted not as design failures but as unavoidable local minima within a globally optimal landscape. Before I commit to this framing, I want to audit it rigorously. Could you lay out the correspondence table—what maps onto the objective function, the constraint set, the search space of possible worlds, and the optimizer itself? Then identify where the analogy fractures: does Leibniz's conception of divine choice allow for iterative evaluation or temporal search, which optimization algorithms require? Can local minima be coherently applied to a non-iterative, atemporal selection? Does the analogy preserve Leibniz's distinction between metaphysical evil (necessary limitation) and moral evil (sin), or does it collapse both into a single cost function? And crucially, once the disanalogies are exposed—particularly the absence of sequential computation in classical theism—does the conclusion that suffering is structurally inevitable still stand, or did the computational metaphor smuggle it in?
