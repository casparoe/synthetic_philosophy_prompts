You are a senior clinical ethicist with training in comparative philosophy and institutional policy design. Before drafting, briefly identify the central philosophical tension and the practical stakes for staff welfare, then write in formal memo style.

The case: Our hospice’s Ethics and Cultural Practice Board meets in ten days to decide on an advance-directive request from the family of an imminently dying resident. The family invokes Zhuangzi’s philosophy of death—specifically the view that death is natural transformation like the turning of seasons, that the body is temporary qi-form rather than a lasting self, and that grief and mourning presuppose a false fixation on individual identity. They request a 72-hour “protocol of non-distinction” after death: (1) immediate return of the body to an on-site garden for natural decomposition, with no embalming, viewing, or naming ceremony; (2) staff must refrain from all sympathy, condolence, or grief-language, since such expressions, in the family’s view, reinforce the metaphysical error of treating death as loss of a permanent self; (3) institutional bereavement support is to be withheld from the family, who regard counseling as encouraging attachment to a self that has merely transformed, comparable to discarding worn clothing; and (4) staff are asked to practice xin zhai—emptying emotional response—rather than standard empathetic engagement.

The nursing staff object that items (2) and (4) risk moral injury, violate professional norms of compassionate presence, and may force staff to suppress legitimate grief. The family responds that partial accommodation—allowing (1) and (3) while requiring staff empathy—would be institutional hypocrisy, compelling them to perform the very rituals their philosophy rejects.

Your memo should recommend either full accommodation with safeguards, partial accommodation with clear boundaries, or denial, specifying an implementation timeline if adopted. Engage the Zhuangzian position seriously: address the transformation view, the dissolution of fixed identity (including how the butterfly-dream problem undermines sharp self-boundaries across life and death), and the claim that institutional empathy can itself perpetuate metaphysical error. At the same time, treat the strongest consideration on the losing side—whether emotional suppression harms staff, whether the family may be conflating philosophical acceptance with emotional avoidance, and whether a hospice’s duty of care includes sustaining those who witness death repeatedly. End with an actionable bottom line the board can vote on.
