I'm a phd student in philosophy of action and I've been stuck on this argumet about incommensurable values. I was talking with my adviseor about hard choices — like leaving academia for art — and I tried to formalize something I'm calling "second-order framework paralysis." The idea is that if two paths embody genuinely incommensurable values, then not only is there no metric comparing them, but there's also no rational basis for adopting the framework that would let you compare them. So here's the chain I built:

(1) If values A and B are genuinely incommensurable, there's no rational metric comparing them.
(2) But rational choice requires at least some metric, even just ordinal ranking.
(3) So incommensurability suspends rational choice at the first order.
(4) Now, because of second-order framework paralysis, adopting any framework to resolve the choice is itself groundless — you can't justify picking the framework that favors A over B, since that adoption involves comparing the frameworks.
(5) So if rational choice is suspended at both orders, then picking path A can't be justified by reasons at all; it can only be a brute causal event, like a coin landing heads.
(6) But if it's just brute causation, then saying "I chose art because I value beauty" is just post-hoc labeling, no different from describing the coin.
(7) We clearly treat people who make these choices as having done something meaningful and defensible, not as having suffered a random event.
(8) So either incommensurability never applies, or our whole practice of praising hard choices is based on a mistake.
(9) I want to keep (1) and especially (7), but (8) proves way too much. Where's the slip? Is (5) smuggling in that reasons must be comparative? Or is the problem in (4), where I assume framework-adoption needs justification?

Please tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs — do I have to accept there's a hidden common currency after all? Also, please say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure of. I have a hunch the problem is in (4) but I'm really not sure if I'm just protecting my intuition.
