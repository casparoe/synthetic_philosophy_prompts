Here is roughly where I have landed on Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments and the philosophy of mathematical cognition, and I would like a consistency audit before I present it in a reading group. My commitments are:

(1) The Lucas-Penrose argument is unsound because it treats the human mind as a single, consistent formal system with a fixed, recursively enumerable axiom set; actual human reasoning is modular, context-sensitive, and can tolerate local inconsistencies without global collapse.

(2) Despite (1), I think there is a genuine cognitive distinction between running an automated proof-checker and genuinely understanding why a formal system—say, Peano Arithmetic—is consistent: the latter involves grasping the structural coherence of the axioms in a way that is not reducible to symbol-manipulation.

(3) This grasp is neither mystical nor Platonic; it is a fallible, trainable expertise realised in ordinary physical brain processes, and it improves or degrades like any other skill.

(4) I am therefore committed to strong anti-mechanism: no physical system operating by a fully specified algorithm—symbolic, connectionist, or hybrid—can possess this kind of structural understanding.

(5) At the same time, I hold that the mechanistic/non-mechanistic boundary is not an ontological divide in nature but a methodological one: a process counts as mechanistic precisely when we can, in principle, provide a complete algorithmic reconstruction of it, regardless of its physical substrate.

(6) I also treat computer-verified consistency proofs (for instance, in Coq or Lean) as genuinely extending my own mathematical knowledge, not as alien outputs, even though I do not personally execute the underlying algorithmic steps.

Please audit this package. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? Rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give. Identify the single revision that cleans up the most problems at once. Be specific about whether (1) undermines the usual support for (4), whether (2) and (3) can be reconciled without abandoning physicalism, whether (5) collapses (4) into a claim about our epistemic limitations rather than about cognition itself, and whether (6) introduces a hidden tension with (2).
