As a retired physicist who has only recently begun working through the literature in social epistemology and formal decision theory, I find myself returning to the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation—particularly the tension between premise-based and conclusion-based rules when individual rationality does not guarantee collective coherence. Consider the well-known episode of the NASA–Thiokol teleconference on 27 January 1986, on the eve of the Space Shuttle Challenger launch. The salient facts, as commonly reported, are these: a group of Thiokol engineers had formed individual judgments about three related propositions—that prior low-temperature launches had shown O-ring erosion (P1), that the forecast for the morning of 28 January was unusually cold (P2), and therefore that the launch should not proceed (C). Several engineers held the conjunction of P1 and P2 but, under institutional pressure and a demand for quantitative proof of failure, revised their recommendation; NASA management, weighing programmatic and political considerations, endorsed the launch. The shuttle was destroyed shortly after lift-off, with O-ring failure the immediate technical cause.

Analyze this episode through the lens of judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the judgment profile of the engineers and the managers as sets of attitudes toward P1, P2, and C. Show whether a premise-based majority rule and a conclusion-based majority rule yield divergent collective judgments—i.e., whether the episode instantiates the discursive dilemma in a literal or near-literal form.

2. Examine what the theory predicts about how the aggregation ought to have been conducted. Would a premise-based rule have blocked the launch? Would a conclusion-based rule have permitted it? Discuss whether either rule captures the normative force of the engineers' technical reasoning, or whether both miss the asymmetry between epistemic authority (the engineers' domain-specific competence) and institutional authority (management's decision rights).

To push the analysis beyond the standard textbook framing, inject a hybrid aggregation mechanism: suppose the group had used sequential premise-first aggregation—first aggregating beliefs about P1 and P2 by simple majority weighted by proximity to the physical evidence, then requiring that the collective conclusion C be endorsed only if it followed deductively and was not subject to hierarchical veto. Apply this mechanism to the Challenger profile. Does it resolve the dilemma, reproduce it in a different form, or expose a limitation in the formal apparatus?

Finally, say what the Challenger episode reveals about judgment aggregation theory itself—whether it is a genuine anomaly, a confirmation that aggregation rules must be supplemented by procedural safeguards, or a case the theory never anticipated because it assumes symmetric rational agents rather than hierarchically structured organizations with divergent incentives.
