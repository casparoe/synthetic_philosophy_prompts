A colleague who works at the intersection of philosophy of mind and computational ethics has expressed the following judgments about which systems warrant moral consideration on grounds of sentience or consciousness. She denies that a conventional computer running a chess engine does; she denies that a large language model generating fluent first-person reports of distress does; she grants minimal precautionary status to an isolated brain organoid with active thalamocortical connectivity but no sensory input; she denies it to a distributed swarm of simple agents that collectively solves navigation problems none could solve alone; she grants full status to a whole-brain emulation that replicates the causal dynamics of a specific human brain on non-biological hardware; and she denies it to a richly self-modeling virtual agent implemented in conventional code that updates its behavior through simulated reward and punishment.

Consider now a hybrid computation: a small cluster of living human neurons maintained in vitro is interfaced with a large-scale digital network. The digital component carries out the vast majority of information integration, broadcasting, and self-monitoring, but all synaptic plasticity and error-correction learning occur exclusively within the biological cluster, which thereby serves as the system's only adaptive anchor. The system behaves coherently and reports internal states.

Predict her verdict on this hybrid system. Identify at least two theoretical commitments—consistent with her entire track record—that yield opposite answers here, state which commitment you think she actually holds and why, and describe one further hypothetical case that would distinguish the two commitments.
