As a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I have drafted the notes below for a short essay on Gödel’s incompleteness theorems and what they do and do not imply when taken outside mathematics. I would be grateful for your comments—particularly on whether my reading of the theorems is accurate, whether the engineering analogy holds, and whether I have avoided common misuses—so that I can refine the argument before writing it up fully.

Notes for the essay: “The Unprovable Bug—Gödel, Legacy Code, and the Limits of Verification”

The starting point is that Gödel’s first and second incompleteness theorems are frequently invoked in popular and even technical discourse to make sweeping claims: that the human mind transcends computation, that physics must be forever incomplete, or that artificial intelligence is inherently limited. I want to avoid these overreaches entirely. Instead, I propose to apply the theorems narrowly and precisely to formal verification in software engineering, specifically to legacy monolithic systems that lack complete formal specifications.

The core analogy is this. A verification framework—comprising axioms (the specification), inference rules, and derived theorems (verified properties)—functions like a formal system in Gödel’s sense, provided it is sufficiently powerful and recursively axiomatisable. The first theorem then implies that, if the framework is consistent, there will exist true statements about the code—e.g., “this module never leaks memory under all valid inputs”—that cannot be proved within the framework itself. In practice, this corresponds to emergent security or correctness properties that automated tools, bounded by their own logic, cannot capture.

The second theorem states that such a system cannot prove its own consistency without stepping outside itself. Translating this into engineering terms: a verification suite cannot certify its own soundness from within. Any attempt to build a “master verification layer” for a continuous integration pipeline—integrating unit tests, static analysis, and formal proofs—therefore introduces new, unverified meta-assumptions at a higher level. I suggest this creates an epistemic regress: every meta-verification relies on an external audit, a different formalism, or human judgement. In legacy codebases, where specifications are always partial, the “Gödelian gap” contains precisely those true bugs arising from unforeseen interactions that no internal framework can derive.

I then want to draw a sharper, somewhat niche distinction. Human code review and runtime monitoring are often treated as mere supplements to automation. I argue they should be understood as functioning analogously to the external oracle or meta-system that Gödel’s argument requires: not a failure of engineering discipline, but a structural necessity when a system is rich enough to talk about itself.

Finally, I intend to clarify what the theorems do not show. They do not entail that all knowledge is incomplete, that software can never be trusted, or that mechanised reasoning is futile. Many practical engineering frameworks deliberately weaken expressiveness—avoiding full arithmetic—to escape Gödelian limits altogether. The correct lesson, I think, is asymptotic humility rather than philosophical despair: verification approaches completeness only in the limit, never reaching it.

Specific points on which I seek comment:

1. Is my technical rendering of the conditions—recursive axiomatisability, consistency, and sufficient arithmetic expressiveness—broadly correct?
2. Does the analogy to legacy-system verification illuminate something genuinely interesting, or does it collapse into the banal claim that “testing cannot catch everything”?
3. Have I successfully avoided the familiar misuses of Gödel—particularly against mechanism or artificial intelligence—while still making the analogy philosophically meaningful?
4. Is the claim about human review acting as an “external oracle” defensible, or does it overstate the parallel and risk conflating practical limitation with logical necessity?

Please treat these as preliminary notes rather than a finished essay. I am especially keen to know whether the central analogy misrepresents the scope of the theorems, or whether the “master verification layer” point needs sharper distinction from ordinary engineering practice.
