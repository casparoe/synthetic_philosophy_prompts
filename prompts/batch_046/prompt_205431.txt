I am working through some contemporary applications of Thomas Szasz's argument that psychiatric diagnoses often function as metaphors that become harmful myths when taken literally—particularly his distinction between genuine problems in living and conditions reified as brain diseases—for a philosophy-of-psychiatry discussion group. Rather than revisiting the classic schizophrenia examples, I want to test whether my intuitions about labeling practices form a coherent pattern across less-discussed modern cases.

Here is how I come out on specific scenarios. First, diagnosing a prisoner's refusal to obey guards as antisocial personality disorder in order to justify prolonged solitary confinement strikes me as plainly illegitimate: it uses medical language to conceal a moral and institutional judgment. Second, describing a teenager's intense emotional volatility and identity experimentation as borderline personality disorder so that her family can access therapy coverage feels acceptable, even though the category is partly constructed; the label is deployed instrumentally to support her without denying her agency. Third, telling a grieving spouse that her sustained sorrow counts as major depressive disorder, solely so that counseling can be reimbursed, seems fine to me, provided she is not simultaneously told that her grief is essentially a biochemical malfunction she is powerless to address. Fourth, classifying widespread climate-related despair among young activists as an eco-anxiety disorder in order to secure clinical funding strikes me as wrong—this appears to be a rational moral response to real conditions, not a pathology—though I keep wavering on whether strategic medicalization might be defensible if it prevents these concerns from being dismissed as mere politics. Fifth, labeling a child who reacts violently to documented abuse with oppositional defiant disorder so that the school can obtain behavioral resources strikes me as not fine, because it transforms an adaptive survival response into an individual defect, even if I go back and forth on whether the practical benefit to the child makes the labeling excusable. Sixth, identifying culturally normative religious visions in a well-integrated community as brief psychotic disorder, in the absence of any severe functional impairment, seems wrong to me; it imposes a biomedical norm on a legitimate form of life.

Is there a precise principle, or a tightly constrained family of principles, that captures why I reject the prison and religious-vision cases but accept the therapy-access and grief-reimbursement ones? I want the principle to account for the tension in my verdicts on the climate-despair and abused-child cases, and I want you to say clearly which of my judgments it cannot save without becoming ad hoc. Please answer in American English, leading with your bottom-line principle (or your conclusion that no unified principle works) in the very first sentence, then giving the point-by-point reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
