As a novelist serving on the editorial board of an independent fiction quarterly, I find myself caught in what feels like a logical trap rather than a mere disagreement among colleagues. This quarter, three submissions have risen to the top: one tracing inherited trauma across generations, one built around a single geographical displacement, and one constructed almost entirely from negative space and withheld dialogue. Each of the seven board members has submitted a strict ranking of these three manuscripts, and together we adopted what seemed like four uncontroversial rules for reaching a collective decision. First, no single member's preference should be decisive. Second, if every board member ranks manuscript A above manuscript B, then A must prevail over B in our final ordering. Third, our procedure must work for any logically possible arrangement of individual rankings, even ones that are internally contradictory or wildly divergent. Fourth, and this is the commitment that feels most essential to our editorial integrity, once we have established a collective preference between any two manuscripts, that preference should not flip if a third manuscript is withdrawn from consideration—the presence of an unchosen text ought not to alter our comparative judgment of the survivors. We have tested plurality voting, Borda counts, pairwise runoff tournaments, and even a custom weighted scheme that gives greater influence to members with longer publication histories, yet in every system we can construct either an actual or hypothetical distribution of our rankings that forces us to violate at least one rule—sometimes producing a hidden dictator, sometimes overturning unanimous agreement, sometimes making the removal of the least-favored manuscript reverse the ordering of the top two. One board member now argues that we should simply abandon the fourth rule, treating stability as an aesthetic luxury rather than a democratic necessity, but I worry that surrendering it collapses the distinction between evaluating literature and playing strategic games. Is there genuinely a fair aggregation method we have overlooked, or is there an inherent structural conflict among these four commitments? And if the conflict is unavoidable, which principle should a collective of writers sacrifice first—the openness to every possible ranking, the respect for unanimous preference, or the stability of pairwise comparison when other options disappear?
