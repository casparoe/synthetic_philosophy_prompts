I am preparing the final examination for my upper-level seminar in moral and political philosophy, and the issue has become personally urgent since I joined a local advisory committee on environmental regulation. Last month we confronted a decision that split the committee along precisely philosophical lines: should we fund a costly upgrade that eliminates a diffuse, statistically distributed respiratory risk affecting tens of thousands—where no single victim is identifiable and each individual burden falls below the threshold that would justify a personal complaint—or should we instead protect a discrete, identifiable community of three hundred people from catastrophic flooding caused by a single point-source failure? The debate turned on whether small, imperceptible harms to many can aggregate into a wrong that justifies imposing costs on others, and I want my students to navigate this terrain without retreating to generic summaries.

Please construct a single 90-minute essay question—answerable in roughly 800 words—aimed at students who understand Scanlon’s contractualist framework but should not be able to answer by reciting the standard “one life versus five headaches” illustrations. The question should require students to articulate the contractualist formula and the individualist constraint that wrongness is determined by what can be justified to each affected individual; to explain why aggregation poses a distinctive challenge, since allowing it seems to violate that constraint while forbidding it seems implausible in cases of widespread blindness; and then to apply this framework precisely to the regulatory case I described. Crucially, the prompt should force them to address a nested aggregation problem: Policy B protects a group of three hundred, so rejecting aggregation for Policy A risks arbitrary acceptance of aggregation for Policy B. They should also evaluate whether Scanlon’s distinction between personal and impersonal reasons helps resolve this tension, and they must take a clear, defended position on whether statistically distributed harm—where no specific agent causes any specific instance—can ever be the object of reasonable rejection.

Then provide a model answer of approximately 650 words demonstrating the required analytical moves, together with a marking scheme totaling 30 marks. The scheme should identify at least five must-hit points—such as correct exposition of the formula, identification of the individualist constraint, precise analysis of the statistical-harm case, confrontation with the nested aggregation in Policy B, and a reasoned conclusion on whether the contractualist can consistently reject aggregation—assigning specific marks to each and noting the typical failure mode for each (for instance, treating the case as a utilitarian calculus, or invoking “reasonable rejection” without specifying whose standpoint counts). Include a brief distinction between an A-range answer, which applies the framework rigorously to both policies and recognizes the unresolved tension, and a B-range answer, which summarizes Scanlon accurately but treats the case as a conventional aggregation problem. Do not ask for bibliographies, specific citations, or page references; the assessment should depend solely on conceptual mastery, and the language should be formal and appropriate for a philosophy examination.
