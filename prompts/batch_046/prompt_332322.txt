I've been developing an argument in my dissertation work on historical institutionalism that seems to show path dependence, taken rigorously, makes normative institutional comparison conceptually impossible. I want to know exactly where it breaks, because I am not willing to abandon the idea that constitutional reform is available as a practical target.

(1) Define an institutional outcome as path-dependent just in case it traces to a contingent branching point B at which an alternative arrangement was genuinely available and was not excluded by functional necessity alone.

(2) Any institutional branching point B — for instance, a constitutional settlement allocating competences between central and subnational levels — is embedded in a prior sequence of interpretive decisions that determine what counts as a unit, a competence, and a legitimate allocation.

(3) Those prior interpretive decisions are themselves outcomes of earlier branching points.

(4) Therefore, specifying a counterfactual in which B resolves differently requires altering the interpretive framework that renders B intelligible as a choice among alternatives.

(5) That framework is itself path-dependent; altering it initiates a recursive revision that must extend backward through the sequence of prior interpretive choices.

(6) There is no non-arbitrary stopping point: selecting any prior stage as a fixed root still relies on categories that are products of still earlier choices.

(7) Consequently, any fully specified counterfactual world either reproduces the same interpretive sequence (yielding the same institutional outcome) or undermines the very categories needed to describe the purported alternative, rendering it conceptually incoherent.

(8) So if path dependence holds generally, then for any actual institution I there is no coherent counterfactual world in which I is absent and a genuinely different institution occupies its structural position.

(9) But normative evaluation of institutions — arguing that a given arrangement is suboptimal and ought to be replaced — presupposes that a different, coherent institutional arrangement is available as a normative target.

(10) Hence path dependence entails that normative institutional design and evaluation are impossible: we can narrate how we arrived here, but we cannot coherently maintain that we ought to be elsewhere.

(11) The conclusion is stronger than I can accept; I do not wish to deny that constitutional reform is conceptually possible, and I particularly want to preserve the claim that interpretive frameworks are recursively embedded (steps 2 and 5).

Which premise or inference should I reject? Is there an equivocation between metaphysical counterfactual specification and practical normative proposal somewhere in steps 7–9? Or does the argument commit a scope or modal slide in moving from the difficulty of specifying a fully altered world to the impossibility of any local institutional revision? What does the least costly rejection require — accepting that some institutional features are functionally determined rather than path-dependent, or allowing that interpretive recursion can be halted at practical, if not metaphysical, boundaries?
