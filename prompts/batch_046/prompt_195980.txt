I'd like to check whether the following commitments hang together, or whether I'm implicitly relying on incompatible motivations.

(1) I accept Bernard Williams's integrity objection in its strongest form: a moral theory that systematically requires an agent to abandon or subordinate her ground projects is not merely demanding but structurally alienating, and this counts as a deep defect in the theory itself.

(2) I think Williams's rescue-case argument shows that the husband ought to save his wife simply because she is his wife; introducing an impartial justification, even one that permits the choice, introduces a corruption into the practical reasoning.

(3) I also hold that in high-stakes cases—say, where saving one's spouse means letting ten strangers die—the correct reasoning is strictly impartial, and preferring one's spouse on the basis of the relationship would itself be the problematic extra thought.

(4) I take integrity to be a condition of personal flourishing and psychological coherence, not a source of moral obligations owed to others; its loss is a harm to the agent, but not necessarily a wrong done to anyone.

(5) I believe agent-relative reasons arising from ground projects have a special, non-aggregable status, but this status is not absolute: beyond a certain threshold of competing need, they are not outweighed but simply silenced, leaving only agent-neutral considerations operative.

Do these form a consistent package? Specifically: does (2) commit me to treating spousal preference as always the correct basis, which (3) then violates? Can (4) be squared with (1), given that (1) treats the violation of integrity as a theoretical defect rather than merely a private misfortune? And does (5)'s notion of silencing avoid the problem, or does it reproduce the alienation that (1) condemns, just localized to extreme cases? Please order the tensions by severity, indicate which commitment is the cheapest to revise, and explain what the revision costs—especially with respect to (2) and (5).
