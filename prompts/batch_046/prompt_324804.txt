I've been thinking about whether characters in video games matter morally, particularly in a case that seems different from the usual debates about graphic violence or player choice. Imagine a procedurally generated world in which every non-player character is assigned an internal emotional state and a hidden personal history that exists only in the code—never displayed to the player, never revealed through dialogue, and never experienced by any conscious being, including the character itself if it lacks consciousness. If a player harms one of these characters, does the fact that its assigned suffering or interests remain completely unobserved change whether the harm is wrong? Please argue for a specific position: either that these hidden, code-level mental states still generate genuine moral reasons not to harm the character, or that moral status requires some form of actual experience, recognition, or relational presence and therefore these invisible states carry no weight. In your answer, separate what is broadly settled in moral philosophy from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
