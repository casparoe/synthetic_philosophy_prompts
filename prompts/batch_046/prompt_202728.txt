I serve on the clinical ethics review board of a university neurology center. A research team has proposed that we replace first-person patient reports with an algorithmic “access-phenomenal divergence score” to guide analgesic and awareness-preserving interventions for patients with disorders of consciousness. They contend that phenomenal consciousness is largely an introspective illusion—subjects systematically confabulate rich qualitative experience from far sparser access-conscious content—so self-reports of pain or awareness are epistemically unreliable and should be overridden by this third-person metric in clinical decision-making. The board must issue a formal memo recommending whether to approve adoption of the protocol, reject it outright, or approve it only under specified conditions, with a clear time frame for implementation or review. The memo should treat the illusionist argument with conceptual seriousness rather than mere dismissal, explain how systematically discounting first-person authority affects informed consent and patient autonomy in this setting, and include an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or requests for further reading; the reasoning should stand entirely on its conceptual and ethical structure.
