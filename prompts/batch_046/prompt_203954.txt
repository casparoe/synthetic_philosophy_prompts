I have come to take seriously the extended mind thesis as Clark and Chalmers originally articulated it—specifically, the claim that when an external artifact is reliably available, automatically endorsed, and directly accessible in the way biological memory is, then the processes involving it are not merely causally coupled to cognition but partly constitutive of it. This question has become personally urgent for me because I now organize my research almost entirely through an external, algorithmically curated note system that I consult dozens of times each day; I find myself treating it less like a tool and more like a prosthetic memory, and I want to know whether I am genuinely extending my mind or merely using an unusually effective crutch.

What would have to be true for this thesis, taken in its robust constitutive form, to be wrong? I want a map of its exposure rather than a recitation of familiar complaints. Beyond the standard coupling-constitution objection and the worry that external processes lack the intrinsic “mark of the cognitive,” which empirical findings in neuroscience or cognitive science would genuinely threaten it? For example, if research on Markov blankets within active-inference frameworks showed that the brain’s statistical boundary remains invariant regardless of how tightly it is coupled to environmental structures, would that be decisive, or could the thesis absorb it by redefining the boundary conditions? I also want you to press on a more niche vulnerability: consider “ephemeral scaffolds”—external structures that are causally coupled and functionally equivalent to internal processes during a task, but that are not stably available over time, not automatically endorsed for future reasoning, and leave no retrievable trace. Must the thesis either include these within cognition (thereby seeming to over-extend) or exclude them by retreating to stability and accessibility conditions that risk making the claim empirically empty?
