I am designing a session for an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar—twelve graduate students, ninety minutes—on the tension between etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function. Rather than walking them through the familiar heart-versus-kidney repertoire, I want them to discover, through structured interaction, how quickly they switch between historical and systemic criteria when assigning functions and malfunction judgments. To prevent them from coasting on pre-trained intuitions, frame the exercise around a niche scenario: a xenobiological review board evaluating “chimeric symbiotes,” engineered organisms with deliberately ambiguous evolutionary histories, where no participant can appeal to standard textbook examples.

Design a runnable game with assigned roles—Field Assessors (who observe only current systemic contributions), Archive Investigators (who may request hidden evolutionary records), and a Review Board (who must issue binding verdicts on whether a trait is functioning properly). Provide rules, a timeline that fits within ninety minutes, and a payoff or scoring scheme that rewards consensus but penalizes the Board if they reverse a verdict after receiving historical data, or if they apply different standards across cases without noticing. Structure the case cards so that in the first round the Board renders judgments using causal-role reasoning alone; in a second round, selected-effect histories are revealed for some but not all cases; and in a final round the Board must reconcile contradictions. The debrief should map each round onto the theoretical distinction, identify what will predictably go wrong the first time the activity is run—such as students treating hidden history as automatically decisive rather than contested—and include an honest admission of which feature of the debate, for instance the normative weight of malfunction claims or the metaphysics of genuinely novel functions, the simulation cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion. Do not ask for bibliographies or citations; the point is conceptual force, not literature review.
