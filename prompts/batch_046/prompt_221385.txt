Okay so I've been chewing on this idea that Darwin and Turing are actually doing the same weird move, just in different directions. Darwin says you can get design without a designer who understands the plan; Turing says you can get understanding-looking behavior without anyone inside who gets it. So both invert the usual competence-needs-comprehension story — Darwin by stripping intention from the outcome, Turing by stripping it from the process. I'm leaning toward thinking this isn't just an analogy but a real structural feature: any explanation that starts with "first you understand, then you produce" is probably backwards, and comprehension is mostly a label we slap on after the fact. But I might be overfiting here. What would actually count against this view? Not just "Darwin and Turing are different fields" — I want the serious defeaters: cases where Darwin secretly needed intentionalist reasoning he just disavowed, or where Turing's imitation game actually presupposes comprehension on the evaluator's side, or formal results showing you can't separate competence from comprehension at all. Rank them fatal to awkward. And be straight with me: has this been qualified into unfalsifiability, where any counterexample just gets called another inversion? If so, is that actually a problem for this kind of claim, or is it fine? Also which parts would defenders of comprehension-first concede are right, and which would they fight to the death?
