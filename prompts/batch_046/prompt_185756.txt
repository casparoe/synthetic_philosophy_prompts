I'm taking a course on moral psycholgy and we got stuck extending Williams's integrity objection and that one thought too many idea past its usual spot. My roommate built this chain and now I can't see the exit. Here's the argument, numbered so it's easier to follow:

(1) Williams says that if I save my drowning spouse only after weighing welfare outcomes, I've added one thought too many — the correct reason should just be that she's my wife, with no general principle behind it.
(2) Let's call these direct, non-derivative reasons ground-level commitments. Their whole point is that they aren't justified by anything more basic.
(3) So if a commitment is genuinely ground-level, then in principle I shouldn't be able to override it just because some other consideration scores higher on a general scale; otherwise it's not really ground-level.
(4) But moral responsibility requires that I can step back and ask whether my commitment is justified. If I can't do that, I'm just acting on impulse, not as a moral agent.
(5) Once I step back and ask whether saving my spouse is justified, I'm treating that commitment as one item among others that can be weighed.
(6) So the very act of refelcting for responsibility turns a ground-level commitment into a weighable one.
(7) Therefore I face a forced choice: either I never reflect (so I preserve integrety but lose moral responsibility), or I reflect (so I keep responsibility but destroy integrity).
(8) But we clearly blame people for failing to save a spouse, which assumes both responsibility and integrity exist together, so (7) must be false.
(9) The chain looks valid, so one of (3)-(6) has to go. I'd really like to hold onto (3) and (4) if I can, since they feel central. Where does it actually break, and what's the price of fixing it?
