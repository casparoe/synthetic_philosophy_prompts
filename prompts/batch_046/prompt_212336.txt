I've just started teaching a first-year philosophy of education module, mostly because my department was short-staffed and I said yes without thinking it through. To stop the course being a complete disaster I've been reading Whitehead's essays — especially that 1929 one, The Aims of Education — and I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole. There's this long-running arguement between people who cite Whitehead's rhythm (romance, precision, generalisation) to defend student-led, almost playful discovery learning, and people who cite the same essays to defend rigorous disciplinary drill, classical literacy and strict timetabling. Both sides claim Whitehead, both have been saying the same things since at least the 1970s, and nobody shifts an inch.

Is this actually a disagreement about education, or is it verbal — are they just using 'education' to mean different things, one side meaning cognitive development and the other meaning cultural transmission? Or is there a hidden empirical question about how minds actually acquire what Whitehead calls inert ideas versus living knowledge, and neither side wants to admit they're guessing about psychology? Maybe it's worse: are they answering completely different questions, one normative (what should schools do) and one descriptive (how does learning happen), but both hiding behind Whitehead's ambiguous phrase about freedom and discipline?

It occured to me that the stall might be because neither camp wants to engage with Whitehead's less discussed claim that mathematics education is essentially the study of relations rather than facts — but I'm not sure if that's relevant or if I'm just projecting.

Anyway, I want a diagnosis, not a victory lap for either camp. Tell me what kind of stall this is — verbal, substantive, bedrock intuition, hidden empirical — using what each side actually says about Whitehead. And given your diagnosis, what would actually move it? A conceptual distinction, a result from cognitive science about the rhythm of attention, or is this one of those debates where progress isn't on the cards because the question is badly posed? I'm genuinely asking because I have to finalise a syllabus next week and I don't want to just reproduce the stalemate in my reading list.
