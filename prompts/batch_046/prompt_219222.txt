I have been trying to understand the antinatalist argument built on Benatar's asymmetry — that missing pleasure is not a harm to the never-born, but suffering is a harm to the living. I think I followed it correctly, but when I applied it to ordinary choices I ended up somewhere implausible. Here is my reasoning:

(1) For any potential person, if they would suffer, it is good that they never come to exist; if they would only enjoy life, there is no loss in their absence.
(2) Because of the non-identity problem, almost any choice I make — where I live, whom I meet, even the timing of ordinary events — determines which specific people, if any, will eventually exist.
(3) So every major life path avoids some possible suffering people (which is good) and misses some possible happy people (which is not bad).
(4) But if I actually take path A and it results in a real child who suffers, that suffering is a genuine harm to that child.
(5) Therefore I should avoid any path that risks producing actual suffering descendants, which seems to require avoiding relationships, moves, or careers that might eventually lead to procreation.
(6) Pushed further, this looks like an obligation to freeze my life in place to prevent accidentally causing anyone to exist and suffer.
(7) That cannot be right.

Which premise should I give up? I am reluctant to drop (1), because the asymmetry still strikes me as true, and (2) seems unavoidable once you accept that identity depends on these small differences. Is the error in (4), where I move from potential harm to actual harm? Or does rejecting (5) require me to deny that I am responsible for indirect effects across generations? I want to know what each rejection costs.
