In debates over whether enormous numbers of imperceptible harms—say, a billion people each receiving a dust speck in the eye—can ever morally outweigh a single severe harm such as torture, one analogy I repeatedly encounter treats aggregate suffering like a mosaic: each individual discomfort is an unremarkable tile, but arranged with others it might form a coherent picture. Proponents of this view use it to argue that torture is analogous to smashing a finished mosaic—destroying a structured, unified whole—whereas distributing dust specks is like scattering loose tiles across a warehouse: no pattern is disrupted, so no comparable value is lost. The conclusion drawn is that aggregation fails unless there is some "adhesive" or organizing principle that binds the tiny harms into a collective subject of experience, and since no such adhesive exists across separate persons, the dust-speck scenario never reaches the moral weight of torture.

Please audit this analogy systematically. First, lay out an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the tile, the grout or adhesive, the artist or designer, the viewer who values the whole, and the act of destruction? Second, identify where the mapping breaks—particularly with respect to whether moral value requires a single conscious subject (as a mosaic requires a viewer), whether harm is intrinsic to each tile or only emergent, and whether the analogy quietly imports an aesthetic or compositional theory of value that has no parallel in the phenomenology of suffering. Third, assess whether the conclusion that unstructured harms never aggregate survives these disanalogies, or whether the analogy was doing all the argumentative work. Finally, if the mosaic picture is deficient, propose a more apt analogy for the aggregation problem and explain why it handles the disanalogy better.
