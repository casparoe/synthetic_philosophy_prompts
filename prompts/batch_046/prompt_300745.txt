I am drafting a philosophical novel in which a neuroscientist protagonist delivers a tightly constructed monologue meant to show that the meta-problem of consciousness, pursued rigorously, collapses physicalism into an intolerable dilemma. She presents the reasoning to an interlocutor as follows.

(1) The meta-problem asks why physical descriptions of neural processes seem to leave phenomenal experience unexplained.

(2) One compelling resolution holds that human cognition includes a dedicated representational mechanism—call it phenomenal contrast—which registers the qualitative features of experience as categorically distinct from functional or structural properties.

(3) If this mechanism operates reliably in the first-person case, then by analogical extension it should operate reliably in the third-person case: observing a brain with sufficient structural similarity ought to trigger the same registration of qualitative properties.

(4) Yet our inference to other minds is notoriously fragile; we possess no direct access to others’ phenomenal states, and behavioral or neural equivalence does not entail phenomenal equivalence.

(5) Consequently, if we invoke phenomenal contrast merely as a representational artifact in order to dissolve the meta-problem—thereby denying that qualitative properties are genuinely inexplicable—we simultaneously strip away the analogical basis for attributing consciousness to anyone besides ourselves.

(6) Conversely, if we preserve other-minds knowledge by treating phenomenal contrast as accurate and analogically transferable, we concede that there exist qualitative properties which physical descriptions genuinely fail to capture—the very anti-physicalist conclusion that the meta-problem was designed to avoid.

(7) Thus any resolution that appeals to the reliability of phenomenal contrast forces an unacceptable choice: either consciousness is genuinely irreducible to physical terms, or we are driven toward a form of solipsism in which only one’s own consciousness is known.

The protagonist insists this is airtight. I need the interlocutor to identify the precise hidden weakness—perhaps an illicit symmetry between first-person and third-person deployment of the mechanism, or a subtle modal slide between representational accuracy and analogical entitlement—without making either horn of the dilemma seem trivial. Which step should be rejected, and what philosophical cost does that rejection exact? Does the argument survive in a weakened form, or does it dissolve entirely once that step is removed?
