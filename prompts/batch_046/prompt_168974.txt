Write a dialogue between two equally competent interlocutors—call them A and B—debating whether Schlick’s account of the meaning of life can withstand a structural critique. According to Schlick, meaning resides exclusively in non-instrumental, autotelic “play” rather than in purpose-driven work or achievement; an activity is meaningful precisely when pursued for its own sake, and a life accumulates meaning through such moments regardless of whether they form any overarching narrative or completed project. A should defend this core distinction and argue that pure play needs no diachronic unity: a sequence of unconnected, momentary autotelic experiences—say, improvised musical phrases with no composer, no audience, and no recollection—can constitute a maximally meaningful life precisely because nothing in it serves an external end. B should attack this with a “narrative-instrumentality” objection: sustained human agency necessarily embeds even apparently autotelic acts within larger teleological structures (skills cultivated for future play, social conditions that sustain it, memory and anticipation that link discrete moments), so that Schlick’s pure play is either impossible or, if achieved, fragments into an incoherent sequence lacking the temporal coherence that meaning requires. B should press A with a specific counterexample: compare that fragmented life to one organized around a single, never-completed project—constructing an infinite, ever-revised encyclopedia of human knowledge—where every particular step is instrumental to the whole, yet the overall form is pursued for its own sake. B should ask whether Schlick’s framework misclassifies this second life as meaningless work, and whether the absence of narrative unity in the first life is a virtue or a defect. A must respond by addressing whether Schlick can accommodate “higher-order” autotelicity—a project valued for its own sake despite being composed of instrumental parts—and by explaining whether narrative coherence is genuinely irrelevant to meaning or secretly smuggled back in through the back door of sustained agency. The exchange should be a real back-and-forth: each speaker should engage the other’s specific example, refine distinctions under pressure, and avoid straw-manning. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific editions or dates. Keep the discussion conceptual, focused on the coherence of the play/work distinction and on what conditions an activity or a life must satisfy to count as meaningful within Schlick’s framework.
