I am organizing a reading group on rational choice and have been trying to map the conceptual neighborhood around Buridan's ass. In our discussions we kept encountering a situation that resembles the classic paradox but behaves differently under closer analysis, and I am not sure whether it already carries an established label or belongs to a recognized family of problems.

Here is the pattern in concrete terms. Consider an agent faced with two options—say, two job offers with identical salary, location, and career trajectory—who genuinely lacks any first-order preference between them. The standard Buridan story treats this as a case of pure indifference, resolved either by paralysis or by an arbitrary tie-breaker such as coin-flipping. But in the cases that interest me, the agent also holds a second-order conviction: that it is rationally defective to settle the tie by a purely arbitrary mechanism, even when the stakes are low. Because of this conviction, the agent refuses to deploy a randomizer; yet abstaining or delaying is itself costly, and there seems to be no reason to wait one more hour rather than one more day, so the same arbitrariness reappears at a higher level. The paralysis therefore does not stem from first-order indifference alone, but from a normative commitment that choice must be backed by reasons, applied in a context where no such reasons are available. I have seen analogues in debates about voting between two candidates with identical platforms and in aesthetic judgments between two indistinguishable performances of the same score.

Does this configuration have a name of its own—perhaps as a recognized variant of Buridan's problem, or as a distinct failure mode within rational-choice theory? If there is no established term, can it be defined with enough precision to distinguish it both from standard first-order indifference and from incommensurability, where values are genuinely incomparable rather than perfectly symmetrical? I am especially interested in the boundary conditions: at what point does the refusal to use an arbitrary tie-breaker become irrational rather than principled, and does the presence of this second-order constraint alter the normative assessment of the agent's paralysis?
