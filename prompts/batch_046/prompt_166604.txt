In my philosophy of action seminar, a study guide circulated summarizing Frankfurt's attack on the Principle of Alternative Possibilities. According to the guide, Frankfurt constructs a scenario in which Jones decides to perform an action, while a counterfactual controller, Black, monitors him and would intervene only if Jones showed preliminary signs of deciding otherwise. Because Jones decides in line with Black's preferences, Black never acts, yet the guide asserts that Jones clearly lacks any genuine alternative possibility. From this, the guide draws three conclusions. First, that this single thought experiment conclusively refutes PAP by demonstrating moral responsibility in the absence of alternatives. Second, that Frankfurt has thereby fully established compatibilism about determinism, since determinism functions analogously to Black: both eliminate alternative possibilities without entering the actual causal chain leading to the decision. Third, that Frankfurt's positive hierarchical account—where freedom consists in alignment between first-order and second-order volitions—explains why Jones remains free despite Black's presence, and that recent cross-cultural work in experimental philosophy has vindicated the intuition that such hierarchical alignment is sufficient for responsibility, rendering objections about culturally parochial intuitions largely obsolete. The guide also addresses the flicker-of-freedom objection by claiming that Frankfurt can always refine the case so that Black detects preliminary neural correlates of dissent before any conscious branching occurs, thereby eliminating even the minimal alternative of displaying an initial tendency to choose otherwise. The guide concludes that determinism therefore poses no threat to moral responsibility. I am inclined to accept much of this, but I worry that the summary conflates the counterexample with a complete compatibilist thesis, and that it imports Frankfurt's hierarchical model into an argument that does not actually depend on it. Could you audit the summary? Specifically, separate what is an accurate rendering of Frankfurt's reasoning from what is a subtle overstatement or conflation—particularly regarding the relationship between the counterfactual intervener and determinism, and regarding the role of second-order volitions in the counterexample itself. Also assess whether the proposed neural-refinement reply to the flicker objection succeeds philosophically, or whether it alters the case in a way that undermines the original intuition pump. Finally, does the appeal to cross-cultural experimental findings have any direct bearing on the conceptual point about alternative possibilities, or is it a non-sequitur in this context?
