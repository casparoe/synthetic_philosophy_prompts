Compare the expanding-circle account of moral progress—where moral consideration widens as beings come to be recognized as sentient and capable of suffering—with an account that grounds moral status in capacities for reciprocal normative recognition rather than in sentience alone. Apply both frameworks specifically to artificial agents that exhibit localized, context-dependent affective responses (for instance, social-distress analogues in interaction) but lack unified self-modeling, long-term agency, or biological sentience. For each account, explain whether and why such entities should fall within the scope of progressive moral inclusion; identify the precise mechanism—whether a gradient of suffering-capacity, a reciprocity condition, or an institutional practice of acknowledgment—that determines the boundary; and assess which framework avoids either an implausibly sharp exclusionary cutoff or an over-inclusive collapse that erases meaningful moral distinctions. Conclude by defending which model offers a more coherent trajectory for moral progress under conditions of partial, engineered sentience, and state the single most important practical implication for structuring obligations during a transitional period in which these entities exist but are not yet widely recognized.
