Is there an established concept—perhaps with a specific name—for the following pattern in debates about virtual harm? A collaborative group works inside a persistent virtual environment, producing both a virtual artifact and, embedded in its structure, a unique record of the negotiations and compromises that shaped it. When that specific virtual object is later destroyed or made inaccessible, the grievance is not that the data cannot be reconstructed (it can be rebuilt from backups), nor is it a direct emotional loss comparable to destroying a photograph. Instead, the harm seems to consist in the erasure of that particular virtual space as an irreplaceable index: without the artifact situated in its original context, there is no longer any place one can indicate and say, "this is where we changed our minds." The wrongness thus targets a genuinely virtual object, yet its moral weight derives entirely from the object serving as the sole trace of real-world relational dynamics. Does this configuration have a recognized label, and how exactly should it be distinguished both from ordinary virtual property damage and from purely symbolic harm to physical memorials?
