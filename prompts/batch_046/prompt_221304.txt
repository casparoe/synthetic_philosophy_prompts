I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a large teaching hospital. Our informatics and palliative-care divisions have proposed an electronic-health-record change: for any inpatient whose prognosis score crosses a specific institutional threshold, the system will automatically generate a palliative-care consultation order unless the attending physician actively opts out within forty-eight hours. The opt-out requires only a single click, but the default framing states that “consultation is standard of care for this risk profile,” and the order set is embedded in the routine admission workflow rather than presented as a discrete clinical decision point. Half the committee regards this as a legitimate welfare-promoting nudge that fully respects physician autonomy, since the veto is technically costless; the other half argues that it exploits status-quo bias and authority heuristics among clinicians, substituting an architectural judgment for reflective clinical reasoning in a way that undermines professional autonomy and, indirectly, patient-centered shared decision-making. I need a short written opinion circulated before Thursday’s meeting that recommends a concrete course—approve, approve with conditions, or reject—together with an explicit implementation or sunset timeline, and that takes seriously the strongest objection from the side you do not endorse. In particular, please engage with whether this case involves a distinct form of epistemic paternalism directed at clinicians rather than at patients, and whether the fact that the mechanism is transparent to the physician (who sees the default) is sufficient to satisfy autonomy concerns even when the patient may never learn that the consultation was triggered by an automatic order rather than by an individualized clinical judgment. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the opinion should stand on its reasoning alone and be written as a formal document the committee could vote on.
