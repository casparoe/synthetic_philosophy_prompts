Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar that now strikes me as suspicious because it seems to refute both leading accounts of de re modality at once. (1) Counterpart theory holds that modal claims about an object are made true by properties of its counterparts in other worlds, not by that very object existing elsewhere. (2) For this to avoid circularity, the counterpart relation must be definable without prior knowledge of which modal claims about the object are true. (3) On the standard Lewisian account, similarity is weighted by importance, but importance is determined by which properties are essential to the object—yet that is precisely the modal knowledge we are trying to explain. (4) Hence counterpart theory is circular and must be abandoned. (5) Transworld identity avoids circularity: if I am literally identical across worlds, my essential properties are just those I possess in every world where I exist, with no similarity metric required. (6) But if I am literally present in a world where I am a prime number and in another where I am not, then I both am and am not a prime number, violating the law of non-contradiction. (7) So transworld identity also collapses, and there is no coherent account of how I could have been otherwise. I would prefer to keep (5) and reject the move to (6), but I fear that doing so forces me either to deny that properties are world-relative or to abandon intuitive essential properties such as being human. Which step is the real error, and what does repairing it cost each theory?
