My friend and I have just finished a long, circular argument about Erwin Schrödinger’s Vedanta-inspired view of consciousness, and rather than continue talking past each other, I should like an impartial adjudication.

We were discussing his claim — developed in his philosophical essays — that individual consciousness is not ultimately separate, but rather a single universal awareness (Atman, or Brahman) appearing through multiple limiting conditions. She took the position that this resolves the quantum measurement problem in a genuinely explanatory way: since there is only one observer, there is no mystery about why the wave function appears to collapse for different people; the multiplicity of experimental reports is merely the same consciousness differentiated by what Vedanta calls upadhis (limiting adjuncts). She added that entanglement is not a correlation between separate systems but the formal expression of this underlying non-partitioned awareness, and that quantum superposition describes the potential contents of consciousness before it focuses into particular experience.

I countered that this confuses metaphysical unity with epistemic multiplicity. Even within Vedanta, the jiva (individual soul) experiences separation due to maya, so Schrödinger cannot simply dissolve the measurement problem by declaring one observer without explaining why that one observer seems to encounter contradictory outcomes in distinct branches. I also argued that treating entanglement as a metaphor for consciousness non-separability inverts the explanatory order: quantum mechanics predicts inter-laboratory correlations that are observed by physically distinct experimenters, and if consciousness is truly unitary, we need an account of why those distinct perspectives report mutually consistent but separate results. I further suggested that Schrödinger’s view risks collapsing into either epistemic solipsism — if the first-person pronoun refers indexically to the universal subject — or ontological monism that undermines intersubjectivity, and that neither option provides a physical mechanism for the differentiation he appeals to.

She replied that the indexical reading is precisely the point: “I” functions like “here” or “now”, pointing to the same universal referent from different embodied positions. The empirical ego (ahamkara) is not an illusion but a conventional mode of appearance; ethical obligations therefore become universal compassion rather than egoism, since harming another is harming the Self experienced through a different upadhi. She concluded that Schrödinger is not abandoning physics but showing that its formalism describes the objectification of a single awareness, not a world independent of it.

Could you go through our exchange round by round and say who is right about which specific claim? In particular, please address: whether Schrödinger’s Vedanta genuinely resolves the measurement problem or merely relocates it into phenomenology; whether the analogy between quantum entanglement and Vedantic non-duality holds up or collapses into metaphor; and whether the indexical interpretation of “I” saves his view from solipsism or leaves it unable to explain intersubjective consistency in scientific observation. Also, please say what, if anything, both of us are missing — for instance, whether there is a distinction between epistemic fragmentation and ontological fragmentation that we have failed to draw, and whether Schrödinger’s position adds something to the philosophy of quantum mechanics that purely physical interpretations overlook.
