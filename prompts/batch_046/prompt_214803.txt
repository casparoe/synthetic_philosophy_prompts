Exam question: "Explain the Frege–Geach problem as it applies to moral expressivism. Your answer should (i) state what expressivism claims about the semantic function of moral sentences; (ii) explain Frege's original observation about embedded propositional-attitude contexts and how Geach extended it to moral discourse, using a concrete example involving a conditional; (iii) articulate precisely why this is a problem for compositionality rather than merely for psychology; and (iv) evaluate the proposal that an expressivist can respond by treating a moral conditional as expressing a single, complex practical commitment—sometimes called a compound planning state—in which the antecedent does not contribute an independent attitude. Does this solve the problem or relocate it? Finally, (v) address whether the same difficulty arises for negation, and explain why or why not."

Student answer: "Expressivism holds that moral sentences like 'Torture is wrong' do not express beliefs with representational content but rather non-cognitive attitudes—typically disapproval or rejection. This distinguishes them from descriptive sentences.

The Frege–Geach problem originates in Frege's 'Über Sinn und Bedeutung,' where he first raised the difficulty specifically for moral sentences, observing that in embedded contexts the usual expressive force is suspended. Geach later generalized this point to all propositional-attitude contexts in his 1965 essay 'Assertion.' He noted that in a conditional like 'If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong,' the phrase 'lying is wrong' in the antecedent does not seem to express the speaker's actual disapproval of lying, since the speaker might be entirely unsure whether lying is wrong. Yet if expressivism is true, that phrase should always express disapproval. So its meaning appears to shift.

This threatens compositionality: the meaning of the whole sentence should depend on the meanings of its parts, but the part in the antecedent lacks its standard expressive function. The problem is not psychological—about what people feel—but semantic: we need an account of how the parts combine.

One response is to treat the conditional as expressing a single compound planning state. On this view, the speaker does not first disapprove of lying and then form a belief about it; instead, the whole sentence expresses a unified practical commitment structured as a belief that one should reject the combination of accepting the antecedent and rejecting the consequent. The antecedent contributes structurally, not as an independent attitude.

This solves the problem because there is no embedded attitude whose absence needs explaining. However, it relocates the difficulty: we now need an account of how these compound states are individuated and how they relate to the simpler attitudes expressed in unembedded contexts.

As for negation, the problem is less severe. 'Torture is not wrong' expresses approval or neutrality directly, without embedding anything inside a larger construction. So negation does not create the same compositionality worry; it just flips the attitude expressed by the sentence as a whole."
