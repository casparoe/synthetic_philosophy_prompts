I'm working through an argument a classmate sketched in our philosophy of climate change seminar, and I keep feeling there's a hidden slide between steps but I can't name it. Here is the chain as he laid it out.

(1) If a choice wrongs some future person S, then there must be a coherent counterfactual comparison in which S exists without the choice and is better off. (2) Any policy with large-scale social effects—say, a rapid decarbonization mandate versus a business-as-usual trajectory—alters the conditions of meeting, mating, and reproducing across generations, so the specific individuals who come to exist depend on which trajectory is taken. (3) Therefore, for any distant-future S who lives under trajectory T, there is no nearby possible world in which T is not adopted and S still exists. (4) Without such a comparison world, we cannot say S is made worse off by T; non-existence is not a harm, and S has no "better-off" self in the counterfactual scenario. (5) The same reasoning applies symmetrically: the people who live under the alternative trajectory T' are equally dependent on T', so they too lack counterfactual comparison worlds. (6) Hence neither trajectory can be said to wrong its future inhabitants; there is no intergenerational harm comparison available. (7) But if no trajectory wrongs anyone, then the only moral reasons to prefer decarbonization are present-directed—avoiding current storms, preserving current ecosystems—which are weak relative to the economic costs. (8) So the nonidentity problem dissolves climate ethics: we have no independent moral reason to care about the far-future quality of life, only about present effects.

My classmate says this proves Parfit's problem is not just an abstract puzzle but a practical dissolution of longtermist reasoning. I want to resist step (6) or (7) without simply denying (4). Which step contains the equivocation, modal slide, or hidden quantifier shift? And if the argument is actually valid, which premise is least costly to reject, and what does rejecting it mean for how we think about "identity-dependent harm" and whether harm requires a stable diachronic subject across counterfactual scenarios?
