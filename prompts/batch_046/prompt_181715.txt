Construct a branching taxonomy for an advanced moral-philosophy seminar that classifies positions on whether an individual has a duty to refrain from participating in a collectively harmful practice when her personal contribution is causally redundant. The taxonomy must be built from logical distinctions rather than assembled from a pre-existing list of named theories, and it should be presentable as a decision tree whose nodes are questions answerable without knowing the position labels in advance.

Begin with the root question: does causal inefficacy — the fact that one more vote, one more purchase, or one more emission makes no discernible difference to the aggregate outcome — defeat all moral reasons against participation?

From that root, branch on the appropriate unit of moral assessment for collective harm: is evaluation directed at discrete individual acts, at rules or maxims of conduct, or at the overall shape of an agent’s life-plan?

Next, branch on whether non-causal, expressive, or symbolic reasons — such as avoiding complicity, signaling refusal, or maintaining personal integrity — can survive the causal redundancy of the act itself.

Finally, branch on whether the sheer magnitude of the aggregate harm generates an independent, scale-sensitive obligation that binds redundant contributors regardless of their individual efficacy.

Each leaf should carry either a conventional label (for example, rule-consequentialist abstentionism, life-plan integrity abstention, threshold deontology, or collective-consequentialist participation) or the explicit mark unnamed, followed by exactly one sentence describing that position’s most awkward practical commitment — particularly how it adjudicates the case of voting in a large democratic election and the case of purchasing meat within an industrial supply chain.

In addition, map every combination of answers that yields an unoccupied leaf: state the combination concisely, and diagnose whether the gap arises from logical incoherence, practical unattractiveness, or mere neglect in the literature. Where two leaves are not genuinely separable by the preceding four questions, flag them explicitly rather than inventing a distinction.

The deliverable should sort respondents by what they affirm about cases and distinctions, not by which label they prefer. Do not include bibliographic references, reading lists, or scholarly citations; the task is conceptual mapping, not attribution.
