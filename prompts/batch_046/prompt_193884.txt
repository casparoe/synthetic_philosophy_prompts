As a software engineer without formal training in philosophy of mind, I have been working through recent writing on illusionism—the view that phenomenal consciousness, the subjective quality that philosophers describe as what it is like to undergo an experience, does not actually exist, but is instead produced by representational mechanisms that misrepresent themselves. I understand the broad architecture: our brains build models of the world and of ourselves, and in one of these models there is an erroneous representation that we possess irreducible subjective qualities. But as I read defenses of this position, I keep encountering a specific explanatory pattern that I do not have vocabulary for, and I am unsure whether it is a recognized conceptual tension, an unremarkable feature of functional explanation, or a problem that has already been named and analyzed.

Here is the pattern in concrete terms. An illusionist will argue that once we have explained all the relevant cognitive functions—discrimination, information integration, report generation, and the construction of a self-model—there remains no further hard problem, because there was never any actual phenomenal property to account for in the first place. The illusion is supposed to be fully explained by describing how these functions interact. Yet when I examine the actual descriptions of these mechanisms, they seem to rely on language that implicitly restores the very thing that was denied. For instance, the brain is said to build a simplified user interface that presents information to the system in a unified, perspectival format; or introspection is described as a monitoring process that delivers representations carrying a particular seeming quality, which then gets misclassified as direct awareness of phenomenal properties. In each case, the explanation treats the system as having a point of view or a minimal form of awareness—something for which the interface is presented, something that registers the seeming—while the official doctrine insists that there is no point of view and no awareness of that kind at all. I have seen this structure again in arguments about why the illusion is so persistent: the illusionist explains its persistence by noting that the self-model cannot easily correct itself because it lacks access to its own construction process. But describing the self-model as lacking access seems to presuppose a subject to whom access is denied.

Is there an established concept for this particular configuration—where a theory denies the existence of phenomenal properties, yet its own explanatory machinery must be described using functional terms that implicitly presuppose a minimal subject, perspective, or awareness? I want to distinguish it precisely from broader notions like self-refutation, the explanatory gap, or the homunculus fallacy, since those appear to capture different failures. Specifically, I am asking whether philosophers have named the case in which the explanation of an illusion about consciousness requires attributing to the cognitive system precisely the kind of perspectival unity that the illusion was supposed to falsely represent. If there is such a concept, how is it delimited from ordinary functionalist explanations that do not involve this tension? And if there is not, what would be the precise boundary conditions that separate this pattern from a benign use of metaphor in cognitive science?
