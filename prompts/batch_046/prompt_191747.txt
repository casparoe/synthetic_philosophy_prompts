Recently I have found myself in a professional situation that grants me what amounts to practical invisibility: there is a specific action I could take that would yield a clear personal advantage, and genuinely no audit mechanism, witness, or record could ever expose me. The realization that I could do this without risk of detection or blame has made Glaucon's challenge in the second book of Plato's Republic feel less like an antique philosophical exercise and more like a direct interrogation of my own motives. In Benjamin Jowett's well-known Victorian English rendering of the dialogue, Glaucon recounts the myth of Gyges, a shepherd who discovers a ring that renders its wearer invisible. He uses it to enter the palace, seduce the queen, murder the king, and seize power. Glaucon then asks us to imagine two such rings, one placed on the finger of a perfectly just person and one on the finger of a perfectly unjust person, and he asserts that neither would behave differently: both would take whatever they desired, knowing they could not be caught. He pairs this with the mirror case: the just man is falsely accused, tortured, and executed despite his innocence, while the unjust man, aided by the ring's concealment, enjoys universal honor and reward. From this pairing he concludes that justice is valued only for its external rewards and for the reputation it brings, never for anything intrinsic to the just life itself.

I am trying to understand whether this pairing is genuinely symmetrical, or whether the two scenarios actually test different features of justice and therefore do not jointly support his conclusion. I want to parse the logical architecture: what is held constant across both cases, what is varied, and whether the inference to the worthlessness of justice for its own sake actually follows. There is also a conceptual difficulty I cannot resolve without help. Glaucon seems to presuppose that if an agent's choice is entirely unobservable—not merely hidden from others but, in a stronger sense, detached from any social recognition—then the category of being just loses its grip on the agent. This sounds close to what contemporary philosophers of action discuss under the heading of transparency conditions for moral motivation, but I am unsure whether importing that framework is legitimate or anachronistic when reading an ancient text. I also wonder whether Glaucon embeds an implicit theory of moral knowledge: does he think justice requires some form of public acknowledgment in order to exist, or is he merely making a psychological claim about human weakness? If the latter, then an internalist reply seems available—namely that a genuinely just person possesses a constitutive commitment to justice that does not depend on observation—without having to deny that most people are indeed motivated by reward and punishment.

Could you help me understand these points? I am particularly interested in whether the ring's power should be read as an epistemic condition, removing all observers, or as a metaphysical condition, removing all consequences, since the two readings seem to expose different vulnerabilities in the argument. I also want to know whether the second half of the comparison—the tortured just man—introduces an asymmetry that undermines the first: if false appearance can destroy the value of justice for its bearer, then invisibility might not be the strongest test after all, and Glaucon may have smuggled in an assumption about the necessity of social recognition that his own framework does not justify.
