I've been working through an argument about whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and whether a culture can collectively choose between them, that seems to prove far too much — specifically, that moral progress through emotional reform is self-defeating. Here are the steps:

(1) Call a moral emotion "institutionally sovereign" if a society can deliberately cultivate or suppress it solely by redesigning its explicit norms, sanctions, and educational practices.
(2) Guilt is institutionally sovereign: its intentional object is always a specific, articulable norm-violation for which the agent holds herself responsible, so a culture can manufacture or diminish guilt by rewriting rules and attaching penalties.
(3) Shame is not institutionally sovereign: its object is a global, pre-reflective assessment of the self as deficient in the eyes of an internalized audience, and this assessment retains a "normative latency" — an irreducible residue that outruns any explicit rule the culture could codify.
(4) Therefore, a culture can choose to become a "guilt-culture," but it can at best suppress or channel shame; it cannot genuinely choose to become a "shame-culture."
(5) If an emotion is institutionally sovereign, then its moral authority is derivative: it is only as authoritative as the institutional design that produces it.
(6) Conversely, if an emotion is not sovereign, its authority is non-derivative and therefore more fundamental.
(7) So shame, being non-sovereign, carries deeper moral authority than guilt.
(8) But then any culture that deliberately replaces shame with guilt — a familiar narrative of modernization — is not progressing morally but regressing, substituting a manufactured proxy for a fundamental check.
(9) More generally, the more a culture succeeds in mastering its emotional life through institutional design, the less morally authoritative that emotional life becomes.
(10) That conclusion seems absurd: we ordinarily think moral progress includes gaining reflective control over destructive emotional patterns, not that such control undermines morality itself. So the argument must fail somewhere.

I am inclined to preserve (3) and (7): shame really does seem to have this opaque, global structure, and that opacity does seem to give it a kind of authority that engineered guilt lacks. But I do not want to accept (9). Which premise or inference should I reject? Does the error lie in the move from sovereignty to derivativeness in (5), in the inverse-relation claim in (6), or in a hidden scope shift between (4) and (8)? And if I reject (5) to save cultural agency, what do I owe — do I have to concede that manufactured guilt can be just as authoritative as inherited shame?
