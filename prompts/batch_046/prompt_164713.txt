I am a senior software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. Next month I will deliver the fifteen-minute opening remarks at my company’s internal Engineering Mentorship Workshop, an event that gathers senior staff mentors and junior engineers together in one room. The audience is split: about half are educators and senior staff who have read Locke’s Some Thoughts Concerning Education and know its standard interpretation well; the other half are engineers and managers with no philosophical training whatsoever. I want the address to treat Locke’s view as a structural constraint on what I can say, not as decorative historical reference. Specifically, I want the speech built around his claim that education is not the transmission of information into an empty mind but the gradual formation of mental habits, and that rational autonomy emerges only after disciplined custom has shaped the association of ideas—never before. Because Locke treats reason as the late product of habituated practice rather than its source, the speech should not promise that our onboarding curriculum can immediately produce independent critical thinkers. Instead, it should argue that the program can legitimately claim only to establish preconditions: embedding repetitive code-review and debugging habits before expecting any open-ended architectural reasoning. For the philosophers present, reference Locke’s distinction between training the body and habits and the subsequent education of the rational faculty, along with his view that early impressions form lasting mental associations; for the engineers, translate this into the concrete difference between structured review drills and unstructured design debates, without diluting the philosophical point. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind. Write the full speech, and end with a line beginning "Summary:" that states in one sentence the core limitation Locke’s model imposes on what a technical onboarding program can legitimately promise.
