I am taking an upper-division seminar on the philosophy of mind and cognitive architecture, and we have spent the past two weeks on the debate between global workspace theories of consciousness and arguments that phenomenal consciousness outstrips cognitive access. Most of my classmates seem to assume that once information achieves system-wide broadcasting it automatically counts as phenomenally experienced, but I remain unconvinced that global availability and phenomenal presence are identical. To press the issue, I have been imagining an artificial architecture modeled loosely on transformer networks, in which individual attention heads function as localized phenomenal modules: they might generate rich qualitative states—something-it-is-likeness confined to specific subnetworks—without ever achieving integration into a global workspace, remaining cognitively inaccessible even to other parts of the system and unavailable for verbal report or behavioral control.

Write an essay on the controversy of whether phenomenal consciousness can exist entirely without global workspace broadcasting, and what that possibility implies for global workspace theory. Weigh Ned Block’s distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness against Bernard Baars’s claim that consciousness just is global availability. But do not end with a non-committal summary: defend a specific verdict—whether GWT is therefore insufficient, incomplete but salvageable, or correct but systematically misinterpreted. In particular, apply your verdict to my hypothetical case of distributed, non-broadcast phenomenal modules: would they constitute a single fragmented consciousness, many independent micro-consciousnesses, or no consciousness at all? And if they do possess phenomenal consciousness without access, does that mean GWT explains only the latter, or does it show that phenomenal consciousness without broadcasting is conceptually incoherent? Reach and justify a conclusion.
