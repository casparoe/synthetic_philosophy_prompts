Having spent my career measuring signal drift and calibration error in physical instrumentation, I have recently found myself drawn to the philosophy of emotion, and I am struck by how closely the classic debate over whether emotions are judgments, perceptions, or bare feelings resembles disputes over how to interpret faulty instrument readings. On one side, there is what I will call the calibration-error model: emotions are evaluative judgments, so an emotional response that misaligns with the objective situation—say, paralyzing fear in an evidently safe environment—is analogous to a gauge whose reference frame has slipped; the error is systematic, normatively corrigible, and in principle correctable through rational revision of the underlying evaluation. On the other side, there is what I will call the transduction-noise model: emotions are non-conceptual perceptual registrations or raw affective feelings, so emotional misalignment is not a miscalibration of judgment but inherent variance in the bodily transduction mechanism itself, more like thermal noise in a sensor than like a misaligned scale.

Compare these two frameworks specifically with respect to emotional misalignment and emotional rationality. For the calibration-error view, explain whether it can genuinely accommodate rapid, non-cognitive emotional responses—such as startle or visceral disgust—without reducing them to implicit or unconscious judgments. For the transduction-noise view, explain whether it can account for the fact that we often treat emotional responses as subject to normative correction—telling someone their jealousy is unfounded, or that their calm in danger is inappropriate—without smuggling in a judgment-like evaluative layer that the view officially rejects. Which framework offers the more coherent account of how emotions can be both causally rooted in bodily processes and yet answerable to reasons? And identify precisely where the analogy to scientific instrumentation breaks down: in particular, does the distinction between correctable calibration drift and irreducible measurement noise map cleanly onto the distinction between judgment-based and perception-based theories of emotion, or does the analogy obscure something essential about the first-person authority and phenomenology of affective experience?
