As a policy analyst recently tasked with revising our institution’s framework for allocating scarce medical resources across regions with wildly different baseline health outcomes, I have become personally invested in whether prioritarianism offers a defensible distributive principle or whether the leveling-down objection undermines it in a way that is not merely verbal. To avoid polemics, I want the analysis structured as an adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint document that should result from a sustained, good-faith exchange between the following two camps.

Camp One: Defenders of continuous-weight prioritarianism. They hold that the moral importance of benefiting a person is given by a strictly decreasing continuous function of that person’s well-being level, with no sharp non-arbitrary threshold, and that this weighting exhausts what is correct about giving priority to the worse off.

Camp Two: Threshold prioritarian critics. They agree that benefiting the worse off matters more, but they maintain that continuous weighting makes prioritarianism structurally vulnerable to the leveling-down objection; they defend a view on which full distributive priority applies only below a contextually defined sufficiency threshold, and above which equality has at most instrumental value.

The document should contain these sections in order:

1. Agreed claims: A numbered list of propositions both camps can sign without reservation—covering the formal structure of prioritarian aggregation (that outcomes are ranked by a weighted sum of individual well-being), the exact logical form of the leveling-down objection (that the principle appears to treat a reduction in well-being for the better off as an improvement even when no one is helped), and the conceptual distinction between intrinsic and instrumental reasons for equality.

2. Precise disagreements: A table with two columns. Each row should state a single divergence in wording that Camp One accepts as its own view and Camp Two accepts as its own view. The rows must cover: (a) whether a non-arbitrary sufficiency threshold can be defined without collapsing into relational egalitarianism; (b) whether comparative fairness is a primitive moral value or merely a by-product of continuous weighting; and (c) whether pure leveling-down—destroying surplus well-being without any compensating gain—is ever, even in a minor respect, made better by the correct principle.

3. Cruxes and predictive tests: Identify three sub-questions such that resolving one in either direction would move at least one camp. For each crux, provide: (i) a concrete, realistic scenario both camps agree is relevant (use a regional health-budget case in which high-cost elective treatments for the relatively well-off are restricted, but administrative leakage prevents any savings from reaching the worse-off, so the well-off are unambiguously worse off and the poor are no better off); and (ii) each camp’s explicit prediction about what its own principle implies for that scenario, stated in advance so that the case could in principle adjudicate between them.

4. Residual verbal disputes: State clearly whether any portion of their disagreement dissolves once terms are fixed—specifically, whether labeling continuous-weight prioritarianism “egalitarian in spirit” is a substantive claim or a rhetorical classification, and identify exactly which formulations become uncontroversial once that label is suspended.

Write in a formal, polished register. Separate what is settled ground in distributive ethics from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present any contested interpretation—such as the claim that prioritarianism is merely egalitarianism in formal dress, or that the leveling-down objection applies exclusively to relational egalitarianism—as if it were the consensus view.
