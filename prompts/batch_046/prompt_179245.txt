I've been extending Frankfurt-style reasoning in a direction that now looks like a reductio, and I want to know where the slip is. (1) In Frankfurt's original case, Jones is responsible for killing Smith despite Black's presence, because Black never intervenes and Jones acts for his own reasons. (2) The relevant factor is not the bare absence of alternatives, but whether that absence plays any causal or explanatory role in the action. (3) So if a blocker is present but unactualized, and the agent's own reasons are sufficient, responsibility survives the loss of first-order alternatives. (4) Now imagine a nested case: Black stands ready to stop Jones from refraining, and Gray stands ready to stop Black from intervening; Jones acts on his own reasons, and neither blocker activates. Intuitively Jones is still responsible, yet he lacks not only the ability to do otherwise but also the ability to alter the conditions preventing him—he lacks second-order alternatives. (5) If responsibility survives here, then unactualized blockers at any level are irrelevant. (6) By induction, determinism is structurally identical to an infinitely nested Frankfurt case: prior states and laws block any deviation, and block any attempt to alter those blocking conditions, though none ever actualize since the agent never tries. (7) So determinism is not merely compatible with responsibility; it is just a universalized Frankfurt situation and poses no special problem. (8) Apply the same logic to belief: every belief I hold is sustained by cognitive mechanisms that would block rejection, and those mechanisms are sustained by deeper ones; I never actually try to reject, say, a prejudiced implicit bias, so the blockers never activate. (9) Therefore I lack alternatives for all my beliefs—carefully examined convictions and deterministically formed biases alike—yet remain fully responsible for them all, since the absence of alternatives is rendered irrelevant. (10) But this collapses the distinction between epistemically praiseworthy beliefs and blameworthy ones, which is absurd. I would most like to preserve (1) through (4), but I need a definite diagnosis. Which step should be rejected, why does it look plausible, and what does giving it up cost—especially for the determinism debate and for epistemic responsibility? Give me a committed answer: take a clear position, defend the specific step that fails, and do not simply list options for me to choose between.
