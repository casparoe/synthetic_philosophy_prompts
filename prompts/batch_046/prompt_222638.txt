I'm trying to work out my own stance on whether numbers should count in rescue cases, partly because I'm taking a course in moral philosophy where we discussed Taurek's challenge to aggregation, but I find my intuitions don't line up neatly with either pure equality or straightforward maximization. Here are my concrete verdicts, and I want to know if there's a coherent principle behind them, or if some have to be abandoned.

If a bridge is collapsing and I can save five strangers or one stranger, I save the five. If the choice is five strangers versus my sibling, I still lean toward saving the five, though it feels far closer; if it were my child instead of my sibling, I think I'd save my child. If I can save five people who each have only one remaining year of healthy life, versus one person with fifty healthy years ahead, I save the one. Now consider a twist: the five are not independent minds but five bodies sustaining a single distributed consciousness that could eventually merge back into one body; saving all five preserves that distributed mind across five bodies, while saving the one preserves an entirely separate, ordinary individual. Here I want to save the single ordinary individual, even though "five lives" are technically at stake. In another scenario, I can save five people only by using a rescue method that slightly diminishes each survivor's cognitive capacity, or save one person using a method that leaves her fully intact; I save the one. By contrast, in harm-prevention cases: if I can stop five people from suffering mild headaches or stop one person from suffering a severe migraine, I stop the migraine; but if I can stop five severe migraines or one severe migraine, I stop the five.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps involving thresholds of harm severity, the moral significance of personal identity or psychological continuity, or the distinction between preserving versus creating value—that captures this pattern, or something close? What does it imply for the case I keep oscillating on: I can save five people who are perfect psychological duplicates (complete copies of one original mind, each with identical memories and personality) or save the original person from whom they were copied. Sometimes I feel I should save the original; other times saving five copies seems no different from saving five people who happen to share a favorite color. Please respond in American English, state the principle exactly, indicate which of my verdicts it accommodates and which it forces me to drop, and give your prediction for the duplicate case without appealing to external citations or reading lists.
