Draft the joint statement that a defender of Dennett-style derived intentionality—treating intentionality as an interpretive stance anchored in predictive success and illustrated by minimal agents such as the two-bitser—and a defender of Searle-style original intentionality—holding that aboutness is an intrinsic, biological feature of minds and cannot be reduced to syntax or observer interpretation—should produce after an adversarial collaboration focused on the following hypothetical case.

Consider a distributed network of binary-state nodes embedded in a building’s climate infrastructure; call it a two-bitser lattice. Each node switches between two local states based solely on temperature differentials, with no central processor, no evolutionary history, and no biological substrate. Collectively, the lattice stabilizes ambient temperature near a fixed set point. Human maintenance staff routinely interpret the lattice’s collective state-transitions as “wanting to maintain 22°C,” and this interpretation yields robust predictive and explanatory control over the system’s behavior. The lattice possesses no consciousness, no internal representations in the standard cognitive-science sense, and was not designed by an agent who endowed it with explicit representational content—though an engineered variant with explicit design goals can be imagined for comparison.

Your document should be structured as follows.

1. Agreed Claims (numbered): state the factual and conceptual points both parties can sign. These should cover the lattice’s architecture (binary nodes, local rules, emergent stabilization), the predictive success and practical utility of the staff’s interpretive practice, the logical distinction between original aboutness and derived interpretability, the irrelevance of consciousness to the lattice’s operation, and the formal properties shared by the emergent and engineered variants.

2. Disagreements (table format): for each residual dispute, state the issue in wording that each side accepts as an accurate formulation of its own view. The table must include at least: (a) whether the lattice possesses any genuine intentionality, derived or otherwise; (b) whether observer-dependent predictive success is sufficient for derived intentionality when no designer’s intentions are present; (c) whether the complete absence of biological substrate is, in principle, a decisive obstacle to original intentionality; and (d) whether functional organization alone, regardless of substrate, can ever generate intrinsic aboutness.

3. Cruxes: identify the precise sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would cause at least one party to revise its position. At minimum, address whether predictive utility distinguishes real intentionality from useful heuristic projection, and whether there exists a principled boundary between systems that merely behave “as if” they have goals and systems that genuinely have them.

4. Tests and Predictions: for each crux, specify a concrete thought experiment or comparative case that both sides agree is relevant—such as contrasting the randomly aggregated lattice with an engineered lattice explicitly programmed to represent temperature targets, or comparing the lattice to a biological homeostatic system with analogous functional organization. For each case, record in advance what each camp predicts the outcome should imply about the presence or absence of intentionality, and state what result would count, for each side, as genuine evidence against its own view.

5. Verbal vs. Substantive Assessment: examine whether any portion of the dispute arises from ambiguous formulations rather than genuine disagreement. If so, identify the ambiguous terms (e.g., “real,” “mere,” “interpretation”), propose neutral reformulations that dissolve the appearance of conflict, and confirm that the remaining disagreement is substantive.

Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for a collaborative forum in philosophy of mind or philosophy of science. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific page numbers or unpublished manuscripts.
