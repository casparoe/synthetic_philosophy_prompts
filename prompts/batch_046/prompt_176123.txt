I'm part of a forecasting collective of about fifteen technically literate people who try to maintain calibrated beliefs about long-run technological risks, and we've been deadlocked over whether our disagreements about AI timelines come mainly from hidden private evidence or from genuinely incompatible prior beliefs. One member, drawing on formal epistemology, cited the agreement result associated with the common prior assumption to argue that persistent divergence among rational agents should be treated as a symptom of either unshared information or irrationality, not as legitimate prior disagreement. Our situation, however, seems to break the standard conditions: we work with heterogeneous causal models rather than a shared state space, full transparency of reasoning is sometimes impossible because revealing particular intermediate conclusions could alter the very outcomes we are forecasting, and several of us think framing the question as a single point-estimate probability for transformative AI by 2040 is conceptually unstable.

I'm writing an internal brief for a workshop on deliberation norms, and I want the most important implications of relaxing the common prior assumption—not abstractly, but for how small, expert groups should design belief-aggregation practices. What normative force, if any, survives in agreement-style reasoning once common priors are abandoned? And which practical structures—such as decomposing forecasts into independently resolvable sub-claims, using prediction markets with heterogeneous weights, or adopting explicit protocols for tracking the source of disagreement—are genuinely supported by the formal analysis versus being merely psychologically comforting? I do not want references or a reading list; I want a structured analysis of how prior heterogeneity should reshape our obligations in rational disagreement.
