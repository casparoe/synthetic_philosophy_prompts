Please write the opening remarks for a one-day interdisciplinary workshop entitled The Bureaucratic Uncanny: Remembrance after Bauman, co-hosted by a university centre for genocide studies and a regional memorial archive. The audience is deliberately mixed: senior historians who lived through or participated in the West German Historikerstreit of the 1980s; postgraduate students in philosophy and sociology for whom Zygmunt Bauman’s Modernity and the Holocaust is a canonical but often second-hand reference; and staff from the partner archive, who are not academic specialists but who have recently been curating routine administrative documents from the 1940s and are acutely sensitive to whether displaying such paperwork as ordinary bureaucracy illuminates or insults the dead.

Your remarks must be in British English and should run to roughly seven hundred words. The central philosophical tension—between treating the Holocaust as historically unique and therefore incomparable, and treating it as an expression of modern bureaucratic rationality that reveals structurally comparable dangers in other institutions—must do real structural work in the speech, not serve as decoration. This means you should not offer a false synthesis or a comfortable middle ground. You must navigate the risk that insisting on uniqueness can let modernity and its administrative instruments off the hook, while insisting on comparability can flatten the specific horror into a generic sociological case study. Because members of the archive staff have lost family members through precisely the kind of bureaucratic processes Bauman analyses—railway timetables, filing protocols, moral distance achieved through division of labour—you must address the ethics of speaking about bureaucratic evil in a room where bureaucracy was not an abstraction but an instrument of loss. Do not aestheticise administration; do not reduce the event to an illustration of Weberian rationalisation. At the same time, you need to speak precisely enough that the historians present will recognise accurate references to Bauman’s argument about the gardening-state metaphor, the role of moral distance in bureaucratic action, and the way modernity produces ambivalence rather than resolving it, without alienating the archive staff or the students. Do not provide a bibliography, suggested reading list, or citations. The speech should feel like an occasion-specific framing—opening a conversation rather than closing it—and should leave the audience with a clear sense of why the day’s papers must hold uniqueness and comparability in genuine tension rather than collapsing them.
