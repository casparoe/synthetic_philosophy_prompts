Popper's critique of historicism denies that there are discoverable laws of historical development and warns that belief in them licenses authoritarian planning. Yet modern governance routinely relies on long-range predictive models—climate scenarios, epidemiological projections, macroeconomic forecasts—to justify preemptive institutional action. Suppose you are directing a three-year research group of five philosophers and political theorists with no empirical modeling capacity. Which open questions about the boundary between legitimate forecasting and Popper-style historicism deserve priority? In particular, address whether scenario-based projections that lack universal laws still fall within the critique; whether democratic legitimacy requires citizens to reject predictive narratives used to justify policy; and whether institutional planning can be justified without teleological assumptions about historical direction. Rank the questions you select, defending the order with reference to tractability for conceptual work, dependency relations among them, and practical stakes for constitutional design. What finding over the next two years would cause you to reorder them?
