I had a long argument last night with a friend who works in finance about whether George Berkeley's philosophy of money offers any genuine insight into modern banking crises, or whether it is merely an antique curiosity. I am writing this after our discussion, trying to clarify my own thoughts for a seminar I am auditing on early modern economic philosophy. The episode I keep returning to is the March 2023 collapse of Silicon Valley Bank and the Federal Reserve's immediate creation of the Bank Term Funding Program, which allowed distressed institutions to borrow against long-duration Treasury securities at their face value rather than their depressed market price. From what I understand of Berkeley's writings on money and credit — particularly his view that currency functions essentially as a token whose legitimacy depends on a reliable correspondence to actual labor or commodities, and his suspicion of standing paper instruments that circulate as if they possessed intrinsic value while being detached from tangible production — this intervention looks like a stark case of authorities treating notional value as real value in order to prevent a systemic run. But I am unsure whether that mapping holds up. Could you analyze the SVB collapse and the Fed's response specifically through Berkeley's conceptual framework? I want you to be disciplined about the mapping: what in Berkeley's account corresponds to the bank's liabilities, to the devalued bonds used as collateral, and to the Fed's decision to honor face value; what features of the episode Berkeley's token analogy illuminates, such as the panic dynamics when depositors doubt the correspondence between account balances and recoverable assets, and what it misdescribes or leaves silent, such as the role of digital speed in the run or the sovereign guarantee that underwrites the collateral. Finally, I want an honest assessment running in the other direction: does the episode confirm Berkeley's anxiety about credit detached from real wealth, or does the Fed's successful stabilization show that his analogy fails precisely where modern central banking operates — by making the token itself a sovereign-backed instrument whose value is maintained by institutional authority rather than commodity backing?
