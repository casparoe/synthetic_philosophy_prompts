As a journalist covering philosophy of mind and value theory, I have been trying to settle my own view on well-being and have arrived at a cluster of commitments that feel defensible in isolation, but I am unsure they form a coherent package. I would like an audit: order the tensions by severity, say which pairs are only apparently in conflict, identify the cheapest repair for each real tension and what it costs in terms of the original motivation, and tell me whether I am helping myself to incompatible premises.

Here is roughly where I have landed. (1) I reject the experience machine not because simulation carries some intrinsic metaphysical defect, but because well-being requires that one's achievements be causally embedded in an actual social and physical network—what matters is the functional role those achievements play in recognition, consequence, and interaction, not a bare property of being genuinely real. (2) I think hedonism is best reconstructed diachronically: the unit of evaluation is not a summed momentary balance but the narrative arc of a life, and a good life must exhibit a particular shape—rising complexity followed by meaningful integration—rather than merely a favorable pleasure-to-pain ratio. (3) I hold that first-person authority about one's own well-being is systematically unreliable; adaptive preferences, narrative self-deception, and false consciousness mean that an outside assessor can sometimes be better positioned than the subject to judge whether a life is going well. (4) At the same time, if someone spends decades inside a perfectly designed experience machine, sincerely believing they have built a career, raised children, and contributed to a community, and if their internal narrative displays the same coherence and shape as that of an actual achiever, then I think we should attribute comparable well-being to them—because the functional and narrative properties that ground well-being are present, even though the causal history is simulated. (5) I also maintain that well-being theories should avoid depending on distinctions—such as real versus simulated achievement, or actual versus merely believed success—that make no detectable difference to the subject's psychology, to predictive accuracy, or to social coordination.

My suspicion is that (1) and (4) pull in opposite directions, and that (2) may implicitly require the truth of the narrative in a way that (5) forbids. Meanwhile, (3) seems to conflict with the deference to internal narrative in (4), though I wonder whether that tension dissolves once we separate first-person epistemic authority from third-person functional assessment. Is this set jointly tenable? Be specific about which commitment must give way, or whether each can be preserved through a minor reinterpretation, and be frank about the cost of every repair—what I would have to abandon in the motivation behind the original view.
