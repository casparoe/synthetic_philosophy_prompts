I am revising the syllabus for my graduate seminar on ontology and technology, and I want to move the students past the familiar debates about whether virtual swords or currencies are "real" in some derivative sense. For that reason, I need a dialogue that stages a genuinely original disagreement—one that requires reasoning from first principles rather than recycling textbook positions—while remaining anchored in concepts that any well-read philosophy student should recognize.

Please write a dialogue between Dr. Alina Petrov, who defends what she terms practice-embedded realism about virtual entities, and Dr. James Okonkwo, who holds an agency-centered deflationist view. They should be discussing a concrete, somewhat unusual case: over eight years, a dispersed collective built an unrecoverable collaborative memorial space inside a persistent virtual environment; when the platform's proprietary server architecture collapsed without backup, the entire structure was irretrievably lost. The users insist that they have suffered a harm comparable to the destruction of a physical monument, and the philosophers are debating whether this intuition can be sustained philosophically.

Petrov should argue that these virtual structures had achieved what she calls distributed ontological standing—meaning they functioned as stable, intersubjective nodes within a shared normative practice that could not be reduced to individual mental representations or to bare executable code—so their destruction damages a real entity outside any single mind. Okonkwo should concede the severity of the users' loss but deny that it requires real virtual objects; he should contend that what was destroyed was not an entity but a functional ecology of recognition embedded in the users' extended cognitive and social practices, and that treating lost data structures as destroyed monuments commits a category error by conflating substrate with commemorative practice. The exchange must contain real back-and-forth across at least four turns: Okonkwo should specifically challenge whether distributed ontological standing can be maintained without smuggling in physical-world analogies, and Petrov should reply by addressing whether an agency-centered account can explain the users' shared intuition that something independent of themselves was violated, rather than merely explaining private psychological distress. Both speakers should be equally rigorous; neither should be a strawman.

Please present the dialogue immediately, with no introductory framing, no stage directions, and no commentary outside the spoken exchange. Begin directly with the first line of dialogue.
