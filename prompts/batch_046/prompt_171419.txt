I'm drafting the formal opinion for circulation before Thursday's meeting of the University Hospital Clinical Ethics and Policy Committee regarding Case 2024-NEU-17. The patient, age 22, meets all clinical criteria for death by neurological criteria under our hospital's whole-brain protocol, which requires irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. The family—who adhere to a philosophical position they call "biocentric integrationism"—objects to withdrawal of ventilation not on religious grounds but on the claim that our protocol illegitimately collapses the death of the person (loss of consciousness and cortical integration) into the death of the organism (cessation of brainstem-mediated homeostasis). They point to a supplementary cerebral angiogram, not part of standard clinical testing, that detects minimal residual perfusion in the lower brainstem, and argue this proves the organism remains an integrated biological unit. They request indefinite continuation of ventilation and oppose any organ or tissue procurement. At the same time, the transplant service notes that our state statute permits "reasonable accommodation" of family objections to brain-death declarations but does not define its duration or scope, and that a time-sensitive neural-tissue research protocol—requiring withdrawal and procurement within six hours of declaration—will be foreclosed if accommodation extends beyond Friday. Our current institutional policy is silent on whether philosophical objections to the whole-brain standard trigger the accommodation clause, and whether minimal non-standard perfusion findings invalidate an otherwise valid declaration.

Please draft the committee's written opinion. Recommend a concrete course of action with a specific time frame (for example, a conditional continuation until a specified date, immediate withdrawal with conditions, or referral to an external mediator). The reasoning should engage the family's distinction between personal death and organismic integration as a serious conceptual argument rather than dismissing it, and it should honestly weigh the strongest consideration favoring the family's position. It should also address the institutional risk to the research protocol without treating that risk as automatically overriding ethical obligations. Write the document in American English, in a formal institutional register suitable for circulation to clinical staff, the family, and legal counsel. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific court cases or statutes by citation. The opinion must reach a clear, actionable bottom line.
