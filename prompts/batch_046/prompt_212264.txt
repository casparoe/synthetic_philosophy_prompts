“Transactional corruption involves an explicit exchange of material benefits for a decision that violates formal institutional rules; it is identifiable across cultures because it presupposes a clear boundary between public office and private gain. Relational corruption, by contrast, occurs when social obligations—kinship, friendship, or reciprocal gift-giving—distort the exercise of a role in ways that are not reducible to a quid pro quo. In this framework, clientelism is not merely bribery by another name but a system of distributed patronage that substitutes for absent welfare structures. Because the obligations involved are constitutive of the social fabric rather than external to it, classifying clientelism as corruption imposes a liberal, atomistic conception of the individual onto societies where personhood is relationally constituted.

However, this does not mean that universal standards are necessarily imperialist. The Western standard is therefore universalizable not because all societies must adopt Western kinship norms, but because every society distinguishes, at least implicitly, between obligations appropriate to a role and those that are not. Thus, while the content of corruption is culturally mediated, the form—role distortion—is not.”
