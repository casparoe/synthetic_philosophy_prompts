After a long argument with a friend about whether restitution of colonial artifacts should be understood as a purely physical transfer or as extending to what might be called the object’s epistemic infrastructure, I want to ask you to adjudicate a disagreement I reconstructed from a recent panel on museum ethics. The exchange was between Dr. Amina Bello, a philosopher of heritage, and Dr. Julian Reeves, a legal historian of museums; though they focused on the Benin Bronzes, their claims bear on restitution debates more generally, including those surrounding the Parthenon Marbles.

Dr. Bello opened by arguing that returning bronze plaques to Nigerian institutions without the accompanying colonial archives—the inventory photographs, conservation reports, curatorial classification cards, and proprietary metadata now attached to museum-held digital twins—reproduces extraction in an epistemic register. She maintained that these documents are not neutral scholarship but administrative artifacts of the looting itself, embedding Western taxonomies that stripped the objects of ritual and genealogical meaning. Without them, she said, receiving communities must either accept silence or reconstruct interpretation within the very framework imposed by extraction.

Dr. Reeves responded that this conflates stolen property with institutional labor. Cataloging and conservation performed over the past century represent new intellectual effort, not part of the original theft. He warned that demanding these archives as part of restitution generates an open-ended obligation: if a bronze requires its documentation, then restitution becomes entangled with the entire scholarly record. He further argued that framing the archive as necessary for proper understanding risks implying that Nigerian institutions lack epistemic agency to interpret their own heritage without European documentation—a subtle but genuine paternalism.

Bello countered that Reeves mischaracterizes the demand. She clarified that she does not seek every subsequent article, but specifically the primary documentation generated through direct colonial custody—the material-alteration reports, inventory cards, and database infrastructures that now govern high-fidelity replicas. These, she said, are direct extensions of the extraction process rather than independent scholarship. She added that the receiving institution should be free to reject the archive if it wishes; withholding it by default, however, is the paternalistic act, because it lets European custodians decide unilaterally what knowledge is relevant.

Reeves replied that even primary documentation is embedded in complex legal and technical systems—database licenses, conservation protocols, and liability frameworks—that cannot be cleanly separated from ongoing museum operations. More fundamentally, he pressed a conceptual distinction: knowledge is non-rivalrous. A museum can share copies of its archives without being deprived of them, which means the moral urgency that applies to physical objects—whose return involves actual loss to the holder—does not apply with equal force to information. He concluded that physical restitution should be clean and unconditional, while knowledge sharing ought to be handled through separate collaborative agreements rather than bundled into restitution as a moral requirement.

My friend and I could not settle how to weigh these considerations. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about whether colonial documentation counts as part of the looted goods; who is right about whether demanding it risks epistemic paternalism; and who is right about whether the non-rivalrous nature of knowledge undermines the moral urgency of including it in restitution. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both parties are missing—particularly regarding the status of digital twins, proprietary metadata, and the practical separation of physical and epistemic return within current international legal frameworks.
