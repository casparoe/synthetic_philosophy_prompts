As a novelist drafting a philosophical fable, I am examining whether the utility monster objection can be reconstructed not as a problem of welfare aggregation but as a problem of narrative focalization. In my manuscript, a single protagonist possesses such overwhelming interior richness that devoting extensive point-of-view chapters to him generates far greater readerly value than distributing that narrative attention across a larger cast, yet this risks reducing the other characters to mere instruments of his story. Please explain the conceptual tension between aggregative narrative value and the moral equality of fictional persons in this framework, and reach a reasoned judgment on whether the novelist's duty to treat characters as ends in themselves—rather than as vehicles for aggregate aesthetic payoff—provides an independent reason to resist this "monster" structure, or whether the analogy ultimately collapses because narrative attention is not commensurable with utility.
