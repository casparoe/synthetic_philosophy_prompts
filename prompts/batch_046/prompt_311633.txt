I'm grading exams for an upper-level seminar on moral theory (about fifteen students). The exam asked students to apply Scanlon's contractualism to a modified aggregation case that introduces a proposed "threshold clause": if the risk to each affected individual exceeds 1%, those affected may be weighed collectively for purposes of reasonable rejectability. Below is the exam question and one student's answer. Please assign a specific letter grade (A through F) and justify it with point-by-point comments; be committed—take a definite view on where the reasoning holds and where it quietly collapses into aggregation, rather than outlining grading options or saying it depends on the rubric. Also flag whether the answer contains any correct but subtle point that a hasty grader would likely undervalue.

Exam question: Scanlon's contractualism avoids the utilitarian aggregation problem because moral justification is owed pairwise to each individual, not to groups. Consider this case: policy (A) eliminates a 0.01 risk of death for each of 10,000 people, while policy (B) eliminates a certain death for one identifiable person, at equal cost. A contractualist seems required to choose (B). Now suppose a "threshold clause" is added: if the risk to each person exceeds a minimal salience level—say, 1%—then the affected parties may be treated collectively in assessing reasonable rejectability. Does this clause genuinely save contractualism from the objection that it cannot handle large-scale risks? And what internal tension does it create with Scanlon's insistence that reasons are pairwise rather than aggregative?

Student answer: Scanlon's contractualism, as developed in What We Owe to Each Other, holds that an act is wrong if it is permitted by a principle that no affected person could reasonably reject. In the modified case, option A distributes a 0.01 risk reduction across 10,000 people, so no single individual has a claim strong enough to reject the principle that permits B. Option B imposes a certain, severe harm on one person, who can therefore reject any principle that allows B for the sake of diffuse benefits. So contractualism correctly selects B.

The proposed 1% threshold clause solves the large-scale problem. Once each person's risk reaches 1%, every affected individual possesses a personally sufficient reason to reject being sacrificed for diffuse gains. Thus, if 100 people each face 1% risk, none can be dismissed in isolation, and their combined presence can legitimately outweigh the one certain victim. This is not true aggregation, because we never weigh a small claim against a large one; we merely recognize that many people each have an independently adequate claim. The clause therefore preserves contractualism's determinacy without importing utilitarian summation.

The internal tension lies in Scanlon's denial that moral reasons multiply with headcount. He treats scaling by group size as a category mistake—something like treating individuals as mere locations for welfare—yet the threshold clause permits collective weighing precisely when headcount is high enough, which seems to reintroduce the scaling he rejects. Still, since each claim above the threshold is already fully adequate, the view remains consistent with the core idea that morality is about justifiability to each individual.
