As a novelist currently developing a manuscript on traumatic memory and narrative structure, I have been studying Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five as a formal model. I should like a philosophical reading of the novel through the debate between presentism — the view that only present entities and events genuinely exist — and eternalism, which treats past, present, and future as equally real. The Tralfamadorians, who perceive all temporal moments as fixed and co-existent, are frequently interpreted as fictional embodiments of eternalist observers. I want to know whether this interpretation is accurate, or whether Vonnegut's portrayal instead approximates a growing-block theory, and whether Billy Pilgrim's dislocation in time can be read as a presentist phenomenology in which only the immediate moment of suffering possesses full ontological weight.

Please examine how this philosophical framework illuminates specific narrative episodes — Billy's abduction, the Dresden firestorm, and the death of Valencia — and, equally importantly, where the novel resists being reduced to either metaphysical position. I am especially interested in a more niche conceptual distinction: the difference between narrative collapse (the formal disintegration of chronological order in the text) and ontological collapse (the presentist thesis that non-present times lack being altogether). Does the novel's fragmented structure imply that human temporal experience is structurally eternalist, or does it defend a presentist account of trauma in which the past and future are unreal except as present mental contents? I should like the analysis to remain faithful in both directions, neither reducing Vonnegut's fiction to an illustration of metaphysics nor distorting presentism or eternalism to accommodate the plot. Finally, please say what this particular work contributes to the philosophy of time that abstract argument alone cannot capture. Please provide your answer in British English.
