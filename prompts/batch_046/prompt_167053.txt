After a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a friend last night about how to act when you are genuinely unsure which moral framework is correct, I have settled on a position that feels more defensible than the standard maximize-expected-choice-worthiness approach, but I am uncertain how exposed it actually is. The view runs as follows: rather than weighting actions by the credence-weighted moral value that each competing theory assigns, we should apply a structural-regret criterion that is inversely weighted by each theory's normative density—where normative density is defined as the ratio of distinct normative relations (duties, permissions, axiological rankings, and exception conditions) that the theory generates to the descriptive facts it takes as basic input. The motivating thought is that high-density theories, such as complex pluralist deontologies with layered exception clauses, produce so many precise normative outputs that they are more vulnerable to false precision; consequently, in conditions of deep uncertainty, they should receive lower decision weights not because they are epistemically less probable, but because being wrong about them produces structurally more chaotic and irrecoverable regret. Once these density-adjusted weights are assigned, we select the action that minimizes the worst-case structural misalignment across the competing frameworks, rather than averaging their recommendations.

What I want is an honest map of this position's vulnerability. What observations, formal results, specific moral cases, or conceptual arguments would count against it—ranking the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward? Please distinguish what defenders of the view should themselves concede as genuinely damaging from what they can legitimately dismiss as missing the point. Does the framework risk being qualified into unfalsifiability—for instance, by allowing defenders to redescribe any apparently correct high-density prediction as an outlier that does not affect the decision rule—or has it retained enough predictive bite to be genuinely at risk? And if it has been insulated, is that insulation a virtue for a decision procedure under uncertainty, or a vice that renders the view vacuous? I would particularly value an assessment of whether the concept of normative density can be made precise enough to do real work without collapsing into a disguised proxy for prior plausibility, and whether there are rival approaches to moral uncertainty that achieve the same practical guidance without invoking this particular metric.
