I am defending a thesis at an interdisciplinary workshop on empire and political economy. The claim is that Adam Smith's opposition to British colonial administration — especially his critiques of the East India Company's chartered monopoly and of trade restrictions on North America — is best understood not as a conventional mercantilist efficiency argument but as a structural objection to distant corporate governance. On my reading, Smith implicitly treats colonial boards and chartered companies as agents exercising discretionary power over local producers and settlers who possess superior knowledge of ground-level conditions; this separation of decision-making from local knowledge makes colonial rule incompatible with the kind of self-directing commercial order Smith favors, irrespective of whether the colony generates profit. The audience includes economic historians who have worked closely with the 1776 text, postcolonial scholars who treat Enlightenment political economy as ideological cover for extraction, and an institutional theorist who knows modern principal-agent literature and is wary of anachronistic framing. I need a speaker-prep sheet for the Q&A session: list the objections in the order they are likely to arise, ranked by how damaging each would be, and phrase each as the relevant constituency would actually put it; for every objection, indicate whether it exposes a real flaw, what I should concede without surrendering the core thesis, and how to reply in roughly two minutes; pinpoint the weakest part of my own argument that I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope remains unnoticed; and identify the one question — probably from the institutional theorist — that I am hoping nobody asks, explaining exactly why it threatens the framing.
