I am a novelist who teaches an occasional seminar on moral philosophy, and I am grading papers for a unit on gradated sentience. One exam asks students to evaluate a hypothetical "thermostatic boundary condition"—the proposal that direct moral consideration applies only when an entity's responses to harmful stimuli are not fully reducible to homeostatic regulation, on the grounds that homeostasis is goal-directed yet phenomenally empty. The student must apply this to a smart thermostat, a nematode withdrawing from heat, and a honeybee navigating a maze to escape an aversive odor; say whether the boundary aligns more closely with Singer's gradated sentience view or with a purely functionalist account of consciousness; and offer one strength and one weakness specific to the boundary.

Here is Student B's answer. The thermostat, the student writes, is clearly below the boundary: its temperature regulation is pure negative feedback without phenomenal experience. The nematode's withdrawal reflex, though mediated by neurons, remains a homeostatic defense mechanism lacking integrated phenomenal states, so it too falls below. The honeybee, however, crosses the boundary, because its maze navigation demonstrates learned, flexible behavior that exceeds reflexive homeostasis, yielding intermediate moral status. The student argues that this approach avoids the anthropocentrism of brain-size criteria by relying solely on functional irreducibility rather than neural architecture, and that it essentially extends Singer's sliding scale by substituting homeostatic analysis for subjective report. For a weakness, the student notes that insects might suffer in simpler, non-cognitive ways, yet the condition correctly excludes them whenever their behavior is purely homeostatic.

Please assign a grade (letter or percentage range) and provide point-by-point comments justifying it. Assess whether the reasoning holds for each of the three cases, whether the comparison to Singer is accurate, whether the strength and weakness genuinely engage the boundary condition, and whether any subtle conflation or unsupported step—particularly around functionalism, phenomenal character, and the status of insects—would escape a hurried reader. Do not signal your own view on whether the boundary condition is sound; evaluate the answer purely on its internal coherence and fidelity to the terms set out in the question.
