The debate over Molinism and middle knowledge — the view that God knows, prior to any creative decree, true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom that specify what every libertarian-free agent would do in every fully specified circumstance — has been at an impasse for decades. Molinists hold that this middle knowledge allows God to providentially actualize one world among infinitely many, arranging circumstances so that free agents produce the desired mosaic of good and evil without God causally determining any choice. Anti-Molinists deny that there are such robust true counterfactuals, or they grant them but insist that if God knew them and arranged the world accordingly, the agents in question would no longer exercise genuinely libertarian freedom. Both sides have sharpened their formal apparatus — possible-worlds semantics for counterfactuals, models of libertarian agency, and distinctions between causal and non-causal control — yet the literature often reads like two parallel monologues: each refutation is anticipated and absorbed without anyone changing position.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not primarily about divine omniscience, nor even about freedom in the ordinary sense, but about a hidden metaphysical divergence masked by shared vocabulary. Molinists tend to treat an agent’s counterfactual behavior as fixed across worlds by her essential properties or by a complete individual concept, so that there is a definite fact of the matter about what she “would” do in world W. Anti-Molinists, by contrast, often treat libertarian freedom as “world-fluctuating”: the very same agent with the very same prior causal history and internal state could choose differently in two worlds that are identical up to the moment of choice. If that is right, then the sides are not answering the same question — one asks whether God can know a pre-fixed modal profile, the other asks whether there is such a profile to be known — and much of the apparent disagreement about “control” is actually a disagreement about transworld identity and modal robustness. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures the real fracture, or whether the deeper problem lies in competing semantics for counterfactuals (Stalnaker-style versus supervaluationist treatments of “would”-statements) or in unacknowledged differences about what counts as a “moral reason” for divine action.

Or is there a hidden formal question — about the logic of counterfactuals under libertarian constraints — that neither side has settled?
