After a recent personal encounter with the difficulty of attributing moral responsibility to actions that feel automatic rather than coerced—particularly in contexts of compulsive habit—I have found the popular summary of Benjamin Libet’s findings repeatedly invoked to settle the matter. In these discussions, the line ‘the brain decides before you do’ is treated as a single, decisive result, used to argue everything from the impossibility of libertarian free will to the conclusion that legal punishment is always unjust because no agent ‘really’ chooses their conduct. I want to understand what that sentence can actually be asserting, disambiguated with enough precision to see where empirical neuroscience ends and philosophical interpretation begins. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses it might stand for—distinguishing, for instance, (i) the claim that neural precursors precede conscious awareness of a specific movement; (ii) the claim that those precursors constitute a fully formed decision rather than a preparatory bias; (iii) the claim that conscious awareness plays no causal role whatsoever, including in veto or modulation; (iv) the claim that the absence of conscious initiation entails the absence of any form of rational control relevant to culpability; and (v) the claim that moral responsibility requires a specific metaphysics of agent-causation that neuroscience has now undermined? For each reading, indicate whether it is a near-tautology given the experimental design, a substantive empirical claim supported by readiness-potential studies, an overinterpretation of the data, or a philosophical non sequitur that the experiments do not address. Then tell me which of these readings is actually being invoked when someone uses the phrase to dismiss arguments about culpability in cases where behavior feels automatic but not obviously coerced, and whether that particular reading survives once we distinguish between initiation and veto, between generic motor preparation and specific intentional content, and between the absence of conscious initiation and the absence of any capacity for reflective endorsement or inhibition.
