At a graduate reading group on political economy, Maya and Eli got into a sustained argument over whether Oskar Lange’s market-socialist reply actually defeats Ludwig von Mises’s calculation critique, or whether Friedrich Hayek’s later knowledge-problem objection survives it untouched. Their exchange went as follows.

Maya opened by saying that Lange’s trial-and-error method shows a Central Planning Board can rationally allocate resources without private ownership of capital goods. The Board sets initial prices administratively, then observes surpluses and shortages in producer-goods markets and adjusts prices iteratively, just as competitive markets do. Because consumer goods and labor markets remain genuinely market-based, prices in those sectors emerge from real exchange, and only intermediate goods require simulated adjustment. So, she concluded, Mises was wrong: socialist managers do have rational prices to guide them, and the unit-of-account problem is solved.

Eli replied that this misreads Mises. Mises never denied that bureaucrats could manipulate numbers; he denied that those numbers could carry the economic meaning of prices formed through actual entrepreneurial exchange with private capital. Without real ownership, there is no risk-bearing, no profit-and-loss accounting tied to property, and therefore no rational computation of alternative uses of capital goods. The Board’s adjustments are bureaucratic guesswork dressed in arithmetic, not economic calculation.

Maya countered that this concedes too much. If the Board’s adjustments converge on the same price vector that a competitive market would have produced, then by definition the allocation is rational, regardless of whether private capitalists feel the risk. The economic function is preserved even if the institutional form changes. She added that Lange’s model explicitly includes consumer sovereignty and labor choice, so the motivational and informational inputs that generate market prices are still present.

Eli then shifted to Hayek. Even granting perfect computational capacity, he said, the Board cannot possess the tacit, local, and constantly changing knowledge embedded in particular circumstances of time and place. Prices in a market are not just data for computation; they are discovery mechanisms that communicate dispersed information without anyone needing to articulate it. A trial-and-error board requires local managers to report conditions upward, but the very act of aggregation strips away the contextual specificity that makes the information economically meaningful. So Hayek’s critique is epistemological, not arithmetical.

Maya responded that Eli smuggles in a false dichotomy. If local managers report shortages and surpluses, they are communicating precisely the economically relevant information—relative scarcity—without transmitting full contextual narratives. The price signal in a market does the same compression: it tells agents what they need to know and hides the rest. Therefore, Lange’s mechanism replicates, rather than eliminates, the communicative function of prices. She added that if Eli insists every bit of local knowledge must remain unaggregated, then no large-scale economic coordination—including within multinational firms—is possible, which seems implausible.

Eli’s final move was to say that Maya has now shown why Lange’s system is not central planning at all but a disguised market system with public ownership. Once you decentralize reporting, allow iterative price adjustment driven by local conditions, and preserve consumer markets, you have abandoned the core socialist claim of conscious, unified direction of production. At best, Lange describes a hybrid; at worst, he assumes the Board can solve a system of simultaneous equations that is computationally intractable even for modern algorithms, which neither addresses Mises’s incentive problem nor Hayek’s epistemic problem.

Then say who is more right overall about whether Lange answers Mises and whether he answers Hayek.
