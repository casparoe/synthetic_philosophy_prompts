I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun serious study of philosophy, and I am preparing a single ninety-minute workshop for a seminar of about fourteen participants—mostly philosophy graduate students, with one or two from history of science. I want a runnable in-class activity, not a lecture, that makes the group feel the force of Burkean conservatism through the lens of Chesterton’s fence, but deliberately displaced from familiar political examples so that the intuition must be reconstructed from scratch. Specifically, I want the “fences” to be epistemic protocols inherited from a superseded scientific framework—inference rules, calibration checks, or evidentiary thresholds that were designed for a theoretical model the participants now reject, but that remain embedded in a collaborative research task they must perform together. The activity should divide the group into Reformers (about five people), who are given a brief describing these protocols as irrational remnants and who must vote to abolish or retain each one with justification, and Operators (the remaining nine), who must then execute a joint classification or coordination task using only the protocols that survive the vote. The task should be engineered—through hidden alignment rules printed on cards that only the facilitator holds—so that the removal of certain seemingly arbitrary protocols silently destroys a coordination mechanism the Operators depend on, producing a failure that feels genuinely puzzling and frustrating from the inside rather than predictable from the outside. I want explicit rules for both roles, a three-phase timeline (deliberation, execution, reveal), the materials required (paper, cards, a printed scenario, no technology), and a success condition measured only by task completion and social credit within the simulation, not by real money. Afterward, I want a debrief that maps each phase onto the Burkean argument about prescription and the Chestertonian warning, explains precisely what kind of hidden institutional knowledge the failure demonstrates, and is frank about which feature of Burkean conservatism—perhaps the slow, intergenerational accumulation of tacit justification that cannot be articulated in a single session—this compressed exercise necessarily cannot reproduce. Please also tell me what is likely to go wrong the first time I facilitate it—dominance by the Reformers, premature guessing of the hidden mechanism, or emotional defensiveness—and how to handle each failure mode. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the point is for the idea to emerge from the interaction itself.
