Here is a rough profile of where I stand on collective harm and local convention, for a consistency check before I present it. I hold:

(1) Moral realism about large-scale harm: climate damage and industrial animal agriculture involve mind-independent moral wrongs.
(2) The literal causal-inefficacy objection succeeds: in global or market-scale cases my individual carbon footprint, purchase, or vote is not the difference-maker.
(3) Despite (2), individuals have pro tanto duties not to contribute, but the ground is expressive complicity—performing the act expresses practical identification with a harmful collective practice—not expected causal impact.
(4) This applies even to private, unobserved acts (eating meat alone), since the wrong lies in one's own normative alignment, not in audience effects.
(5) I reject universalization reasoning of the form if everyone did it as a basis for these duties; they do not depend on counterfactual group behavior.
(6) I deny a comparable duty to vote in large elections, because democratic participation expresses civic endorsement rather than alignment with harm.
(7) For everyday conventions—queuing, jaywalking, tipping—I think visitors should follow local norms only when they function as harm- or unfairness-preventing coordination devices; purely arbitrary conventions generate no direct moral obligation for visitors, though social disruption can supply indirect reasons.
(8) Even in massive coordination settings (say, a stadium queue of tens of thousands), the queuing obligation persists despite my individual breach being causally negligible, because fairness fixes my role.
