Draft the joint statement that the Catastrophic Discontinuity camp and the Institutional Transformation camp could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on whether Rome fell and what that claim means.

Camp A holds that the Western Roman system underwent a rapid, nonlinear collapse of centralized fiscal-military capacity in the fifth and sixth centuries—marked by demographic contraction, technological simplification, and the dissolution of imperial extraction apparatus—such that “fall” is the correct category, not merely a moral narrative.

Camp B holds that the same period was a prolonged, regionally heterogeneous reconfiguration: Roman legal, administrative, and cultural forms persisted in mutated but recognizable structures (local curiae, episcopal courts, barbarian kingdoms operating within Roman law), and “collapse” mistakes decentralization and local adaptation for institutional annihilation.

The deliverable is a single document both sides can endorse. It must contain:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. Include agreement that monocausal explanations are inadequate; that late antique textual sources require genre-sensitive interpretation; that the disappearance of a centralized Western imperial office in the late fifth century is a shared reference point rather than an interpretation; and—crucially—that assessing institutional survival requires specifying which functions (legal, fiscal, military, cultural) are being tracked, since the two camps may agree on functional persistence in some domains and disagree in others.

2. A disagreements table with at least three entries. Each entry must state the divergence twice: once in wording Camp A accepts as accurate to its view, once in wording Camp B accepts. Cover (i) whether the loss of imperial tax-collection and army-recruitment in Gaul, Hispania, and Britain represents a collapse of the state’s extractive core or its fragmentation into subordinate structures that retained Roman procedural form; (ii) whether archaeological settlement-abandonment and material-culture patterns indicate a uniform empire-wide rupture or a regionally varied economic restructuring; and (iii) the ontology of institutional identity—whether formal sovereignty continuity is necessary for an institution to survive, or whether functional and normative continuity across formal rupture is sufficient to deny “fall.”

3. A cruxes section listing at least three sub-questions such that resolving one in a particular direction would move at least one camp. For each crux specify: (a) the comparative case, argument, or evidential pattern both sides agree is relevant; (b) Camp A’s explicit prediction about what it shows; and (c) Camp B’s explicit prediction. One crux must turn directly on the ontology of institutional persistence—specifically, whether an institution survives when its sovereign command structure dissolves but its administrative norms continue in local bodies. Another should address the comparative weight of state fragmentation—e.g., comparing the Western experience with the Eastern Empire’s continuity, or with other large-scale imperial dissolutions—stating in advance what each camp expects the comparison to demonstrate.

Write in formal, precise prose. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. Do not invoke highly specialized archaeological reports or unretrievable climate-proxy datasets; work within the standard historiographical terrain (Gibbon’s narrative framework, barbarian settlement, climate and economic stress, the transformation thesis) but frame the residual disagreement with the conceptual precision above, especially the distinction between formal sovereignty and functional continuity.
