Please construct an argument for the claim that in any representational or causal hierarchy, the notion of an inviolate level—a stratum that is causally or semantically closed off from modification by higher levels—is not just empirically elusive but conceptually unstable once we allow for mutual entailment between adjacent strata. Use M.C. Escher's Drawing Hands not merely as an illustrative metaphor but as a structural model: treat the two hands as representing adjacent hierarchical levels that each instantiate the conditions for the other's existence, such that neither can serve as a unilateral ground. To keep the argument from being a rehearsal of standard self-reference paradoxes, frame your reasoning through a specific distinction you introduce: call it the difference between vertical entailment (where a lower level deterministically fixes the properties of the level above) and lateral entailment (where entities at adjacent levels co-constitute one another without directional priority). Your task is to show that any proposed inviolate base must either (i) secretly depend on lateral entailment within the base itself, thereby forfeiting its status as a pure foundation, or (ii) be so causally isolated that it cannot enter into representational relations with the levels it is supposed to ground, rendering it epistemically inert. In developing this, please separate what is relatively settled in the philosophy of hierarchical systems (for instance, that feedback loops complicate simple bottom-up determination) from what remains genuinely contested (such as whether mutual entailment eliminates the need for any grounding relation whatsoever, or merely relocates it). Do not present either horn of your dilemma as the consensus view among philosophers of mind or metaphysics; instead, flag precisely where knowledgeable disagreement lies, particularly regarding whether lateral entailment is a feature of all hierarchies or only of representational ones.
