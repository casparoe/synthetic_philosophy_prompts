Next month I am giving a short talk at a university philosophy-of-mind reading group that I was invited to join after attending some public lectures. The group is mostly doctoral students in philosophy of mind plus two computer-science researchers who work on language models. I do not have an academic background; I have been thinking through these issues on my own after reading popular accounts of artificial intelligence and consciousness.

My thesis is this: Searle's Chinese Room argument is usually read as showing that syntax is not sufficient for semantics, but I think it actually demonstrates something stronger — that intrinsic intentionality requires a specific kind of causal-historical embedding in a biological or physically continuous environment, and that without this embedding, neither computers nor brains (if described purely as syntactic engines) possess real aboutness. I developed this idea after reflecting on how my own understanding of a word like water seems inseparable from my bodily history of drinking, feeling rain, and being burned by steam, not merely from internal symbol manipulation. I want to defend this extended reading against the standard replies — the systems reply, the robot reply, and the biological-naturalism reply — but I need to anticipate how this particular audience will challenge me.

Please prepare me for the objections I will actually face in the Q&A, ordered roughly by how likely and damaging they are. For each objection, phrase it as a philosophy graduate student or a CS researcher would actually say it. Include what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and a brief reply I can deliver in about two minutes, with a frank note on where that reply is weakest. I especially need to know the one objection I am hoping nobody asks — likely something about whether my causal-historical embedding merely restates Searle's biological naturalism in different vocabulary, or whether it commits me to an implausible panpsychism about physical systems.

Please separate what is broadly settled in the philosophy-of-mind literature on the Chinese Room from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed interpretation — for instance, whether the argument succeeds against functionalism, or whether the systems reply is adequate — as if it were the consensus view.
