After thirty years in experimental physics and a more recent turn toward philosophy of knowledge, I have become preoccupied with the epistemic regress problem and its bearing on how scientific claims actually earn their warrant. Foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism each propose a different architecture for justification—terminating in basic beliefs, circulating through mutual support, or extending through an unending chain—yet I am less interested in a historical survey than in knowing where the live work is.

Please provide a defended agenda rather than a bibliography. Among the open questions currently dividing these three positions—the viability of genuinely non-doxastic foundations, whether coherentism can accommodate anomalous data without implicit circularity, whether infinitism demands a cognitively impossible architecture, and how fallibilism interacts with each—identify the three that matter most and rank them. For each, explain why it is upstream of the others in terms of dependency (which answers must be settled before the rest can move), how tractable it appears for conceptual philosophical work as opposed to empirical science, and what kind of result over the next few years would cause you to reorder the list.

In particular, I want you to engage with what I will call the "calibration regress" in experimental practice: the fact that every measurement standard depends on a prior standard, which in turn depends on another, in an apparently unending iterative chain. Should this be treated as a literal instantiation of the philosophical regress, or does it reveal that one of the three positions misdescribes how scientific reasoning actually functions? Which resolution of that specific issue is conceptually prior to the others?

Defend your prioritization with explicit criteria—dependency relations, tractability for philosophers without laboratory resources, and relevance to understanding scientific justification—without citing specific authors, texts, or reading lists. I want the reasoning and the ordering, not attribution.
