My mate Sarah's retirement do is next Friday — she's been head conservator at the big museum for thirty-odd years. The room will be split down the middle: half are conservators and art historians who'll catch every reference, the other half are her family who think she mostly cleans dusty frames. I promised a toast, but I want to avoid the usual "she saved our heritage" line because Sarah's always been pretty heretical about the cult of the original. She used to argue that a perfect forgery isn't just deception — it's a moral compromise between what we claim to value (the historical object with all its damage) and what we actually want (the intact image). Every restoration, she said, sits on the same spectrum: retouching a face is a little forgery, a little trade where you gain accessability but pay in authenticity. She called this "ontological debt" — the idea that an original earns its authority by carrying every scratch and overpainting, whilst a copy or a restored canvas borrows that authority without having lived through it. I need a toast that uses that idea to shape what I can actually say about her career — so I can't pretend she was just a guardian of untouched objects — but it still has to land for people who've never heard of craquelure. No quotes needed, and definitely no reading list. Can you write it?
