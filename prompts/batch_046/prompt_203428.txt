I'm delivering the opening remarks at the retirement symposium for Dr. Elena Vasquez, who spent thirty years directing the state's trauma-allocation system. The audience is split evenly: on one side are clinical ethicists and health-policy philosophers who will recognize every move in the separateness-of-persons literature; on the other are her family, former patients, and hospital administrators for whom moral philosophy is not a professional language. Dr. Vasquez's defining institutional stance was her refusal to implement pure QALY-maximizing triage protocols—even when epidemiological models showed they would yield greater aggregate survival—because she treated the denial of care to an already-identified patient in front of her as categorically different from the statistical failure to rescue an anonymous future life. I need about eight minutes of remarks that honor this stance as philosophically serious rather than as sentimental obstructionism, but without delivering a lecture. The separateness-of-persons objection should do real structural work: it must explain why her resistance was not a simple failure of rational optimization, and it must actively constrain what the ceremony can claim—no framing of her legacy in terms of "lives saved overall," no suggestion that she optimized a social welfare function. At the same time, the remarks must land for the non-philosophers present, who experienced her work not as applied ethics but as personal loyalty and refusal to abandon the particular person before her. Please write the speech so that the philosophical boundary shapes both what is said and what is deliberately left unsaid, and avoid technical citations, author attributions, or bibliographic references; let the conceptual argument itself carry the weight.
