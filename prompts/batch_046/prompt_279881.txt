I'm trying to work out a consistent philosophical position on education that takes Locke's framework seriously but updates it for contemporary conditions, and I want an audit before I present it. Here are the commitments I've arrived at: (1) I accept Locke's anti-nativism about ideas—there are no innate conceptual contents or propositional knowledge in the mind at birth—but I also think that predictive-processing models show the brain comes with strong, domain-specific priors (for object permanence, agent detection, numerical approximation, and the like) that shape learning without constituting "ideas" in Locke's sense. (2) From this I draw that education should still be primarily habituative and experiential, yet it ought to be calibrated to work with these innate priors rather than treating the mind as uniformly plastic. (3) I hold, with Locke, that the chief end of education is virtue and practical wisdom, not ornamental learning or vocational preparation. (4) At the same time, I think modern education must include explicit, formal training in critical evaluation of testimony and authority—direct epistemic defense skills—because the information environment requires defenses that habituation alone cannot supply. (5) I reject Locke's specific prescriptions for physical hardening and emotional suppression—cold baths, withholding affection—as ethically unacceptable, though I retain a weaker commitment to cultivating stoic self-regulation. (6) Finally, I believe education is fundamentally a matter of parental authority and private judgment, but I also maintain that severe inequalities in educational access create obligations for substantial state redistribution and standard-setting that go well beyond Locke's minimal commonwealth.

My question is whether this package actually hangs together. In particular, I am worried about hidden tensions: between accepting innate priors while denying innate ideas in a way that might collapse the distinction I need; between making virtue the primary end while insisting on explicit critical-reasoning instruction that Locke would treat as premature and potentially disruptive to habit formation; between rejecting Locke's hardening methods while still claiming his habituative model as the backbone of education; and between defending parental authority as fundamental while endorsing robust state intervention for distributive justice. Could you audit these commitments, rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, identify which pairs only appear to conflict and dissolve on closer inspection, specify the least costly revision that would restore full consistency, and explain what that revision costs me?
