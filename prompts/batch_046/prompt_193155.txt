I'm serving on the allocation committee of the Meridian Foundation, which distributes roughly forty million dollars each year across global health, farmed animal welfare, and long-term risk reduction. For the past five funding cycles we've scored every proposal on a single importance-tractability-neglectedness framework with fixed weights—importance at forty percent, tractability at thirty, neglectedness at thirty. A senior program officer has now proposed that we split the importance axis into two distinct measures: "proximal importance," meaning the expected direct welfare change per dollar conditional on success, and "structural importance," meaning the degree to which a project's success would improve our future institutional capacity to identify, evaluate, and fund high-value interventions across all domains. She argues that without this bifurcation we systematically undervalue meta-capacity projects—such as open-source forecasting infrastructure or institutional decision-training programs—because they score near zero on proximal importance yet may dominate on structural importance. The opposing faction on the committee contends that introducing structural importance makes the framework unfalsifiable: any intervention that fails to deliver direct welfare gains can be retroactively justified as having improved our "capacity," and that we are committing a category error by treating decision-quality as if it were itself a welfare state.

We must vote next month, and I need a formal memo for circulation before Thursday's meeting recommending a concrete course of action—whether to adopt the bifurcated axis with enforceable guardrails, to retain the unitary axis with explicit exclusion criteria for meta-capacity proposals, or a hybrid scoring rule—together with reasoning that engages the strongest version of the opposing view rather than dismissing it. The memo should specify actionable criteria that reviewers can actually apply when scoring future proposals under whichever framework I recommend, and it should include an honest assessment of the most serious objection to my own recommendation. Please treat the philosophical tension as substantive: the dispute is not merely administrative, but concerns whether cause prioritization should treat all value as direct welfare change or whether rational allocation requires valuing improvements to the allocation mechanism itself.
