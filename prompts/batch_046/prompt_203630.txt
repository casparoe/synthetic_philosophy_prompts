Construct a decision-tree taxonomy of philosophical positions on whether Max Black's thought experiment of two qualitatively identical spheres in an otherwise symmetric universe genuinely refutes Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. The tree must be built from diagnostic questions rather than from named labels: each internal node should pose a concrete commitment that a student could answer without knowing which interpretation it leads to.

Begin with the root question: if two objects share every qualitative property — intrinsic and relational — are they necessarily one and the same object?

From there, branch through the following distinctions in order:
1. Whether spatial relations (for example, being separated by a given distance, or standing in a particular orientation relative to an axis of symmetry) count as qualitative properties capable of distinguishing the spheres.
2. Whether the global symmetry of the universe in which the spheres are embedded undermines the claim that they are genuinely relationally distinct, or whether symmetry is itself a feature compatible with numerical plurality.
3. Whether modal properties — specifically, what each sphere could have done or where it could have moved had the other not existed — are admissible as distinguishing features, or whether they illicitly presuppose the very distinctness at issue.
4. Whether accepting two spheres without qualitative difference forces one to adopt a primitive thisness (haecceity), or whether the distinction can be sustained by a structuralist account that individuates objects solely by their place within a relational structure.

The tree should terminate in named leaves representing at least: a strong Leibnizian position that denies the coherence of the scenario; a Black-style position that accepts the spheres as numerically distinct despite qualitative identity; a relationalist position that denies qualitative identity by insisting that spatial relations are fully real and distinguishing; a structuralist position; and a haecceitist position. You must also identify at least one logically available combination of answers that yields an apparently unoccupied leaf, and provide a brief diagnosis of why it is unoccupied — for instance, whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Finally, flag any pair of named leaves that your tree cannot genuinely separate given the questions asked, and explain exactly at which branch the distinction collapses. Present the tree in a clear hierarchical format, using British English throughout, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific texts.
