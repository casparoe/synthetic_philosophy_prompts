I'm trying to write a philosophy paper comparing the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda with modern logical treatments of vagueness. Anekāntavāda holds that reality is irreducibly many-sided, so any single description of an object captures only one partial perspective; syādvāda reinforces this through a seven-fold qualified predication (the saptabhaṅgī) that systematically avoids unqualified assertions like "this is only X." By contrast, supervaluationism addresses vagueness—say, in terms like "dense" or "diffuse"—by treating them as lacking sharp boundaries and assigning truth-values only relative to admissible precisifications. Could you compare these two frameworks? In particular, does syādvāda's schema mainly serve as epistemic humility, reminding us that our knowledge is limited, or does it carry a metaphysical commitment that genuinely contradictory properties can co-inhere in the same substance? Furthermore, is supervaluationism's strategy of selecting precise cutoffs compatible with anekāntavāda's refusal to privilege any single description, or do the two ultimately diverge over whether ambiguity belongs to our descriptions or to the world itself? I'd like to know which framework offers the more coherent account of consistently describing something—like a mist that seems both present and absent—without collapsing into simple relativism.
