I've been tracking arguments about cosmological multiverses and string-theoretic frameworks—domains where direct, discriminating experiments are currently out of reach. There's a recurring move I can't place. A critic notes that the theory hasn't made a precise, risky prediction that was subsequently verified. The proponent grants this but then catalogs other merits: resolving internal inconsistencies, accommodating known measurements more naturally than rivals, or explaining parameter values that otherwise appear arbitrary. That part is familiar. What strikes me is the next step: the proponent treats this catalog not just as motivation for further research, but as collectively supplying the same epistemic standing that a confirmed risky prediction would have—almost as if indirect theoretical virtues can be summed, with an implicit exchange rate, to substitute for one direct empirical success. Critics reply that these goods aren't interchangeable, yet the defense proceeds as though they are. Does this specific move—aggregating non-empirical theoretical virtues into a fungible substitute for predictive empirical confirmation—have an established name in philosophy of science? If not, please define it precisely, distinguish it from ordinary inference to the best explanation and from the Duhem-Quine problem, and indicate where its boundaries lie.
