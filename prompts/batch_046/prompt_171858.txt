You are a philosopher of environmental ethics and epistemology; think carefully and begin by brainstorming at least three distinct implicit theories that could license the pattern below, then evaluate them.

Consider this institutional practice: environmental regulators typically apply a precautionary standard to novel synthetic chemicals—treating the absence of long-term safety evidence as a reason to withhold approval—while applying a risk-proportionate, permissive standard to ecological interventions such as assisted migration of climate-threatened species or the release of self-limiting gene drives—treating the absence of long-term harm evidence as sufficient for conditional approval. Both involve irreversible, spatially distributed effects; both operate under deep uncertainty about multi-generational ecosystem dynamics; and both can be initiated by actors who do not bear the full costs of failure. Yet the burden of proof is inverted.

What philosophical commitments about harm, uncertainty, and moral agency does this bifurcation presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does it depend on a categorical distinction between synthetic and biological causation, on a normative default status assigned to "natural" processes, on a theory of who bears the burden of justification (corporate producers versus conservation authorities), or on an implicit ontology of ecosystem integrity? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say whether its parts are philosophically consistent with one another, and identify where it is underdetermined—compatible with rival views of precaution. Finally, describe what a consistently applied precautionary framework would have to change in the regulation of ecological interventions, and name the specific feature of that revised practice that would reveal which implicit theory was actually doing the work.
