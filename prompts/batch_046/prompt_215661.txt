I'm currently serving on a seven-member municipal panel tasked with selecting a permanent public-space art installation, and the deliberation has forced me to think about the moral structure of collective choice in a way that my usual intuitions don't cover. We must choose between two finalists: Proposal A is widely regarded as genuinely transformative—architects and critics agree it would become a lasting civic landmark—yet the lead artist has well-documented, unretracted labor violations in prior large-scale projects, and awarding the commission would confer substantial public prestige and funding upon them. Proposal B is competent but uninspired, avoiding any association with exploitation but committing the city to decades of aesthetic mediocrity. Our charter requires unanimous consent; I am the only dissenting member. If I withhold approval, the panel deadlocks and an automatic fallback procedure selects B; if I consent, A wins and I become one of seven co-signatories to the decision.

Several colleagues have framed my position using analogies to democratic participation that I can recognize but cannot confidently apply here. They argue that in a collective body of seven, my vote is functionally expressive rather than causally decisive—like casting a ballot in a large election—because the outcome is overdetermined by the other six members, and that using my veto to impose a private moral judgment on a shared aesthetic legacy is a kind of moral overreach. Others counter that because I am the decisive blocker, my refusal creates a direct causal pathway to preventing harm, transforming what looks like symbolic dissent into an active duty to withhold consent regardless of the aesthetic cost. I am particularly troubled by a third possibility: that consenting to A makes me structurally complicit in honoring an exploiter, yet refusing B imposes my values on the community's collective judgment about public beauty, especially since my refusal does nothing to alter the artist's behavior in other contexts. I want to understand whether my obligation is best analyzed as an expressive act with negligible causal weight, as a lesser-evil choice between complicity and mediocrity, or as something else entirely—perhaps a case where the institutional design of unanimous consent creates moral duties that wouldn't exist under majority rule. How should I evaluate the weight of a vote that is simultaneously symbolic affirmation, potential veto, and contribution to a collective aesthetic commitment that will persist long after the panel dissolves?
