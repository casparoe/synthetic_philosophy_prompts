In a seminar comparing contemporary machine-learning practice to Alan Turing’s 1950 proposal for intelligent machinery, the discussion kept returning to the gloss that one should construct a child machine and subject it to education rather than attempting to program an adult brain directly. A participant used this to contend that modern large-scale training regimes miss Turing’s point, because they lack the two conditions he apparently distinguished—discipline and initiative—arranged in a genuine developmental sequence.

I want to understand exactly what that slogan could be committing us to. Could you disambiguate it into a menu of precise claims? Please include: (i) the weak, almost tautological reading (for instance, that immature systems require more iterations or data); (ii) intermediate structural readings (that intelligence requires staged development, or that explicit rule-coding must yield to adaptive conditioning); (iii) stronger institutional or social readings (that education requires an interactive pedagogical relation, or that discipline and initiative are separable sequential conditions rather than simultaneous optimization targets); and (iv) radical or metaphysical readings (that consciousness or moral standing can only emerge through a socially structured upbringing). For each reading, say whether it is defensible on its own, whether it is actually supported by Turing’s discussion of learning machines and education, whether it applies fairly to current practice, and—crucially—whether it licenses the specific criticism that modern training is not genuinely Turing-style education.

Please separate what is settled about Turing’s view from what is genuinely contested among historians and philosophers of artificial intelligence, and do not present any contested interpretation—especially about whether Turing intended a literal developmental analogy or only a heuristic—as if it were the consensus.
