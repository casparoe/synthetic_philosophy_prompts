I've been trying to work out my actual views on remembering and forgetting, partly after skimming Margalit's ethics of memmory and Rieff's stuff about culture losing its dead, and I keep running into cases where my gut pulls in opposite directions.

Here are my verdicts: A nation that tears down every physical trace of a past atrocity and replaces it with generic development feels like it's committing a kind of second erasure — wrong. But an individual survivor who decides to stop speaking about her trauma, burns her personal letters, and asks friends never to mention it again feels like she's exercising a legitimate right to move on — not wrong at all, maybe even healthy. A government that seals archives for fifty years to "protect social cohesion" strikes me as wrong, though I can't tell if it's the same wrong as the demolition case. A community that holds an annual ritual where they collectively list the names of the dead but then symbolically "release" them for the rest of the year — that feels respectful, not like betrayal. But then there's the case of a historian who, knowing survivors are dying out, uses AI to synthesize realistic but fabricated testimonies to keep the emotional impact alive for students — that feels grotesque, like a different kind of violence. And finally, a child of a perpetrator who legally changes her name and refuses to learn about her parent's crimes — I think she's not obligated to carry that weight, but there's still something cowardly about it?

Is there one principle behind these — maybe something about who controls the memory, or whether remembrance serves the dead versus serving the living? Or do I just have to accept that my judgments split between "mnemonic duty to victims" and "mnemonic autonomy of survivors and descendants"? What's the principle, and which of my verdicts has to go? Also, apply it to a case I keep going back on: a museum that digitizes every survivor testimony, then deletes the entire database after the last survivor dies because storage costs money and "the point was to honor them, not archive data." Is that closer to tearing down the monument or closer to the survivor burning her letters?
