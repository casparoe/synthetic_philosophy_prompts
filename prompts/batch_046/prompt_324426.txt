I am not an academic philosopher; I have been reading and thinking about moral justification on my own for several years, and next Thursday I present at a community ethics forum. The audience will include two philosophy graduate students who know Scanlon’s contractualist framework—wrongness as failure of justifiability to each affected individual—an applied ethicist who works on disaster triage and thinks individualist ethics collapses in mass-casualty cases, a political theorist who treats liberal contractualism as ideology that masks structural inequality, and several thoughtful non-specialists who care more about practical outcomes than about meta-ethical machinery. I want to be ready for their questions, not just defend my idea in the abstract.

My thesis is a modification I call nested contractualism. I accept the Scanlonian core that morality is about what we can justify to each person, and I accept that this generates the familiar aggregation problem: saving one life versus saving thousands from headaches seems to require summing harms that contractualism refuses to sum. But I argue that aggregation is not always impermissible; it is justified inside what I term institutional reciprocity webs—shared structures of expectation such as workplaces, citizenship regimes, or long-term care networks—where each member has standing to demand that others honor the web’s norms. Within such a web, breaking expectations for many people does not merely add up individual wrongs; it produces a distinct relational rupture, the collapse of a shared framework of mutual accountability. So limited aggregation is permitted, but only through this relational mechanism, not through utilitarian summing of welfare.
