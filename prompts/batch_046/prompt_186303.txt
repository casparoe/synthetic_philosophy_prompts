I am writing a long-form feature on the gender-equality paradox—the regularity that countries with the strongest institutional gender-equality measures often exhibit more pronounced horizontal occupational segregation by sex rather than less. Through my reporting I have encountered two interpretive camps that talk past each other. The first, which I call the constraint-release camp, holds that egalitarian policies strip away artificial barriers and allow underlying, biologically influenced distributions of vocational interest and risk tolerance to express themselves without distortion. The second, which I term the semiotic-reinforcement camp, contends that formal equality eliminates overt coercion but leaves intact, and sometimes intensifies, cultural schemas that code high-autonomy sectors—such as certain branches of engineering or care work—as masculine or feminine, thereby channelling choices through meaning rather than through explicit restriction. I should like you to draft the joint statement that representatives of these two camps ought to produce after a rigorous adversarial collaboration.

Please structure the document in three parts. First, provide a numbered list of factual and methodological claims about the paradox itself—its cross-national robustness, the distinction between horizontal and vertical segregation, the role of sector-level autonomy, and the limits of existing datasets—that both sides can endorse without reservation. Second, present the precise disagreements in neutral wording that each camp would accept as a fair statement of its own position; distinguish clearly the empirical dispute (what mechanism explains the correlation between institutional equality and segregation) from the normative dispute (whether the resulting pattern is unjust, and whether policy ought to reduce it). Third, identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete study design, natural experiment, or cross-national comparison that both sides agree would bear upon it, and record in advance what result each camp predicts. In particular, include one crux concerning whether segregation in high-autonomy professions tracks institutional-equality indices more closely than indicators of cultural gender essentialism, and another asking whether symmetric parental-leave policies designed to equalise uptake reduce segregation more effectively than policies that maximise individual choice regardless of sex. Finally, indicate whether any part of their disagreement is verbal—arising from divergent uses of terms such as “preference”, “choice”, or “barrier”—and, if so, specify exactly which portion and why clarifying that usage dissolves the appearance of conflict.
