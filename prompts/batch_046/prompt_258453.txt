As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I am attempting to clarify a longstanding dispute in the philosophy of mind for a technically literate but non-specialist audience. I need you to draft the joint statement that would emerge from an adversarial collaboration between two opposing camps on eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires.

The two camps are defined as follows. Camp A, the Hard Eliminativists, holds that beliefs and desires—as characterised by folk psychology—are theoretical posits comparable to phlogiston; they lack ontological reality and will eventually be replaced entirely by descriptions drawn from neuroscience and dynamical systems theory, with no residual explanatory role. Camp B, the Functional Realists, holds that beliefs and desires denote genuine, if abstract, explanatory kinds; mental-state attributions capture real behavioural and cognitive patterns that lower-level descriptions necessarily miss, and this applies to both biological organisms and to sufficiently complex artificial architectures.

Please produce a formal, structured document in British English, with no bibliographic references, reading lists, or citations to specific scholars beyond the camp labels above. The document should contain the following sections in this order.

1. Agreed Claims (numbered list): At least six statements that both camps can sign without reservation. These should cover shared empirical premises—for instance, about the predictive limitations of folk psychology in certain domains; about the capacity of recurrent neural architectures to produce stable, goal-directed behaviour without explicit symbolic representations of propositional content; and about the present absence of a complete, lossless translation manual between dynamical neural descriptions and mental-state attributions. Formulate each claim in language that neither camp would reject as slanted.

2. Residual Disagreements (table format): For each camp, present in neutral wording the exact position it holds regarding (a) the ontological status of beliefs and desires, (b) the criteria for legitimate explanation in cognitive science, and (c) the applicability of mental-state talk to artificial systems lacking biological substrate. Each entry must be phrased so that the named camp accepts it as an accurate rendering of its own view, and so that the opposing camp accepts it as an accurate rendering of the other side’s view. Explicitly indicate, for each row, whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal—that is, whether both sides describe the same underlying phenomena using different terminology.

3. The Cruxes: Identify three precise sub-questions such that resolving any one would move at least one camp closer to the other. Examples might include whether explanatory indispensability entails ontological commitment; whether a complete dynamical description of a system could in principle replace mental-state attribution without explanatory loss; and whether an artificial system that never evolved biological cognition could genuinely instantiate belief-like states.

4. Concrete Test Case and Predictions: Define a hypothetical system—call it System X—which is a recurrent neural architecture trained via reinforcement learning in a partially observable environment. System X achieves robust, stable goal-pursuit and adaptive behavioural adjustment without containing any internal data structures that explicitly encode propositional content. Describe its behavioural profile in entirely neutral terms, agreed upon by both camps. Then, for each of the three cruxes listed in section 3, state in advance what Camp A and Camp B respectively predict about System X: specifically, whether mental-state attribution is ontologically appropriate, merely heuristically useful, or should be eliminated in favour of a purely dynamical description. The behavioural description of System X must remain identical across both predictions; only the interpretive conclusion should differ.

Maintain a polished, formal tone throughout, clarify unavoidable philosophical terminology with brief parenthetical explanations suitable for a reader without formal philosophical background, and ensure that the document reads as a constructive text both parties could endorse, with the remaining disagreement rendered exact rather than obscured.
