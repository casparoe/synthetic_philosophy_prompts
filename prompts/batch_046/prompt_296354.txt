I've been arguing with friends about whether "democratic backsliding" is actually describing something real or just a fancy insult for governments we don't like. One friend says it's measurable: you look at whether courts still check the executive, whether elections are genuinely competitive, whether the bureaucracy is partisan-captured — she points to Hungary's constitutional rewriting, Poland's judicial discipline chambers, Turkey's shift to presidentialism, and even some US state-level election administration moves as clear cases where formal rules changed to weaken accountability. The other friend says that's just cherry-picking: every government tweaks institutions, and calling it "backsliding" assumes a liberal-democratic baseline that isn't neutral. He thinks the concept smuggles in normative assumptions about what democracy should look like and gets applied selectively to right-wing populists while ignoring similar moves on the left.

But there's this thing I keep noticing that neither of them addresses well — call it "paper democracy" for now: situations where the formal democratic architecture is left mostly intact, even strengthened on paper, but is funtionally decoupled from actual decision-making. Like, elections still happen and courts still exist, but the ruling party builds parallel mechanisms — party-controlled media regulators, captured constitutional courts that rubber-stamp, bureaucratic appointments that hollow out independent agencies — so that voter influence gets rerouted without ever being formally abolished. Hungary looks like this to me: the Fundamental Law wasn't a coup, it was a legal rewrite that layered new controls onto old structures. Poland's judicial reforms did something similar — legal on their face, but specifcally disabling checks. Even in the US you see versions: state legislatures changing election administration rules through perfectly legal statutes, not banning voting outright but shifting who certifies results. So I'm wondering if the real divide isn't just "is backsliding real?" but whether we should define it by formal rule-change, by functional democratic capacity, or by this decoupling mechanism specifically.

Could you draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a "formal-institutionalist" camp (backsliding requires observable constitutional or statutory erosion of checks) and a "functional-decoupling" camp (backsliding is present when formal structures remain but are systematically disconnected from actual accountability, regardless of legality)? I want the standard format: agreed claims about the four countries and about measurement; disagreements in neutral wording; cruxes — especially whether decoupling can be identified without smuggling in a normative baseline, and whether formal legality should matter; and for each crux a concrete comparison or scenario both sides agree is relevant, with predictions. Don't just lay out options — take a committed stance on which formulations are genuinely neutral, which cruxes are substantive versus verbal, and whether the decoupling frame actually captures something the other two miss. No citations or reading lists needed — just the argument and the document.
