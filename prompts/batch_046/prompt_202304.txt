I serve on the Verification Board of the Riverside Municipal Archive. We are implementing an exhaustive digital authentication protocol: every record in our holdings must carry exactly one of two stamps—“confirmed authentic” or “rejected as inauthentic”—and the system is designed so that no record remains unlabeled.

Last week we received Document 774-B, a physically genuine handwritten ledger page from 1962, paper and provenance verified. Its sole substantive entry reads: Every record that bears this office’s seal of authenticity is false, and this very entry is itself one of those sealed records. The material origin is not in dispute; the dispute is how the protocol should classify it.

The board is split. One faction says we should apply the seal and treat the statement as rhetorical or historical commentary rather than a logical claim. Another faction says that applying the seal makes the statement self-undermining, while withholding the seal makes the statement appear correct, which also collapses the system’s authority. A smaller group proposes creating a third “indeterminate” label, but our charter currently permits only the two.

I have no formal philosophical training, but I have spent several evenings trying to think through whether there is a consistent way to handle this without breaking the protocol entirely. The difficulty seems deeper than bureaucracy. It concerns what we actually mean when we call a document “true”—whether we mean it corresponds to the facts, whether it coheres with the rest of the archive, or whether “truth” here is just a practical tool that should not be turned back against the system that applies it.

Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—apply the seal, reject the document, apply the seal with a new exception clause, or suspend the binary protocol for this class of cases—with a clear bottom line the board can vote on. The reasoning should engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side, not merely restate our charter’s legal definitions. I also want the memo to state what governing principle, if any, should guide future submissions that refer back to the protocol’s own authority, even if adopting that principle requires amending our rules.
