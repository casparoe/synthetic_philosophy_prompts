After thirty years in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have recently turned to philosophy of mind with the same expectation that conceptual clarity should eventually map onto observable behavior. In my retirement reading group we have been debating Daniel Dennett's multiple drafts model and its rejection of the Cartesian theater—the notion of a single internal stage where conscious contents are presented to a central observer. Rather than stay at the level of abstract argument, I want to test these two frameworks against a well-known clinical episode: the split-brain studies carried out by Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga from the 1960s onward, in which patients with severed corpus callosum display behaviors that seem to involve two independent streams of awareness, including the left hemisphere's tendency to invent coherent narratives for actions initiated by the right.

Please analyze this episode as a case study that runs in both directions. First, for each framework—Cartesian theater and multiple drafts—state precisely what it predicts about mental life after commissurotomy: whether there should remain one conscious subject, two, or none in the usual sense; how it explains the left hemisphere's confabulatory interpreter; and whether the fragmentation of narrative coherence is an anomaly or exactly what the model expects. Second, identify the points where the clinical data resist clean mapping: are there behaviors that neither model anticipates without ad hoc adjustment, or that both can absorb only by redefining the boundaries of the subject? I am particularly interested in whether the split-brain case genuinely adjudicates between the two views, or whether it reveals that both were designed for intact brains and therefore speak past each other when applied to this kind of neurological division. Keep the analysis conceptual rather than clinical—do not list bibliographies or citations, and avoid appeals to papers I could not access myself; I want to see whether the theory illuminates the episode and whether the episode in turn exposes commitments the theories had not previously been forced to defend.
