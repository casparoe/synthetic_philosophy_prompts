I am preparing notes for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of computation and have recently returned to von Neumann’s unfinished study comparing neural architecture to computing machinery, published posthumously. In my reading, he treats the brain as fundamentally a digital system, regarding its analog and statistical features as merely implementation-level noise rather than as structurally significant, and he seems to hold that mental states can be exhaustively characterized as high-level descriptions of underlying binary neural states. From this, I have drawn the conclusion that he was an unacknowledged precursor to modern computational functionalism—perhaps even more systematically so than Putnam, since von Neumann allegedly derived the digital character of cognition directly from physiological considerations about neuronal redundancy rather than from a Turing-machine argument.

Yet I find myself unable to reconcile this digitalist reading with his repeated emphasis on the brain’s reliance on a peculiar statistical language unlike that of artificial computers, as well as with his well-known observations about the system’s remarkable reliability despite the frequent failure of individual components. Should I interpret these statistical and reliability-related remarks as mere qualifications within an essentially digitalist framework, or does the thesis about statistical language actually undermine the claim that mental states are reducible to binary descriptions? In short, was von Neumann advancing a specifically digital form of functionalism that contemporary neuroscience has abandoned, or have I misidentified the architectural core of his argument?
