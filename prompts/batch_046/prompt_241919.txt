As a novelist developing a multigenerational saga, I have organized my thinking about the past according to Nietzsche's three modes of history and their respective benefits and dangers for living creation. Here is where I stand: (a) I regard monumental history—the isolation of exemplary deeds and heroic figures—as vital for giving my characters aspirational magnitude, even while I grant that it necessarily falsifies the past through idealization; (b) I regard antiquarian history—the meticulous preservation of customs, dialect, and material detail—as indispensable for narrative authenticity, though I recognize its tendency to paralyze present invention through excessive reverence; (c) I regard critical history—the deliberate rupture with inherited forms—as necessary to prevent my work from becoming mere archival reproduction, despite its risk of leaving both characters and author without historical ground; (d) I maintain that a single artistic project should actively integrate all three modes, allowing each to correct the excesses of the others; (e) I hold that the disadvantages Nietzsche describes—delusion, stagnation, and destructive rootlessness—are not accidental byproducts of dominance but inherent risks present whenever the corresponding mode is employed; and (f) I take it that monumental history's necessary selectivity and antiquarian history's demand for comprehensive fidelity are so opposed in their cognitive requirements that they cannot be sustained by the same artistic consciousness without self-deception. Do these commitments cohere? In particular, does (d) survive the tension between (b) and (c), and does (e) undermine (d)'s claim that integration neutralizes danger? Furthermore, is (f) a genuine contradiction, or does it dissolve once we distinguish between the author's research posture and the narrative's selective representation? Rank the tensions by severity, and identify the least costly revision that would restore consistency without abandoning the core artistic aim.
