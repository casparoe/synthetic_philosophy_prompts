I'm preparing notes for a philosophy of biology reading group, and I've become preoccupied with a tension in how molecular biologists talk about DNA. We routinely describe nucleic acids as carrying "genetic information" and refer to the triplet-to-amino-acid mapping as a "code," yet philosophers often warn that this is merely metaphorical. My concern is narrower and, I suspect, conceptually muddled. Suppose we grant, for the sake of argument, that DNA literally encodes representational content—propositions about which proteins ought to be synthesized—rather than merely participating in causal regularities. In that case, there must be something that serves as the interpreter, assigning semantic value to the syntactic structures. In human language, that interpreter is a conscious agent; in computer systems, it is ultimately derived from human designers. But in a bacterium, there is no cognitive system and no designer present in the moment of transcription. One could propose that the ribosome itself, together with the associated tRNA adaptors, functions as the interpreter, yet this seems to attribute derived intentionality to a non-conscious biochemical complex in precisely the sense that makes Searle's Chinese Room argument troubling. Meanwhile, if we deny that any interpretation occurs—if we say DNA is just chemistry—then it becomes puzzling why we classify certain base-pair substitutions as "errors" or "misreadings" rather than as neutral causal variations, since the normative vocabulary of error seems to presuppose that there is a correct representational content being violated. How should I think about this? Is the confusion rooted in assuming that information-theoretic descriptions of gene expression already carry semantic commitments, or is there a coherent way to maintain that DNA is genuinely representational while locating the interpretive function in molecular machinery without collapsing into a panpsychist view of cellular processes?
