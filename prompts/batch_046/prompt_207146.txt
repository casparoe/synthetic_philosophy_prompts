I'm writing an essay on Avicenna's flying man for my philosophy of mind course and keep going round in circles with an argument my tutor said was "too ambitious". I was discussing it with a friend over coffee and she said the conclusion can't be right, but she couldn't point to the exact slip.

(1) The suspended man knows he exists with no sensory input at all, so that knowledge can't come from the body. (2) If knowing yourself is independent of bodily experience, then the self isn't essentially physical. (3) So Avicenna's thought experiment supports substance dualism. (4) But if the self is genuinely non-physical, it shouldn't have spatial or temporal properties either. (5) Still, Avicenna describes him suspended in air at a particular moment -- so he still has location and duration. (6) That means either we get a ghost with actual coordinates, which sounds ridiculous, or the argument only proves self-knowledge is non-inferential, not that the soul is separate from matter. (7) So the flying man either proves too little or proves something absurd. Which step should I give up? I'd realy like to keep (1) and (3), but I want to know what that costs me.
