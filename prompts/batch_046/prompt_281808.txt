I've been working through a line of reasoning about deontological constraints in institutional contexts, and I keep ending up with a conclusion that seems to dissolve the framework rather than refine it. I want to see whether the fault lies in my reasoning or in the theory itself, and I'm hoping to keep my grip on the core deontological idea that an agent can be forbidden from performing a certain kind of harm regardless of the aggregate outcome. Here is the argument, numbered for clarity.

(1) A central deontological commitment is that certain acts — for instance, intentionally harming an innocent person — are wrong for the agent who performs them regardless of overall consequences, because the agent stands in a direct moral relation to the victim.

(2) This wrongness is agent-relative: it binds the particular agent who would carry out the act, not merely anyone who happens to be in a position to prevent it.

(3) Now consider an organizational structure in which no single employee makes the harmful decision; instead, each person inputs data into an automated system, and the system's output causes the harm. No individual intends the harm, and each person's contribution is causally necessary but not sufficient for the result.

(4) If the deontological constraint applies only to individual intentional actions, then the organization as a whole can produce outcomes that any individual is forbidden to produce directly — for example, selecting one person to bear a severe burden so that hundreds are saved.

(5) But this makes the moral status of an outcome depend on whether it is produced through a centralized or a distributed causal structure. That looks like a distinction without a moral difference: the victim is harmed just as severely, and the organizational design was adopted precisely to achieve that result.

(6) So either the constraint applies also to collective agents — institutions, corporations, or states — or it is systematically evaded by restructuring agency.

(7) If it applies to collective agents, we need an account of collective moral agency robust enough to bear agent-relative constraints. But collective agents lack the unity of consciousness and intentionality that typically grounds such direct moral relations; treating them as bearers of deontic duties seems to require treating them as metaphysically real persons, which is implausible.

(8) Therefore deontology either collapses into consequentialism at the collective level — since the institution should maximize welfare without individual-level barriers — or it applies only to individuals and is rendered practically irrelevant in a world where most harmful actions are organizationally mediated.

I would strongly prefer to keep (1) and (2) rather than abandon them, and I do not want to be forced into accepting (7) unless it is genuinely unavoidable. Where does the argument slip? Is there a hidden equivocation between acting and contributing, or between causal distribution and moral permission? And what does rejecting the faulty step cost — do I have to concede that organizational design can manufacture moral exemptions, or do I need to develop a full theory of collective agency to block the move from (3) to (4)?
