I've been thinking about hard choices—the kind where two options don't just feel equally good, but seem genuinely incomparable in a deeper way. Most discussions I encounter frame the problem as a missing common metric: artistic fulfillment and financial security can't be weighed on the same scale. But I've started to suspect there's a different kind of incommensurability that gets overlooked, which I'll call directional incommensurability. Some values pull backward—fidelity, gratitude, loyalty to the person I've already become—while others pull forward: curiosity, transformation, openness to becoming someone new. The difficulty isn't only that there's no scale; it's that the very act of choosing one direction reshapes the self who must live with the other option. If I stay in a career out of loyalty to my past self, I'm not merely forgoing growth—I'm actively preserving an identity that wouldn't survive the choice to grow.

I've sketched notes for an essay that applies this framing to three decision-types: whether to remain in a long-term career that defines me, whether to forgive an old friend knowing it will reopen a wound I've already processed, and whether to have children recognizing that it will permanently restructure my priorities. In each case, I argue the agony comes not from weighing outcomes but from recognizing that the chooser after the decision is a different evaluator than the chooser before it.

I'd welcome comments on these notes. Specifically: Does the distinction between metric-less comparison and directional self-transformation actually hold up, or am I just redescribing the standard problem in new language? Are there concrete cases where the directional framing clearly explains the difficulty better than ordinary value pluralism? And is there a hidden contradiction in treating the pre-choice self as having legitimate authority over the decision while simultaneously claiming that choice transforms the very self that holds that authority? I am not asking for citations or a reading list—just an honest assessment of whether the idea is coherent, interesting, and worth developing further.
