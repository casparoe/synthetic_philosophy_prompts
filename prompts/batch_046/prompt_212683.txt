I'm a science journalist who writes about neuroscience and philosphy — bit of a niche beat, I know. Next Thursday I'm giving the opening remarks at a small workshop called 'When Pain Forgets to Complain: Asymbolia and the Value of Suffering'. The room will be split between clinical neurologists who've actually treated pain asymbolia patients and philosophers who work on affect and value, so I need the talk to land for both without either side feeling patronised.

The framing I want to structure everything around is a split between the 'register' and the 'adress' — by register I mean the bare sensory map that tells you where tissue damage is and how intense; by adress I mean the direct motivational command that makes you want it to stop immediately. In asymbolia the register works fine: patients can localise a burn, rate it eight out of ten, describe its quality. But the adress is silent — they don't pull away unless they decide to think about it.

This messes with the usual story that pain is bad just because it feels awful. If the awful-feeling part (the adress) can vanish while the intensity (the register) stays, then saying pain is intrumentally bad starts to look like we're blaming the messenger; but saying it's only instrumentally bad makes a silent alarm feel like a broken smoke detector rather than harm done to a person. I want the remarks to sit in that tension structurally — I shouldn't pretend we've solved whether suffering is the value-laden layer, and I shouldn't let the clinicians think the philosophers are just playing word games.

Please write it in British English, conversational like coffee-chat with smart friends rather than a lecture. One or two minor typos in your output are actually welcome — makes it feel authentic.

Structure the remarks in these sections, in this order:
1. The silent alarm (around 150 words) — open with the clinical picture, casual.
2. Register and adress (around 200 words) — lay out the split and why it complicates the idea that pain is intrinsically bad.
3. What we can't claim yet (around 100 words) — close by admitting both sides still have a problem, and invite the room to argue.
4. Why this framing (around 60 words) — quick meta-note on why I picked this split for a mixed audience.
