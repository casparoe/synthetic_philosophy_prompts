I'm currently taking an introductory ethics course, and after our unit on speciesism and animal moral status I found myself in a heated but friendly disagreement with my study group about exactly which capacities ground moral consideration. Rather than just repeating standard lines about equal moral consideration, I want to test whether my own judgments hang together or whether I'm being inconsistently guided by hidden biases. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases. First, I think it is seriously wrong to subject a conscious adult chimpanzee to painful experiments in order to save a human life, and I believe the wrongness remains even if we substitute a human being with comparable cognitive capacities who happens to lack language—so my judgment does not seem to turn on species membership alone. Second, I think it is permissible to swat a mosquito that is about to bite me, even if mosquitoes possess some minimal sensory experience; but I concede that if we discovered mosquitoes have a continuous, integrated subjective life comparable to ours, my judgment might shift. Third, I think factory farming pigs is wrong mainly because of the suffering involved, yet I feel far less certain about whether farming insects for protein is wrong if they genuinely lack nociception and future-oriented preferences—though I worry this makes my view suspiciously convenient. Fourth, I think clear-cutting an old-growth forest for a luxury resort is wrong, but I believe the primary wrongness concerns ecological harm and obligations to future humans rather than direct wrongs to individual plants or non-sentient organisms; still, if the forest housed sentient beings with rich mental lives, that introduces an independent moral layer. Fifth, I recently invented a hypothetical for our discussion: a bioengineered phenomenal shell that possesses all the neural architecture for pain but is designed so its experiences are never integrated into any self or memory. I feel genuinely unsure whether harming it is wrong, and this makes me wonder whether my other judgments depend on something like integrated phenomenal experience over time rather than on bare sentience.

Can these verdicts be captured by a single principle—perhaps one about the moral weight of temporally integrated consciousness, or about capacities for future-directed concern, or about relational obligations of care? Or must I choose between a simpler principle that overturns at least one of my judgments and a more complex, perhaps ad hoc account that saves them all? If coherence demands that I abandon one verdict, which should it be? Finally, please apply the resulting principle to a case I have not listed: an artificial system that behaves exactly like a suffering mammal but is verified to be a non-conscious simulation—would treating it well be morally required, morally neutral, or required only for indirect reasons?
