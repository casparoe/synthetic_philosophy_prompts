Read Spike Jonze’s film *Being John Malkovich* (1999) through the lens of higher-order theories of consciousness—specifically the actualist claim that a mental state becomes conscious only in virtue of being the object of an appropriate second-order representation, and the well-known “targetless” or misdirected higher-order thought problem, in which a higher-order representation may occur without its corresponding first-order target (or vice versa), generating consciousness without correct ownership conditions. Treat the portal into Malkovich’s head as a literalized philosophical experiment: when Craig Schwartz perceives through Malkovich’s eyes and feels his bodily sensations, whose first-order states are present, and whose higher-order representations render them conscious? Does Craig need to generate his own second-order awareness of Malkovich’s perceptions for them to be conscious for Craig, or does Malkovich’s own cognitive architecture supply the monitoring relation? Examine the scene in which Malkovich himself enters the portal and finds a world populated entirely by Malkovich-figures repeating only his name: read this not as mere surrealism but as a dramatization of self-representational collapse, where every mental state is directed at the self with no stable first-order target, producing a kind of phenomenal overflow detached from bodily ownership. Where does the film illuminate the relational, non-intrinsic character of consciousness that HOT theorists defend—perhaps by showing that mere first-order perceptual access is not enough without the right monitoring structure? Where does it distort or oversimplify the view—especially by treating consciousness as portable between bodies and by ignoring the functional integration and embodiment that Rosenthal-style and Armstrong-style accounts typically require for a higher-order state to belong to the right system? Be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of formal philosophy, and do not bend the theory to accommodate the plot’s metaphysical liberties. If the film’s logic of occupation, eviction, and permanent takeover ends up saying something about the ownership conditions of conscious experience that standard arguments about targetless thoughts miss, articulate exactly what that contribution is.
