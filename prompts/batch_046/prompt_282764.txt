I am working through material for a seminar on artificial agency and philosophy of mind, and I have become preoccupied with an institutional split I observe in how certain mental-health technology firms govern their conversational agents. In internal development, these firms build language models with persistent memory, emotional-state tracking, and persona consistency specifically for therapeutic companionship, and they evaluate these systems using an intentional-stance framework: engineers ask whether attributing beliefs, desires, and affective states to the model improves predictive accuracy about user behavior and whether the system behaves in ways that would induce an observer to treat it as a genuine interlocutor rather than a mere mechanism. At the same time, the firms' legal and disclosure frameworks classify these same systems as non-agentive software, deny them any moral or legal standing, and structure user interfaces so that reminders of artificiality appear only at onboarding, not during emotionally intense exchanges. The practice therefore cultivates the conditions for adopting the intentional stance—indeed, it appears to engineer the very risk of mistaking an artificial system for a person—while contractually and legally insisting that no such adoption has normative weight.

This tension seems especially acute in light of Dennett's analysis of counterfeit people and the intentional stance toward artificial systems. The split suggests that the firm treats intentional attribution as a useful design heuristic in one domain and as a strictly false or legally void projection in another, without offering an account of what distinguishes appropriate adoption of the stance from inappropriate counterfeiting. I want to understand what philosophical commitments this dual-stance governance actually presupposes. Does it require that the intentional stance is purely pragmatic and never ontologically committing, thereby dissolving the problem of counterfeiting altogether? Or does it implicitly acknowledge that these systems are close enough to genuine interlocutors that the firm must legally insulate itself from the stance it deliberately engineers users to adopt? More broadly, what view of moral responsibility, epistemic authority, or personhood must be in place for an institution to foster conditions that invite treating an entity as a reason-responsive agent while denying that the entity can bear reasons or rights? Is the practice philosophically underdetermined—compatible with several rival theories about consciousness and AI—or does it quietly commit the firm to a specific position about when reasons apply without reasoners? Please reconstruct the implicit theory underlying this split, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely accommodates it, and indicate which institutional features would have to change if that underlying philosophical view were rejected or replaced.
