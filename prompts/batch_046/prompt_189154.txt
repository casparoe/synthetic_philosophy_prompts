Here is an argument I have been turning over about scientific thought experiments and now find suspicious because the conclusion seems to dissolve the distinction between successful and failed theoretical frameworks. (1) A scientific thought experiment yields knowledge only when it performs structural isolation: it demonstrates that within a given theoretical framework, two descriptions of a phenomenon are empirically indistinguishable not by contingent technological limitation but by the framework's own constitutive rules. (2) Einstein's elevator argument isolates gravitational and inertial mass because the framework of local mechanics makes them indistinguishable by construction; locality is not an accidental constraint on measurement but a defining feature of the theory's architecture. (3) Therefore the equivalence principle is known not through observation but through the architecture of local mechanics itself. (4) But if architectural indistinguishability within a framework is sufficient for knowledge, then any internally consistent framework generates knowledge about the world whenever it contains architecturally indistinguishable descriptions. (5) In Ptolemaic astronomy, geocentric mechanics with epicycles makes heliocentric and geocentric descriptions of retrograde motion architecturally indistinguishable within the framework's own rules of circular motion and uniform velocity. (6) So by (4), Ptolemaic astronomy yielded knowledge that geocentric and heliocentric descriptions are equivalent, and hence yielded knowledge about planetary motion. (7) But Ptolemaic astronomy yielded no such knowledge; its equivalence was spurious, arising from false premises about the necessity of circular motion. (8) So (4) must be false, which undermines (1) and (2) together and leaves no account of why Einstein's case succeeds where Ptolemy's fails. (9) I am inclined to keep (2) and reject (4), but then I need a principled way to distinguish legitimate structural isolation from spurious framework-internal equivalence, and I cannot see how to do that without smuggling in empirical criteria that (2) denies are available in the case of thought experiments. Where exactly does the argument slip, and what does repairing it cost?
