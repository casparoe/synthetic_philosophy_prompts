I've been trying to extend Anscombe's distinction between intended killing and foreseen but unintended harm to autonomous weapons, and I've built an argument that seems to license total algorithmic war. Here are the steps.

(1) For Anscombe, the moral status of an act depends on what is directed in the agent's will: intentionally killing the innocent is always wrong, whereas harming them as a foreseen side-effect of a legitimate aim can be permissible if proportionate.

(2) In a fully autonomous system—where a commander sets strategic goals, engineers calibrate targeting classifiers, and the algorithm executes strikes—no single human will directs harm at non-combatants. The commander wills victory, the engineer wills accuracy, the algorithm wills pattern-matching.

(3) If intentionality is distributed across this functional architecture, then the collective act lacks a unified intention to kill civilians, even when civilian deaths occur. Each death is statistically foreseen by the system's risk model, but intended by no constituent agent.

(4) Therefore, under Anscombe's framework, such a system never commits murder, regardless of scale; at worst, it produces foreseen collateral damage, which is assessed by proportionality, not by an absolute prohibition.

(5) By the same reasoning, scaling the system globally—say, to suppress an entire enemy nation's military infrastructure—would not change the moral analysis. Millions of statistically foreseen but collectively unintended civilian deaths remain outside the scope of intentional killing.

(6) But that is intolerable: we should be able to say that a system designed to operate at that scale, knowing it will devastate civilian populations, has done something morally catastrophic even if no individual "meant" each death.

(7) So either Anscombe's distinction cannot apply to distributed or algorithmic agency, or one of the premises fails.

Where does this go wrong? I want most to preserve (1) and (6)—that intentional killing is uniquely wrong, and that large-scale algorithmic devastation should be condemnable—but I'm unsure whether the damage is done by (2)'s picture of distributed intention, by (3)'s move from individual absence to collective absence, or by (4)'s application of proportionality at massive scale. Which step should I reject, what does rejecting it cost for thinking about AI and moral responsibility, and where is my reasoning most vulnerable?

Please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to accept that (2)-(4) hold and (6) must be qualified, or conversely that (6) forces us to abandon (1) in collective contexts. Also indicate where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
