I'm a final-year philosophy undergraduate, and I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks—about eight minutes—at next week's interdisciplinary colloquium, "Embodied Thought: Philosophy, Nutrition, and Practice," hosted by my department. The room will be split between philosophy graduate students who have read Nietzsche closely and nutrition-science undergraduates who have not; I need the remarks to land for both groups without either feeling patronized or lost. The philosophical idea should do real structural work, not decorative work: I want to build the speech around Nietzsche's actual views on diet, digestion, climate, and bodily regimen—specifically his prescriptions for small meals, long walks, avoidance of alcohol and heavy foods, and careful attention to climate—treating them not as biographical quirks but as part of a single argument that health, for him, was never a medical state to be optimized but a philosophical achievement that protects the conditions for honest thinking. I was discussing this with a friend who worried the talk might collapse into modern wellness culture, and that concern should be answered inside the speech itself: I want an explicit argument that Nietzsche's rules are incommensurable with optimization culture because he treats the body not as a machine to be tuned for output but as a hierarchy of drives that must be ordered rather than maximized, so that "better health" can actually mean a more dangerous form of disorder if the ordering is wrong. The philosophers in the audience should hear accurate reference to his late writings—particularly the sections on diet and climate in Ecce Homo and the discussions of bodily decadence in Twilight of the Idols—without the talk becoming a citation-heavy lecture, and the science students should leave with a precise, non-caricatured sense of what he actually believed about food and environment. Please write the remarks. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus; do not direct the audience to specific editions; and make sure the speech resists the reduction of philosophy to lifestyle advice even as it takes bodily practice seriously.
