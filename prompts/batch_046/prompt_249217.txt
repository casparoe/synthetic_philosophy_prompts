In my seminar on Nyāya epistemology, our professor has offered verdicts on several belief-forming cases without committing to a unified theory. She held that a blind listener can know a sunset's color through a sighted friend's testimony; that a geologist can know of an underground river by inferring it from vegetation patterns; that a traveler can identify an unfamiliar species by comparing it to a known one; that a physician can postulate an unseen pathogen to explain symptoms and thereby know it; that a reader can accept an ancient historical claim from a text of unknown authorship provided its statements have been consistently corroborated; and that a subject can know a jar is absent in a dark room through non-apprehension. Next week we examine a case in which a researcher acquires a true, highly specific belief about protein folding solely from the output of a large language model that possesses no consciousness, no direct perceptual access to molecules, no biological understanding, and opaque internal weights, where experimental verification confirms the result but the system's reasoning remains inscrutable. Predict her verdict—would she treat this as śabda, as anumāna grounded in inductive verification of the system's reliability, as pratyakṣa of the displayed text coupled with inference, or as falling outside the pramāṇas entirely? Identify the two or three implicit rules—such as a classical account requiring an āpta with direct perception and compassion, a functional-reliability account, or a reductionist account that collapses testimony into perception-plus-inference—that fit her earlier rulings, say what each predicts about this case, give your best guess with reasoning, and name the single contrasting case—perhaps a conscious sage who lacks compassion but has direct experience, versus an unconscious system with perfect predictive accuracy—that would best settle which rule she is actually running.
