I've been preparing material for a workshop on suffering risks—specifically, the possibility that advanced artificial systems or large-scale digital processes could generate morally significant suffering, and whether this prospect should shape present-day priorities in AI development. Among philosophers and researchers I've spoken with, the debate has settled into what looks like an enduring stalemate: one side stresses structural analogies between biological and artificial information processing, arguing that if minds can be instantiated at scale then suffering could be instantiated at scale; the other side tends to treat the worry as conceptually premature, often appealing to the absence of phenomenal consciousness in current architectures or to the sheer speculative distance of any relevant empirical prediction. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not fundamentally about forecasts of AI capability, nor even mainly about the metaphysics of consciousness, but about what an adequate explanation of suffering has to include—specifically, whether suffering requires a form of embodied, temporally extended narrative vulnerability that artificial substrates structurally lack, or whether it can be fully characterized by functional and valence-related properties that could in principle be realized in silicon. Is that diagnosis correct?

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement's actual structure rather than a survey of positions: is it verbal or substantive; do the sides differ about facts, about which cases count as relevant data, about standards of evidence, or about starting intuitions that neither side can independently argue for; is a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work; are the sides answering different questions under the shared label of "s-risk"; is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is genuinely hard? Please support the diagnosis by engaging with what each side actually says, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis—whether a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no resolution should be expected and why. Where your analysis touches genuinely contested terrain, please separate what is relatively settled from what remains open among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
