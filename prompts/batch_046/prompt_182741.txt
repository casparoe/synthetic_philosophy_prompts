I just came out of a long, circular argument with a close friend about whether Malthus should be regarded as right but early — whether the absence of a global subsistence crisis after two centuries of exponential population growth falsifies his mechanism, or merely shows that its time horizon exceeds our current observational window. I am less interested in demographic data than in the conceptual logic of long-run prediction and theoretical rescue. Specifically, I want to understand the distinction between rescuing a theory by restricting its domain to pre-industrial agrarian conditions and rescuing it by treating technological progress as an exogenous shock that delays but does not disable the geometric-arithmetic gap. There is a related problem that my friend kept pressing: in physics, a superseded theory can sometimes be rehabilitated as a limiting case of a broader framework — Newtonian mechanics within general relativity — because the parameter space specifies exactly when the approximation holds. Malthus, however, seems to offer no analogous parameterization that determines precisely when the carrying-capacity constraint should bind and when technological change should be treated as an exception. Can you explain, at a conceptual level, whether this absence means that Malthusian predictions are structurally unfalsifiable, or whether there is a principled distinction between legitimate boundary-condition rescue and mere immunization? More generally, I want the intuition for why predictive theories with very long time scales face a distinctive falsification problem — one that is not just about waiting longer for the evidence — and whether the Malthus case tells us something about the logic of theoretical rescue itself rather than just about population dynamics.
