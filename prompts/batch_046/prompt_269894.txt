I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I got obsessed with the Liar after using "this sentence is false" as a metaphor in a draft and then realizing I don't actually understand the mechanics behind it. So here's my conceptual profile for a consistency check: I think truth is a substantive, mind-independent property — correspondence with reality, not just a linguistic gadget — but I also think the T-schema is basically analytic and captures the core of the concept. I think natural language can contain an unrestricted truth predicate; people say things like "everything she said was true" without any level-shifting, so Tarskian hierachy feels like solving the problem by changing the subject. I think the Liar is genuinely paradoxical, not just a pragamatic glitch or hidden ambiguity, and it reveals that our ordinary concept of truth is incoherent when pushed. But I also reject paraconsistent logic outright — contradictions can't be true, full stop — and I think gap theories like Kripke's miss the mark because the liar isn't "undefined" or gappy; it's determinatly false, since it's asserting its own falsity and that assertion fails. At the same time I think we should keep using "true" in science and everyday talk exactly as we do now, with no revisionary restictions, and I also think it's conceiveable that a language could have a fully unrestricted truth predicate without generating paradox, maybe through some syntactic constraint I haven't pinned down yet. Do these commitments actually hang together? Which pairs are in real tension versus just looking weird together? Rank the tensions by severity, say what'd have to give for each, and tell me the single revision that'd clean up the most mess — and please don't just list citations, I want the conceptual audit.
