Read Christopher Nolan's film Memento (2000) through the lens of the epistemological bootstrapping problem and the related worry about easy knowledge—specifically the circularity that arises when a belief-forming method is used to generate and justify its own outputs without independent warrant, producing substantive beliefs that seem to come too cheaply. Leonard Shelby's anterograde amnesia forces him to maintain his identity and purpose through an external mnemonic apparatus of tattoos, Polaroid photographs with handwritten captions, and notes to himself. Treat this apparatus as an epistemic framework rather than a mere plot device. Does Leonard actually know that John G. is the target he should kill, or does he merely bootstrap the authority of his own past inscriptions? Examine the scene in which he consults a Polaroid of Teddy and acts on the caption he himself wrote, the moment he decides to burn his wife's belongings based on a note whose origin he cannot verify, and the tattoo that reminds him about Sammy Jankis—asking in each case whether Leonard's belief revision is rational, circular, or something the standard philosophical models miss. Where does the film illuminate the distinction between having a warranted belief and merely acting on a record whose reliability is assumed? Where does it resist reduction to an epistemic parable—perhaps in the emotional and practical need for narrative continuity that seems to override evidential concerns? Conclude by saying what Memento adds to the philosophical debate that abstract arguments about bootstrapping and easy knowledge do not: does the fragmentation of memory show that practical identity is prior to epistemic justification, or that the boundary between knowing and merely proceeding on belief collapses when the self that would verify the belief is itself reconstructed?
