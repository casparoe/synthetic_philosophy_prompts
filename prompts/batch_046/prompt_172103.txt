I'm a voting member of the institutional review panel for critical-care resource allocation at a large regional hospital, and I need a formal memo to circulate before Thursday's meeting. We are revising Protocol 7-B, which governs ICU ventilator triage during sustained surge conditions. The protocol currently treats withholding initiation from a new admission and withdrawing support from an existing patient as ethically equivalent within its scoring algorithm, but a coalition of staff clinicians and family advocates has filed a formal objection. Their core claim is not about outcomes—mortality is identical in the cases they cite—but about the causal and agential structure of the act: withdrawal is an institutional intervention that directly terminates a life-sustaining process, they argue, whereas withholding allows an independent pathology to run its course without the hospital's direct causal contribution. They concede that triage is unavoidable, but they insist that if the board encodes withdrawal and withholding symmetrically, we erase a morally significant boundary that distinguishes killing from letting die, especially now that the protocol uses an automated default: ventilation is discontinued at hour seventy-two unless a physician actively overrides the system. They ask whether that default makes the institution the agent of death in a way that a passive non-admission decision does not.

The panel must recommend one of three concrete courses by Friday's board vote: (A) retain the equivalence and keep the automated protocol unchanged; (B) amend the protocol to prohibit algorithmic withdrawal of existing support while retaining withholding for new admissions, with a six-month sunset clause and explicit review triggers; or (C) suspend automated withdrawal entirely and require case-by-case panel review for any discontinuation, accepting the administrative burden and potential delay in bed turnover. The memo should be no more than three pages, written as a polished institutional document that a rationalist-minded committee member could circulate without embarrassment. It must include a clear recommendation with an implementation timeline, and it must treat the objectors' strongest point seriously—not by citing legal brain-death criteria or invoking institutional authority, but by analyzing whether an institutional default with an opt-out architecture genuinely transforms omission into action at the collective level. I want the reasoning to stay in the weeds: avoid the standard introductory trolley-problem framing, which I think flattens the institutional layer, and instead examine how distributed agency, counterfactual dependence, and the design of default rules interact with the doing/allowing distinction in a protocol that no single person fully controls. The memo should also be frank about the practical costs of each option, including staffing, family conflict, and the risk that asymmetry in the rules will produce worse aggregate outcomes, and it should say explicitly which consideration on the losing side remains genuinely weighty even if the recommendation goes the other way. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations; the board has no access to external literature and needs a self-contained analysis it can act on Thursday.
