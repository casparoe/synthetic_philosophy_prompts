I'm writing a novel about a disaster-planning bureacrat who starts reading Machiavelli, and it's made me notice something weird about how we actually run things. Every big organisation — governments, hospitals, even film studios — has these massive risk-modelling departments that try to forecast every shock. They spend millions on it. But when crisis actually occures — a pandemic, a supply collapse, whatever — the official post-mortems never ask whether the forecasting was sound. They ask whether the leader 'rose to the occassion' or 'lacked the stomach' for hard choices. The institutional design is treated like background scenery; all the moral weight lands on one person's ability to ride chaos. It's like we build a ship with no lifeboats and then judge the captain on whether he can swim.

So what philosophical commitments does that pattern presuppose about fortune and human agency? Does it assume Machiavelli's view that fortuna is a wild river you can only channel with personal virtù, and that structural safeguards are actually a kind of moral cowardice? Or does it presuppose something closer to determinism — that institutions are fixed and only individual will breaks through? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Is it consistent with a view where virtù is just institutional design by another name? And if we genuinely believed fortune could be structurally domesticated rather than heroically confronted, what would have to change in how we write reports, hire leaders, or split disaster budgets?

Also, is the practice philosophically underdetermined? Could the same praise-for-the-leader ritual fit with a purely consequentialist framework that just needs a decision-node, or does it strictly require virtue ethics where character is the primary unit of evaluation? Don't just list rivals — tell me which ones the practice genuinely rules out and which it merely tolerates. And please, no reading lists or citations; I'm a novelist, not a librarian.
