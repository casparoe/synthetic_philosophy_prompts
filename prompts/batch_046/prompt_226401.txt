I've been advising this tiny regional ethnographic museum — just me and two staffers, no conservator on payroll — and every Tuesday we have to decide whether to sign an acqusition form for some donated or loaned object where "original" status is totally murky. Like, a ceremonial mask was destroyed in the 70s and rebuilt in 2010 by descendants using the original molds and rituals, but zero of the old wood or fiber survives. Or there's a painting with flawless paper-trail provenance that was clearly done by a forger who copied the artist's brushwork. I need a reusable decision procedure my staff can actually run in about 20 minutes before they sign anything, since I can't be there every week and they're not philosophers.

Ground it in whatever philosophy of art actually knows about originals versus fakes — not just the usual autographic/allographic split, but the idea that being original involves more than material persistence; it involves some kind of entitlement to be treated as the source of value, which can survive material replacement if there's continuity of practice and recognition by the originating community. I'm especially playing with a three-way distinction I haven't seen named clearly in the literature: material persistence, practice continuity, and what I'm calling "response entitlement" — whether the relevant expert or originating community would treat engagement with this object as engagement with the original itself. The procedure should ask ordered questions, say what counts as sufficient answer at each step, include quick test sentences or checks they can apply, flag exactly where it stops with a hard no, and mark where it hands the call to me or the board because it's a values question not a fact question. Be honest about where it will misfire.

Then run it on three cases: (A) the rebuilt ceremonial mask with original molds and ritual authorization but no original material, where the community says "this is it, the old one died"; (B) a forged painting with perfect provenance paperwork but wrong response entitlement — experts wouldn't treat seeing it as seeing the artist's hand; and (C) a restored antique where 60% is replaced substrate but the rest is original and the workshop that did the repair was the maker's own. One of these should come out in a way I'll dislike — probably case A gets rejected for title/insurance even though I think it should count. Also say flat-out what the procedure can't do, especially when practice continuity and material persistence conflict and there's no community left to ask. And please, no bibliography or reading list — my staff wouldn't use them anyway. There's probably a typo or two here, sorry, I'm writing this between classes.
