I'm a retired chemist who just got into philosphy of mind, and I'm helping my neice grade papers for an intro course she's TAing. The exam asked: Illusionists deny there's any real qualitative what-it-is-likeness in experience. How do they deal with the feeling that our first-person reports are immediate and transparent? Must they treat all introspection as false, or can they preserve some first-person authority?

Here's one answer:
Illusionism, especially in Frankish's version, holds that phenomenal properties literally don't exist—no qualitative feel at all. But our brains build introspective representations that portray mental states as having these feels. The transparency intuition is just that these representations are self-targeting and don't feel mediated, like clear glass. So illusionists can keep first-person authority in a weak sense: we're accurate about what our representations present, but wrong only about whether those presentations correspond to real phenomenal properties. Frankish thinks this dissolves Chalmers's hard problem, because once you see the feel is just a representational format adopted for evolutionary reasons, you only need to explain why the brain uses that format, not why tissue produces consciousness. So introspective reports aren't false across the board; they're ontologically mistaken but epistemically reliable about their content.
