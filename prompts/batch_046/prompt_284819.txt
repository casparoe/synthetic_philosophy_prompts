I have been developing, in a graduate seminar on historical consciousness, a somewhat unorthodox extension of Nietzsche's tripartite schema — the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical modes of relating to the past — to the conditions of algorithmically mediated memory. My working thesis is that digital archiving and recommendation systems have not simply multiplied these modes but fused them into what I term recursive monumentalism: the antiquarian impulse is satisfied by the exhaustive preservation of every trace, yet retrieval is governed by a monumental logic that selects only those fragments capable of serving present identity-performance, whilst the critical capacity for deliberate forgetting and rupture is systematically disabled by the architecture of search. I take this fusion to explain the distinctive historical paralysis of contemporary culture — an obsessive curatorship of heritage coupled with an apparent inability to achieve genuine historical discontinuity.

If one grants this diagnosis, I should like to know what would genuinely count against it. Not merely that people still study history critically, or that some archives remain open-access, but rather what observations about the phenomenology of digital memory, what structural features of algorithmic curation, or what normative arguments about collective agency would have to obtain for the thesis to collapse? In particular, I am interested in whether the claim risks being qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance, by treating every apparent critical rupture as secretly monumental, or every instance of forgetting as merely an algorithmic omission rather than an active critical act — and, if so, whether that insulation is a defect in the position or an appropriate theoretical robustness. Could one point to concrete episodes in which a community achieved a genuinely critical relation to its past through, rather than despite, digital mediation? And are there rival conceptual frameworks — perhaps drawn from the philosophy of technology or from theories of collective memory — that describe the same phenomena without invoking the Nietzschean architecture at all, and that do so more parsimoniously?

Please treat the position straight: distinguish between evidence that would be merely awkward and evidence that would be decisive, say what defenders of the view ought themselves to concede as counting against it, and be willing to conclude that the position is either well-exposed or unduly protected. I am especially keen to avoid the impression that the thesis is merely metaphorical, so if there are formal or empirical results — for example, from studies of recommendation dynamics or from the sociology of commemorative practice — that bear directly on the possibility of critical history under conditions of total archiving, I should like those brought into view.
