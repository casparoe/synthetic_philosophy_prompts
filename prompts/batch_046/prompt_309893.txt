I'm delivering the closing remarks at the retirement symposium for my doctoral advisor, Professor Alistair Chen, who spent thirty-eight years in philosophy of science and logic. The room is split: about half are philosophers and historians of science who will catch every reference to the Vienna Circle, the other half are his family, former students from other disciplines, and university administrators who won't recognize a citation if it walked in with a name tag. I want the address to be structured around Moritz Schlick's distinction between a life organized by purpose (Zweck) and one that carries meaning (Sinn) — specifically the view that meaning is never found in achievement, legacy, or in being a means to some further end, but only in present, intrinsic engagement that Schlick analogized to play (Spiel). The philosophical framework should do real structural work rather than serve as ornament: I need to honor his career without framing it as valuable because of his publications, his trained students, or his institutional footprint, since on Schlick's account that would reduce his life to a chain of means and ends and thereby deny precisely what made it meaningful. At the same time, I must avoid telling his family anything false about eternal significance, about his watching over them, or about work having been a "calling" that transcends this life.

There's an additional layer I want woven in that will shape what can and can't be said. Schlick's essay suggests that professional adulthood is structurally hostile to meaning because it is organized entirely around purpose — grants, tenure lines, peer review, metrics — and that only youth and, paradoxically, retirement can recover the conditions for genuine play. So the speech must frame retirement not as a withdrawal from meaningful labor into emptiness, but as a return to the only state in which meaning was ever genuinely available. I need this to land for the experts without sounding like a seminar, and for the family without sounding like I'm dismissing the life he built. Please write a five- to seven-minute address that navigates this tension: it should sound like affection, not like a lecture on logical positivism, and it should leave both halves of the room feeling that something true was said about him rather than about philosophy.
