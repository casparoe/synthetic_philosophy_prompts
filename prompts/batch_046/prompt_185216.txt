I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, but I've been trying to apply the literature on bounded rationality to how we design training pipelines, and I ended up with an argument that feels like it proves too much. I'd like someone to diagnose where it slips.

(1) Satisficing—accepting the first option that clears an aspiration threshold rather than searching for the global best—is rational precisely when the expected cost of further search exceeds the expected gain from finding a better option.

(2) This is usually framed as a descriptive constraint on human cognition: we have limited memory, time, and computational bandwidth.

(3) But if rationality is normative—about what any agent ought to do given its actual resources—then the principle applies universally: whenever continued search is net-negative for an agent, that agent ought to satisfice, regardless of substrate.

(4) Modern machine-learning training is explicitly anti-satisficing. We use gradient descent across massive parameter spaces, scale compute to reduce loss globally, and treat local optima as failures to be engineered away.

(5) So either current AI architectures are systematically irrational by a universal norm of rationality, or satisficing is merely a descriptive quirk of biological agents and not a normative principle at all.

(6) One might reply that silicon-based systems face no genuine scarcity: computation is cheap, so their "search costs" are never net-negative. But this seems false in practice—FLOPs, energy, and wall-clock time are always finite, and we constantly stop training when marginal improvement doesn't justify marginal cost. In other words, we already satisfice at the infrastructure level.

(7) If we acknowledge that, then the contradiction in (5) returns with sharper teeth: we satisfice in how we build the system but demand global optimization inside the system, with no principled boundary explaining why one level should obey bounded rationality and the other shouldn't.

(8) The only remaining escape I see is to claim that an artificial agent's "goals" are defined by its objective function, and that objective function can mandate unbounded search regardless of resource cost. But then rationality becomes whatever the programmer writes down, and satisficing loses its critical force as an independent normative standard.

The conclusion I can't accept is that our entire optimization culture in ML is either conceptually irrational or resting on an arbitrary double standard about where scarcity counts. Where is the error—an equivocation between descriptive and normative satisficing, a scope mistake about what counts as an agent's resources, or something else? If I have to preserve one claim, I'd prefer to keep (1) intact. What does rejecting each of the other steps cost?
