Here's an argument my study partner cooked up about dialetheism and now i'm stuck. (1) Dialetheists accept that the liar sentence is both true and false, so there exists at least one true contradiction. (2) If there's no logical law blocking that contradiction, then consistency isn't a necessary condition for rational belief in general—it's just a contingent feature of well-behaved domains. (3) Now take any ordinary belief, say that Paris is in France. We can embed that claim in a self-referential loop (like a Curry-style construction) that forces it into contradiction with its negation. (4) So if dialetheism licenses the liar contradiction, it also licenses contradictions involving Paris and not-Paris. (5) That means a dialetheist should be rationaly permitted to believe both that Paris is in France and that it isn't. (6) But nobody—dialetheists included—thinks that's rational. (7) So either dialetheism collapses into total epistemic chaos, or it secretly relies on a hidden distinction between "good" contradictions (liar-type) and "bad" ones (empirical). (8) But that distinction looks ad hoc, since the dialetheist's whole point is that logic doesn't care about subject matter. (9) Therefore dialetheism is either self-defeating or arbitrary.

I feel like step (4) is where the sleight of hand is, but i can't quite name the mistake. Is it that embeding a claim in a Curry loop changes what we're actually talking about? Or is the problem earlier, in treating logical possibility as belief permission? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) if i can, but i'm not sure what i have to pay to save them. Which premiss do i give up, and what does giving it up cost?
