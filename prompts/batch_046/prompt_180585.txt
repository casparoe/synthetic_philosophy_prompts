You are a professional philosopher with expertise in development ethics and the political economy of famine; please take a moment to brainstorm the conceptual distinctions hidden in the steps below, think through what each rejection costs, and answer systematically rather than rushing to the obvious fix. Here is an argument I built for a seminar on Amartya Sen’s entitlement approach, and it now strikes me as suspicious because its conclusion seems to erase a distinction I want to preserve. (1) Sen demonstrates that the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine, and mortality during China’s Great Leap Forward all occurred without a sustained collapse in aggregate food availability; in each case, entitlement failures—whether through wartime inflation and speculation, colonial export policies, or bureaucratic misreporting and procurement—were sufficient to cause mass starvation. (2) If aggregate food availability can remain adequate while people starve, then the presence or absence of food in the physical environment is not the defining feature of a famine. (3) Therefore, “famine” should be defined not as a natural scarcity event but as a specific kind of distributive or institutional breakdown. (4) But any instance of starvation—say, an isolated village cut off by an avalanche with no state presence, or a hermit lost in a blizzard—can also be redescribed as an entitlement failure: the victim lacked the legal, economic, or social means to access available resources, or to be rescued. (5) So by the reasoning in (3), every starvation death is technically a famine, and since Sen treats famines as political crimes rather than natural disasters, every starvation death is a political crime. (6) This is clearly false: not every death from starvation is morally attributable to a government or institutional actor. (7) Hence Sen’s entitlement framework proves too much.

Which premise or step should I reject? If I must preserve the idea that the Bengal, Irish, and Great Leap cases were politically caused rather than purely natural, but I also want to maintain that some starvation deaths genuinely are natural disasters with no culpable agent, where exactly does the slide occur? Is there a hidden ambiguity in “entitlement failure” between systemic distributive collapse and individual resource inaccessibility, or a scope error in extending institutional analysis to non-institutional contexts? Please identify the weakest link, explain why it looked plausible, and note what I have to pay—conceptually and politically—if I give it up. I would especially like you to address the objection I am hoping nobody asks: whether my desire to protect the category of “natural disaster” is itself an ideological move to excuse states from responsibility for remote or marginalized populations whose entitlements they have already eroded.
