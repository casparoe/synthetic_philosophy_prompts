I'm drafting the final exam for an informal but conceptually rigorous philosophy-of-computation seminar that grew out of a long-running rationalist-forum thread. The participants have all worked through popular treatments of Gödelian self-reference, but I want an assessment that separates people who can actually manipulate the structure from those who can only summarize the usual narrative.

Please draft one 90-minute essay question, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks. The topic is the relationship between Hofstadter's notion of jumping out of the system—JOOTSing—and the MU-puzzle. The question must not be answerable by reciting a standard chapter summary.

To force genuine conceptual work, frame the question around the following injected hypothetical: a formal system called MU-Prime extends the original MU system with a single internal meta-rule that permits derivations describing the syntactic form of other derivations inside MU-Prime itself. There is no external interpreter, no change to the base axioms, and no semantic layer—only object-language syntax referring to object-language syntax. Ask students to determine whether this extension allows MU-Prime to jump out of itself in Hofstadter's sense, or whether it remains inside a larger but still closed syntactic loop. They must compare this explicitly to (a) the original MU-puzzle, in which deriving MU is impossible from within, and (b) a genuinely external intervention, such as a mathematician introducing a new axiom from outside.

The model answer should distinguish syntactic self-reference from semantic interpretation, explain why adding internal meta-rules does not automatically create an external vantage point, and address whether such a system can ever genuinely surprise itself in the way Hofstadter describes.

The marking scheme should identify five must-hit conceptual moves, assign marks to each (total 20), and include for each a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode—such as conflating meta-rules with axiom addition, treating syntactic description as semantic understanding, or assuming that any self-reference constitutes a jump outside.

Keep the exam fully self-contained: no required bibliography, no citation of specific editions or page numbers, and no dependence on obscure secondary literature. Paraphrase well-known formulations rather than quoting them; do not place anything in quotation marks that derives from a real published text. The tone should be polished and formal, suitable for an advanced exam, but should still read like a request from someone accustomed to spending too much time in conceptual-weeds forum threads.
