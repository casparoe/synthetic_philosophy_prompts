I'm writing a metaphysics dissertation chapter on personal identity and origins, and I'm trying to map a precise conceptual tension that I think is underexplored in the literature. The standard origin essentialist claim holds that you are essentially the product of the particular sperm and egg from which you actually developed; had a different gamete pair fused, the resulting person would not have been you. But this seems to sit uneasily with monozygotic twinning, in which a single zygote divides to produce two genetically identical, clearly distinct individuals. If both twins share the exact same gamete origin and the same initial cell, then gamete-level essentialism appears either to collapse them into one person or to leave it mysterious why only one of them is you.

One might try to rescue the view by retreating to what I will call first-division essentialism: you are essentially the product not merely of that gamete pair, but of the specific first mitotic division event that yielded your particular cell lineage. Yet this seems to relocate rather than resolve the difficulty. The first division is a causal process that produces two cells; if we individuate you by reference to that event, we appear to be individuating you partly by reference to the causal outcome that includes your twin, which looks circular if origin is supposed to explain why you are not identical to your twin.

Could you explain this conceptual knot with precision? Specifically: (1) whether monozygotic twinning genuinely undermines gamete-level origin essentialism, or whether the essentialist can consistently maintain that both twins share your origin while only one is you, and if so on what non-circular grounds; (2) whether first-division essentialism escapes the twinning problem or merely reproduces it at the level of cell-lineage individuation; and (3) whether there is a stable intermediate position—perhaps one that distinguishes genetic-origin essentialism from zygotic-origin essentialism—capable of avoiding both the twinning problem and the circularity worry, without collapsing into a purely psychological or narrative account of personal identity. I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between being essentially caused by an origin and being essentially identical to an originating substance can do the necessary work here.
