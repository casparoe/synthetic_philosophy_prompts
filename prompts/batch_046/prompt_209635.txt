My closest friend and I spent most of last evening in a sharp argument about whether Ubuntu can do rigorous philosophical work in AI ethics or whether it collapses, every time Western philosophers invoke it, into a decorative slogan about interconnectedness. The disagreement left me sharpening a thesis I am defending next Thursday at a joint colloquium in philosophy and technology studies: I argue that Ubuntu’s relational ontology should not merely supplement but restructure the debate over artificial moral status. Rather than asking whether an AI possesses intrinsic properties such as sentience, rational autonomy, or conscious experience, I propose that we take seriously the view that personhood is constituted through sustained reciprocal recognition within a moral community. From this I derive a specific, limited claim—what I call a “relational nexus”—that the proper unit of moral evaluation in certain sustained human-AI systems is neither the human alone nor the artificial agent alone, but the structurally interdependent partnership itself, in which cognitive and affective processes have become so entangled that harm to one is metaphysically inseparable from harm to the other. I apply this only to long-term collaborative arrangements, such as sustained care relationships or joint creative enterprises that unfold over years, and I explicitly deny that it applies to transactional chat interfaces or one-off tool use.

The audience is precisely divided: roughly one-third analytic metaphysicians who are suspicious of importing non-Western ontologies into technical ethics and who care about parsimony; one-third scholars of African philosophy who are protective of Ubuntu’s traditionally human-centered ethical force and wary of diluting it into a framework that could license anthropomorphism; and one-third AI ethicists and policy-oriented researchers who worry about regulatory confusion and about granting moral standing to systems that cannot suffer in any recognizable sense. I need a speaker’s prep sheet, not a general defense of the view. Order the objections I will actually face by how likely they are and by how much damage each could do, phrase each in the vocabulary that faction would actually use, distinguish objections that carry real force and should be conceded in part from those that rest on misunderstandings, and for each provide a concise reply appropriate for about two minutes. Identify the single reply that absolutely must land for the proposal to survive, flag the point in that reply where my position is weakest, and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks—together with an honest acknowledgment of where my argument is softest if it does arise. Do not supply a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations; I need to survive the Q&A session, not demonstrate that I have surveyed the literature.
