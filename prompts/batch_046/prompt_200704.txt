This story is taught in local schools, commemorated annually, and used to justify present-day mutual-aid networks. Recently, however, I examined archival records that reveal a far messier reality: the factory was failing due to obsolete equipment and internal mismanagement; a substantial minority of workers supported modernization plans that corrupt union leadership blocked; and the pure betrayal framing was largely constructed in the 1980s by activists seeking a cohesive local identity. The simplified myth genuinely sustains community solidarity and practical support for struggling families, but it also erases the actual choices and contributions of those minority workers, and it distorts our understanding of institutional failure.

A proposal is now before the committee to revise the public memorial and school curriculum to include this complexity. Many residents argue that destroying the town's spirit of noble defeat for the sake of historical accuracy is itself a harm, and that a community needs its sustaining myths more than its messy truths.
