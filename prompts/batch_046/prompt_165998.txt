Having retired from a career in experimental physics, I have lately been working through classical problems in philosophical theology with the same analytical habits I once applied to boundary-value problems. One issue that continues to trouble me is the omnipotence paradox in its standard formulation—whether an omnipotent being can create a stone so heavy that it cannot lift it. In physics we routinely dissolve apparent paradoxes once we recognize that a parameter is being treated as frame-independent when it is in fact relational: velocity, for instance, has no absolute value without a specified reference frame, and a Hamiltonian evolves with the system it governs. I wonder whether the omnipotence paradox commits an analogous category error by treating "the power to lift" as an intrinsic, monadic property of the agent rather than as a relational, state-dependent capacity that is calibrated to the modal conditions of the world in question. If omnipotence is formalized not as a static predicate—"can perform all logically possible acts"—but as a dynamic operator analogous to a Hamiltonian that updates its available eigenstates whenever the system changes, then the being's creation of the stone simply redefines the state-space of actions available to it. Its subsequent inability to lift the object would then be not a failure of power but a consistent feature of the updated dynamics, much as a measurement interaction in quantum mechanics does not represent a personal limitation of the observer but a reconfiguration of the observable eigenstates.

What I am trying to understand, then, is whether the standard theological resolution—that omnipotence necessarily excludes logical contradictions—is merely a special case of this more general point about state-dependent modal capacities, or whether it identifies a genuinely distinct logical feature of self-reference that has no counterpart in dynamical systems. Specifically, if the being creates the stone and thereby updates the boundary conditions under which its power is exercised, does this not dissolve the paradox without invoking any special exemption for logical impossibility? Or does the physical analogy obscure the fact that "creating an unliftable stone" involves a direct contradiction in the description of the agent's capacities within a single world-state, rather than a lawful transition between two compatible state-spaces? I would appreciate an explanation of whether this Hamiltonian framing is a legitimate reconceptualization of omnipotence or a confusion that improperly imports the logic of physical state-evolution into a domain governed by modal coherence rather than dynamical law.
