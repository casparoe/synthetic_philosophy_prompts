I serve as the senior review officer for a comparative evolutionary biology unit that evaluates proposed adaptive explanations for morphological and behavioral traits in wild and captive-bred populations. Our reviewers have formal biological training but no background in philosophy of science, and we make decisions under time pressure with a single senior sign-off required. We have been criticized for adaptationist overreach—treating every observable feature as an optimal solution to a selective problem—while also missing genuine cases of exaptation in which a spandrel has been co-opted. I need a reusable decision procedure they can apply repeatedly to any submitted proposal.

The procedure should be grounded in the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique and should distinguish, in strict order: (1) direct adaptations, (2) exaptations in which a non-adaptive spandrel has been co-opted into functional integration, (3) structural or developmental spandrels that remain architecturally necessary but serve no current selective function, and (4) artifacts of captivity, measurement, or phylogenetic inertia. For each stage, specify the exact questions reviewers must answer, what counts as sufficient evidence, and the point at which the procedure stops with a negative verdict. Mark explicitly where judgment cannot be eliminated—especially where distinguishing an exaptation from a primary adaptation requires inferring selective history rather than observing present function.

The procedure must include three practical tests reviewers can run without specialized statistical software: a load-bearing removal test (would the organism’s viability or development collapse if the trait were absent?), a phylogenetic inertia check (does the trait persist in sister lineages lacking the proposed selective pressure?), and a developmental pathway independence check (can the trait be traced to a growth mechanism that serves an unrelated structural role?). Provide a brief worked example for each of the four categories above, using concrete but hypothetical traits—specifically, a behavioral pattern in a reintroduced mammal, a morphological feature in a captive-bred fish, and a sensory structure in a population subjected to artificial selection.

Crucially, include one case where the procedure yields a verdict that conflicts with the intuitive adaptive story: a trait that appears functionally perfect for its current environment but is classified as a load-bearing spandrel because its architecture originates as a necessary byproduct of an unrelated developmental constraint, with current functional integration being secondary. Then state the procedure’s hard limits: what it cannot determine, when it requires hand-off to an evolutionary geneticist or to ethics review (for instance, when a spandrel classification would justify discontinuing a welfare intervention), and under what conditions it will systematically misfire—such as when convergent evolution produces architecture that mimics spandrel origin. Deliver this as a flowchart in words, not as an essay, with clear stop points and sign-off conditions.
