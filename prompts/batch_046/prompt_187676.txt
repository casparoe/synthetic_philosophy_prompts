I facilitate an upper-division philosophy of social epistemology seminar with 16 students, meeting once for 75 minutes. Design a runnable, technology-free classroom simulation that makes participants experience—before any concept is named—how individually rational credibility-weighting, applied under conditions of correlated source visibility and a scarce attention budget, produces collective epistemic closure that is structurally different from mere informational isolation.

Use a fictional but realistic local-policy case (for example, whether a zoning change caused a decline in small-business revenue). Give each student an initial “reliability profile” that assigns mildly—never extremely—higher baseline credibility to two designated “local” sources and mildly lower credibility to two “outside” sources. Give each student an attention budget of three “processing slots” per round. Circulate eight evidence cards whose content is genuinely ambiguous in valence: each card can be read as supporting either side depending on framing, and no card is self-interpreting.

Structure three rounds with these mechanics. Round 1: students exchange cards only within assigned pairs, so information neighborhoods form organically. Round 2: all cards move to a visible central pool, but before a student can cite a card in discussion they must spend one attention slot to “verify” its source; verification reveals that others are also selectively checking sources, and students quickly notice they are skipping checks on cards from low-credibility sources even when those cards are physically present. Round 3: students cast a collective judgment vote and simultaneously score “alignment points” for matching the emerging factional consensus, plus “accuracy points” tied to the true state of the case, which is revealed only after voting.

The rules and payoffs should be arranged so participants run into the problem themselves: by Round 2 they should discover that they are not merely missing contrary evidence (bubble dynamics) but are actively discounting visible outside evidence because verifying it costs attention they prefer to spend defending their own position; by Round 3 they should have formed a self-sealing credibility hierarchy without ever having been instructed to distrust outsiders.

Specify the physical layout, materials list (cards, tokens, score sheets), the exact payoff scheme using only non-monetary points, the round-by-round timeline with speaking rules, and the role of the facilitator (who should stay silent during play except to enforce mechanics).

Then provide a debrief that maps Round 1 onto informational isolation, Round 2 onto credibility cascades and attention-driven filtering, and Round 3 onto echo-chamber exclusion; identify the precise moment where rational individual updating tips into collective irrationality; and be frank about which real-world feature—such as institutional gatekeeping, identity-protective cognition, or long-term social punishment for dissent—the classroom version cannot reproduce and you must supply verbally.

Finally, predict what will go wrong the first time you run it (for instance, premature factional convergence in Round 1 that collapses ambiguity too quickly, or students gaming the alignment points) and propose a concrete facilitator adjustment (for example, injecting a deliberately ambiguous “disruptor” card at the start of Round 2). Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; the deliverable should stand entirely on its own rules, materials, and analysis.
