I am designing a graduate seminar on moral philosophy and social ontology, and I want to use Dostoevsky’s The Brothers Karamazov—specifically Ivan’s arguments against divine justice and the Grand Inquisitor episode—to stage a debate about whether religious belief produces genuinely better moral agents, or merely sustains a different form of social coordination. The novel appears to separate personal moral motivation, as seen in Alyosha’s compassion and Dmitri’s guilt, from institutional moral order, embodied by the Church’s authority and the Inquisitor’s management of human freedom. I want to ask whether secular societies can replicate the coordination function—maintaining trust and normative expectation—without the metaphysical commitments Ivan rejects, and what “more moral” would mean if it refers to social sustainability rather than individual virtue. Please read the novel through this lens: identify scenes that dramatize the distinction between being morally improved and being morally governed, indicate where the text suggests secular trust is viable or necessarily collapses, and note where the literary form—Dostoevsky’s dialogue, narrative irony, and strategic silences—resists reduction to philosophical argument. Which character choices should I direct students to, and what misreading should I warn them against? I am particularly interested in what the novel adds to the conceptual debate about sustaining moral order in post-religious societies that purely argumentative treatments miss.
