As a retired physicist who spent three decades modeling statistical and complex systems and has only recently begun reading moral philosophy seriously, I find the so-called inefficacy objection in collective harm cases conceptually troubling, yet my own intuitions about concrete situations seem to resist any neat unification. I should like to know whether there is a defensible principle behind them, or whether some of my judgments must be abandoned.

Here are five verdicts I hold with some confidence.

First, in a national election with well over one hundred million voters, I believe I have a genuine moral obligation to vote for a climate-protective candidate, despite being virtually certain that my single ballot will not determine the outcome.

Second, I have adopted strict vegetarianism, knowing that my individual grocery purchases do not alter factory-farm production schedules because supply contracts are fixed months in advance. I regard this dietary abstention as morally required rather than merely supererogatory.

Third, I drive an efficient hybrid vehicle about twenty miles each day for work that I consider essential. The aggregate emissions of such driving contribute to climate harm, yet I do not feel that I violate a moral duty by continuing, provided I also support institutional changes such as public-transit expansion.

Fourth, I recently refused to purchase goods from a specific corporation that actively lobbies against carbon regulation, fully aware that my personal boycott has no measurable impact on its revenue. I regard this refusal as admirable but not obligatory.

Fifth, I regularly contribute measurements to a local climate-monitoring citizen-science network that has no direct emission-reducing effect, and I consider this contribution genuinely valuable. By contrast, I see little point in signing online petitions for climate action, despite the fact that both activities are causally inefficacious at the individual level.

What puzzles me is whether these verdicts depend on a distinction I can only gesture at: perhaps between actions embedded in necessary institutional roles, actions that express a correct normative stance, and actions that participate in what I would call a configurational rather than purely aggregative harm structure. The tension is sharpest between the citizen-science contribution and the petition signature—both are symbolically meaningful and causally negligible, yet one strikes me as worthwhile and the other as empty.

Is there a single, precise principle—one that does not merely list the cases—that can accommodate all five verdicts? If such a principle exists, please state it exactly, explain which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts for a situation I have not mentioned: an individual investor who divests from fossil-fuel equities knowing that the divested shares will be immediately purchased by other buyers with no change in corporate behavior. If no single principle can save all five judgments, then please identify which verdict is the outlier that should be overturned, and defend that choice.

Finally, please tell me explicitly what argument, empirical finding, or conceptual distinction would cause you to revise or reject the principle you propose, and indicate precisely where in your reasoning the failure is most likely to occur.
