Since a recent medical prognosis made the abstract problem of inevitability suddenly intimate, I have been returning to the logical-fatalist reading of Death in Damascus: the argument that if a particular death is truly predicted now, then no present flight can alter it, not because anyone enforces the outcome, but because the fixity of past truth and the principle of bivalence seem to close off alternatives. I want to close a small philosophical salon—attended by both specialists and friends—with a villanelle that treats this not as fairy-tale but as logical structure. Use the two required refrains: Refrain A, opening the poem and returning on lines 6, 12, and 18, should state that the appointment is already inscribed; Refrain B, closing the opening tercet and returning on lines 9, 15, and 19, should state that every road taken to avoid it is the road that fulfills it. Through the five tercets and closing quatrain, let these refrains shift valence—first as dread, then as rationalization, then as resignation—so that the form's inevitable convergence in the final couplet enacts the very closure the argument describes. Keep the strict villanelle form—nineteen lines, only two rhymes, the refrains rotating as specified—but allow the meter to breathe. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the fatalist argument recognizes the machinery—future contingency negated not by causal force but by prior truth—while remaining accessible to listeners who have never encountered Damascus. End without resolving whether freedom survives, and without either sermonizing on acceptance or shrugging at destiny.
