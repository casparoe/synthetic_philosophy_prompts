I sit on my university's Committee on Human Subjects Research in Perception and Cognition, and we have received a protocol proposal from the Department of Psychology that I find philosophically treacherous. The study uses color-phi stimuli—presenting a green dot at position A followed, after a brief interval, by a red dot at position B—to investigate what the researchers call "postdictive consolidation of experiential reports." Participants provide an immediate verbal report of what they saw, then, after a 400-millisecond delay during which the actual stimulus sequence is revealed to them, are invited to "update" that initial report. Only the revised report enters the official dataset; the original is sequestered in a separate file that the team describes as methodologically irrelevant but does not destroy.

The philosophical stakes turn on whether this procedure treats conscious experience as genuinely revisable, and if so, whether the revision is Orwellian—actively rewriting the record of what was experienced to match the later narrative—or Stalinesque—leaving the original report intact but suppressing it from the authoritative archive, like a photograph airbrushed from public display while the negative remains in a vault. This distinction bears directly on the timing of experience: if the conscious perception of motion and color change is genuinely constituted only after the second stimulus has been processed, then the updated report might be more accurate than the initial one. But if the initial report captures a real, if fragmentary, experiential state that is later overwritten rather than refined, then the protocol risks systematically substituting post-hoc construction for first-person data, treating perception as if it were always already subject to editorial revision.

Half the committee regards this as routine methodological refinement—participants are merely correcting errors once they possess full information, comparable to allowing a witness to revise testimony after viewing replay footage. The other half, including me, worries that institutionalizing a separation between "processed" and "unprocessed" reports does not solve the Stalinesque problem but formalizes it, embedding a presumption that the brain's postdictive assembly is the true experience and the immediate report is disposable noise. I need a short written opinion—no more than two pages—to circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend whether we approve the protocol, approve it with conditions, or reject it outright, with a concrete timeframe for any required modifications. The memo should engage the "standard refinement" position seriously rather than dismissing it, and it should address specifically whether the archive's two-file structure genuinely protects the original experiential record or merely buries it.
