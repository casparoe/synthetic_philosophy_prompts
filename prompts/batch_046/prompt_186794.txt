As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have a working familiarity with distributed-systems theory—particularly Byzantine fault tolerance, gossip protocols, and the consistency-availability-partition trade-off—though I am not a specialist. I want you to restate the debate over policing—reform, defunding, or abolition of the state's monopoly on legitimate violence—using exclusively the vocabulary of distributed consensus engineering. Map reform onto hardening a centralised validator set without removing it; map defunding onto reducing resource allocation to that set while preserving its authority; and map abolition onto replacing it entirely with decentralised gossip protocols for conflict resolution. In particular, treat the abolitionist claim that state violence is structurally irredeemable as analogous to an impossibility result in asynchronous networks with faulty nodes and malicious behaviour. Then provide a residue report: which elements of the three positions—such as the historical contingency of policing, the affective experience of safety without armed enforcement, or the claim that reform always preserves the core validator—translate cleanly into distributed-systems concepts; which require distorting either the political claim or the engineering concept; and whether any part of the argument, especially around sovereignty or lived experience, has no counterpart whatsoever in fault-tolerance theory. Do not supply citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I need an answer that stands on its own without external lookup. Finally, analyse whether this framing illuminates something about policing that conventional sociological vocabulary obscures, or whether the unmapped residue shows the analogy is merely decorative.
