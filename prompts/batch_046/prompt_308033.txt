Below is a draft outline for a short philosophical essay I am preparing. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want conceptual feedback on the argument itself, including whether I have misrepresented any well-known positions and whether the central analogy holds up.

The essay examines Erwin Schrödinger's sympathy for Vedantic non-dualism, particularly the claim that the multiplicity of individual minds is an illusion produced by the same kind of spatial separation that distinguishes otherwise identical atoms in a crystal lattice. Schrödinger suggests that there is only one consciousness, not a plurality.

The draft then introduces a tension that Schrödinger does not fully resolve. If consciousness is genuinely non-indexical—there is no this consciousness distinct from that one—then ordinary first-person reports seem to presuppose a unique subject. To address this, the essay proposes what it calls a decentered indexical framework. On this view, each embodied perspective functions not as a separate mind but as a modal boundary condition on the single consciousness, comparable in structure to the way Schrödinger treated the wave function as describing one physical reality under different boundary conditions. The first-person pronoun, on this account, picks out a local excitation rather than an ontologically distinct bearer.

To make the framework more concrete, the essay injects an analogy drawn from quantum mechanics—though strictly as a philosophical metaphor, not a physical claim. Just as a quantum system in superposition describes one reality in multiple potential states that become definite upon interaction with an environment, the unified consciousness might be described as having multiple definite perspectives that are not separate minds but decohered modes of the same underlying reality. The essay calls this modal persistence across perspectives and uses it to argue that intersubjectivity is preserved: other people are not illusions, but are rather other decohered modes of the same consciousness, with no single mode enjoying metaphysical priority.

The essay concludes by asking whether this solves the problem or merely renames it. Specifically, it asks whether recognizing others as modal aspects of one's own consciousness collapses into a form of epistemic solipsism that Schrödinger explicitly rejected, or whether the crystal-lattice analogy—where no atom is the true atom—successfully blocks the privileging of any one perspective.

Please read through the outline and offer detailed comments. In particular: (1) Is the interpretation of Schrödinger's Vedanta and the crystal-lattice analogy accurate, or does it distort his view? (2) Does the decentered indexical framework genuinely resolve the tension between non-dualism and first-person language, or does it smuggle dualism back in under a different name? (3) Is the analogy to decoherence philosophically illuminating, or does it stretch Schrödinger's physics beyond recognition? (4) What, if anything, is the strongest objection the essay is missing—whether from Vedanta scholarship, philosophy of mind, or the physics-philosophy interface?
