I'm preparing a feature on historical arguments for epistemic autonomy, and I've been working through Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz's 1691 letter, Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz. In one passage, she answers objections to her study of secular subjects by contending that the rational faculty was given to women no less than to men, and that suppressing its exercise frustrates the purpose of creation itself. She further distinguishes knowledge mediated through institutional authorization—granted by those who hold teaching office—from knowledge pursued through direct reading and reflection, noting that many who claim authority to restrict study have never themselves engaged with the texts they condemn. She closes by stating that she writes not to instruct others but to understand for herself.

I'm trying to parse the precise philosophical architecture of this move. Is she advancing a natural-right claim (reason implies its own exercise), an argument from silence (no explicit prohibition exists), or a critique of credentialism that anticipates modern distinctions between epistemic authority and institutional power? And does her framing of writing as self-education rather than pedagogy strengthen her defense, or does it risk conceding that women should not hold public teaching roles? I'd appreciate help working through the logic of the passage, particularly how her distinction between learning and teaching functions within the broader structure of her defense of women's study.
