As a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years auditing philosophy of education seminars, I am preparing a ninety-minute session for a graduate reading group of about ten to twelve participants, seated in a circle with only paper, colored cards, a shared whiteboard, and a single large printed image of an ambiguous natural phenomenon—say, an anomalous crystal growth pattern with incomplete measurements. I want them to feel, not merely discuss, Alfred North Whitehead’s account of education as a rhythmic process—romance, precision, and generalization—and especially the danger of inert ideas, knowledge that has been received but never actively utilized because the rhythm has been broken or inverted. Please design a runnable in-class simulation with explicit roles and small stakes, not a lecture: some participants begin as discoverers who must propose speculative explanations for the phenomenon using only vivid description and intuition; others become assessors equipped with a rigid taxonomic grid and a payoff scheme—perhaps points that determine who must defend a classification, with collective penalties if the group forces an outlier into the scheme. The rules should then require a third rotation in which all participants apply their refined categories to a new case that violates every boundary they established, so that the group physically experiences generalization feeding back to destroy the initial romance rather than completing it. The idea must show up in what happens: participants should encounter the sterility of premature precision and the failure of forced generalization before anyone names Whitehead’s stages. Include a debrief that maps each phase onto the rhythm, identifies the precise moment when precision becomes sterilization rather than clarification, states what will predictably fail the first time—likely that participants enjoy the bureaucratic assessor role too much or that the discoverers never fully commit to romance because they anticipate assessment—and be frank about which feature of the view this cannot reproduce, namely the months-long temporal unfolding of a genuine educational rhythm, which I as facilitator must supply. If my framing is confused—particularly if Whitehead does not treat generalization as potentially destructive to romance, or if I have wrongly cast the rhythm as strictly sequential rather than interpenetrating—please say so plainly and correct the premise rather than designing around a misconception.
