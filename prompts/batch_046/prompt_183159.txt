Write the opening remarks—approximately six minutes, ceremonial but intellectually sharp—for a workshop titled "Consciousness: The Meta-Problem and Its Discontents," convened to honor the retirement of a senior researcher who has worked across neuroscience and philosophy of mind. The audience is split: experimentalists and cognitive scientists who treat consciousness as an empirical target, and philosophers who regard the hard problem as conceptually resistant. Your remarks must set up the day's discussion by clearly distinguishing the hard problem (why physical processes are accompanied by subjective experience) from the meta-problem (why we judge that explanation to be especially elusive), and then use that distinction to diagnose the persistent miscommunication between the two groups—showing that they often share premises but diverge in what they take explanation to require. To avoid generic framing, incorporate a specific conceptual angle: treat first-person phenomenological reports as performative utterances in the speech-act tradition—acts that establish epistemic authority or mark conceptual limits rather than transparently describing inner states. This should structurally constrain your remarks: you must not resolve the dispute in favor of either camp, you must not treat phenomenological disagreement as reducible to empirical prediction, and you must not imply that neuroscience alone dissolves the meta-problem. The philosophical idea should shape the rhetoric—what can be affirmed, what must be held in abeyance, and how the speaker addresses both halves of the room—rather than serve as ornament. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or references to specific texts that listeners should consult; write so that the speech stands on its own reasoning.
