Analyze the 1976 United States national swine flu immunization campaign through the lens of Nelson Goodman’s new riddle of induction, treating the public-health decision as a case of predicate projection across a hidden temporal boundary. The episode is familiar in outline: after an outbreak of swine flu at Fort Dix and in light of the 1918 pandemic, federal authorities projected lethal pandemic potential and launched mass vaccination, only to halt the program when the predicted pandemic failed to appear and reports of vaccine-associated Guillain-Barré syndrome accumulated. Your task is not to rehearse the epidemiology but to examine the cognitive structure of the projection itself. First, identify the specific predictive predicates the authorities treated as projectible—for example, properties tied to viral strain, transmission dynamics, or population susceptibility—and map them onto Goodman’s framework: what counts as the observed sample before the implicit cutoff, and what disjunctive “grue-like” counterpart (say, a property that holds for observed outbreaks before the vaccination threshold but reverses or dissipates afterward) could have been constructed instead. Second, evaluate whether Goodman’s notion of entrenchment explains why the 1918 analogy was privileged over competing projections, and whether institutional urgency and political pressure constitute external distortions or are themselves part of what determines entrenchment in policy contexts. Third, consider whether the episode is best read as confirming Goodman’s point that induction is a matter of predicate choice rather than logical inference, or whether it instead exposes a gap in the theory—namely, that real-world predictions involve action under uncertainty where the cost of false positives and false negatives is asymmetric, a factor Goodman’s logical analysis does not capture. Finally, say what the case reveals about the limits of applying the grue problem outside philosophy of science: does the presence of decision costs and organizational incentives mean that the riddle is misapplied here, or does it show that every practical induction faces a structurally similar problem of hidden disjunction?
