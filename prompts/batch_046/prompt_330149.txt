I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, and I have been tracking a persistent impasse in debates over whether evolutionary and neuroscientific accounts of culture and cognition provide Dennett-style cranes—bottom-up explanations that build complexity without miracles—or instead represent greedy reductionism that eliminates the autonomy of higher-level sciences. The empirical common ground is substantial: researchers agree, for instance, that dopaminergic reward prediction errors modulate moral judgment, and that gene-culture coevolution shapes normative practices. Yet interpretive divergence is total. One camp reads these mechanisms as constitutive explanations that preserve the autonomy of the phenomenon in question; the other reads the same mechanisms as eliminative, showing that the phenomenon is nothing but its biological substrate. What makes the stall peculiar is a recurrent rhetorical structure I have not seen adequately named in the literature on explanation and reduction. Each side simultaneously operates with two explanatory registers—mechanistic decomposition and normative or functional autonomy—and switches between them defensively. The reductionist appeals to mechanistic vocabulary when attacking autonomy, but then invokes constitution or implementation when accused of elimination. The anti-reductionist demands irreducible autonomy when resisting reduction, yet describes that autonomy in richly functional, almost teleological terms that borrow the very biological vocabulary they reject. This is not mere equivocation on the word explanation; it feels as though both sides are answering different questions—one about compositional mechanisms, one about the conditions of normative authority—while insisting they address a single shared question.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is substantive in ontology but disguised as a verbal dispute about reduction, with the hidden work being done by incompatible standards of explanatory adequacy: predictive unification versus the preservation of intentional content. But I want a sharper diagnosis. Is this primarily a verbal disagreement masked by strategic register-switching, a substantive ontological dispute that has been mislocated onto the concept of explanation, or a case of two disciplines answering different questions under one heading? What exactly is the role of this double-register maneuver in sustaining the impasse—does it protect each side from refutation, or does it reveal that the framing itself is ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal distinction between levels of explanation, an empirical result bearing on downward causation, a reconceptualization that abandons the crane and skyhook framing altogether, or an acknowledgment that no further resolution should be expected and why?
