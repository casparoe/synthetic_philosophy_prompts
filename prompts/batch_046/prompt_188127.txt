I retired from experimental physics about five years ago and have since developed an intense, somewhat amateur interest in the philosophy of economics, which I discuss regularly with two former colleagues who have turned to political theory. Last month our conversation turned to the socialist calculation debate, and I found myself dissatisfied with the standard framing in which Oskar Lange's trial-and-error pricing mechanism is presented as a decisive answer to the objections raised by Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek.

Please argue for the following position: Lange's proposal does not solve the knowledge problem but transforms it into a different kind of problem entirely. Specifically, contend that algorithmic adjustment of prices in response to surplus and shortage signals is not an exercise in rational economic calculation but rather operates as an evolutionary search through a high-dimensional space of possible allocations. The selection pressure—the quantitative signal of surplus versus shortage—functions as a lossy compression of the very local, contextual, and often tacit information that Hayek argued was distributed among individual actors and therefore irreplaceable by any centralized procedure. Moreover, because this evolutionary trajectory unfolds without distributed ownership, individual risk-bearing, or entrepreneurial discovery, there is no internal mechanism that aligns the direction of the search with human preference satisfaction or welfare; the system selects for allocation patterns that survive the signal, not for outcomes that people genuinely value. Keep the argument conceptual and self-contained: do not provide bibliographies, cite specific texts, or invoke modern computational analogies unless they genuinely illuminate the philosophical structure. I am particularly interested in whether this evolutionary reframing exposes a normative gap that the original debate missed, and whether it undermines the claim that market socialism can retain the epistemic virtues of actual markets while eliminating private property.
