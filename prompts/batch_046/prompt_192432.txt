Consider the following argument, which I developed for a philosophy-of-science seminar. Each premise seems defensible on its own, yet the conclusion strikes me as self-defeating.

(1) A claim belongs to science rather than to pseudoscience only if it is empirically falsifiable—that is, only if there exists some possible observation that would genuinely count against it.

(2) Because of the Duhem-Quine thesis, any claim can be shielded from a recalcitrant observation by revising auxiliary hypotheses, calibration procedures, or background conditions rather than abandoning the claim itself.

(3) Consequently, for every scientific claim and for every possible observation, a sufficiently resourceful defender can always construct some revision that preserves the claim intact.

(4) If a claim can always be preserved in this manner, then there is no observation that genuinely counts against it.

(5) Therefore, by (1) and (4), no scientific claim is actually falsifiable, and hence nothing is genuinely scientific.

(6) Yet evolutionary biology, quantum mechanics, and climate science are plainly scientific.

(7) So (1) is false, and falsifiability fails as a demarcation criterion.

The reasoning looks valid, and (2) is widely accepted in the literature, but the conclusion is absurd. I would strongly prefer to keep (1) and (6). Which step should be rejected—whether through a hidden scope ambiguity, a modal slide between possibility and actuality, or an unacknowledged cost embedded in (3)—and why did that step appear sound? Please also price the alternatives: what does giving up that particular step require me to concede about scientific risk, about the testing of theories in isolation, or about the boundary between legitimate revision and ad hoc rescue?
