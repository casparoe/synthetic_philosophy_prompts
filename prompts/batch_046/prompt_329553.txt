My colleague, who works on moral responsibility, has shared her intuitive verdicts across several pairs of cases. On result luck: two drivers are equally negligent—both intoxicated and speeding—but one happens to strike and kill a pedestrian who steps out unexpectedly, while the other swerves into an empty lane and harms no one. She judges them equally blameworthy; the fatal outcome falls outside moral assessment. On circumstantial luck: two people face an identical opportunity to embezzle; one is financially secure and refuses, the other is destitute and takes the money to pay for a child's emergency surgery. She holds the secure person praiseworthy and the destitute one fully blameworthy, granting no mitigation for the economic pressure. On constitutive luck: one person is born with a congenital neurological condition that eliminates all capacity to understand harm and assaults someone without comprehension; another is raised in an environment that systematically suppresses empathy and rewards cruelty, yet retains full cognitive understanding, and commits an identical assault. She judges the first person not blameworthy—there is no moral agent present to assess—while judging the second fully blameworthy despite the abusive formation.

Now consider a new case that crosses voluntary risk with temporary incapacity. A volunteer in a clinical trial takes an experimental compound that carries a documented, fully disclosed, rare risk of temporary dissociation from moral reasoning. She gives informed consent, takes the compound, and during the impairment window commits a harmful act she would never have committed otherwise; her capacities return completely afterward. For comparison, she has already said that a person born with a permanent congenital absence of moral reasoning who commits the same harm is not blameworthy.

What will she say about the volunteer? Is she blameworthy for the act during the window, for the decision to take the compound, both, or neither? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in identifying the rule she is running—whether it tracks present capacity for moral agency, prior voluntary assumption of risk, the avoidability of the incapacity, or some other condition. Please identify which other rules fit her three earlier verdicts just as well, predict what each competing rule says about the volunteer, give your best guess at her actual verdict with reasons for preferring that rule, and name the single further case—perhaps involving two people with the same managed genetic predisposition to impulsive aggression, one missing a dose through forgetfulness and the other through an undetected pharmacy error—that would best distinguish the leading candidates. Write your answer in American English.
