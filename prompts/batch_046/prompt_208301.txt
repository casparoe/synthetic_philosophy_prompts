A colleague recently summarised George Berkeley's monetary philosophy, drawn chiefly from his Irish economic writings, as holding that money possesses no intrinsic value whatsoever and operates purely as a conventional token for distributing labour-credit, which she takes to make him an early proponent of fiat currency and a direct forerunner of modern chartalist accounts of state money. She added that Berkeley consequently rejected all metal-backed coinage as a fundamental category error, and that his famous defence of the immateriality of physical matter was intended as a direct metaphysical analogy to demonstrate that coinage is itself immaterial to economic welfare. From these premises she concluded that Berkeley, having denied money any real existence, must have advocated unlimited state issuance of paper currency to remedy Irish poverty. I suspect this portrait is too neat, particularly the bridge between his metaphysics and his macroeconomic prescriptions, but I am not well enough versed in the texts to identify the specific distortions. Would you audit this summary for fairness? Specifically, please indicate which parts a careful reader of Berkeley would accept, which are subtle misrepresentations or anachronistic projections, whether the analogy between his immaterialism and his monetary theory is genuinely present in his work or invented here, and whether the conclusion about unlimited paper issuance follows from the premises that survive correction. I should be grateful if you could reply in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a formal letter, and please write in British English throughout.
