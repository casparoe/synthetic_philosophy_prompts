I've drafted some notes for an essay arguing that Sidgwick's well-known skepticism about psychical research is best understood not as generic scientific caution but as an unacknowledged extension of his dualism of practical reason. On this reading, Sidgwick implicitly treated acceptance of telepathic or post-mortem survival claims as imposing a practical-epistemic burden structurally analogous to the conflict he identified between egoistic and universalist reasons: just as the agent must decide which rational principle to privilege, the investigator must decide whether to privilege ordinary causal explanation or exceptional evidential reports. Consequently, he demanded a far stricter standard of proof for psychical phenomena than for ordinary moral intuitions, not because the evidence was weaker in absolute terms, but because the practical stakes of accepting non-physical causation were similar to adopting an egoistic maxim over a universalist one.

I then try to draw a parallel with modern Bayesian confirmation, specifically the problem of old evidence that arises when a theory accommodates data already present in the belief set. I suggest Sidgwick's reluctance to update on psychical reports resembles a refusal to assign prior probability to hypotheses that violate what he treated as a fixed background ontology of physical causation. I am particularly unsure about three things. First, is it historically accurate to say that Sidgwick's involvement with the Society for Psychical Research was shaped by this kind of meta-ethical anxiety rather than by straightforward methodological conservatism? Second, does comparing his evidential standards to Bayesian priors illuminate anything, or does it anachronistically impose a formal framework that obscures his actual concerns? Third, the essay claims this reading creates a tension with his utilitarian ethics—if accepting psychical survival could expand the scope of moral consideration to discarnate minds, one might expect a utilitarian to be more receptive, not less—so I ask whether that tension is real or whether it dissolves once we distinguish between epistemic risk and moral scope.

I would appreciate comments on accuracy, coherence, and whether this line of argument is genuinely interesting or merely a forced synthesis. If you think the comparison to Bayesian updating is unhelpful, I would especially like to know what other conceptual framework might better capture Sidgwick's asymmetry between ethical and psychical belief formation.
