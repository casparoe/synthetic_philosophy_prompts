I am a novelist preparing a short philosophical essay for a cross-disciplinary review, and I would be grateful for rigorous comments on its argument before I finalize the manuscript. The essay develops a conceit drawn from my fiction—that every intentional act leaves a “narrative residue,” altering not only the physical world but the interpretive structure through which events are read as causes, effects, or subplots. I apply this to the problem of low-impact artificial intelligence and ask whether “avoid side effects” can be defined without a full specification of value.

The essay proceeds in three parts. First, I sketch the narrative-residue thesis: that causal histories possess something like narrative coherence, and that an agent’s action can be evaluated not only by its physical divergence from a baseline world but by whether it disrupts the “plot” that makes the world intelligible to its inhabitants. Second, I examine impact measures—formalizations that compare a world with the agent present to one without it—and argue that they appear to require only a local utility function but in fact depend on a global interpretive frame. To classify an altered climate trajectory or a displaced cultural practice as a “side effect” rather than an integral subplot, one must already know the genre, so to speak, of the world: the axiological framework that distinguishes essential threads from expendable ones. I then connect this to the narrow definition of the alignment problem, suggesting that intent alignment (matching the designers’ instructions) can fail precisely because the designers have not specified which narrative threads are inviolable, leaving value alignment undefined. Third, I propose, speculatively, that a genuinely low-impact agent might be defined not by minimal causal footprint but by maximal “narrative transparency”: the property of leaving the world’s interpretive structure intact even when its physical state is altered.

Please comment on the essay with particular attention to the following. First, is the analogy between narrative coherence and axiological specification rigorous enough to be more than a metaphor, or does it risk obscuring the technical distinction between instrumental convergence and value alignment? Second, in the second part, I claim that the dependency on a global interpretive frame is hidden inside the narrow alignment problem itself—an agent aligned with its designers’ intent may still violate value alignment if the designers have not specified which narrative threads are inviolable. Does this framing illuminate the distinction, or does it conflate epistemic uncertainty about values with metaphysical claims about narrative structure? Third, regarding the speculative concept of “narrative transparency”: is this coherent as a formal constraint, or is it merely an aesthetic redescription of existing impact-penalty formulations?

I ask that you address these questions with the precision of a manuscript review, noting where the argument relies on intuition rather than proof, where it is vulnerable to objections from decision theory or philosophy of action, and whether the injection of literary concepts into a technical domain produces genuine conceptual leverage or only decorative ambiguity. I am especially interested in identifying the weakest point in the second part’s claim about the narrow alignment problem, as that is the section I intend to defend in a subsequent seminar discussion, and I want to know which objections will have real force and which I should concede up front.
