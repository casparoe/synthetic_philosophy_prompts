In my philosophy of biology seminar, a recurring analogy is being used to adjudicate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function: people compare the etiological view to the design specifications of an engineered artifact—say, a thermostat or a knife—where what makes something malfunction is its deviation from the selection history that originally fixed its purpose, and they compare the causal-role view to the runtime operational profile of a part inside a working system, where function is just whatever contribution the part currently makes, regardless of why it is there. The analogy is being pressed into service to argue that only the etiological account can genuinely distinguish malfunction from mere difference—just as a broken thermostat is defective in a way that a paperweight used to hold down a thermostat manual is not—while also being invoked to claim that causal-role theories cannot accommodate exaptation, since a feature now performing one job but originally selected for another must be misdescribed if we only look at present causal contribution. How far does this artifact-to-organism mapping actually hold?

Please give me the explicit correspondence table—what plays the role of the designer, the manufacturing process, the user’s manual, the runtime environment, the malfunction report, and the exapted component—then lay out the disanalogies that matter for the normative conclusion versus the ones that do not, especially with respect to the absence of intentional design in evolutionary history, the multiplicity and conflict of selection pressures, and whether causal-role theorists can recover normative or exaptive talk through other conceptual resources. Tell me whether the conclusion that etiological accounts are uniquely adequate survives once the analogy is audited, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work all along, and suggest what a better analogy might be if this one collapses.
