I'm trying to work out my actual commitments on when cultural values count as genuine causes rather than post-hoc glosses, especially in light of Weber's Protestant ethic thesis and the broader problem of explaining outcomes with values. Here are my verdicts on specific explanatory claims; I want to know whether a single principle can capture them, or whether coherence requires me to abandon one.

Weber's claim that Calvinist predestination theology produced an ascetic psychological drive that causally accelerated capital accumulation in early modern Europe strikes me as legitimate value-causation. The mechanism is specific, and the counterfactual seems stable: without that theological anxiety, the behavioral pattern of systematic reinvestment wouldn't have emerged in the same form.

The claim that Confucian values explain East Asian economic growth in the late twentieth century I reject. Those values were constant across centuries of stagnation and rapid growth alike; the explanatory work is done by institutional and geopolitical shifts, with culture functioning as a decorative overlay.

A biographical explanation of a particular Silicon Valley founder's extreme reinvestment behavior by reference to an internalized Protestant work ethic I accept. At the individual level, values can directly reshape decision weights without being reducible to material incentives.

The Gibbon-style explanation of the Roman Republic's decline by a decay in civic virtue I also reject. The values appear to track material and institutional conditions—patronage networks, military professionalization, resource concentration—rather than drive them; citing virtue-loss explains nothing that isn't already accounted for structurally.

Two neighboring agricultural communities with near-identical land and market access show persistently different savings rates, and the difference tracks a local religious tradition that stigmatizes conspicuous consumption. I think this is a valid cultural cause, but only because we can isolate the norm's behavioral mechanism. If the correlation disappeared once we controlled for informal lending networks, I would withdraw the verdict.

The case I keep oscillating on is the persistence of honor-based dueling in the antebellum American South explained by an autonomous culture of honor. Sometimes it feels like a real causal factor—people genuinely feared social death more than legal punishment. Other times it looks like rational adaptation to weak state enforcement: where the state fails to protect you, reputation becomes capital, so honor is just a label for expected-utility maximization under institutional failure. Is this a genuine value-cause, or a misattribution of structural logic?

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps about isolability, level of analysis, counterfactual stability of the value, or independence from institutional explanations—that captures all the settled verdicts except the dueling ambiguity? Or must I drop one of the settled cases to keep the principle simple? Please also state precisely what the best principle predicts for a case I haven't listed: whether a society's widespread belief in fate can explain its low rate of entrepreneurial investment, assuming the belief is genuinely widespread and institutionally unconnected to property rights.

Finally, please separate what is settled in the philosophy of social science about value-based explanation from what remains genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a disputed position—such as whether Weber's thesis survives modern economic historiography—as if it were the consensus view.
