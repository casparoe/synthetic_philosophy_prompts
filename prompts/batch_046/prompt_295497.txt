Please explain the conceptual tension between Norman Malcolm's verificationist argument that dreams are not experiences—on the grounds that there is no concurrent criterion for verifying them during sleep—and Daniel Dennett's opposing constructivist view, often described via a cassette-style model, according to which the narrative content of a dream is assembled during REM but only constituted as an experience upon awakening. I am particularly interested in a crux that standard overviews tend to gloss over: if Dennett is correct that experiential content is retroactively assigned to prior neural states through a process of narrative stitching, then what distinguishes this assignment from ordinary waking confabulation or false-memory insertion? More precisely, does the temporal gap between the REM-state neural events and the awakening report undermine the claim that there was ever a unified phenomenal experience, or can Dennett defend a form of delayed constitution without collapsing into the view that dreams are merely fictional reports? Take a committed position. Either defend Dennett by specifying the exact conceptual feature—perhaps related to causal continuity or narrative coherence conditions—that separates retroactive experiential constitution from mere confabulation, and identify the specific premise in Malcolm's verificationism that this feature renders obsolete; or defend Malcolm by arguing that without contemporaneous accessibility conditions the very concept of an experience dissolves, and show precisely why Dennett's retroactive model cannot rescue it without begging the question. Do not merely map the dialectic—choose a side and state the decisive error in the opposing view.
