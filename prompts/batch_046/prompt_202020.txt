Consider the well-known distinction between operating within a formal system according to its fixed rules and jumping out of the system—often abbreviated JOOTSing—to apply judgments about the system's own structural limitations, a distinction classically illustrated through the MU-puzzle. Argue for the following position: JOOTSing is not a genuinely separate logical or cognitive capacity, but rather a mischaracterized form of deep recursive self-reference, and that once we impose a constraint of symbolic continuity—meaning any meta-rule invoked to escape the system must be fully expressible using only the original system's own symbols and syntactic rules, without importing external semantic interpretation—the sharp boundary between inside and outside collapses into a gradient of recursive depth.

In defending this, address three specific objections. First, respond to the claim that resolving the MU-puzzle requires an external observer who recognizes patterns of symbol manipulation rather than extending those manipulations, and that such recognition is irreducibly outside the system. Second, engage with the fact that formal arithmetic can represent its own syntax via Gödel numbering: explain why this capacity for self-representation does not restore a meaningful object-level versus meta-level distinction, but instead demonstrates that all apparent jumps are internal recursive moves. Third, counter the worry that without a sharp inside-outside boundary, we lose the normative force of the idea that certain problems are genuinely unsolvable within a given framework, as opposed to being merely unsolved at a particular recursive stage.

Your argument should precisely specify what symbolic continuity demands and excludes, should avoid empirical psychological claims about human cognition, and should conclude by assessing whether this dissolution of the boundary strengthens or weakens the broader analogy between formal systems and human reasoning about formal systems.
