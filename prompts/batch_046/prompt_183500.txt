Question: In The Prince ch. 25 Machiavelli compares fortune to a violent river and to a woman who should be struck down, and he claims she governs about half our actions. Some readers take virtù to mean technical skill for controlling an external chaos. Others offer a relational reading: fortune is not an independent force but the structural gap between an agent's plan and the world's resistance, so virtù reshapes both sides rather than conquering something "outside." Evaluate the relational reading against Machiavelli's imagery and his examples. Is fortune purely external?

Student answer: The relational reading is basically correct. Machiavelli sees fortune as a river you build dikes against, so its clearly an external obstacle that virtù fights from the outside. He also says fortune governs half our actions, which means the other half is pure free will in the modern sense, like an agent just picking what to do. The gendered language is just Renaissance window dressing: he calls fortune a woman because women were seen as wild, so beating her symbolizes male virtù dominating chaos. That means if you had perfect virtù there'd be no gap and fortune would vanish completely. Machiavelli proves this with Moses and Cyrus, who had so much virtù that they never needed fortune at all.
