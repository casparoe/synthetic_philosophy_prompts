I facilitate an advanced undergraduate seminar on rational agency and commitment, twelve students, one ninety-minute session. I want them to experience the structural tension at the heart of Kavka's toxin puzzle: that an agent can have overwhelming reason to form an intention she has no reason to carry out, and that carrying it out seems to erase the very condition that made the intention rational. Design a runnable in-class simulation using real but harmless stakes — for instance, temporary forfeiture of a small privilege or a token exchangeable for a snack — so the choice is lived rather than purely hypothetical. The simulation should move through four stages: public deliberation, a binding commitment made via a physical token or signed card, a trigger event that delivers the reward, and an isolated execution decision. Specify rules for pairs, what each participant knows about the others, the payoff structure, and how information is revealed at each stage. Include a debrief that maps every stage onto the puzzle's logic, especially the separation between reasons for intending and reasons for acting, and be explicit about which feature — particularly the claim that the rational agent genuinely wants not to drink once the reward is secured — the simulation cannot reproduce without conceptual supplementation by the facilitator. Predict what will go wrong on the first run and how to correct it. Add a structural twist: assign half the group to a "mirror" role in which they observe and must predict another participant's execution decision without being able to influence it, so the seminar can compare first-person and third-person judgments about whether execution is irrational.
