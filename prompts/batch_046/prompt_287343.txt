I've been trying to advise a close friend who is facing a painful decision after a terminal diagnosis, and our conversation has left me tangled in a philosophical problem I can't quite untangle, even though I'm taking a course on value theory this term. She must choose between an aggressive experimental treatment that could extend her life by a few months but would deplete the savings she intends to leave her grandchildren, and palliative care that preserves the estate but offers no prospect of prolonging her life. She explains that she feels little fear of death itself, reasoning that once she is dead there will be no subject present to experience any deprivation or suffering, and therefore death cannot be a harm that befalls her personally. Yet she is simultaneously consumed by anxiety that spending the money on treatment would constitute a failure to her family—a harm, as she describes it, that she wants to avoid even if she will not be alive to witness their disappointment or need.

What troubles me is that these two convictions seem to pull in opposite directions about what counts as harm and when a subject must exist for harm to occur. If she is right that death does not harm the dead person because there is no longer anyone to be harmed, then it is unclear why she should treat her posthumous legacy as a domain in which she can still be harmed, or why her current obligations depend on a state of affairs she will never experience. I am wondering whether her position can be made consistent by distinguishing between direct experiential harm and indirect or relational harm, or whether she is implicitly relying on different conceptions of personal identity—perhaps one that treats her present self and her future descendants as parts of a continuous narrative—without recognizing the tension. Could you help me think through whether accepting that one's own death is not a personal harm undermines the idea that one can be harmed by events occurring after one's death, and whether her concern for her grandchildren's welfare requires an account of selfhood over time that conflicts with the intuition that makes her unafraid of dying?
