Please draft the joint statement that a committed defender of the all-subjected principle and a committed defender of the all-affected-interests principle could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the boundary problem of democratic membership, but do so through the specific lens of intergenerational subjection and asymmetrical presence: that is, treat the problem as one in which democratic authority binds future generations who are not yet present, while some present non-citizens are affected by decisions but not fully subjected to the state's coercive apparatus. Write entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if composing a formal letter summarizing the collaboration. Begin with the claims both sides can endorse about democratic legitimacy, about the wrongness of arbitrary exclusion, and about what standard no-go arguments concerning boundary circularity or infinite regress actually establish rather than merely suggest. Then set out the precise disagreements in wording each camp would accept for its own view, especially regarding whether being affected without being subjected can ground full membership rights, and whether being subjected without present residence can justify inclusion. Identify the cruxes: I take one to be whether democratic legitimacy requires actual consent or merely the absence of arbitrary domination, and another to be whether future persons count as members of the present demos or only as protected stakeholders with interests. For each crux, present a concrete comparative case that both sides agree bears directly on it—for instance, the long-horizon climate voter whose unborn descendants will live with the policy's consequences, set against the undocumented resident worker who pays taxes and suffers enforcement but lacks the vote—and record plainly what each side would predict about that case. Throughout, avoid bibliographies, citations, named authorities, or appeals to specific texts; the reasoning should stand entirely on conceptual argument.
