I am a novelist drafting a long work about inherited temperament, and in constructing character arcs I have repeatedly encountered a structural pattern I cannot name with any confidence. Consider a protagonist who is genuinely disposed toward a particular response—say, an unguarded generosity—yet in every scene where that response would be tested, an external contingency intervenes not by altering her internal state but by removing the opportunity: the intended recipient dies before she can act, a bureaucratic error redirects the resource elsewhere, or she is physically restrained by accident rather than by choice. Other characters within the fiction treat her as generous; they plan around that expectation; and if one imagines the interfering conditions stripped away, she would indeed behave generously. Yet within the narrative she never does. I have also written the mirror case: a figure whose underlying disposition is toward cruelty, but every time cruelty would manifest, an unrelated protective mechanism—perhaps an accidental dependency that makes violence self-defeating—blocks it, leaving observers to mistake the absence of cruelty for its opposite. These are not cases of hypocrisy, since the internal quality and the blocker remain genuinely distinct, nor are they simple failures of opportunity, since the blocker is systematically coupled to the very conditions that would reveal the trait.

In the metaphysics of properties or dispositions, is there an established concept—perhaps a family of them—that captures this exact configuration, where a real disposition is present but systematically neutralized by an interfering factor that is not part of the disposition itself? I am particularly interested in whether philosophers distinguish the case where the blocker removes the occasion for manifestation from the case where the blocker actively produces an opposing behavior, and whether either is treated as undermining the claim that the original disposition was genuinely instantiated. If the literature offers precise terms, I would like to know how they differ from one another and from the simpler claim that the trait was never there to begin with; if no single framework unifies both patterns, I would welcome a careful delineation of where the edges lie and what each variant should be called.
