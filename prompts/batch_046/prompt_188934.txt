As a novelist, I am accustomed to excising scenes that do not alter the ending, and I have begun to suspect that rigorous historical explanation should obey the same economy. Yet when I apply this logic to overdetermination in history, I arrive at an absurd conclusion, and I cannot locate the flaw.

(1) If citing a cause C in explaining event E is legitimate, then E must be counterfactually dependent on C; otherwise C is narrative dead weight.

(2) In standard multi-causal accounts—say, of a regime's collapse—historians cite economic strain, military defeat, and ideological erosion as distinct contributing forces.

(3) If any one of these forces had been sufficient by itself, its absence should not have changed the outcome, since the others would still have operated; but historians treat the absence of any one as altering the trajectory.

(4) Therefore historians implicitly treat every cited factor as necessary, not as an independently sufficient cause.

(5) But if every cited factor is necessary, they form a single jointly sufficient set, leaving no logical space for true overdetermination, in which two or more causes are each sufficient.

(6) Historians nevertheless routinely claim that certain events are overdetermined—that economic collapse alone might have brought the regime down, and so might military invasion alone.

(7) So either historians redundantly pack a single necessary-and-sufficient condition into the language of "several causes," or they violate (1) by allowing redundant, counterfactually dispensable causes into explanation.

(8) If we choose the latter and allow dispensable causes, then explanatory legitimacy no longer tracks counterfactual necessity, and any factor that actually contributed may be cited regardless of whether its removal changes the outcome.

(9) If we choose the former and deny overdetermination, then all apparent multi-causal explanations reduce to descriptions of one indivisible mechanism, and historians' talk of independent contributing forces is merely rhetorical.

The result is that either historical explanation tolerates systematic redundancy, or it never genuinely invokes multiple independent causes. I find both options intolerable, especially because I wish to preserve (1)—the intuition that explanation should not accommodate dead weight—while also preserving the historians' practice of naming several distinct forces without collapsing them into one mechanism. Where does the reasoning slip? Is it in the move from narrative economy to counterfactual necessity, in the factor-by-factor assessment of sufficiency in complex path-dependent systems, or elsewhere? And what exactly must one pay to reject that step?
