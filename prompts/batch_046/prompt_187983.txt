You are an expert philosopher of language and an experienced university lecturer. Before you design the session, take a moment to think through the conceptual dependencies: identify which ideas must be secured before a student can understand the claim being made, and which common confusions must be separated out at each stage.

I am preparing a single 75-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates. They have completed introductory logic and are familiar with Frege's distinction between sense and reference, but they have never studied Quine. The topic is Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation, centered on the radical-translation scenario and the gavagai case, and specifically as it connects to the problem of identifying an ontology when internal structural information is inaccessible.

Plan the explanatory arc for me. Do not deliver the explanation itself; deliver the plan for how I should explain it.

Structure your response around the following:

1. The dependency-ordered sequence: list the components in the order they must be introduced, with a brief justification for each dependency (what must be in place before the next step can land).

2. The carrying example: describe the one concrete scenario that should carry the whole idea. It should be a field-linguist scenario in which a native speaker utters gavagai in the presence of a running rabbit, but modified so that the observer has no access to the internal mereological structure of the entities—meaning that rabbit, undetached rabbit part, and instantaneous rabbit-stage are behaviorally and observationally indistinguishable. Specify exactly what properties this example must have and what it must lack in order to do its pedagogical work.

3. The misconception map: identify the predictable misconceptions these students will form or bring—specifically, that Quine is saying translation is practically impossible rather than systematically underdetermined; that gavagai is simply the word for rabbit and the translator is being obtuse; that ontological relativity means ontology is purely subjective or that anything goes; and that the problem applies only to exotic field linguistics rather than to scientific theory choice. For each, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and the specific move that defuses it.

4. The check for understanding: propose one exact question or brief task I can pose at the end of the session that will reveal whether the students have grasped that the indeterminacy is a feature of all possible translation manuals compatible with the evidence, rather than a temporary gap in data.

5. The omissions: state what I should explicitly refuse to explain or develop at this level—particularly any formal apparatus, broader epistemological debates, or connections to other philosophers' work that require background they do not have—and indicate how I should signal that refusal openly.

Keep the plan entirely self-contained: do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations, or recommendations for further texts.
