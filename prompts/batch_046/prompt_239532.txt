Here are my concrete gut reactions to welfare-conditionality cases. I want to know whether a single principle captures them, or whether I must revise one or more verdicts.

Case 1: Unemployment insurance requires recipients to document twenty job applications each week. This feels acceptable—a reasonable verification of availability.

Case 2: The same recipient is sanctioned for missing one documentation deadline because they were attending a parent's funeral. This feels wrong; the rule ignores legitimate context.

Case 3: A person with documented, permanent spinal damage is forced to undergo full medical reassessment every six months to keep disability benefits. This feels like bureaucratic cruelty—verification without purpose.

Case 4: A recipient hides $250 in cash gifts from relatives to avoid a reduction in food-stamp allocation; she is prosecuted for fraud and banned for life. This feels wildly disproportionate, even if technically incorrect.

Case 5: A wealthy individual uses legal offshore structures to avoid taxes that fund welfare programs. Society treats this as greedy but rarely frames it as moral fraud equivalent to Case 4. This feels like a categorically different wrong—less visceral, not a betrayal of mutual obligation.

Case 6: A universal unconditional cash grant is given to everyone, including able-bodied people who choose never to work and spend it on leisure travel. This does not feel morally wrong, though perhaps inefficient.

Case 7: A parent is cut off from housing assistance for refusing a job offer ninety minutes away, because accepting it would leave their disabled child without care. This feels wrong—the condition ignores relational obligations.

Case 8: A workfare program requires thirty hours of unpaid labor in exchange for basic food support; missing one day due to untreated depression results in immediate suspension. This feels coercive and unjust.

Case 9: A recipient works cash jobs under the table to pay for prescription drugs not covered by the program, hiding the income. If discovered, they are sanctioned. The deception is technically wrong, but the sanction feels too harsh; the underlying need makes the act understandable.

My tentative organizing idea is a distinction between verification conditions, which check objective eligibility or need, and transformative conditions, which force recipients to alter their behavior, relationships, or self-conception to prove moral worth. Case 1 seems like verification; Case 3 like pointless verification; Case 7 like transformative demand; Case 6 like absence of both; Case 8 like coercive transformation. But I keep going back and forth on Case 9: is hiding income to cover medical costs a failure of verification, or does the transformative frame apply because the recipient is circumventing rules about work and dependency? And Case 5 sits awkwardly—tax avoidance is neither verification nor transformation, yet it feels structurally related to fraud.
