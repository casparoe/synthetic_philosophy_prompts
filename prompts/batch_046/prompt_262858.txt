Please write the opening remarks for a one-day workshop titled "Skillful Means and Aggregate Welfare," convened jointly by a university Buddhist studies program and its philosophy department. The audience is divided: about half are scholars of Abhidharma and Mahayana ethics who read Pali and Sanskrit sources closely; the other half are moral philosophers and welfare economists accustomed to thinking in terms of utility aggregation and expected value. You are speaking not as an academic but as a palliative-care clinician who has worked for fifteen years in both Nepal and the United Kingdom, invited precisely because you inhabit both worlds without being fully at home in either.

The philosophical tension must structure the speech, not decorate it. Frame your remarks around the distinction between the cessation of dukkha—which, in the tradition you are addressing, requires a dissolution of the subject-object boundary that makes suffering assignable to a self—and the minimization of suffering, which requires sharpening that same boundary to weigh outcomes across individuals. Because of this structural difference, you must not offer a standard comparative literature review or rank thinkers. Instead, build the address as a single sustained clinical analogy that illustrates why treating the end of suffering as an optimization problem alters the kind of care one delivers, and pose the shared problem: can a metric that has no register for the absence of a self that suffers capture what the ethical tradition aims at?

The talk should run about four minutes spoken aloud. Speak with authority but avoid technical jargon that depends on footnotes; do not name specific texts or scholars unless you are describing a well-known concept in plain language. Do not resolve the tension; leave it as a productive friction for the day's discussions, so that both halves of the room depart with a common question rather than a common answer.
