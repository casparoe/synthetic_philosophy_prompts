I am composing a decision tree for a philosophical fiction manuscript that sorts how thinkers respond to belief-formation when evidence is genuinely insufficient, especially in moments of narrative or existential crisis. The root asks whether a lack of sufficient evidence ever permits belief formation at all. From there, branch by whether such permission requires a live and forced option; whether it extends to beliefs that actively reshape one's narrative identity rather than merely describe external facts; and whether the moral cost of being wrong is weighed differently when the belief concerns one's future self rather than an external state of affairs. The leaves should include a strict evidentialist prohibition, a pragmatic permission grounded in vital benefit, a hybrid view that restricts permission to live and forced options alone, and an unnamed position—call it the novelist's exception—that permits belief solely for its narrative coherence regardless of evidential status. Each node must be phrased as a concrete claim or case that a reader could endorse without knowing which philosopher defends it, and the tree should locate every combination in logical space, noting which cells remain unoccupied—whether because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored—and flagging any pair of leaves the branches cannot genuinely separate. Keep the instrument free of citation requests, bibliographic dependencies, or appeals to texts I might not recall precisely.
