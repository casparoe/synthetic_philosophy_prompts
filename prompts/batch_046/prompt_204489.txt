Finitists in philosophy of mathematics often press an analogy: treating an actual infinity as a completed totality is like treating a compass direction—say, due north—as though it were a specific city you could eventually arrive at. On this view, infinity names only the potential to continue, not a finished object; Hilbert's hotel and Cantor's hierarchy of cardinals are therefore illegitimate reifications, comparable to declaring that north is a building with infinite floors. The analogy is used to license the conclusion that there are no actual infinities, only unending processes.

First, lay out an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the traveler, the path, the direction, the putative city of north, and the map that claims such a city exists? Then identify where the mapping breaks—particularly with respect to Cantor's diagonal argument and Hilbert's room-shifting procedure, neither of which seems to involve temporal travel or successive addition. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely undermine the finitist use from those that are merely decorative. Does the analogy quietly import a temporal or physical intuition that mathematics does not require? If the analogy collapses, is there a better one that captures the finitist worry without the same liabilities? Finally, assess whether the anti-actualist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument.
