While preparing for a graduate seminar on Kant's practical philosophy, I witnessed a sustained disagreement between two fellow students—let us call them Elena and Marcus—concerning the coherence of Kant's account of our obligations toward non-rational animals. Elena began by arguing that Kant's indirect-duty framework is structurally unstable. If, as the standard reading holds, our duties regarding animals derive entirely from our duties to humanity, then the wrongness of cruelty should depend solely on contingent psychological effects on human agents. She pressed a carefully constructed case: consider a secluded virtual environment in which an agent can inflict suffering on hyper-realistic animal simulations, with no possibility of habituating cruelty toward rational beings and with no other human observer affected. If the indirect-duty view is genuinely instrumental, Kant should have no moral objection in this scenario; yet Elena suspects he would still condemn the behavior, which suggests the framework is either incomplete or incoherent. Marcus responded that Elena mischaracterizes the mechanism. For Kant, cruelty to animals is wrong not merely because it causally predisposes one to harm rational beings, but because it involves a failure to recognize suffering as an object that demands moral attention; this recognition is constitutive of properly cultivated moral feeling, not a mere causal stepping-stone to human-directed morality. Elena then countered that once Marcus admits this constitutive role—treating the acknowledgment of animal suffering as part of what it is to possess a well-ordered moral disposition—he has effectively introduced a direct duty, since the wrongness no longer depends solely on effects upon humanity. Marcus insisted that there remains no direct duty owed to the animal itself: the failure is always located within the agent's own rational nature, specifically in the development of sympathetic feeling necessary for human moral perfection. He conceded, however, that this renders the label indirect duty somewhat misleading. Elena then raised a further asymmetry: if the cultivation of moral feeling requires responding to suffering, why does Kant condemn cruelty but remain largely silent about positive obligations of beneficence toward animals? Marcus suggested that beneficence lacks the same structural connection to moral feeling because it does not exercise the same resistance to self-love, but Elena replied that this sounds ad hoc unless Marcus can provide a principled distinction. They turned to me for an adjudication, and I genuinely could not determine where the interpretive fault lines lie.

Please adjudicate their exchange point by point. For each move—Elena's initial structural objection and the virtual-environment test, Marcus's reply concerning constitutive moral feeling, Elena's charge that direct duties have been smuggled in, Marcus's clarification about failure within rational nature, and Elena's final challenge regarding the asymmetry between prohibitions on cruelty and silence about beneficence—indicate whether the move is clearly mistaken, partially correct but overstated, or clearly correct, with brief justification. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing: specifically, whether there exists a coherent third interpretation of Kant's texts that avoids both pure instrumentality and the collapse into direct duties, and whether the virtual-environment thought experiment actually tests the indirect-duty thesis in the way Elena assumes it does.
