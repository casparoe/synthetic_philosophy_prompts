Write the after-dinner speech for Professor Elena Vasquez’s retirement dinner. She spent thirty-five years defending epiphenomenalism together with a rigorous account of phenomenal judgment — the view that reports of pain or colour are caused not by the experiences themselves but by parallel neural mechanisms, which raises the paradox of how we can ever be correct about our own minds. The audience is split evenly: philosophers of mind who will catch any misstatement of her position, and family and administrators who have never read the literature. The philosophical tension must shape the speech structurally: you must honour her long advocacy for clinical mental-health funding without implying that her beliefs about suffering causally motivated that advocacy, since on her view mental states do not cause behaviour; yet you must also avoid reducing her advocacy to a mere by-product of brain chemistry, which would erase the moral seriousness her family witnessed. Do not resolve the paradox with a false comfort; let it determine what kind of tribute is honest. Keep the address to about five minutes, formal but warm, and observe British spelling and punctuation conventions.
