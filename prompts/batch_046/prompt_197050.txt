A common move in discussions of Adam Smith’s moral psychology is to liken the impartial spectator to a theatre critic who occupies a permanent box seat, watching the drama of one’s life unfold and delivering a detached verdict of applause or censure; this comparison is then used to claim that Smithian sympathy is essentially an aesthetic and passive affair, in which moral learning consists merely in adjusting one’s performance to win the spectator’s approval, leaving little room for active practical reasoning or for the correction of the spectator’s own biases. I want you to audit this analogy thoroughly: in continuous prose, lay out what corresponds to the critic, the actor, the audience, the script, and the verdict within Smith’s actual framework of sympathy and the internal tribunal; then identify precisely where the theatrical picture breaks down, paying particular attention to Smith’s insistence that sympathy requires an imaginative exchange of situations rather than mere observation, to the presence of two distinct tribunals — the court of mankind and the court of one’s own conscience — and to the possibility that the spectator himself can be misled and stand in need of further sympathetic correction. You should also say what features of theatrical judgement — such as its aesthetic detachment, its singularity, and its absence of reciprocal obligation — are quietly imported into the moral domain by this comparison, and whether the conclusion that Smith offers a passive, audience-oriented ethics survives once these disanalogies are fully on the table. Please write entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as one would in a letter, and conclude by assessing whether a better analogy — perhaps drawn from legal arbitration or from the mutual calibration of shared measures — would carry the weight that the theatrical one fails to bear.
