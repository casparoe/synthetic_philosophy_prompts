I have been trying to systematize my judgments about care obligations around a tentative framing I call narrative-continuity care: the idea that care, at its best, helps another maintain or restore the coherent thread of their life story, and, at its worst, either fractures that thread in the other or demands that the caregiver dissolve her own. Here are my verdicts on concrete cases; I want to know whether a single principle can accommodate them, whether my framing is salvageable with refinement, and what I must abandon.

Case 1: A daughter relocates across the country and abandons her career to nurse her mother through advanced dementia, knowing the mother no longer recognizes her, because she believes her mother’s life story should not end in an anonymous facility. I judge this admirable, and possibly obligatory, despite the absence of any reciprocal psychological benefit.

Case 2: In an alternate scenario, that same daughter refuses all contact with her dying mother because the mother was chronically abusive, saying that caring for her would mean allowing her to destroy the narrative coherence of the daughter’s own life. I judge this refusal permissible and not blameworthy.

Case 3: A pediatric nurse spends her unpaid break sitting with and holding the hand of a frightened child in an ICU whose parents are barred from visiting; she learns almost nothing about the child’s life outside the hospital. I judge this exemplary care.

Case 4: That same nurse learns that a foster child assigned to her ward is suffering minor but real neglect by foster parents. Rather than report it and risk disrupting the child’s placement, she spends her own salary buying the child gifts and providing extra emotional attention to compensate. I judge her emotional engagement good, but her failure to report a serious moral failure—yet both impulses seem to stem from the same protective orientation toward the child.

Case 5: A wealthy donor gives millions anonymously to famine relief in a distant country, never learning recipients’ names or life histories. I judge this morally worthy but not care in the full sense; it belongs to justice or charity, not to care.

Case 6: A therapist maintains strict clinical boundaries with a severely depressed client, refusing personal warmth, self-disclosure, or emotional presence, explicitly to prevent dependency. I judge this professionally correct but not an instance of care; it operates in a different moral category altogether.

Case 7: A partner, upon discovering betrayal, suppresses her own anger and focuses entirely on her partner’s emotional needs, telling herself that real care means absorbing the pain so that the relationship’s shared story can continue. I judge this wrong—a form of self-erasure that corrupts care into subjugation.

Case 8, the one I keep reversing: A high-school teacher spends hours tutoring a struggling student who reminds her of her deceased brother. She hides this personal motivation and frames all assistance as pure professional duty. Is the hidden personal narrative corrupting the care, or is it precisely the source of its moral depth?

My questions: Is there a coherent principle—perhaps tied to narrative continuity but precisely refined—that delivers all eight verdicts? If so, state it exactly, identify which clause handles which case, predict its verdict on a further case, and say honestly which of my judgments it forces me to drop. The further case is this: a community establishes a collective, anonymous childcare rotation in which every adult cares for every child equally, deliberately erasing special parent-child bonds in the name of equality. Would your principle classify this as an expansion or a destruction of care? If no single principle works, present the two best competing candidates, note which verdicts each saves and which it sacrifices, and recommend which I should overturn.
