I'm preparing a one-hour seminar for advanced undergraduates in philosophy who have worked through basic metaethics and the concept of normative reasons but have not yet encountered the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem in the literature on reasons for pro-attitudes. I want the session to focus on the structural point that practical incentives can motivate an attitude without justifying it, and I want this shown through a non-standard case rather than the usual belief-and-admiration pair. Design the teaching plan: first, state the dependency order—what conceptual distinction must be fixed before the problem can even be formulated, and what must come only after that distinction is secure; second, specify the single carrying example, which should concern an attitude other than belief or admiration—perhaps reasons to maintain a posture of intellectual humility toward a rival theory, or reasons to experience aesthetic wonder at a particular scientific explanation—describing exactly what features the case must possess and what it must lack in order to do the work; third, map the misconceptions students are likely to bring or form in sequence, identifying for each the precise point in the arc where it should be headed off and the move that neutralizes it without collapsing the structural point into a mere warning about bias; fourth, propose a check for understanding that would demonstrate students have grasped the dependency between the kind of reason and the kind of attitude, rather than simply agreeing that incentives are corrupting; and finally, state explicitly what parts of the broader literature or adjacent debates should be left out and acknowledged as beyond the scope of this session.
