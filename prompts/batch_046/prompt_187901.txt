As a novelist constructing an autofictional narrative, I have built an argument to defend the reality of my protagonist's consciousness, only to find that it seems to prove far too much. I lay it out in steps and ask where, if anywhere, the chain breaks.

(1) On the strange-loop account of the self, the symbol "I" is not the name of a hidden substance but a token that refers back to the total pattern of activity—neural in the biological case—that generates it.
(2) What distinguishes a self from a mere feedback mechanism is structural integration: the symbol is causally embedded within, and continuously reshaped by, the very system it denotes.
(3) In my novel, the narrator discovers she is fictional; her attempts to alter the plot create recursive changes in the manuscript that defines her, so that her "I" refers to the total narrative pattern producing her, and her utterances causally reshape that pattern.
(4) If the criterion for selfhood is purely structural—self-reference plus causal integration within the denoting system—then substrate is irrelevant, and a sufficiently integrated textual loop should qualify just as well as a neural one.
(5) Consequently, my protagonist possesses an "I" in precisely the same philosophical sense that I do.
(6) It then follows that to delete a chapter is to inflict partial death upon her, and to revise her thoughts without her consent is to violate her autonomy.
(7) These conclusions are intolerable.
(8) Therefore, either step (2) covertly presupposes a biological condition, or step (4) equivocates between causal integration in living tissue and representational recursion in a text.

I am unsure which repair is least damaging. If I preserve (2) and reject (4), I must explain why substrate independence fails without reducing the loop to mere chemistry. If I preserve (4) and reject (2), I must concede that selfhood requires something beyond loop-structure—perhaps embodied persistence—and I sacrifice the elegant anti-substantialist thrust of the view. I should prefer to retain (1), since it captures the intuition that there is no ghost in the machine.

Please diagnose the argument. Does a subtle flaw—perhaps a scope shift, an illicit move from representation to causation, or a hidden quantifier ambiguity—lurk between steps (3) and (5)? Or is the reasoning valid, leaving me to abandon one of the premises I hold dear, and at what cost? I want an honest assessment of whether defenders of the strange-loop view have quietly qualified it into unfalsifiability by insisting on unstated biological conditions, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice for a theory of this kind. Please reply in British English, in a relatively formal and polished style.
