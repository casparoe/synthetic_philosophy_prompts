I've been thinking on my own about what it actually takes for something to count as alive, especially with news about synthetic cells and brain organoids that blur the line between artifact and organism. Without any formal training in biology or philosophy, I've been trying to work out whether being alive is mainly about maintaining one's own organization — eating, repairing, responding to injury — or whether it requires something like belonging to an unbroken chain of reproduction stretching back to the origin of life. I realize this distinction might be clumsy, but it seems to capture why people hesitate to call a lab-grown tissue "alive" in the full sense, even when it pulses or metabolizes.

This made me return to Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, which I remember mainly for its central horror: a scientist constructs a body from dead tissue, animates it, and produces a creature who thinks, suffers, learns language by observing a family in the woods, seeks warmth and companionship, and eventually demands a partner. The story is widely known, but I want to read it philosophically rather than just as a warning about hubris. Does the novel present the Creature as genuinely alive, or as a sophisticated mechanism that only mimics life? Which scenes — his self-education, his physical needs, his moral reasoning, or his request that Victor build him a mate — show organizational self-maintenance versus mere imitation of human behavior? And does the narrative treat his manufactured origin, with no parents and no evolutionary lineage, as the root of his monstrosity, or is that only Victor's prejudice?

I'm wondering whether my distinction between organizational life and lineal continuity illuminates the story, or whether the novel suggests that being alive is really about being recognized and treated as alive by others. If the Creature had reproduced, or if he had been accepted into human society, would that change his metaphysical status, or is there a deeper gap? I am preparing this for a discussion group of non-specialists, so I would value a reading that takes both the philosophical problem and Shelley's narrative seriously — one that says where the idea helps us see the Creature more clearly, and where the story resists being reduced to an illustration of a definition. I don't need bibliographies or references to specific scholars; I'm trying to see if my own intuition holds up against a work that has been worrying about these boundaries since long before modern synthetic biology.
