In a graduate seminar I have been running on evidential and causal reasoning in strategic interaction, my students and I have repeatedly encountered a structural pattern that I can locate only imperfectly within the existing taxonomy of Newcomb-like problems. The familiar landmarks—counterfactual mugging and the XOR-style blackmail scenarios discussed in the literature—both involve a predictor or blackmailer whose action depends on a forecast of the agent's choice, arranged so that evidential reasoning and causal reasoning diverge. But in every classroom example we construct, a further layer appears: the predictor's correlation with the agent's behavior is not deterministic but noisy, governed by a calibration parameter that the agent knows only through a prior distribution. The payoff structure remains an exclusive disjunction—either the agent complies and avoids harm, or refuses and suffers it, with both outcomes being impossible—yet because the predictor's reliability is itself uncertain, the agent's update on the interaction changes not only her beliefs about the world but the very weight she should assign to evidential versus causal considerations. I have seen this emerge in a contractual penalty clause triggered by an XOR relation between breach notification and automatic sanctions, in an automated medical-adherence protocol that conditions future coverage on a noisy prediction of patient compliance, and in a trading algorithm that executes a punitive position unless it forecasts withdrawal, where the forecast's false-positive rate is unknown to the trader.

What I am trying to identify is whether there is an established term for this calibration-dependent variant—one in which the predictor's accuracy is part of the agent's epistemic state rather than a fixed background condition. It differs from standard counterfactual mugging, as I understand it, because there the predictor's model is usually treated as perfectly calibrated, and it differs from ordinary XOR blackmail because the latter typically presumes a deterministic logical gate rather than a probabilistic channel whose parameters must be learned. If the literature has named this hybrid, I would like to know how it is bounded: does it collapse into ordinary evidential reasoning once the uncertainty is made explicit, or does it constitute a distinct failure mode of rational choice? If there is no name, I am inclined to call it something like noisy XOR dependency or calibrated counterfactual blackmail, but I want to be sure I am not reinventing a concept that already has precise edges and counterexamples. A bare label will not satisfy me; I am after the carving—what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie relative to predictive blackmail and evidential decision theory.
