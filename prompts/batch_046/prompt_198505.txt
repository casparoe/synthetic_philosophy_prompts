I'm drafting a taxonomy for a philosophy of language seminar that sorts interpretations of Wittgenstein's private-language argument not by author but by how they answer the solitary-calibration problem: when no other speaker can correct you, what—if anything—supplies the normative distinction between correctly applying a term for an inner sensation and merely believing you have done so?

Please construct a branching tree. The root node should ask whether private-language users need any normative distinction between correct and incorrect application at all. From there, split on whether (i) the qualitative state itself can serve as its own standard; (ii) the norm must be inherited from prior public practice; (iii) solitary dispositional regularities or a functional-cognitive role within the agent's broader psychology can close the gap; and (iv) the gap is unbridgeable, so the alleged language collapses into non-cognitive expression.

Every branch should be labeled with the claim that divides it from its siblings, phrased so a student could answer it without already knowing the corresponding interpretation's name. Each leaf should carry either the name of an actual defended position—such as a specific reading of Wittgenstein, a Kripkean skeptical solution, a phenomenalist reply, or an eliminativist option—or the label unnamed if the combination is unoccupied. Alongside the name, include one sentence describing the view's most awkward commitment: the consequence that sounds least plausible on first encounter.

The tree should contain at least six leaves. In addition, identify any pair of leaves that your taxonomy cannot genuinely separate, and briefly diagnose whether they are conceptually identical, terminologically variant, or unstable in combination. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; the goal is to map the logical space of answers, not to survey the literature.
