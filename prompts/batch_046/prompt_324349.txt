I'm running a readin group for philosophy-curious adults who've never touched Plutarch. Next session I want to teach the idea that biography can work as moral technology — not just recording lives but calibrating the reader's judgment through comparison. I want to use Plutarch's Parallel Lives against hagiography, but I'm afraid they'll collapse it into "so it's like a celebrity profile with morals attached." Plan the session: what idea has to land first before they can even see the difference between learning from a life and idolizing it, the one pair of lives that should carry the whole explanation and what makes them good for this (and why not just Alexander/Caesar), the misconceptions they'll hit — probably that Plutarch is just praising his guys, or that hagiography is just "biography but religious" — and where in the arc to defuse each, and a final check question that shows whether they've grasped the comparative mechanism rather than just memorized "Plutarch compares Greeks and Romans." Don't ask me for a syllabus, just map the dependency order and the misconception traps.
