I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or evolutionary biology, but I recently encountered the handicap principle and costly signaling theory in a long-form essay, and it sparked a debate over dinner with two colleagues that I couldn't adjudicate. Both are technically literate but not philosophers, and I'd like a rigorous point-by-point assessment of where each of them stands.

My colleague Marcus argued that Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle—where an organism bears a genuinely fitness-reducing trait precisely to prove it can survive despite it—explains a pattern we both recognize in open-source software culture: senior maintainers often write documentation that is deliberately dense, highly abstract, and difficult for newcomers to penetrate. Marcus claimed this obscurity is not poor communication but costly signaling. Only a developer who truly masters the codebase can afford documentation that takes far longer to write and that actively suppresses adoption, because the reduced audience and increased maintenance burden constitute a real handicap. If the maintainer survives this self-imposed burden—retaining credibility and authority despite alienating casual users—the community receives an honest guarantee of competence that cannot be faked by someone with shallow knowledge.

Our colleague Sarah raised three objections in sequence. First, she said Marcus confuses costly signaling with simple gatekeeping, and that he misapplies the handicap principle because it requires the signal to reduce the signaler's literal survival or reproductive fitness, not merely to filter an audience. In open-source ecosystems, widespread adoption increases a maintainer's reputation, job prospects, and social capital, so the "cost" of obscure documentation is illusory; it functions more like a peacock's tail that attracts mates rather than genuinely endangering the bird.

Marcus replied that Sarah was treating fitness too narrowly. In cultural and professional domains, fitness should be understood as influence and reputation within a specific expert community, not biological survival. The obscure documentation does reduce broad adoption, which in a rational world should diminish a maintainer's influence, yet they bear it precisely because only genuinely skilled practitioners can survive that reduction without being exposed as incompetent. The handicap is calibrated to the expert niche, not the general population.

Sarah's second move was empirical: even granting cultural fitness, the handicap principle requires some correlation between the cost borne and the underlying quality being signaled. But in software, obscure documentation frequently correlates with poor code quality—developers who write disorganized code often produce disorganized documentation—so the signal fails to guarantee mastery. Marcus answered that signaling theory never demands perfect correlation; it only requires that cheating be unprofitable on average. The existence of some incompetent developers who write poorly does not break the logic, because the community eventually penalizes them.

Finally, Sarah argued that if Marcus's model were correct, we should observe the handicap disappearing in competitive labor markets, where developers seek employment rather than niche reputation and therefore need broad adoption. Instead, she claimed, the most employable senior engineers often maintain the most impenetrable documentation, precisely because employers and hiring committees value the exclusivity itself as a prestige marker. Marcus responded that this actually confirms his view: in labor markets, the audience shifts from casual users to other experts and gatekeepers, so the handicap is recalibrated to impress that specific audience rather than eliminated.

Neither of them seemed satisfied, and I genuinely could not tell who was closer to being right. Could you adjudicate their exchange move by move—rating Marcus's initial claim, Sarah's three objections, and Marcus's two replies, as clearly mistaken, mostly right, or genuinely ambiguous, with brief justification for each rating? Then say who is closer to being right overall. Finally, please identify what both parties seem to be missing—perhaps a distinction, an empirical condition, or a theoretical refinement—that would make their disagreement sharper and more productive if it were included.
