I am trying to reconstruct the implicit principle behind a series of verdicts that a metaphysician I study with has delivered in our seminar on material constitution and diachronic identity. Across the cases below she has never appealed to a named theory, but her answers seem to follow a pattern—possibly more than one.

Her rulings so far:

1. The statue of David and the lump of marble that currently composes it. Verdict: they are not identical. Reshaping the marble into a vase destroys the statue, yet the marble survives; therefore they have different persistence conditions.

2. The ship of Theseus, in which every plank is replaced over time while the design and function are preserved. Verdict: the original ship is destroyed; the present vessel is a new ship, despite continuous use and recognition by the crew.

3. A cerebral-cortex transplant: the tissue is moved from one living human body to another, sustaining psychological continuity. Verdict: the person travels with the tissue; the donor body, now lacking that tissue, is no longer that person.

4. A cloud that maintains its macroscopic shape and meteorological role for hours, even though every water droplet within it has been replaced. Verdict: it is the same cloud; material turnover does not break its identity.

5. A corporation in which every board member, employee, and physical office is replaced, but the charter, legal registration, and institutional purpose remain intact. Verdict: it is the same corporation.

6. A digital text file copied bit-for-bit onto a new server, after which the original storage medium is destroyed. Verdict: the file survives; its physical substrate is inessential.

Now the new case, which she has not yet addressed:

A patient receives an allogeneic bone graft: a segment of femur taken from a donor is implanted in the recipient. The graft’s original collagen scaffold remains intact, but over a decade every original cell is replaced by the recipient’s osteoblasts, so the living tissue is now genetically the recipient’s. Meanwhile, the donor’s body regenerates bone at the original site using stem cells preserved and cloned from the original tissue; the regenerated bone is genetically identical to the donor’s original cells and occupies the original anatomical location, though it was never part of the recipient. The metaphysician is asked whether the bone now functioning in the recipient’s femur is the same biological object that was originally in the donor, whether the regenerated donor bone is that original object, and how to adjudicate if both claims seem plausible.

Please answer in the following sections, with the approximate lengths indicated:

1. "Closest Precedent and Its Weight" (about 150 words): Identify which of the six rulings is the closest analog to the graft case, explain the analogy precisely, and say whether it pulls strongly in one direction or is genuinely ambiguous.

2. "Candidate Principles Behind the Record" (about 250 words): Articulate at least two distinct, philosophically plausible principles that could jointly explain rulings 1–6. For each principle, state its core claim about what determines persistence, and show how it accounts for the statue, the ship, the brain, the cloud, the corporation, and the file.

3. "What Each Principle Predicts for the Graft" (about 200 words): Apply both principles to the bone-graft case. Be explicit about whether the recipient’s graft, the donor’s regenerated bone, both, or neither counts as the original bone, and justify the prediction by reference to the principle’s core claim.

4. "Best-Guess Verdict with Justification" (about 150 words): Give the verdict you think best coheres with her track record, state the reasoning, and identify which principle it implicitly favors.

5. "The Underdetermination and the Cost of Each Choice" (about 150 words): If the record genuinely underdetermines the case, say so honestly. Then specify the general principle she would be adopting if she rules for the recipient’s graft, and the different principle she would be adopting if she rules for the donor’s regeneration (or for neither).

6. "The Decisive Further Case" (about 100 words): Propose one further hypothetical case—different in kind from the graft—that would distinguish the competing principles you have identified.

Keep the response focused on consistency with the track record and on conceptual clarity; do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific published works.
