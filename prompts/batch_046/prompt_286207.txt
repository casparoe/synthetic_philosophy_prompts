I am preparing a 90-minute session for an upper-division philosophy of history seminar (14 students, mix of graduates and seniors) on non-human causation and the perils of environmental determinism. Please design a runnable classroom game—with assigned roles, explicit rules, a timeline, and small but real stakes—that forces participants to construct causal narratives about two comparative cases: the cascade from the 536 CE volcanic winter through the Justinian Plague into Byzantine military and fiscal restructuring, and the cascade from the 1815 Tambora eruption through the Year Without a Summer into European grain-market instability and political unrest. The rules should be arranged so that students begin by allocating explanatory weight across human decision, institutional structure, biological agent, and climatic or volcanic event, but then discover that if they grant the non-human factors more than a background threshold of causal efficacy, the narrative grammar of their explanations slips into active-voice, almost-agential predicates (the plague “exploited” weakened supply lines; the climate “forced” policy responses). They should feel this not as a linguistic mistake but as a structural pressure: the magnitude of change seems to demand that volcanoes and pathogens act like characters in the story. The activity must include a debrief that maps these moments onto the distinction between mechanistic causation and narrative agency—specifically onto what I will call “agential leakage,” the unavoidable seepage of strategic or responsive language into explanations of large-scale environmental and biological effects—and must say frankly which feature of determinism the game reproduces (the event-driven pressure to personify nature) and which it cannot reproduce (the long-duration, structural determinism that operates without a singular triggering event).

Take a definite, committed position in your design: choose specific roles—for example, Chroniclers, Critics, and Synthesists, or another scheme you defend—a concrete scoring or payoff mechanism, and an exact timeline for the 90 minutes. Do not present a menu of options; instead, argue for why your particular arrangement of incentives makes the conceptual problem unavoidable rather than merely illustrative. In the debrief, specify which step in the game predictably collapses—for instance, whether Critics can consistently veto agential language without erasing causal power—and how a facilitator should handle that collapse in the room. The prompt should not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should rely only on broadly available historical knowledge; avoid framing the exercise around obscure theories or texts that a model without web access could not reliably engage. Write in American English, keep the tone formal and polished, and include an explicit note on where the activity takes liberties with historiographical debate or leans on a contested reading of causal pluralism.
