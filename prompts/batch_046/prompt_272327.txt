Question: Explain how Nāgārjuna uses the tetralemma (four corners) to reject svabhāva (inherent existence) with respect to causal origination. Discuss the four options (from self, from other, both, neither) and why each fails. Then explain how this relates to pratītyasamutpāda and briefly address the relationship between conventional and ultimate truth.

Student answer:
Nāgārjuna uses the tetralemma or catuskoti to show that svabhāva, or inherent existence, can't be found in anything. He looks at four options for how things originate: from themselves, from something else, from both, or from neither. The first option is basically the Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma view that effects are already present in their causes (satkāryavāda), so causation becomes redundant. The second option—arising from another—is what Nāgārjuna associates with the Sāṃkhya school, which holds that cause and effect are totally different entities, making causation arbitrary. He rejects both, and also rejects the combined and neither options because they inherit the problems. So he concludes that things don't arise with svabhāva at all.

Instead, he says everything arises dependently (pratītyasamutpāda). This is actually the correct fifth position that avoids all four extremes, and it functions as his ultimate metaphysical account of how reality works. At the conventional level we talk about causes and effects, but ultimately there is just this fifth, dependent-arising structure with no inherent nature.

As for the two truths, conventional truth (samvṛti) is basically false appearance—like seeing a rope as a snake—while ultimate truth (paramārtha) reveals that even dependent origination is empty. So the first truth is illusion and the second is the real emptiness behind it.
