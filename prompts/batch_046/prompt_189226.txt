I recently learned that the oral tradition maintained by my family and our neighbors about the 1974 closure of the local textile mill—remembered collectively as a unified strike broken by outside scabs—conflicts sharply with recently digitized payroll records and county newspaper archives, which indicate a gradual, non-unionized attrition with no evidence of organized labor action. Because this discrepancy has become personally significant to me, I am designing a final examination for my graduate seminar on collective memory and historiography that requires students to work through the conceptual tension rather than summarize standard authors.

Please write one essay question suitable for a 90-minute exam at the graduate level, expecting a response of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words. The question should present a generalized version of the conflict I describe: a community remembers an event as an intentional collective action carrying specific moral and identity-forming significance, while the archival record depicts the same events as fragmented, unintended, and morally neutral. Students must evaluate three stances: (a) archival realism, which holds that the record corrects the memory; (b) mnemonic sovereignty, which grants the community's self-understanding precedence regardless of the record; and (c) a third position—define it within the question as narrative co-constitution—according to which the causal-historical sequence and the social fact of collective identification are not competing descriptions of a single object but descriptions of different objects altogether. The prompt must require students to argue which stance best handles the case, to examine whether mnemonic sovereignty collapses into epistemic injustice against the archive or whether archival realism commits a category error by treating collective identity as an empirically falsifiable claim, and to defend a specific conclusion with conceptual precision rather than authorial summary.

Then provide a model answer of approximately 800 to 1,000 words, written in American English, that takes a clear position and demonstrates the analytical moves required for top marks.

Finally, provide a marking scheme totaling 25 points. The scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain—for instance, correctly distinguishing the causal sequence from the social fact of collective identification, identifying the category-error risk in archival realism, assessing whether mnemonic sovereignty is self-undermining, and applying the co-constitution framework without reducing it to relativism. For each of five must-hit points, state the marks allocated and include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail that point. Also list specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as treating the conflict as purely empirical, invoking Halbwachs or Nora without applying their distinctions, or neglecting the moral dimension of collective identification.

The exam question and model answer must not contain bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and should not depend on knowledge of obscure theories outside standard philosophy of history and collective memory studies. Present all of this in American English.
