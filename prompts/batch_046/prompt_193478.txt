I am preparing materials for an interdisciplinary seminar on self-reference and symbolic cognition, and I want a poem that performs philosophical work rather than merely illustrating a familiar concept. Please write a villanelle—nineteen lines, two refrains, a strict ABA rhyme scheme—spoken from the position of the capital-letter symbol in Hofstadter’s account of the self as a strange loop: not a soul or substance, but a self-referential pattern that emerges when a symbol embedded in a substrate points back to the very pattern it instantiates. The two refrains should be, respectively, the loop’s internal claim—"I am the pattern that reads itself"—and an external interruption—"You are the substrate that hears me"—with the poem’s formal machinery enacting the theory rather than describing it.

Specifically, each repetition of the refrains should shift grammatical role—subject becoming object, speaker becoming spoken—so that by the final couplet the two lines have exchanged syntactic positions, mirroring the idea that selfhood can migrate across substrates without dissolving its loop-structure. Use slant rhyme or identical rhyme precisely at the points where the loop closes, so that the sound-pattern itself performs the recursion. I want the result accurate enough that someone familiar with strange loops recognizes the syntax as the argument, and compelling enough that someone unfamiliar with the theory still feels the vertigo of a voice consuming its own speaker. Do not mention Gödel by name or supply page citations; let the formal recursion carry the mathematical analogy, and present the poem as a single, self-contained artifact with no appended notes, bibliography, or explanatory commentary.
