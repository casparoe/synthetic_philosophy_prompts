I was prepping my grad seminar on AI safety via debate—the Irving/Christiano-style protocol where two models argue in front of a weaker judge—and my colleague mentioned a move she keeps seeing that neither of us can place.

The pattern goes like this: Debater A wants to defend some risky course of action. Instead of arguing for it head-on, they construct an extremely precise taxonomy—say, splitting 'alignment failures' into 'outer misalignment' and 'inner misalignment' with boundary conditions that just happen to place all the scary counterexamples in the second category, which is then declared 'outside the scope for this round.' The taxonomy isn't false; it's just one of a dozen reasonable ways to slice the space. But once it's locked in, every objection B raises either gets sorted into the 'already accounted for' bin, or dismissed as addressing the wrong type. B can point out that the framing is arbitrary, but then B sounds pedantic—like they're quibbling over definitions rather than engaging with substance. Meanwhile A never technically lied or concealed evidence; they just made the space of admissable rebuttals assymetrical. The judge, especially if it's a smaller model, tends to read A as responsive and B as evasive.

I've seen analogues in legal reasoning, but in AI debate it feels more dangerous because the judge can't always recognize the framing move as a strategic move. Is there an established name for this kind of obfuscation-by-taxonomy? I'm not satisfied with just 'framing' or 'moving the goalposts'—this is more specific: the strategic selection of an ostensibly neutral classificatory scheme that silently loads the conclusion into the premises about what counts as relevant. If there's no term, how would you draw its boundaries, and how is it different from ordinary question-begging or from just defining your terms advantageously? I want to know whether the debate literature treats it as a distinct failure mode, or whether it just gets folded into generic 'obfuscated arguments.'
