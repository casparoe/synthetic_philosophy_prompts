Since my daughter was born six months ago, I have been trying to determine what I actually owe to people who will not exist for centuries, and whether my judgments about climate obligations form anything coherent. Here are my gut verdicts on concrete cases.

First, obligations to the near future. I think a wealthy nation should accept a sustained 4% reduction in GDP now to prevent catastrophic agricultural disruption in 2100. That feels clearly required. But I do not think it should accept a 20% reduction now merely to preserve the exact present coastline for people in 2300, especially if those people will live in climate-adapted societies and can relocate without comparable harm. Distance combined with adaptability weakens the duty sharply.

Second, specific harm versus diffuse contribution. I think it is wrong to site a long-lived toxic storage facility in a region if there is a meaningful risk it will poison a specific, identifiable community living there in 300 years. Yet I do not think my personal carbon footprint wrongs any specific future individual—there is no one I can point to and say “I harmed you”—even though it contributes to general warming that makes future lives somewhat worse. This distinction feels central, but I am unsure it survives scrutiny.

Third, present need versus distant preservation. I think directing all charitable resources to a trust fund for people in 2400 rather than relieving present extreme poverty is clearly wrong; present suffering takes priority. At the same time, I think we should preserve an irreplaceable watershed ecosystem primarily for the sake of people in 2200 who will not be able to recreate it, even if developing it now would produce more welfare for present people. So preservation can outweigh present development, but only up to a point that does not compete with present desperate need.

The pair I keep returning to: I think having my daughter was not morally wrong, despite knowing her lifetime emissions will be substantial, and I do not think people in my position are required to remain childless for climate reasons. Yet I also think it would have been genuinely admirable—not foolish—to make that sacrifice, and I feel uneasy treating her existence as a complete moral excuse for the emissions she will cause. Is there a principle—perhaps about wronging versus failing to benefit, or about identity-dependence and the non-identity problem—that saves both verdicts without ad hoc adjustments? Or must one of them be abandoned?

Be explicit about which of my verdicts the best account cannot accommodate. Finally, if my framing rests on a confused premise—perhaps in how I apply the non-identity problem, or in treating future generations as a single collective subject of rights—say so plainly rather than accommodating it politely.
