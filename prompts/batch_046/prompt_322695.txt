I've been thinking about this thing that happens on social media and in universities now, where someone says something that violates a norm and instead of a court or formal disciplinary board you get this rapid stacking of sanctions—deplatforming, disinvitation, boycott, algorithmic suppression—and then sometimes theres a path back, but only if the person does a public apology that hits certain keywords. My roommate and I were arguing about whether this is actually punishment or just a kind of collective filtering mechanism. He thinks its obviously retributive because people clearly want the offender to suffer. I think it looks more like norm maintenance or maybe restoritive justice, except theres no actual harmed community sitting in a circle—its just thousands of strangers coordinating through likes and shares. But heres the part that confuses me: in criminal law we usually say punishment requires a sovereign authority, like the state, and it has to be intentional. Here theres no single agent deciding the sentence, and yet the outcome is exclusion from public life.

So what philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Does it only make sense if you reject the idea that punishment needs a central authority, and instead think moral desert can be distributed across a crowd? Or can a consequentialist about norms explain the whole thing without any talk of desert at all? And theres this weird tension: the system often demands a public apology for reinstatement, which looks like its trying to restore relationships, but the mechanism is still exclusion and reputational damage. So is the implicit theory here that punishment should be both expressive—communicating that the norm was violated—and rehabilitative, but without any of the procedural safeguards we demand from state punishment?
