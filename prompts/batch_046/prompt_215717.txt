My uncle suffered a catastrophic brain injury three weeks ago and remains in an intensive care unit. Because he is a registered organ donor and the family must make decisions about his care, two of my relatives have been arguing about whether he is dead. I am writing to ask you to adjudicate their exchange, because I need to understand the conceptual structure at stake before any further steps are taken.

The disagreement is between my cousin Elena and my aunt Mara.

Elena argues that the hospital’s neurological examination showed absence of all brainstem reflexes, no spontaneous respiration, and no cerebral blood flow. By the medical and legal standard in our jurisdiction, that constitutes death. The heart continues only because of mechanical ventilation and vasopressors. To claim he is still alive is to confuse the functioning of individual organs with the life of the organism as an integrated whole. The brain is the coordinating center; without it, there is no unified biological life, only a collection of maintained tissues. The dead donor rule requires that death precede any procurement, and he already satisfies that criterion, since the loss of integrative brain function is irreversible.

Mara responds that his heart is still beating with minimal support, his body regulates temperature, and he has shown spinal reflex movements. If we declare him dead solely because of brain failure, we are making a philosophical choice, not observing a biological fact. The heart and lungs can sustain an integrated body without cerebral function, and history includes patients who lived for years with minimal brain activity while retaining brainstem reflexes and spontaneous breathing—precisely the capacities my uncle lacks. Furthermore, if we accept brain death, we must accept that a person can be dead in one jurisdiction and alive in another, since some legal systems require circulatory cessation. That relativism proves death is not being discovered but decided. She adds that institutional pressure around organ donation makes the brain-death standard look convenient rather than true.

Elena replies that temperature regulation and reflex movements are isolated brainstem and spinal cord activities, not evidence of integrated organismal function. The comparison to long-term minimal consciousness is misleading, because those patients retain the brainstem reflexes and spontaneous breathing that my uncle has lost. As for jurisdictional variation, that reflects legal conservatism, not biological ambiguity. The integrative function of the brain is unique: no other organ coordinates respiration, circulation, and homeostasis simultaneously. Once that coordination is irreversibly lost, the organism has ceased to exist, even if individual parts continue. She also notes that the dead donor rule is an ethical safeguard, not a bias; it prohibits causing death through procurement, and since he is already dead by neurological criteria, removing organs does not kill him.

Mara says that Elena is treating integration as if it were a purely empirical property, but integrated whole is a normative concept disguised as biology. A body on a heart-lung machine is also kept integrated by technology; we do not call that patient dead. The reason we privilege brain failure is practical—organ transplantation—not metaphysical. And if the standard is truly the irreversible loss of coordination, then we should wait for circulatory collapse, because only then is every vital function irreversibly lost without technological replacement. Otherwise we risk declaring death based on a theory of what the brain does, rather than on what has actually stopped, especially when institutional interests align neatly with that theory.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the four positions above, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. In particular, I want to know whether Mara’s charge that integration smuggles in a normative judgment is a serious objection to Elena’s view, and whether Elena’s reply about jurisdictional variation successfully answers Mara’s concern about relativism. I also want you to note what, if anything, both sides are missing—especially regarding whether there is a principled way to distinguish the loss of integrative function from the mere replacement of that function by technology, and whether the dead donor rule introduces a non-biological consideration that either party has failed to address.
