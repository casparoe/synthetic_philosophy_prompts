I have come to hold a qualified version of historical inevitabilism about macro-structural transitions: specifically, that the emergence of sustained industrial-capitalist growth in Northwestern Europe by the late eighteenth century was not merely probable ex post, but structurally overdetermined in a way that makes nearby counterfactual worlds—in which a single contingent factor, such as a particular dynastic crisis, a lost war, or a regional harvest failure, is altered—incoherent at the system level. On this view, local perturbations are absorbed by deeper demographic, energetic, and institutional path-dependencies; the macro-trajectory reproduces itself within a narrow temporal band regardless of surface-level variation. I do not claim universal physical determinism, only that for this class of macro-historical events the space of historically viable alternatives is far thinner than conventional counterfactual history assumes.

What would have to be true for this position to be wrong? I want an honest assessment of its exposure—what it risks, where it might be insulated, and whether that insulation is a virtue or a vice—not merely a list of familiar objections. In particular:

- Which kinds of evidence—comparative-historical, quantitative, archival, or modeling-based—would genuinely threaten the claim that these transitions are overdetermined rather than merely highly path-dependent? Distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would only be awkward.
- The view depends on a sharp distinction between structural pressure and contingent trigger, and on treating system boundaries and temporal bands as principled rather than ad hoc. Could those distinctions collapse? Under what conditions?
- Are there analogies from other domains—evolutionary theory, climate dynamics, or the philosophy of scientific explanation—that either license or undermine treating historical systems as the kind of system for which inevitability claims are legitimate?
- Has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If every apparent counterexample is met by widening the temporal band, redefining the system boundary, or treating the deviation as a local perturbation that eventually reconverges, does that render the position methodologically defective for a claim of this kind?
- Rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Which ones would a committed defender of this view concede as genuinely counting against it, and which would they dismiss as misunderstandings of the claim?

Please address what predictions or exclusions this view makes that rival accounts—strong contingency theories, methodological individualism in history, or quantitative counterfactual modeling—do not share.
