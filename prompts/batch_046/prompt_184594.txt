I'm in this philosophy of action course and we spent like half an hour on that digging wasp, sphex ichneumoneus, the one that drags a cricket to her burrow, checks inside, then pulls the cricket in, but if you move the cricket away while she's checking she just starts the check over again from scratch, like she's stuck in a loop she can't interupt. The prof kept saying people use this as an analogy for human compulsions or addictions — that you're "sphexish" when you run through the same routine even though the situation changed, and that free will must be the flexibility that breaks the loop, like a computer subroutine that can actually handle an interupt instead of restarting. But then my friend pointed out that a thermostat is flexible in responding to temperature shifts and nobody thinks it has free will, so flexibility alone can't be doing the work, and also that if someone has really stable values and always acts on them consistently, they might look sphexish from the outside even though their rigidity comes from commitment not mechanism. I want you to audit that analogy properly: map out what corresponds to what between the wasp's burrow-check loop and human habit or addiction, where the computer-subroutine comparison holds and where it collapses, what features of biological rigidity the analogy quietly imports that human psychology might lack, and whether the conclusion that flexibility equals agency actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Also, does the "reverse sphex" worry — that consistent value-driven behavior can look mechanically rigid — undermine the whole framing, and is there a better analogy for mechanical rigidity that doesn't make flexibility sound like magic?
