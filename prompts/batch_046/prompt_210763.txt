So I was reading these notes from a philosophy of well-being seminar I’m not actually enrolled in, and my friend tried to summarize tranquilism for me over coffee. He said it’s the view that the only thing that matters for a good life is the absence of craving and suffering — positive pleasure doesn’t add value, it’s just the temporary return to an affective baseline, like reaching a hedonic zero-point. He claimed Schopenhauer basically invented this position, arguing that all pleasure is just the will being momentarily unsatisfied, so it’s ontologically negative rather than a real positive good. From there the summary jumps straight to ethics: if pleasure has zero weight and suffering has negative weight, then we should never risk bringing about a happy life if there’s any chance of suffering, which means tranquilism is basically negative utilitarianism wearing well-being language. The notes also brought in affective homeostatis — the idea that people always rebound to a baseline — to argue that chasing pleasure is like running on a treadmill, so the rational strategy is just to lower your cravings until you hit a stable neutral state. I feel like there’s definatly a sleight of hand between describing what well-being is and deriving rules about what we ought to do, and maybe Schopenhauer’s metaphysics of the will is being treated as part of the philosophical view when it’s really just his personal explanation. Is this summary actually fair? I want to separate what tranquilists really claim about well-being from what’s smuggled in from negative utilitarianism or pop-psychology, and whether the slide from 'pleasure has no value' to 'we shouldn’t create happy lives' follows from anything in the view itself, or if it’s an extra normative premise the summary added without notice.
