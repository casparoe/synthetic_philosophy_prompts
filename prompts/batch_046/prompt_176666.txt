I work as a mediator for a small co-op housing board and lately half our meetings turn into people saying "you dont respect who I am" or "you erased my identity" when really they just changed a rule or used a different name. I was reading about buddhist anatta - that there's no permanent self, just five skandhas that shift around - and I want to turn that into a practical checklist I can actually use sitting between two angry neighbors, usually with about fifteen minutes before someone storms out. I'm not a philosopher and whatever I recommend has to be signed off by our board chair who hates abstract talk, so the procedure needs to be something I can run without citing books.

The idea is I check each skandha - form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness - and ask whether the dispute is about a real change in that layer or just someone clinging to an old picture. But I also want to add a sixth check I made up called narrative continuity, based on how people in my board fight more about their life-story being interrupted than about any physical thing. For each check give me the exact question to ask, what counts as enough to stop with "this is values not facts", and where I have to make a judgment call. Include the hard cases: where does the procedure say no to an identity claim, where does it hand the decision to me because it's values, and where will it misfire? Then apply it to three actual-style disputes: a dad who wont call his grown daughter by her new name because "shes still my little girl", a tenant who claims a banned hallway decoration is part of their religious identity, and one where the answer feels wrong - maybe someone who quit our shared garden project and now says the group is denying their "true self" as a gardener. Make sure it says what anatta can actually settle and what it cant.
