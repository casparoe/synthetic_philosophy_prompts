I have been working through the adaptationism debate in a philosophy of biology seminar, and rather than arguing over definitions I want to see whether my intuitive classifications of particular biological traits cohere under a single principle. Here is the pattern of my verdicts. The panda’s thumb strikes me as a classic exaptation—structurally it is a modified radial sesamoid bone, not an optimized grasping digit—so while selection refined its use, its origin is spandrel-like; I therefore reject pure adaptationist accounts of its initial form, though I accept that current maintenance involves selection. The human chin, by contrast, feels like a pure spandrel: a structural consequence of reduced jaw size and dental changes in Homo sapiens, with post-hoc sexual-selection narratives sounding implausibly contrived to me, so I treat adaptationist explanations here as likely just-so stories. The aposematic coloration of poison dart frogs is, in my view, unambiguously adaptive—costly, specialized, and tightly coupled to predator avoidance—representing a clear case where selection for the trait dominates its history.

The mammalian appendix is the case I keep reversing: if it is largely a vestigial remnant of the cecum with only incidental immune tissue, then it is a spandrel that selection has not fully eliminated; but if its lymphoid tissue is actively maintained because it serves as a reservoir for gut flora during infection, then it may be a maintained adaptation despite its origin. I currently lean toward the spandrel-with-co-option view, but I am unsure whether co-option transforms the category. The middle-ear ossicles present a deeper tension: they originated as jaw articulation bones in early synapsids, which makes their origin spandrel-like relative to hearing, yet they are now exquisitely tuned for acoustic transmission—so are they adaptations, spandrels, or sequentially both? I want to say they are exaptations that subsequently became adaptations, but I need a principle that distinguishes this sequential story from mere just-so storytelling. Meanwhile, I reject adaptationist explanations in evolutionary psychology—say, that partisan tribalism is a direct adaptation to Pleistocene inter-group conflict—because the cognitive mechanisms involved are domain-general, the environment has radically changed, and the design specificity required for adaptation seems absent; yet I accept that language, despite arising in the context of expanding brain size, is likely a genuine adaptation rather than a spandrel of encephalization, because its structural features are too costly and too specifically configured for communication to be accidental byproducts. So the principle I am groping for might distinguish selection-for from selection-of, or historical origin from current functional maintenance, but I cannot apply it consistently without either forcing the middle-ear bones into an artificial either-or box or allowing evolutionary psychology to claim adaptation status whenever a behavior is useful.

Can you formulate a precise principle—or a small family of competing principles—that captures these verdicts, stating which clause is motivated by each case? Please also apply it to two further cases I have not mentioned: first, the belly button as an inevitable morphological consequence of umbilical separation, which nevertheless hosts a microbiome with potential immunomodulatory effects; and second, the claim that human laughter is an adaptation for social bonding rather than a spandrel of respiratory and vocal control mechanisms. And be explicit about which of my verdicts—especially the appendix and the evolutionary psychology case—must be overturned if the principle is to remain simple rather than baroque.
