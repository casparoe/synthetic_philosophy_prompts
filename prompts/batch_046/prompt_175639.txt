I've been working through F.H. Bradley's argument in Appearance and Reality regarding the regress that arises when relations are treated as fully real entities that must, in turn, be connected to their relata by further relations. In a reading group I'm organizing, we are focusing less on historical exegesis and more on whether the major responses to this regress can solve what I will call the "binding problem for falsehood"—the challenge of explaining how a false proposition possesses enough internal unity to be one definite falsehood rather than a scattered collection of terms, without requiring that all of its constituent relations actually obtain in reality.

Please rank the following five philosophical strategies from 1 (most adequate) to 5 (least adequate) specifically with respect to how well they resolve this binding problem for falsehood while simultaneously blocking Bradley's infinite regress. The strategies are: (A) treating relations as internal to the natures of the relata so that no external connector is needed; (B) accepting an infinite hierarchy of relating relations but denying that the hierarchy is vicious because each level is metaphysically dependent on the one below; (C) appealing to a special mental or intellectual act of synthesis that binds terms together, where this act is not itself a relation of the same kind; (D) taking propositions or states of affairs as primitive structured entities whose unity is basic and not constructed out of relations; and (E) denying that there is any real metaphysical unity in propositions at all, treating false propositions as merely misleading grammatical strings with no internal structure requiring explanation. For each assigned rank, provide a single sentence identifying whether the strategy succeeds or fails primarily on relational grounds—internal, hierarchical, mental, primitive, or illusory—and what specific philosophical cost it incurs regarding the reality or intelligibility of false judgments.
