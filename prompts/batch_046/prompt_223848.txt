Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers debating whether care ethics can ground obligations to distant strangers without collapsing into universalist consequentialism. The first philosopher maintains that care is structurally anchored in asymmetric vulnerability within concrete, face-to-face relations; extending it globally, she argues, strips away the normative specificity—direct responsiveness to particular needs—that distinguishes care ethics from other moral frameworks. The second philosopher counters that the core commitment of care ethics is not physical proximity but attention to particularity, which can be preserved through mediated structures such as institutional advocacy and narrative imagination. Their exchange should be genuinely dialectical: the first should press the second on whether mediated care retains the phenomenological weight of direct responsiveness, and on whether scaling care risks reducing particularity to an abstract calculus; the second should challenge the first on whether asymmetry describes an empirical feature of relations or functions as an unstated normative criterion that care ethics must independently justify, and on whether rejecting distant obligations implicitly privileges the already-privileged. Write in British English, maintain a formal tone throughout, and ensure neither interlocutor is presented as a strawman.
