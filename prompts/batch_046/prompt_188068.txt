As a novelist long preoccupied with the architecture of narrative—how every story demands a crisis, a reversal, and some form of resolution—I have grown suspicious of the word "collapse" as it is applied to civilizations. I am currently auditing a course on comparative historical sociology, and I keep noticing how the Bronze Age systems crisis, the so-called Classic Maya collapse, and Easter Island's deforestation are taught with the rhythmic inevitability of a five-act tragedy: rising complexity, hidden stress, sudden rupture, silence. Write an essay that treats "collapse" not merely as an archaeological problem but as a conceptual one. Examine whether these three cases genuinely represent societal failure, or whether they are better understood as structural reconfigurations—interregional trade networks dissolving and re-forming elsewhere, lowland Maya populations dispersing into highland continuity, Rapa Nui societies adapting after resource stress—that only appear catastrophic because we read archaeological absence as narrative absence. You must weigh both interpretations seriously, but do not conclude with a non-committal balance sheet. Defend a clear verdict: is "collapse" an indispensable analytical category for understanding large-scale societal change, or is it largely a projection of our own dramatic expectations onto the past? If you argue for retirement or refinement of the term, explain what historians and policymakers should replace it with; if you defend it, explain what distinguishes genuine collapse from mere transformation in a way that does not depend on the observer's taste for tragedy.
