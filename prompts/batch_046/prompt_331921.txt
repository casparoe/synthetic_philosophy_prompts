I recently joined the advisory board of a regional sustainability initiative tasked with fifty-year resource and demographic forecasts, and in our first meetings Malthus was invoked both as a cautionary tale and, unexpectedly, as a model of predictive patience. Next month I am recording an interview for a philosophy-of-science podcast with a historian of economic thought—let us call her Dr. Elena Voss—who defends the specific claim that Malthus exemplifies a category she terms "temporally displaced verification": theories that accumulate what she calls "falsification debt" in the short run but can be structurally vindicated when the underlying mechanism operates on a longer amortization schedule than the original prediction allowed. I want eight to ten interview questions that prevent her from merely restating this framework. At least one question should press on how she distinguishes genuine temporal displacement from ad hoc rescue—particularly through the lens of falsification debt—anticipating that she will appeal to independent mechanistic confirmation; include a follow-up that asks what rate of "interest" on that debt would make the rescue indistinguishable from special pleading. At least one question should ask how her framework treats predictions that are not merely delayed but directionally reversed in intermediate periods—anticipating her reply that intermediate reversals are noise relative to the structural trend, with a follow-up probing whether that move collapses her view into unfalsifiability. Another question should address comparisons with other long-run predictions—say, in climate dynamics or epidemiological transition theory—where early falsification was followed by later partial vindication, and ask what criteria separate legitimate rescue from post-hoc rationalization in those parallel cases; anticipate her answer that mechanistic continuity across scales is the decisive factor, and follow up by asking whether that criterion can be applied without already assuming the conclusion. I also want one question about whether her defense requires treating Malthus's arithmetic-geometric framing as a literal quantitative model or as a structural metaphor, anticipating that she will defend a middle-ground "mechanism-first" reading, with a follow-up that presses on whether that reading preserves enough of Malthus's specific claims to count as rescuing the theory rather than replacing it. Please construct the sequence so that each follow-up is keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Finally, include an explicit epistemic framing: in her responses, she should clearly separate what is settled among historians of Malthus—such as the actual content and revisions between the 1798 and 1803 editions—from what remains genuinely contested, including her own "falsification debt" framework, and she should not present the "right but early" interpretation as if it were the consensus view in the literature.
