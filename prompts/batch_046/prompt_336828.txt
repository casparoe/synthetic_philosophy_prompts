Read Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life through the lens of historical contingency and chaos theory, specifically the "for want of a nail" problem of how small perturbations amplify through nonlinear historical systems. Clarence's vision of Pottersville—where George Bailey's absence cascades into Harry's drowning, Mary's withdrawn spinsterhood, and the town's corruption—seems to dramatize sensitive dependence on initial conditions. But treat it philosophically: does the film model causation as Lewisian counterfactual dependence, with each absent George producing the next outcome deterministically, or as dynamical chaos, where a minor initial difference amplifies in genuinely unpredictable directions? Does its branching timeline commit to a "great man" theory of history, or can it be read systemically? Identify where the film's moral ordering—Pottersville decays precisely because George is missing—confuses narrative determinism with genuine historical chaos, and say whether the film knows the difference. Which scenes best illuminate the distinction, and where does the work resist being reduced to an illustration of either theory?
