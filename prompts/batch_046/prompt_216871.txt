The student's answer:
Moore's paradox is shown by sentences like It is raining, but I don't believe it is raining. The strange thing is that this can be completely true—maybe I am outside in the rain but, because of self-deception, I genuinely do not believe it—yet it sounds absurd to say. It is not a logical contradiction like the liar paradox (This statement is false), because the liar paradox creates an impossible loop with truth-values, whereas Moore's sentence just describes a possible state of affairs.

G. E. Moore introduced this in a 1942 lecture. He said the sentence is not formally contradictory but is nevertheless absurd in the first-person present tense. The best explanation is pragmatic: when someone asserts P, they conversationally imply that they believe P. So denying the belief cancels the implicature without producing a logical contradiction. Moore himself resolved this by appealing to Gricean implicature: the speaker violates a conversational maxim about belief, which explains the absurdity without any deep psychological problem.

However, this resolution has a limitation. Even if assertion carries an implicature of belief, people can suffer from self-deception or implicit bias, so the link between what we say and what we believe is not absolute. In fact, the paradox reveals a hidden logical tension masked by surface grammar: the first clause commits the speaker to P, while the second clause denies the corresponding mental state, and together they form a contradiction that only looks non-logical because of how English sentences are structured. So Moore's paradox ultimately shows that belief must be transparent to the subject—if it were not, then Moorean utterances would be common rather than absurd. Therefore, the paradox is really about the conditions of self-knowledge.
