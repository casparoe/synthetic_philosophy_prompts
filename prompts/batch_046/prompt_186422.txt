Having spent three decades measuring adaptive dynamics in microbial populations, I have recently begun studying philosophy of mind and am troubled by attempts to define intelligence without circularity. Here is an argument I have constructed that seems to collapse the concept entirely, and I cannot locate the precise error.

(1) Intelligence is best defined functionally: it is open-ended, domain-general problem-solving that is not specified in advance by evolutionary programming or system design.

(2) By this standard, large language models exhibit intelligence in linguistic and logical domains, though they are trained on specific corpora; bacterial quorum sensing integrates chemical, thermal, and mechanical signals across varied contexts, though it is usually dismissed as reflex. The boundary between domain-general and domain-specific therefore appears to rest on an observer's judgment about openness rather than on any formal property of the system.

(3) If we apply (1) consistently, either both LLMs and bacteria qualify as intelligent, or neither does, since both solve genuinely novel problems within input spaces that are constrained but not fully predetermined.

(4) The first option renders intelligence co-extensive with adaptive information processing, thereby including immune antibody generation, river erosion, and market equilibria. The second option denies intelligence to entities—such as a chimpanzee fashioning an unprecedented tool—that intuitively possess it.

(5) One might reply by restricting intelligence to systems that represent problems to themselves, but that requires a prior theory of representation or consciousness, which is precisely the commitment the functional definition sought to avoid.

(6) Hence any functional criterion either proves too much or quietly smuggles in the biological or phenomenological assumptions it claimed to exclude.

Which premise should be rejected? Is there a principled, non-circular way to draw the domain-general boundary that does not depend on our prior intuitions about which substrates count? Or does the argument contain a hidden equivocation between solving a problem and representing it?
