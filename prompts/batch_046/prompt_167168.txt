I'm on the city's cultural heritage funding board and were meeting thursday to vote on a $400k grant for the museam's proposed show "Reason's Double Bind: Enlightenment Archives and Colonial Governance, 1750-1850." The curators want to display canonical Enlightenment texts—Kant, Condorcet, Diderot—alongside colonial administrative records from the same archives: tax ledgers, ethnographic field notes, slave-ship manifests—to ask whether the same rationalist methods that produced human rights discourse also produced racial taxonomy and bureaucratic domination. Half the board thinks this is exactly the kind of critical history we should fund; the other half, including a community historian who spoke at our last session, argues that putting them in the same gallery "juxtaposes" them as if they were separate traditions, when in fact Enlightenment reason was the operating system of colonial domination, not its innocent twin. She wants us to reject the grant unless the museum restructures the show around domination as the primary frame, with Enlightenment texts shown only as instruments of governance.

I need to draft the memo i'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course—approve, reject, or approve with conditions and a timeline—and the reasoning has to actually engage her point, not just say "both sides matter." Our board's mandate is to fund exhibitions that present historically grounded arguments to the public, not ones that take a predetermined political line, but we also have this weird "framing reproduction" clause in our policy: funded shows must not reproduce the very framing they claim to critique. So there's a real tension—if we approve, are we endorsing the "juxtaposition" framing that she says reproduces the problem by treating reason and domination as twins? If we reject, are we deciding a philosophical question—whether the Enlightenment was mainly progress or mainly domination—by administrative fiat?

Also, one detail that might matter: the curators plan to use a "layered caption" system where each Enlightenment text is paired with a specific administrative document that cites it directly—like a colonial governor referencing Locke on property rights. To be honest i'm not sure whether that makes the framing genuinely critical or just decorative, and i think the memo should take a position on that. Can you write it with an actionable bottom line, maybe conditions tied to that caption structure, and honestly treat the strongest reason for going the other way?
