Question: In patients who have undergone complete surgical section of the corpus callosum, the left and right cerebral hemispheres can operate with markedly reduced interhemispheric communication. Classic experiments show that stimuli presented to the right visual field are verbally reportable by the left hemisphere, while stimuli presented to the left visual field can guide non-verbal actions—such as selecting an object with the left hand—without the left hemisphere being able to articulate what was seen. Some theorists take this to show that two distinct streams of consciousness, or even two subjects of experience, inhabit a single body. Others maintain that a single subject persists, albeit with severely disrupted internal access.

Evaluate the argument that the right hemisphere’s goal-directed, apparently recognition-guided behavior establishes the presence of a “silent subject” with its own intentional states. In your evaluation, carefully distinguish phenomenal unity—the sense that experiences belong to one and the same subject of consciousness—from access unity, which concerns the availability of information across cognitive subsystems for report and action coordination. Additionally, consider the following hypothetical extension: imagine a modified split-brain patient fitted with an artificial interhemispheric bridge that restores smooth motor coordination between the hemispheres but still prevents the left hemisphere from verbally accessing or reporting the contents of right-hemisphere processing. Does this hypothetical case strengthen or weaken the claim that split-brain phenomena undermine phenomenal unity? Defend your answer.

Student Answer:

The split-brain studies demonstrate that after the corpus callosum is cut, the hemispheres become functionally independent. The left hemisphere dominates speech and can describe objects shown to the right visual field. The right hemisphere, linked to the left hand, can match or retrieve objects shown to the left visual field, yet it cannot speak. This is direct proof that two separate consciousnesses exist in one skull, because the right hemisphere clearly understands the task and acts on its own intentions without the left hemisphere knowing anything about it.

To analyze this properly we need the distinction between phenomenal unity and access unity. Phenomenal unity is the feeling that all of my experiences belong to one me. Access unity is just the brain’s ability to share information between modules. In split-brain patients, phenomenal unity is destroyed: the right hemisphere has its own private experiences that the left hemisphere never feels, so there is no longer one subject but two. The hypothetical bridge case confirms this conclusion. Even when the bridge restores behavioral coordination, the left hemisphere still cannot report right-hemisphere contents. That shows coordination is not the same as consciousness, and that phenomenal unity was never present to begin with—only access unity was artificially repaired.

Critics who defend a single-subject view usually claim that the right hemisphere is not conscious at all, just an automatic processor. This is implausible, because the right hemisphere shows emotional reactions and solves practical problems, which are clear markers of conscious intentionality. Meanwhile, the left hemisphere’s well-known confabulations—fabricating explanations for actions that were actually initiated by the right hemisphere—reveal that verbal report is not a reliable guide to where the real subject is located. In fact, the confabulation data suggest that the left hemisphere is more like a spokesperson than the true seat of agency. So the minimal conclusion is that there are at least two subjects, and possibly that verbal narration is peripheral to personal identity.

The hypothetical bridge does not rescue the unity view. If two hemispheres can coordinate behavior without sharing a phenomenal field, then unity was always a matter of access, not of being one conscious subject. Therefore, split-brain cases genuinely undermine the traditional assumption that consciousness must be unified in a single person.
