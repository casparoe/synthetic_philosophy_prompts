I'm constructing a framework for assessing theories of the meta-problem of consciousness—accounts of why we judge consciousness to present an explanatory gap that physical science seems unable to close—before I survey the current literature in detail. Write the full specification of desiderata: what any adequate theory in this area must be able to deliver. For each requirement, state the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind it, distinguish genuinely non-negotiable constraints from camp-specific prejudices, and flag any pair you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable. Keep the conceptual weight on the criteria themselves rather than on scoring existing views, though you may briefly indicate how phenomenal-concept strategies, illusionist accounts, and deflationary realist explanations fare.

Specifically, include and justify:

1. The explanatory boundary condition: the theory must distinguish explaining the source of our hard-problem intuition from explaining consciousness itself, with the case behind it—namely, that a successful explanation of the intuition should not automatically dissolve the underlying phenomenon, and that conflating the two levels changes the dialectic in ways that either over- or under-achieve.

2. The phenomenological fidelity constraint: it must account for the specific qualitative character of finding consciousness mysterious—why it feels different from finding, say, quantum entanglement or life mysterious—rather than treating the intuition as a generic cognitive error. Include the consideration of naive subjects who lack technical vocabulary but still report the gap.

3. The introspective-access compatibility requirement: the explanation must be consistent with the fact that subjects have direct but limited access to their own conscious states, without requiring that they possess tacit knowledge of neural mechanisms or representational architecture. Provide the contrasting case of trained philosophers versus untrained subjects.

4. The modality-independence check—an injected criterion—requiring that the theory explain why the hard-problem intuition can arise selectively across modalities (perceptual, emotional, conceptual) rather than uniformly, and whether it can accommodate a subject who finds emotional consciousness puzzling but perceptual consciousness transparent.

5. The cross-cultural stability desideratum: whether the intuition persists in populations without exposure to Western philosophical or Cartesian framing, and what this implies for explanations that depend on specific conceptual frameworks or linguistic practices.

6. The therapeutic neutrality condition—another injected criterion—stating that the theory's adequacy should not depend on whether its proponent wants the hard problem to be dissolved, preserved, or suspended, with the case being rival philosophers who share the explanatory data but differ on metaphysical ambition.

7. The non-deflationary boundary: which results about first-person authority, epistemic asymmetry, or the transparency of mental states the theory must preserve without reinstating a full-blown hard problem.

Then, briefly: how do phenomenal-concept strategies of the Chalmers-type, illusionist explanations treating the intuition as misfiring, and deflationary realist accounts score against this list? And which single desideratum, in your view, excludes the largest number of popular candidates?
