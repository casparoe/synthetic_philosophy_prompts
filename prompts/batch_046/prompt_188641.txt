I serve on the governing council of a highland watershed cooperative that manages irrigation rights through direct deliberation rather than market pricing or state regulation. Our founding charter restricts voting membership to persons who have maintained a primary residence within the watershed for at least eleven months of the preceding year, a rule originally designed to ensure that those who decide bear the immediate ecological consequences of water allocation. Recently, this boundary has been contested by three groups whose claims resist easy classification under the residence criterion: seasonal agricultural technicians who live in the valley for roughly four months each year to maintain the terraced irrigation channels and monitor soil salinity, arguing that their labor sustains the very system being governed and that excluding them creates a class of profoundly affected workers without voice; descendants of families displaced from the valley two generations ago by a state dam project, who hold no present property within the boundary but maintain that the cooperative's authority derives partly from an ancestral agreement that included their lineage, and who now seek representational seats on that historical rather than residential basis; and residents of an adjacent lowland region who claim that because our surplus runoff flows directly into their river system, any alteration in our diversion practices immediately changes their flood risk and crop yields, meaning they are causally affected enough to deserve inclusion regardless of geographic residence. Meanwhile, the permanent local residents insist that broadening membership beyond continuous presence would erode accountability, since neither the seasonal technicians, the displaced descendants, nor the downstream neighbors experience the same daily vulnerability to drought, soil erosion, or crop failure that those living inside the watershed endure.

I find each of these positions intuitively compelling in some dimension, yet I cannot articulate what principle ought to adjudicate among them without simply defaulting to my own unexamined preferences. To complicate matters further, a colleague recently proposed that we should determine membership through a counterfactual test: a person belongs in the circle of decision-makers only if, were they absent from the world, the outcome of our deliberation would differ in a way that materially affects their core interests. I am unsure whether this counterfactual standard clarifies the boundary or merely introduces new ambiguities about identity, causation, and the scope of moral consideration. Could you help me understand the philosophical structure underlying this dispute over who should sit in the circle of decision-makers, apply the relevant theoretical framework to determine where the boundary ought to be drawn, and specifically address whether this counterfactual criterion offers a coherent way to resolve the competing claims? Please respond entirely in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written as one might compose a careful and reflective letter.
