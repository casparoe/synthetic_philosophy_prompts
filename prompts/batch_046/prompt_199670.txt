Illustrate your analysis with the following scenario: an engineer knows that a cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator specifies an objective chance of exactly 0.5 for producing the value 1, and she also knows the specific seed and deterministic algorithm that fix the next output to be 1. Student answer: The Principal Principle, introduced by David Lewis in his 1973 book Counterfactuals, states that if an agent knows the objective chance of an event is x and lacks other relevant information, then her rational credence in that event should equal x. This serves as a bridge principle connecting physical probability with epistemic rationality. In the generator scenario, the statistical specification gives an objective chance of 0.5 for output 1. Because the engineer also knows the seed and algorithm, she possesses what philosophers call inadmissible evidence: information lying outside the chance law but causally upstream of it. According to the Principal Principle, when an agent holds inadmissible evidence she is required to suppress it and defer solely to the objective chance. Therefore, despite knowing that the deterministic sequence will yield 1, she should still assign a credence of exactly 0.5 to the next output being 1, because the principle mandates alignment with the specified distribution regardless of upstream causal knowledge. More broadly, the principle demonstrates that all probabilities are ultimately objective frequencies that rational agents must track; subjective Bayesian priors are only provisional approximations that should be abandoned once the true chance is known. Consequently, deference to objective chance is unconditional: the admissibility condition is not a restriction on when the principle applies, but rather a label for evidence that must be ignored.
