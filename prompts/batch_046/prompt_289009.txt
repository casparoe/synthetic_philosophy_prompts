I am drafting the final examination for a graduate-level epistemology seminar, and I want one 90-minute essay question that cannot be answered by summarizing Kyburg’s lottery paradox and Makinson’s preface paradox from standard textbooks. The prompt should require students to engage with a specific analytical framework I have been developing in class—what I call a “stability-under-reflection” constraint—which holds that a rational belief set must not contain pairs of beliefs that become jointly untenable once the agent reflects on her own epistemic limitations and on the structural dependencies among her pieces of evidence. The exam should ask students to apply this constraint to a hybrid case: a senior researcher who holds high credence in the failure of each of 500 individually risky hypotheses in a large-scale project (suggesting she should accept that at least one fails), yet also holds that the project as a whole will succeed because she believes the failure modes are systematically correlated rather than independent; she simultaneously includes a preface admitting she is likely wrong about some particular claims. Students must determine whether the stability constraint resolves both the lottery-style aggregation problem and the preface-style self-referential humility problem without either collapsing into full probabilism or licensing flat contradiction, and they must identify precisely where the constraint succeeds and where it merely relocates the tension.

Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of approximately 800 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit analytical moves totaling 25 marks. For each move, specify the point value, describe correct execution in one sentence, and include a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks—such as treating non-independence as automatically dissolving the lottery structure, or conflating epistemic humility with a direct contradiction rather than with a stability failure. The difficulty should come from forcing students to diagnose the interaction between aggregation rules and self-reference, not from recalling historical formulations.
