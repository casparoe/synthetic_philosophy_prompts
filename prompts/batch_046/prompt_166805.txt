I'm teaching a graduate seminar on Newton's bucket and the contemporary substantivalism-relationism dispute, and a student recently invoked the maxim "no difference without a difference-maker" to dismiss relationalist reconstructions of the bucket scenario. The student seemed to treat it as self-evident that if two universes differ only in which parts of absolute space are occupied, there is no genuine difference at all; but I suspect the maxim is doing far more work than its surface grammar reveals. Could you disambiguate the precise theses this slogan could be advancing—distinguishing, at minimum, an epistemic variant about detectability, a causal variant about physical mechanisms, a metaphysical variant about the identity of indiscernibles applied to spacetime points, and a semantic variant about the meaningfulness of attributing properties to unobservable structures? For each reading, indicate whether it is trivial, defensible, or false; whether a sophisticated relationist could consistently accept it; and, crucially, whether it actually delivers the anti-relationalist conclusion about absolute rotation in an otherwise empty universe. I am particularly interested in whether any plausible reading conflicts with the standard interpretation of general covariance in general relativity, where diffeomorphically equivalent models are routinely treated as representing the same physical situation despite differing in their assignment of spatiotemporal properties to bare manifold points. Present your analysis as a menu of interpretations rather than a single authoritative gloss, and indicate which reading the student in my seminar was likely relying on and why it may not do the work they assigned it.
