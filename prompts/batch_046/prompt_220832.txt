I just spent two hours arguing with a friend about whether the "Middle Ages," the "Renaissance," and the "Scientific Revolution" name real historical discontinuities or are later inventions that distort what actually happened. We kept talking past each other because we never mapped the actual logical space of positions. Please write a taxonomy tree — built from the splitting questions rather than from a roster of famous historians — that classifies views on whether these three labels denote genuine macro-structural breaks, useful pedagogical fictions, or actively misleading anachronisms.

The root question should be whether macro-period labels can ever track real discontinuities or are always retrospective constructions. From there, branch first by the kind of break claimed: institutional (changes in universities, state patronage, print infrastructure), conceptual (shifts in world-picture, method, or norms of explanation), or both — but force the tree to show whether these two tracks align or come apart. Then branch on the "Renaissance": is it a distinct epoch with its own institutional and conceptual profile, or is it an elite cultural fashion embedded within longer medieval and early-modern continuities? Next branch on the "Scientific Revolution": is it a discrete, physics-centered rupture anchored roughly between Copernicus and Newton, or a gradual, multi-disciplinary drift spanning medicine, alchemy, and natural philosophy with no clean boundary? Include a further split separating those who defend the tripartite scheme only as a negative definition of the Middle Ages (defined by the absence of modern state forms, print culture, and empirical method) from those who think it can stand positively on its own internal characteristics.

At each leaf, provide the position's name if it has one — for instance, "institutional discontinuity without conceptual rupture," "gradualist continuity," "Burckhardtian cultural epoch," "negative-definition tripartism" — or label it "unnamed" if the combination is defensible but apparently unoccupied. Each leaf must carry exactly one sentence stating its most awkward commitment: the consequence its defenders have to accept but rarely advertise. Build the tree from logical combinations of answers rather than from a list of scholars: lay out the diagnostic questions first, then locate named and unnamed positions inside that space, and flag any combination that is logically possible but apparently empty, with a brief diagnosis of whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect.

The diagnostic questions should be concrete enough that someone could answer them without knowing historiographical labels. For example: did the printing press and university curriculum change faster between 1450 and 1600 than between 1200 and 1350? Was the shift from Aristotelian to mechanistic explanation concentrated in one generation of physicists, or diffused across medicine and alchemy over two centuries? Does treating the Middle Ages as "not modern" collapse once we recognize that modern state and scientific institutions themselves only crystallized in the nineteenth century?

The audience is a philosophy-of-history seminar: people who know the basic chronology but have not memorized historiographical camps. Aim for about ten to twelve leaves. Do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; the tree should be constructible from conceptual distinctions alone by a model without web access. Keep the prompt formal and polished, and make sure the tree explicitly addresses whether the misalignment between physics and the other disciplines undermines the unity of the "Scientific Revolution" as a period.
