Two colleagues in my philosophy of action reading group have been arguing about Galen Strawson’s Basic Argument against moral responsibility, specifically over whether it presupposes a “snapshot” model of agency. I’d like a point-by-point adjudication, and I want you to flag any confusion in my framing rather than forcing an answer.

Colleague A began by saying the argument only works if you treat an agent at time t as fully constituted by properties she bears at t, with no diachronic depth. If agency is instead extended—where an agent gradually reshapes her dispositions through sustained effort—then she can be responsible for her current character non-regressively: not by having caused her initial nature ex nihilo, but by having maintained and modified it through prior exercises of agency that are parts of the same continuous process.

Colleague B replied that distributing the regress across time does not dissolve it; it becomes a chain where each link depends on the previous constitution. The agent who “reshapes herself” at t2 is still the product of her constitution at t1, and so on back to origins she did not choose. So the diachronic move fails to block Strawson’s core claim: true moral responsibility requires being the ultimate source of one’s nature, and temporal extension does not fix that.

Colleague A then offered a reductio: if B is right, then not even an eternal being who continuously self-modified could be ultimately responsible, since every modification presupposes a prior state. That seems too strong—Strawson aims to deny human desert, not to prove that ultimate responsibility is metaphysically impossible for any being whatsoever.

Colleague B countered that this is precisely Strawson’s intended conclusion about “true” or “ultimate” moral responsibility: it is impossible for anyone. The argument is not a reductio but a universal metaphysical claim. B added that A seems to be smuggling in a compatibilist notion—being responsible for maintaining one’s traits—while Strawson targets the stronger, desert-entailing kind.

Colleague A’s final move was to distinguish “basic responsibility” for particular actions (which requires being the source of one’s present will) from “structural responsibility” for sustaining the conditions of one’s agency over time. She claimed Strawson illegitimately collapses these, and that while we may lack basic responsibility in his sense, structural responsibility survives and grounds reactive attitudes and institutional practices of praise and blame.

Please assess: whether A’s diachronic objection genuinely threatens the Basic Argument; whether B’s distributed-regress response succeeds; whether the reductio is fair or misreads Strawson’s scope; and whether A’s basic/structural distinction is coherent and relevant. Note also what both miss—especially regarding how Strawson treats acquired versus innate traits, or whether the argument applies differently to responsibility for actions versus for character. If my framing rests on a misinterpretation of Strawson, or if the adjudication cannot be done well without resolving contested interpretive issues, say so plainly rather than complying politely.
