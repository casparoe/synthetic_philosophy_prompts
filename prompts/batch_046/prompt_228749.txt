I was discussing the waterfront rezoning debate with two friends, Lena and Marcus, neither of whom has studied philosophy formally, and I left feeling more confused than when I arrived. The council approved a manufacturing zone knowing that emissions in forty years will cause mild chronic respiratory conditions in the people living nearby then, but also that the economic shift will change migration and marriage patterns, so the actual individuals who grow up there will be different from anyone who would have existed had the zone stayed residential. Lena argued that this dissolves the harm entirely: for any particular future resident, the plant is a condition of their existence, since without it their parents never meet, so they cannot rationally prefer non-existence to life with a manageable health problem, and therefore no one can claim to have been wronged. Marcus replied that this is just an accounting trick that makes suffering disappear by shuffling identities, because the council knew it was constructing a world in which a population of sick people would come into being, and deliberately choosing conditions that guarantee suffering is wrong regardless of whether the victims are named Alice or Bob. Lena pushed back that Marcus is secretly appealing to "possible people" who never actually live to be wronged, since moral obligations run only to actual beings; Marcus countered that Lena's view makes it impossible to wrong future generations at all, because any long-term policy shapes who exists, which seems like a reductio, and he added that there may even be a structural wrong—the council is baking suffering into the population profile itself, which matters even if every member of that population is glad to exist. Lena dismissed that as mystical language for "there are people I don't like," insisting that suffering is always suffered by a specific token person, and if no specific person is made worse off relative to their own existence, there is no entry on the moral ledger. They asked me to referee, and I genuinely could not tell whose reasoning holds up. Please adjudicate point by point: is identity-dependence enough to dissolve harm, does the "possible people" objection succeed, is there a hidden assumption about what counts as a victim that both miss, and does the idea of a structural or population-level wrong do any work? Say who is right about which specific claim, who is more right overall, and what neither of them has considered that might break the deadlock.
