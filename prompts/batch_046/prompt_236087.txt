I have been working through an argument with my graduate seminar on singular prediction and practical reasoning, and I find the conclusion intolerable—yet I cannot locate the precise misstep. I should be grateful if you could diagnose it, indicating exactly which premise or inference fails and what abandoning it costs.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Every rational prediction about a particular event must be grounded in an objective probability assigned via a reference class.

(2) For any particular individual—say, myself deliberating whether I shall finish a manuscript by Friday—there exist indefinitely many reference classes (professional philosophers, people with comparable sleep habits, those who made similar commitments in 2028, and so forth), each yielding a different base rate.

(3) There is no non-arbitrary, non-circular principle for selecting the correct class: appeals to causal relevance presuppose statistical knowledge of precisely the kind we are trying to justify, and narrowing to the class containing only me reduces the frequency to 0 or 1, which is uninformative.

(4) Therefore, any assignment of a particular probability to my finishing the manuscript is rationally unjustified unless I abandon reference-class reasoning altogether.

(5) But first-person practical reasoning—deliberating about whether to accept a deadline—seems rational and indeed indispensable; and it typically draws on privileged access to my intentions, fatigue, and motivation, none of which are captured by any reference class.

(6) So either I must treat my own practical reasoning as irrational (because it ignores reference classes), or I must treat reference-class reasoning as dispensable for singular prediction (contradicting (1)).

(7) Most of us are unwilling to declare our own deliberation irrational; hence (1) is false and singular prediction requires no reference class.

(8) Yet if singular prediction requires no reference class, then clinical prognosis, insurance risk assessment, and scientific forecasting about particular instances are all equally groundless, since they too move from population data to individual cases.

(9) Hence the denial of (1) proves far too much: it undermines every empirical discipline that relies on statistical inference about particulars.

Where exactly does the argument slip? I should prefer to retain (1) and (5) if that is defensible, but I want to know precisely what I am paying whichever step I reject. Please reply in British English.
