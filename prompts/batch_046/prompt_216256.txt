I am writing a short essay for a postgraduate course on historiography, where we are comparing microhistory and 'history from below' with the study of political elites, state institutions, and their archives. In our last session, while discussing how local parish registers survive largely because ecclesiastical or seigneurial bodies had an administrative interest in recording them, a classmate shut down an analysis of parliamentary papers by saying that 'history is written by the victors'. I think the remark is doing more work than it looks, but I cannot tell whether it is being used as an empirical claim about document survival, a normative claim about whose lives deserve study, or something more subtle about the framing power of institutions that outlast any single conflict. Could you lay out, as a clear menu, the different precise theses that slogan could be expressing — distinguishing, for example, between victory as military or political triumph and victory as the long-term structural capacity to generate durable textual traces (the kind of bureaucratic self-documentation that churches and states perform irrespective of battlefield outcomes); between an epistemic claim about the incompleteness of the archive and a moral claim about the entitlement of the defeated to be remembered; and between a claim that elite sources are useless and a claim that they must be read against the grain? For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, whether it is defensible, and, crucially, whether it actually supports dismissing the study of state and elite records or whether it secretly relies on those very records to reconstruct what has been omitted. I would like the answer in British English, with the readings presented so that I can take them back to the seminar.
