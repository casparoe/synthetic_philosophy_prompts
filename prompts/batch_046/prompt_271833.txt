Write a triptych poem in blank verse—three sections of eight lines each, headed Frame, Outer, and Inner—on the problem of where meaning resides when a message describes the conditions of its own interpretation. In Frame, treat the poem's own material form—line breaks, headings, spacing—as the message's substrate: a speaker notes that ink and margin establish the rules by which anything here can be read. In Outer, present the literal propositional content: a speaker describes a phonograph record that contains only an account of the player on which it spins, stating the acoustic properties, the groove's depth, the needle's pressure. In Inner, reproduce the same vocabulary and syntax as Outer, changed only in lineation and punctuation, so that the lines now assert that the poem itself cannot be completed within the formal system of its own verse structure and that the reader must step outside the frame to finish the meaning. End all three sections with the identical refrain, "The groove holds what the needle cannot say," readable in Frame as physical engraving, in Outer as acoustic content, and in Inner as the limit of self-reference within the system. The turn between Outer and Inner should occur not through new vocabulary but through the reader's recognition that the framing has shifted: meaning is not inside the words but in the location assigned to them. The poem should leave open whether frame, outer, or inner message has priority, so that a reader familiar with the GEB-style distinction recognizes the strange loop, and a reader who is not still finds a coherent poem about interpretation, media, and the failure of complete self-description.
