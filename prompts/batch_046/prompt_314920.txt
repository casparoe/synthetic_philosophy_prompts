At our philosophy club, two friends, Jordan and Riley, had an extended argument about whether the only thing that makes a life genuinely good is the absence of craving and suffering, a position Jordan called tranquilism. Jordan claimed that if one takes this view seriously, the best possible human life is one entirely free of anxiety, longing, physical pain, and regret, even if that also means there is no exhilaration, no romantic love, and no artistic triumph, because all of those positive states come bundled with the risk of loss and disappointment and are therefore just interruptions in a baseline that should be calm. Riley responded that this sounds less like an ideal and more like a description of a well-medicated coma, and that without any positive orientation toward anything at all there is no subject for whom the absence of suffering counts as a benefit; tranquility needs a bearer who at least prefers calm to turmoil, otherwise the absence is not good for anyone. Jordan replied that Riley is assuming exactly what needs to be proved, namely that positive states have independent value rather than being brief, contingent relief from cravings that should never have existed in the first place, and that every so-called emotional peak is really just a temporary escape from a baseline of discontent. Riley then brought up the case of a musician who spends years in disciplined practice, experiencing both deep frustration and eventual mastery; Riley argued that if all craving is eliminated, the directed energy that makes mastery possible also disappears, so tranquilism either collapses into inertia or secretly relies on the very craving it claims to reject. Jordan countered that some Buddhist psychological frameworks distinguish harmful craving, tanha, from a benign form of intentional direction, chanda, which means one could practice with calm, focused attention without the clinging attachment that produces suffering, and therefore Riley’s musician example fails. Riley conceded that the distinction might hold in theory but insisted that in practice no one can cleanly separate the two mental states, so Jordan’s view demands an impossible psychological surgery that would leave people flat rather than serene. Jordan concluded that even if the separation is difficult, suffering is intense and real while joy is fleeting and contingent, giving tranquilism a stronger asymmetry argument, and that Riley is letting nostalgia for emotional peaks bias his evaluation of what actually matters. I listened without taking sides.
