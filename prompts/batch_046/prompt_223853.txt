Over the past semester I have been redesigning an undergraduate syllabus on the Enlightenment, and a student’s essay has pushed me into a corner I cannot quite see out of. The essay defends what it calls the isomorphism thesis: that the logical form of Enlightenment universalization is structurally identical to political domination, not merely historically correlated with it. From this it derives an argument that seems to prove far more than it should. Here is the chain, reconstructed as precisely as I can:

(1) Enlightenment rationality operates by substituting context-independent reasons for local, tradition-bound norms.
(2) Any substitution of local autonomy by an external, context-independent standard is, by definition, an act of domination—what the essay terms epistemic extraction.
(3) Therefore, Enlightenment rationality is not accidentally linked to colonial domination; its core procedure is domination.
(4) Modern science, constitutional rights, and public-health protocols are all institutional descendants of that core procedure.
(5) If a practice is constituted by domination, then invoking it to resist domination is conceptually self-defeating: one cannot deploy the logic of extraction to cure extraction without presupposing the very framework being criticized.
(6) So anyone who appeals to scientific evidence, human rights, or democratic procedure to criticize imperialism or extraction is engaged in a performative contradiction.
(7) But I want to do exactly that in my syllabus: use Enlightenment-derived frameworks to critique the Enlightenment’s own imperial legacy.
(8) Therefore, either my syllabus is incoherent, or one of the premises above must fail.
(9) The disjunction in (8) seems unavoidable, yet giving up (3) feels like special pleading, and giving up (5) seems to require showing that domination can be self-correcting, which looks like an implausible compatibilism about domination.

I am not asking for a bibliography or reading list—without access I could only guess at recent work in critical theory. What I need is a diagnosis: which step is the weakest, why it looked compelling, and what each standard position—those who defend Enlightenment universalism, those who reject it root and branch, and those who try to separate its rational kernel from its domination—has to pay to reject that step. If I must choose, I would rather preserve (7) than (3), but I want to understand the price.
