I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science preparing the interview guide for an upcoming episode of my department's research podcast. Our guest—unnamed, to avoid putting words in a living scholar's mouth—is a philosopher of biology and cognitive science who has recently defended what I will call structural-functional continuity: in mechanistic anomaly detection, a system still realizes the same mechanism when its organizational relations among parts are preserved, even if individual components are substituted, degraded, or operate outside typical parameter ranges, provided the mechanism's characteristic activity is maintained. The view is intended to distinguish genuine mechanistic breakdown from mere statistical deviation or component-level noise.

Please draft a sequence of eight interview questions that turn on the actual content of this position rather than on biography or general methodology. I want at least one question pressing the boundary between component substitution and organizational preservation—specifically, a hypothetical case in which substitute parts possess different intrinsic causal powers but are arranged so that the overall organizational profile is restored—followed by a follow-up keyed to the likely reply that the substitutes are functionally equivalent within that organizational context. Include one question on how the view distinguishes a mechanistic anomaly from a non-mechanistic statistical outlier, with a follow-up that anticipates the answer that the distinction hinges on causal relevance to the mechanism's organization. Add a question addressing the individuation problem: how organizational relations are specified without circular appeal to the mechanism's normal or intended activity, and what prevents that specification from becoming ad hoc in complex, nested mechanisms; provide a follow-up for the reply that organizational relations are defined by mutual manipulability conditions. Finally, include one question about what empirical or modeling result would genuinely count against the structural-functional continuity criterion—what evidence could show that organizational preservation is insufficient for mechanistic identity, or conversely that mechanistic identity can survive organizational disruption? Conclude with a brief assessment of which of these four lines of questioning exposes the weakest point in the view, but keep the primary deliverable the questions themselves.
