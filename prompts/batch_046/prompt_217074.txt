For my philosophy of mind seminar, I am writing on Churchland-style eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires. I understand the basic move: folk psychology is a false theory, and neuroscience will eventually replace it rather than reduce it. The usual self-refutation objection—that the eliminativist must herself hold beliefs—strikes me as weak, since the standard reply is that scientific practice can eventually dispense with propositional-attitude vocabulary altogether. But I think there is a deeper problem that this reply does not address. If beliefs and desires are eliminated root and branch, then there is no interpretive framework left to fix the representational content of the neurocomputational states that are supposed to take their place. Without an interpreter who holds beliefs about meaning, or without a subject whose desires select one functional profile over another, a neural activation pattern seems to have only causal covariance, not directional content: it can be correlated with cats-on-mats, but there is no fact of the matter that it represents cats-on-mats rather than mats-on-cats. So it looks as if the eliminativist must either smuggle propositional attitudes back in at the neurosemantic level, or concede that neuroscience is merely instrumentally useful and does not describe genuinely representational states. Is this a real dilemma, or is there a principled account of how purely neurocomputational dynamics—say, high-dimensional vector-coding spaces—can carry determinate representational content without relying on the belief-desire framework they are meant to replace?
