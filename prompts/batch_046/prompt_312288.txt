I'm trying to work through an argument my physics teacher sketched about time and existence, and I think I've driven it to a conclusion that feels absurd, but I can't locate the exact exit.

(1) Special relativity denies any absolute present: for two events separated in space, whether they occur "now" depends entirely on the inertial frame of the observer.

(2) Presentism holds that only present objects and events exist; past and future ones literally do not.

(3) So if presentism is true, whether a distant event exists depends on which frame we happen to choose.

(4) Consider Andromeda, roughly 2.5 million light-years away. For me sitting still, an event there can be simultaneous with my present. For someone walking past me at ordinary walking speed, the Lorentz transformation shifts that plane of simultaneity by thousands of years, so that same Andromedan event falls deep in the past or deep in the future relative to his "now."

(5) Presentism therefore implies that my walking across the room can change whether events in Andromeda exist.

(6) That is ontologically unacceptable: what exists should not depend on pedestrian velocity.

(7) So presentism is false, and the block-universe view is correct—all spatiotemporal events exist tenselessly in a four-dimensional manifold.

(8) Now apply this to my own life. My college interview next month occupies specific spacetime coordinates. Under eternalism, those coordinates exist right now with exactly the same ontological weight as the coordinates of me writing this sentence.

(9) If both sets of coordinates exist equally, then there is no metaphysical distinction between "present" and "future"—only a relational one, like "to the left of" versus "to the right of."

(10) But causal influence seems to require a metaphysical distinction: I can affect what is not-yet-real, but not what is already fully real in every respect.

(11) Since eternalism removes the category of "not-yet-real" entirely, it removes the very target that my present actions are supposed to act upon.

(12) Therefore eternalism implies that my studying for the interview is causally redundant, like trying to push a rock that is already resting at the bottom of a hill.

(13) Yet I clearly experience my preparation as genuinely altering outcomes.

(14) So one of steps (7) through (12) must fail.

Which step should I reject? It seems to me that (7) is well-supported by the physics of simultaneity, and (13) is undeniable from first-person experience, so the damage must be done somewhere in (8)–(12). But I want to know exactly where the slide happens—whether it is an equivocation on what "exist" means in (8) versus (9), a confusion about how causation works inside a static block, or a modal slide between "exists tenselessly" and "is causally fixed." And I want to understand the price of each repair: if I save present agency by denying (7), do I have to reject the relativistic treatment of simultaneity? If I keep (7) but attack (10), what does that do to the intuition that the block is static? I would rather keep (7) and (13) intact if possible, but I want to see what I am paying to do so.
