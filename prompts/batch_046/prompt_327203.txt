I'm convening a seventy-minute discussion for a small circle of intellectually curious adults—mostly professionals without philosophy training—who have been debating whether present-day citizens can bear guilt for wrongs their state committed long before they were born. I come to this without academic credentials myself, having encountered Karl Jaspers's framework somewhat haphazardly through essays on historical responsibility, and I want to guide the group through it without letting the conversation collapse into either courtroom logic or an unstructured session about feelings. I need a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself: the precise order in which the conceptual layers must be introduced, with the dependency structure made explicit so that I do not present a later distinction before the ground for it has been secured.

The core idea is Jaspers's layered separation—criminal liability that attaches to specific individual acts, political responsibility that belongs to citizens as members of a continuing state, moral accountability that reaches into private conscience, and that further metaphysical dimension arising from solidarity with human suffering—though I want them to grasp the architecture rather than memorize four labels. For the carrying example, I want to use a constructed case rather than the familiar historical one: imagine a town called Millbrook, where a chemical plant operated for thirty years with active municipal approval, poisoned the regional aquifer, and was eventually shuttered by regulators; now, three generations later, the water runs clean, yet the descendants live amid repaired but still visible infrastructure, inherited municipal debt, and a persistent social stigma attached to the town's name, despite the fact that none of them worked at the plant, voted for the officials who permitted it, or personally profited from the pollution. Specify exactly what features this case must include and what it must deliberately lack—what boundaries it needs—so that it illuminates the distinctions between the layers rather than collapsing them into a single diffuse feeling of collective shame.

Next, map the misconceptions I should anticipate: that if no individual descendant can be criminally convicted, then all talk of responsibility is unjust; that political responsibility is merely a diluted or metaphorical version of moral blame; and that feeling sorrow about history is equivalent to being guilty. For each, indicate the precise moment in the explanatory arc where it is likely to surface and how I should head it off—what concrete question I can pose at that juncture—without derailing the sequence. Then propose one final check, framed as a specific question about Millbrook and its descendants, that would reveal whether the group understands the dependency structure—whether they can apply the right layer to the right feature—rather than just repeating terms. Finally, state clearly what aspect of Jaspers's broader argument should be omitted entirely at this level, and acknowledge that omission explicitly. Do not provide readings, bibliographies, or citations.
