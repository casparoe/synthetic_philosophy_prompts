(1) If causing avoidable suffering is wrong, and you can prevent it without catastrophic harm to yourself, then doing the harmful thing is wrong.
(2) Insects have nociceptors and avoidance learning, so they probably suffer when exposed to neurotoxic sprays like neonicotinoids — it doesn't kill them instantly, they twitch for hours.
(3) For most people in wealthy countries, losing some lettuce to aphids isn't catastrophic — you can net the plants, accept smaller harvests, or pay more for organic.
(4) So spraying chemical pesticides for garden or even large-scale agricultural convinience is causing avoidable suffering.

(6) Therefore if I think factory farming is wrong because chickens suffer, I should also think conventional farming is wrong because it kills millions of insects with prolonged agony.
(7) The scale actually makes it worse: one sprayed field might kill billions, so the total wrong is enormous.
(8) By the same token, if I shouldn't spray my tomatos, I shouldn't kill a mosquito with a coil when I could just use a net — it's avoidable suffering for minor convinience.
(9) And if I shouldn't do that, then walking across my lawn and crushing ants is also wrong, since I could have walked around.
(10) But nobody actually thinks stepping on ants is a serious moral failure, and if it is, then basically every human action involves massive wrong.
(11) So either suffering doesn't matter at all (which kills the argument against factory farming too), or there's a hidden cutoff that saves humans but I can't justify it without being a total specist.
(12) So the argument seems to prove that either we're all deeply immoral or insects don't count, but both conclusions feel wrong.

Where does the logic break?
