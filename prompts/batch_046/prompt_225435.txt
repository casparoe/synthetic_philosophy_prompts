At a recent philosophy reading group, two members argued about how to interpret G. E. Moore's treatment of friendship and beauty in Principia Ethica. Jordan claimed that Moore's doctrine of organic unities applies symmetrically to both goods: just as the appreciation of a beautiful object forms a whole greater than the mental state plus the object taken separately, so too does friendship form a whole greater than the sum of its pleasant interactions. Riley objected that this symmetry ignores the diachronic, relational structure of friendship — it unfolds over time and is constituted by reciprocal recognition, not by a single conscious state directed at an object. Jordan replied that Moore's analysis can accommodate temporal extension within the object-complex of the whole, so friendship remains an organic unity, only one whose parts are distributed across time. Riley then pressed a deeper worry: if the parts of the friendship-whole include years of interaction, then comparing its value to that of a momentary aesthetic experience becomes incoherent on Moore's own framework, because organic unities are not decomposable into commensurable parts. Jordan answered that comparison is still possible at the level of the whole — we judge one life better than another — but Riley insisted that this collapses Moore's distinction between intrinsic value and the value of parts, leaving friendship either incomparable with beauty or reducible to a discrete series of mental states. Please go through their exchange point by point: assess the strength of Jordan's initial symmetry claim, Riley's diachronic objection, Jordan's reply about distributed object-complexes, Riley's incoherence charge, and Jordan's final defense. In each case, say who has the stronger position and where the reply is weakest. Also identify the objection that neither raised but that a defender of Moore's view should fear most, and explain how it threatens the argument.
