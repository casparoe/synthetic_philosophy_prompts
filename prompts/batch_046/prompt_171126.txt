In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of translation, we are examining Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot as a limit case: rather than treating Clément Marot's poem Ma mignonne as a fixed semantic object to be rendered into English, Hofstadter treats its rhyme scheme, meter, and syntactic architecture as binding constraints that the translation must reproduce, even when this requires replacing the original imagery and propositional content almost entirely. I want you to draw out the most important philosophical implications of this methodological commitment—not for translation studies generally, but specifically for the ontology of poetic form and the conditions of artistic identity. Please address the following in order of importance: First, does Hofstadter's willingness to sacrifice lexical and imagistic fidelity in order to preserve formal structure imply that rhyme and meter possess a kind of semantic autonomy—that they carry poetic meaning independently of any particular semantic filling—or is form better understood as a purely pragmatic framing device that shapes reception without itself being meaningful? Second, if two texts can share an identical formal skeleton while diverging radically in imagery, reference, and emotional register, should we regard poetic form as an intrinsic property of the textual object, as a relational feature constituted by the expectations of a historically situated readership, or as a set of performative instructions that only achieve determinacy in execution? Third, what does this practice presuppose about the identity conditions of a poem: is the same work defined by its formal architecture, by its affective trajectory, or by its causal-historical origin? In your answer, please adjudicate between these three conceptions—semantic autonomy, relational framing, and performative instruction—indicating which is most defensible in light of Hofstadter's practice, and explain what this adjudication implies for the possibility of preserving poetic form in automated translation systems that do not share the translator's embodied cultural competence.
