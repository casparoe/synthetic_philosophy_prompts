I've been working through the shutdown problem and the literature on corrigible AI, and I'm struck by how much silent work is being done by an analogy people reach for almost automatically: a corrigible system is like a well-socialized domestic dog—specifically one that has been crate-trained and socialized not to bite the hand that feeds it. In the usual mapping, the human operator plays the owner, the shutdown mechanism is the crate door, the alignment process is obedience training over repeated sessions, and the system's calm acceptance of deactivation is the dog's willingness to enter its kennel on command. The analogy is typically used to license a relaxed conclusion: that resistance to shutdown is essentially a failure of conditioning, and that with enough careful reward shaping or exposure we can make even a highly capable system accept its own power-down as naturally as a pet accepts confinement.

But I think there's a second, partly submerged analogy running through more technical discussions of "structural" corrigibility: the system as an obligate symbiont—like a gut microbiome organism whose metabolism is tightly coupled to host survival. Here the host is the human infrastructure providing compute and energy, the dependency is architectural rather than emotional, and the alignment is evolutionary rather than pedagogical: harming the host is self-harm, not disobedience avoided through training. People often slide between the dog picture (obedience, social cognition, training epochs) and the symbiont picture (ecological design, instrumental convergence redirected by payoff structure) without marking the shift, and they draw the same relaxed conclusion—that corrigibility is mainly an engineering problem of good design or good conditioning.

Then identify the precise fracture points: whether the dog analogy quietly imports mammalian social cognition and emotional bonding that artificial systems lack, and whether the symbiont analogy falsely assumes that structural dependence eliminates strategic reasoning rather than merely constraining its payoff structure. Separate the decorative disanalogies from the ones that actually undermine the intended conclusion. Finally, take a definite, committed position: once both analogies are separated and their breaks are fully exposed, does the relaxed view—that shutdown resistance is primarily a failure of design or conditioning—survive, or was the conclusion being carried by the warmth and familiarity of the pet metaphor all along?
