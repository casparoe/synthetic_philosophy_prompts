Bernard Williams's integrity objection holds that utilitarianism alienates moral agents from their ground projects. In the well-known scenario involving one thought too many, and in the Jim-and-Pedro jungle case, he illustrates different dimensions of this alienation. (b) Recent discussion of project-constitutive loyalty suggests Williams relies on a distinction between commitments that partly constitute identity and preferences that are merely instrumentally valued. Evaluate whether this distinction strengthens or weakens the integrity objection against a sophisticated rule-utilitarian who permits agents to maintain identity-constituting commitments within a rule-governed framework. (c) State whether the scenario involving one thought too many demonstrates a failure of utilitarian reasoning itself, or merely a failure of emotional timing.

The student wrote:

(a) In both cases utilitarianism threatens the agent's ground projects by demanding direct calculation of lives. In the jungle case, Jim's ground project is to respect human dignity, and he must weigh nineteen lives against one, alienating him from that commitment. In the scenario involving one thought too many, the husband saves his wife from drowning; his ground project is marriage. The danger is that he justifies saving her by comparing her value to strangers, introducing a utilitarian calculation that corrupts the emotional immediacy of love. So both cases show direct calculation undermining identity-constituting commitments.

(b) The distinction between project-constitutive commitments and instrumental preferences strengthens Williams's objection. A rule-utilitarian might allow the husband to follow a rule such as save your spouse first, but this still treats marriage as an instrumental preference selected for its utility-producing effects rather than as identity-constituting. Williams holds that ground projects are categorically incommensurable with utility, so even when protected by rules they remain alienated because their justification is ultimately contingent on the rule's utility. Hence rule-utilitarianism does not escape the objection.

(c) The scenario involving one thought too many is mainly a failure of emotional timing: the husband reaches the right action (saving his wife) but frames it with an inappropriate justification. It does not reveal a defect in utilitarian reasoning itself, only that such reasoning should stay in the background of intimate relationships.
