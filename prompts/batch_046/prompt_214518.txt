Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on diachronic rationality, and I now find it suspicious because the conclusion seems to dissolve independent reasoning altogether.

(1) Van Fraassen's Reflection principle requires that a rational agent's present credence in a proposition should match her expectation of her future credence in that proposition, conditional on whatever evidence she anticipates acquiring.

(2) In many scientific domains, a rational non-expert expects that her future credence in a theoretical claim H will be determined not by her own further reasoning, but by the consensus of a specialised community whose evidential standards she already trusts.

(3) Therefore, applying Reflection, her present credence in H should already equal her expectation of that future expert consensus.

(4) But if she currently possesses strong first-order evidence against H—say, an anomalous observation she can understand—Reflection still demands that she weigh her expectation of future expert opinion more heavily than her present counter-evidence, provided she expects the experts to discount her anomaly.

(5) By parallel reasoning, if she anticipates that future expert opinion might shift due to political or funding pressures rather than evidence, she should preemptively adjust her credence to match that predicted shift, since Reflection concerns expected future belief regardless of its cause.

(6) Hence Reflection appears to oblige rational agents to adopt beliefs that track predicted social dynamics rather than present evidence, effectively replacing first-order reasoning with a form of epistemic surrogacy.

(7) Yet this proves too much: it implies that independent scientific judgement is irrational whenever expert consensus is anticipated, and it allows belief manipulation simply by altering an agent's predictions about future opinion.

(8) I am inclined to reject (5) rather than (1), because I want to preserve Reflection for purely epistemic updates; but I worry that distinguishing epistemic from non-epistemic predictions is ad hoc. Where does the argument actually go wrong, and what does giving up the weakest link cost?
