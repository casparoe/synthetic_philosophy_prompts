The student submitted the following answer. The three conditions for a genuine moral dilemma are that the agent faces two obligations that cannot both be satisfied, that neither obligation is canceled by the other, and that the agent is blameworthy or rationally at fault no matter which option is chosen. Marcus argues that if we have O(A) and O(B), and A and B cannot both be done, then by agglomeration we also have O(A and B), but since ought implies can and A-and-B is impossible, O(A and B) is false, which contradicts the original premises; therefore, genuine dilemmas are logically impossible. In the ventilator case, the physician has obligations both to X and to Y, but since only one can be saved, Marcus would say the apparent conflict dissolves once we recognize that the obligations are conditional on resource availability. Turning to Williams, he claims that after choosing X the physician experiences a rational residue: the unfulfilled obligation to Y is not merely a feeling of sadness but an objective reason that continues to apply. However, Williams treats this residue primarily as a psychological burden that rational agents carry, functioning as a persistent sense of moral loss rather than as an unfulfilled deontic reason. This suggests that what looks like a genuine dilemma is really just an emotionally costly choice. Finally, the suffering-focused objection holds that negative utilitarianism assigns lexical priority to preventing severe suffering over extending life. Because Patient Y would endure severe chronic pain while Patient X faces only moderate disability, the obligation to avoid severe suffering dominates, leaving only the single duty to save X. Thus the conflict between saving X and saving Y disappears: there was never a genuine obligation to save Y, since doing so would violate the lexically prior suffering-prevention principle. Consequently, Williams’s residue never gets off the ground, and Marcus’s consistency conditions are satisfied trivially because only one unconditional obligation remains. This shows that adopting the right normative framework eliminates dilemmas rather than resolving them within pluralist deontology.
