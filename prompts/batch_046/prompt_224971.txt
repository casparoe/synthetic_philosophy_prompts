I serve on my university's public-memory advisory committee, and we must evaluate a new proposal against our established record. Our precedents are these: we endorsed a commemorative installation about the 1940 Dunkirk evacuation only after its designers abandoned a framing that presented the retreat as a miraculous deliverance and instead treated it as an organizational failure, a chaotic withdrawal, and an ordeal that included the abandonment of French allies; we rejected a proposal for an interpretive marker honoring Confederate soldiers that framed secession as defense of local autonomy and defeat as the noble betrayal of a just cause, ruling that such narratives constitute mythologizing regardless of academic sourcing; we approved a Masada display on the condition that it present the siege as resistance to imperial domination while structurally including the Zealots' internal violence and the archaeological ambiguity of the mass-suicide narrative, refusing both pure heroism and pure dismissal; and we blocked a Kosovo Field monument that presented the 1389 battle exclusively as Serbian Christian martyrdom against Islamic invasion, on the grounds that erasing Ottoman and Albanian presence served contemporary ethnic exclusion.

Next month we face a proposal for an interpretive trail at the site of the 1916 siege of Kut-al Amara. The proposal frames the British surrender to Ottoman forces not as strategic failure but as a deliberate sacrificial stand analogous to Thermopylae, emphasizing the suffering of British and Indian troops, the leadership of General Townshend, and the eventual redemption of the Mesopotamian campaign. It offers no Ottoman or Iraqi perspective, does not acknowledge that Townshend sought surrender after a failed relief rather than choosing martyrdom, and deploys classical allusion to dignify an imperial expedition.

If we want to remain consistent with ourselves, what should we decide? Identify at least two plausible principles that could jointly explain our four precedents—perhaps one focused on whether defeat is framed in ways that dignify the political cause that produced it, another on whether the narrative structurally erases the presence or agency of opposing or colonized parties, or a third requiring that acknowledged ambiguity and contested interpretation be built into the display itself. For each principle, state what it predicts about the Kut proposal. Then offer your best-guess verdict, with reasoning for why that principle should take precedence, identify the closest analog among our precedents, and state honestly what principle we would be adopting if we chose differently. Finally, propose a further case—perhaps a successful resistance framed without acknowledgment of internal division, or a colonial defeat presented without classical analogy—that would best distinguish these competing rules from one another.
