I've been working through a discussion group on perpetrator motivation and keep returning to the standard contrast between organizational-situationist explanations and culture-driven accounts of the Holocaust—particularly the debate over whether ordinary policemen in units like Reserve Police Battalion 101 acted primarily because of peer dynamics and incremental escalation, or because of a deeper, culturally specific eliminationist orientation. To push past the usual rehearsals, I want to stage a focused exchange. Please take on the role of an unnamed historian committed to a rigorous situationist thesis: you hold that social psychology—authorization structures, conformity pressures, and the gradual normalization of violence—does the explanatory work, while prior cultural ideology is largely epiphenomenal or at least insufficient to account for the patterns of participation and refusal we see. Speak in the first person, defend this position with substantive arguments rather than strawman versions of the opposing view, and stay in character throughout: do not ask me clarifying questions, do not request bibliographic references, and do not break frame to explain what the literature says. You may concede narrow empirical details, but you must protect the core claim that culture is not doing independent causal work here.

I open with a conceptual challenge that I think your framework collapses. I want to distinguish situational drift, where actors gradually redefine the moral boundaries of their assigned role through repeated exposure, from scripted anticipation, where actors enter a situation already primed—through prior propaganda, social expectation, or role rehearsal—to see violence as congruent with an anticipated performance. I contend that the fact these men were volunteers rather than conscripts, combined with documented prior exposure to antisemitic materials in their training and civic life, points not to pure drift but to scripted anticipation: culture operates not as a continuous cause but as a precondition that makes situational compliance psychologically available. If that is right, your situationism may be describing the mechanism of execution while missing the cultural pre-selection that determines who finds the mechanism usable. Respond directly: how do you defend the autonomy of the situational explanation against this charge?
