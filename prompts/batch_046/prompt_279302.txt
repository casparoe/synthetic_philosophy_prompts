Here's an argument I cooked up about memory and forgetting that feels like it proves too much, and I can't tell if it's just sloppy or if it actually kills the idea of a duty to remember.

(1) If a society has a duty to remember a grave injustice, that duty exists because forgetting would erase the moral standing of the victims — this is roughly the Margalit line.
(2) If the duty is real, it can't be satisfied by some abstract collective entity; actual people have to do the remembering, or there's no mechanism.
(3) In an age where records are digital and globally accesible, anyone who *could* access and preserve the record is part of the mechanism, so the duty distributes across anyone with the information.
(4) Therefore, if I learn about a past atrocity and then deliberatly don't teach it to my kids, or delete an old archive file, or even just stop thinking about it so I can focus on my job, I'm failing the duty.
(5) But if that's true, then every person is morally required to spend all available mental and material resources maintaining perfect recall of every injustice they know about, since any lapse is partial forgetting.
(6) Nobody can do that without destroying their own life, and demanding it seems obviously wrong.
(7) So either (1) is false — there's no real duty to remember — or we're all constantly failing a massive moral obligation.
(8) I want to hang onto (1) because it feels right that some things shouldn't be erased, but I don't want to be committed to (5). Where does the chain break? Is there a hidden equivocation between institutional memory and personal cognition? Or is the problem in (3) distributing the duty like that? I also keep thinking about Rieff's point that forced remembrance can trap descendants in trauma they never lived, but I don't know how to fit that in without breaking the argument in a different place. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) than (6), but I want to know what I'm paying.
