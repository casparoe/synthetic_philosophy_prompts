Drawing on Adam Smith’s argument in The Wealth of Nations that maintaining colonial monopolies imposes hidden fiscal, military, and domestic-market costs that typically exceed their mercantile returns—particularly his observation that the apparent commercial gains are largely offset by defense expenditures, administrative corruption, and distorted consumption—outline the most important conceptual implications for a contemporary intergovernmental trade council weighing whether to sustain preferential economic partnerships with resource-dependent territories governed by weak regulatory institutions. Specifically, address: (1) how Smith’s distinction between genuine national wealth and monopolistic extraction applies to modern economic dependency relationships in which partner territories lack autonomous fiscal capacity; (2) whether the council’s prior establishment of those preferential terms generates a remedial moral obligation if withdrawal would precipitate institutional collapse, and how that obligation relates to Smith’s skepticism about state-engineered commercial advantage; and (3) how the tension between a principle of non-intervention and a duty to prevent exploitative extraction should be resolved within a framework that rejects both mercantilist domination and unconditional withdrawal. Focus on conceptual reasoning suitable for a framework document; do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific secondary scholarship.
