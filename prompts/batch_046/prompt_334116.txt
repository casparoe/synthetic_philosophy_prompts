I have recently returned to the Nyāya account of the four pramāṇas—pratyakṣa, anumāna, upamāna, and śabda—after many years away from Indian epistemology, and I find myself struck by structural parallels with the predictive-processing framework I regularly invoke in conversations about perception and cognition. Rather than asking for a bibliographic orientation, I want a rigorous conceptual translation: restate the functional role of each pramāṇa within the vocabulary of active inference—generative models, precision-weighted prediction errors, hierarchical priors, interoceptive and exteroceptive channels, and socially transmitted model parameters. I take myself to be fluent in both domains: I understand the basic Nyāya taxonomy, including the division of perception into savikalpaka (determinate, concept-laden) and nirvikalpaka (indeterminate, pre-conceptual) forms, and I am comfortable with predictive-processing talk of bottom-up signals, top-down predictions, and the gating function of precision in belief revision.

Within this translation, I am especially interested in a specific tension that I do not see clearly named in either literature. Nyāya treats comparison (upamāna) as an irreducibly independent source of knowledge—distinct from both inference and direct perception—yet in predictive-processing terms analogical reasoning seems to reduce either to structural alignment between generative models or to hierarchical prior transfer across domains. Please address whether there is genuine residue here: does the Nyāya claim about the autonomous epistemic authority of comparison survive translation, or does it dissolve into mechanisms already covered by inference and perception? Similarly, examine testimony (śabda): Nyāya insists that linguistic understanding requires grasping speaker-intention in a way not reducible to inference from signs, whereas in predictive terms social testimony might appear as nothing more than high-precision priors transmitted through linguistic channels. For each pramāṇa, report what translates cleanly into error-minimization dynamics or model architecture, what requires distortion, and what appears inexpressible within the predictive vocabulary altogether.

Finally, in assessing these translations, please distinguish sharply between what is broadly settled—such as the fourfold taxonomy itself, the Nyāya critique of Buddhist apoha (exclusion) theories of concept-formation, and the standard predictive-processing account of perception—and what remains genuinely contested among specialists. In particular, do not present disputed interpretive questions, such as whether nirvikalpaka perception should be understood as genuinely non-conceptual content or merely as pre-linguistic conceptualization, as if they commanded consensus. I want the residue report not merely as a mapping exercise, but as a diagnostic: where the predictive vocabulary fails to carry a Nyāya claim, is the failure a defect of that framework's expressive resources, or does it reveal that the original Nyāya claim was less determinate than its formal presentation suggests?
