I have just come from a long and still-unresolved argument with a friend about whether institutions should honor the legal precision of the 1948 Convention or the broader moral vision in Raphael Lemkin’s original 1944 conception, which included political groups and what he called cultural genocide—categories that were dropped during the drafting process. I sit on the advisory ethics panel for the National History Museum’s forthcoming exhibition on imperial violence, and by Friday we must circulate a formal memo recommending how the central caption should treat the 1904–1908 German suppression of the Herero and Nama peoples in South West Africa. The curators want the word “genocide” used; legal counsel warns that the Convention’s narrower ethnic and national framing does not clearly cover an atrocity in which victims were targeted partly as rebellious political subjects, and some panelists fear that extending the term to colonial campaigns dilutes its specific juridical weight. Others counter that using a legally narrowed category for institutional comfort is itself a political act, and that Lemkin’s original framework captures the moral reality more honestly.

Please draft the panel’s decision memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action for the caption—whether to apply the term “genocide,” to qualify it explicitly, or to substitute an alternative formulation—and it should include reasoning that takes the strongest opposing consideration seriously rather than dismissing it. Address specifically how the museum should navigate the tension between Lemkin’s conceptual origins and the politics of naming atrocities in a legal-institutional context.
