I am drafting an argument for my high school philosophy seminar on the metaphysics of self and moral responsibility, and I want an internal consistency audit before I present. My position combines Buddhist and analytic commitments as follows:

(a) The doctrine of anatta is correct: there is no permanent, unitary, substantial self underlying experience; what is conventionally called a person is only the impermanent flow of the five aggregates.

(b) In analytic terms, personal identity over time is best explained by psychological continuity—the persistence of consciousness, memory, and intentional states—rather than by bodily continuity, since physical substance is also impermanent and lacks intrinsic essence.

(c) Moral responsibility requires karmic continuity in a precise sense: an unbroken causal-cognitive chain must link the present agent to the past action, and this chain must preserve a single, unitary narrative perspective that can endorse or disown the deed.

(d) Therefore, in a case of severe retrograde amnesia in which bodily continuity is intact but all psychological and narrative continuity is irreversibly destroyed, the post-amnesia individual is not identical to the pre-amnesia agent and should not be held responsible for prior actions.

(e) I am committed to reflective equilibrium: if my theoretical framework yields concrete judgments that conflict with well-considered moral intuitions, I ought to revise the framework rather than dismiss the intuitions.

(f) I have recently been persuaded by a hypothetical argument about distributed cognition: if two individuals were joined by a sustained direct brain-to-brain interface that merged their mental streams into one continuous causal flow—without either stream dominating—this would constitute a single extended self under anatta, because the absence of a substantial ego removes any intrinsic boundary.

Please examine whether these six commitments form a coherent set. Identify which pairs are genuinely in tension—not merely apparently conflicting—rank them by severity, and for each indicate the least costly revision and what that revision sacrifices. Also distinguish the tensions that dissolve on closer inspection, explaining why they are only superficial. Do not treat every friction as equally damaging; I have not indicated which commitment I am most willing to abandon, so audit all of them neutrally.
