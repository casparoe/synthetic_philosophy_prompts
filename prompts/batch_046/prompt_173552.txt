I'm designing a one-hour seminar for second-year philosophy students on material constitution, centered on the statue-and-clay puzzle but using a single carrying case rather than the usual parade of examples. The case is a bronze monument that, over two centuries, has had every original atom replaced by conservators using chemically identical alloy; the museum catalog still lists it as the original work, while the replaced atoms sit melted into an unshaped ingot in the vault. Plan the explanatory arc: the precise order in which the metaphysical pieces must be introduced so that the conflict between identity conditions feels like a genuine tension rather than a verbal trick; what this particular example must possess and must lack in order to do that work, and why the standard lump-of-clay example fails at this level; the misconceptions students predictably bring—likely about essential properties, artistic intention, or institutional labeling—and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a brief check for understanding that reveals whether they have grasped the constitution relation rather than merely picked a side; and which standard moves (for instance, four-dimensionalism or dominant sorts) you will deliberately omit and explicitly mark as beyond this hour.
