Here's an argument I drafted for a philosophy-of-science discussion group and now distrust, since its conclusion seems to dissolve the determinism problem by technical fiat. (1) A deterministic system is one whose future states are uniquely fixed by present conditions plus dynamical laws. (2) Chaotic deterministic systems have positive Lyapunov exponents: infinitesimal differences in initial conditions diverge exponentially, making long-term prediction impossible for any physically embedded observer. (3) If two metaphysical descriptions are indistinguishable in principle by observers inside the system, they are epistemically equivalent with respect to the reasoning those observers can deploy. (4) So for any embedded agent, a chaotic deterministic system is epistemically equivalent to an indeterministic system with the same macroscopic distribution. (5) The free-will debate concerns whether determinism is compatible with moral responsibility, and this compatibility turns on whether an agent's choice is metaphysically fixed prior to the choice, not on whether an observer can compute it. (6) But if "being fixed" is only epistemically accessible determinism, then a chaotic cognitive system is not fixed in the relevant sense. (7) Therefore, if human brains involve chaotic dynamics, libertarian openness is already present without abandoning determinism, and compatibilism becomes unnecessary. (8) Yet the same reasoning applies to weather patterns, which are chaotic but clearly lack moral agency. (9) Hence either chaos confers libertarian freedom on storms, or the argument fails. Where is the error? If forced to choose, I prefer to keep the epistemic gap in (3)-(4), but I want to know precisely what that commitment costs.
