In a working group on collective scientific reasoning, a senior researcher invoked Clifford’s dictum—“It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence”—to insist that no laboratory should register a working model in publication unless every competing hypothesis had been individually falsified by direct experiment. Before I respond, I want to map exactly what that sentence could be committing her to. Could you lay out the distinct precise theses concealed in Clifford’s line? For each reading, clarify: (a) the analysis of “belief” being targeted—whether occurrent mental assent, dispositional acceptance, or an institutional commitment expressed in publication; (b) the standard of “sufficient” evidence—whether it requires deductive certainty, a fixed probabilistic threshold, or merely the absence of reasonable doubt within the relevant disciplinary framework; (c) the force of “wrong”—whether it expresses a categorical moral prohibition, an intellectual-vice judgment, or a consequentialist warning about societal harm; and (d) the scope of “always, everywhere, and for anyone”—whether it binds all cognitive agents, only those in specific epistemic roles, or only in domains where belief-formation is genuinely voluntary. For each combination, indicate whether it collapses into a tautology, represents a defensible Cliffordian thesis, or is false; and specify which particular combination my colleague actually needs for her argument against publishing preliminary working models to succeed. Finally, say whether James’s well-known conditions for permissible belief—an intellectually undecidable choice between live, forced, and momentous options—could apply to that institutional case, and if so whether they would undermine her use of the slogan. Present the readings as a verdict-labeled menu I can take back to the seminar, not as a single favored interpretation.
