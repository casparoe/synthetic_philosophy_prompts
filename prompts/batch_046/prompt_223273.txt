I've been reflecting on a pattern in secular moral reasoning that seems to sit somewhere between karma, retributivism, and moral luck, and I am unsure whether it has an established name in philosophy or moral psychology. The pattern involves what I can only call a kind of implicit thematic accounting: when someone commits a wrong of a particular character—say, exploiting another person's trust for financial gain—observers often treat a later misfortune that structurally mirrors that wrong, such as being betrayed in a financial arrangement by a trusted partner, as somehow settling the moral ledger, even though no human agent intended the misfortune and even though the observers would reject any institutional punishment for the original wrong. By contrast, if the same wrongdoer suffers an unrelated misfortune of equal or greater severity—say, a random injury or an illness with no narrative connection to the betrayal—the sense that moral equilibrium has been restored seems markedly weaker, as if the suffering failed to address the specific debt.

This reaction feels distinct from standard retributivism, since nobody proposes that punishment be deliberately administered; the satisfaction attaches to misfortune that arises on its own. It also differs from mere poetic justice, because the observers do not seem to be enjoying an ironic narrative so much as genuinely registering a change in the wrongdoer's moral status, as though some portion of the debt had been discharged. And it is clearly not karma in any metaphysical sense, since those displaying the intuition explicitly deny any causal law linking moral action and consequence. Is there an established concept—perhaps within discussions of moral desert, fittingness, or the psychology of justice—that captures precisely this intuition: that moral debts can be satisfied only by suffering that is thematically appropriate to the wrong, regardless of agency, intention, or institutional design? If no such concept exists, how should one distinguish it from neighboring notions like cosmic justice or moral luck, and what precise definition and boundary conditions—particularly for cases of partial or metaphorical rather than literal thematic match—would best isolate it?
