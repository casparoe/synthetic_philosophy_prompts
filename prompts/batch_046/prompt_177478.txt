You chair the Governance and Ethics Committee of the Transatlantic University Consortium, which operates degree-granting campuses in Oxford, Heidelberg, Lyon, and Chicago. The consortium currently mirrors each host country's religious-arrangement regime: in Oxford, an Anglican chaplaincy receives a block grant tied to historic establishment; in Heidelberg, students opt into or out of the Kirchensteuer through registration, with funds distributed to recognized religious communities; in Lyon, no religious funding flows through university administration, per laïcité; in Chicago, student religious groups apply for voluntary fee allocations through an independent student-government process.

A proposal before your committee asks the consortium to abandon this four-track model and adopt a single unified policy: either (a) a standardized opt-out "religious services contribution" collected administratively at all four campuses, or (b) a uniform prohibition on any university-mediated religious funding, leaving all support to private donation.

The philosophical stakes are not merely administrative. The proposal forces the committee to decide whether secular neutrality is best understood as equal treatment of all religions (which might require extending the German fiscal mechanism everywhere), as strict separation of religious and public institutions (the French model), as a historically embedded establishment that can accommodate pluralism (the English model), or as a marketplace of voluntary association protected from state interference (the American model). Crucially, the Heidelberg mechanism raises a specific conceptual problem: because the Kirchensteuer is collected by the state agent only for registered members, it makes religious affiliation administratively visible in a way that private donation does not. Some committee members argue that this visibility is precisely what renders the system neutral—no hidden subsidy—while others argue that any state-mediated collection, even with opt-out, symbolically endorses religion as a public identity category.

Draft the committee's decision memo, addressed to the consortium's Board of Trustees, recommending either option (a), option (b), or a qualified hybrid with explicit conditions. Specify which campuses, if any, would transition, on what timeline, and under what oversight mechanism. The memo must treat the strongest argument for the rejected option seriously—not as a misunderstanding, but as a genuine conception of neutrality that your recommendation must explicitly weigh and overcome. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; reason from the structural logic of the four regimes and the visibility-versus-endorsement distinction introduced above.
