I serve as the student delegate on my high school's International Civic Dialogue Oversight Committee. The committee must issue a formal decision memo—recommending approval, rejection, or conditional approval—regarding the proposed Universal Radical Honesty Charter for our school's new Global Civic Dialogue Program.

The Charter was drafted after a specific incident: a Dutch exchange student, guided by the cultural expectation of bespreekbaarheid—the principle that any subject should be openly discussable without rhetorical softening—delivered direct, unsoftened written feedback on a Japanese exchange student's collaborative policy proposal, describing the argument as structurally confused and unsupported by evidence. The Japanese student, who operates within the honne-tatemae framework—distinguishing between one's true feelings (honne) and the public presentation necessary to maintain social harmony (tatemae)—interpreted the unmediated criticism as a deliberate humiliation and withdrew from the program. The student filed a grievance arguing that the program environment privileges a specific Northern European communicative norm as universal.

The Charter would mandate that all peer feedback in the program be delivered without hedging, euphemism, or what the drafters term protective ambiguity, and it explicitly prohibits white lies intended to preserve face or avoid discomfort. Proponents argue that institutional fairness requires a single transparent standard and that face-preserving indirectness is a form of deception incompatible with democratic deliberation. Opponents argue that mandating directness imposes cultural imperialism, that indirect refusal and strategic ambiguity can be moral obligations rather than lies, and that the Charter risks excluding students from communicative traditions where explicit negative judgment violates basic ethical duties.

The memo should be addressed to the committee chair, should state a clear bottom-line recommendation, and should present reasoning that both sides can actually respond to—including an honest account of the strongest consideration favoring the position you do not take. The analysis should engage the philosophical question of whether white lies are wrong in institutional cross-cultural contexts, whether directness can be ethically universalized, and what conditions—if any—would make such a charter defensible. Please write in American English, do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or references to specific scholarly works, and assume the reader has no access to outside sources. The reasoning should rely on conceptual analysis rather than on highly specialized or obscure theoretical frameworks.
