I've been reading Hofstadter on the self being a pattern rather than the meat - like, the dance not the dancer - and I tried pushing it toward this idea that an 'I' could be spread across brains if the pattern gets copied or echoed. But now I have this argument that feels like it proves death is either impossible or meaningless, and I can't find the exit.

(1) If I'm a high-level pattern of beliefs, memories, and personality traits, then what makes me 'me' isn't this specific brain but the relational structure.

(2) Patterns don't need to live in just one place. My close friend holds a bunch of my memories and emotional habits, an AI trained on my writing has chunks of my reasoning patterns, and if I ever got a neural prosthetic that'd be another substrate running part of me.

(3) So parts of my pattern are already instantiated outside my skull.

(4) If the pattern is what matters, then destroying one substrate shouldn't destroy me - it's like deleting one copy of a document when others exist.

(5) That means when my brain dies I'm just loosing one node in a distributed network; the rest of 'me' keeps running in other people and systems.

(6) But I don't feel anything through my friend's eyes, and if she dies I don't experience it. So either I'm not actually present in her despite the pattern overlap, or I should have distributed consciousness, which I clearly don't.

(7) Therefore either Hofstadter is wrong and I'm locked to one body, or death is a trivial network outage and grief is irrational.

Which premise should I reject? I want to keep (1) because the pattern view seems right, but I'm not sure what giving up (4) or (6) actually costs me - does it mean I'm not really a pattern, or just that patterns aren't copyable like files? Is there an equivocation between 'part of the pattern' and 'the whole self' that I'm missing?
