I'm drafting a novel and my psychiatrist character, Dr Elena Voss, has these firm takes on self-deception: a bloke who forgets his anniversary despite phone reminders — not it, just rubbish memory; a politician who genuinely thinks her corrupt deal is for the public good but instructs staff to filter every critical report before it reaches her — yes; a student who avoids opening his exam results and says "I just haven't looked yet" — yes, definately; a trauma survivor with no conscious recall who claims he's "totally over it" but flinches at loud noises — no, that's dissociation, not self-deception; and a writer who bins unopened rejection letters and insists his manuscript is brilliant — yes.

Now I'm stuck on the next case for her: a musician who sets up an algorithm to auto-delete negative reviews before she ever sees them. She genuinely believes critics adore her album, and she's never actually read a bad one. Do you think Elena'd call that self-deception?

I'm less interested in the answer than in the rule she's running — maybe strategic division of mind, motivated belief-formation, or performative contradiction — plus the other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well. Which fresh case would best tell them apart? I've also been playing with this niche idea of epistemic outsourcing, where you delegate the hiding to a system so your own hands stay clean. Does that shift anything?
