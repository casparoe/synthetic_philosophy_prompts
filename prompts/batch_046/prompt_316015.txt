I'm writing a dissertation chapter on the architecture of phenomenal consciousness, and in our philosophy-of-mind seminar the same rhetorical move keeps recurring: whenever a colleague proposes a unified workspace, a global integration mechanism, or even a second-order meta-draft selection layer that picks among competing narratives without itself being a further draft, someone objects, "There's no Cartesian theater." At first I took this as the uncontroversial claim that consciousness is not localized in one brain region or captured in a single neural snapshot. But lately the slogan is being used to dismiss any functional architecture that integrates distributed representations into a stable report, as if integration necessarily introduces a homuncular spectator. I want to disentangle the precise theses compressed into that one line. Could you lay out the menu of readings: the trivial localization denial; the specific anti-homunculus claim that motivates Dennett's multiple drafts model; the metaphysical claim that there are no intrinsic phenomenal properties requiring "presentation" to an inner audience; and the stronger eliminativist claim that there is literally no unified "show" occurring anywhere in cognition? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible commitment of the multiple drafts framework, or actually false if pushed too far. Then tell me which reading is required to reject global workspace theories, and whether that rejection follows from the slogan alone or needs an independent argument that a centralized integrator—without a little audience inside—is redundant given parallel draft competition. I am especially interested in whether accepting the critique of the theater forces one to deny that any form of global broadcasting can play a causal role in producing the narrative coherence we observe in conscious report, or whether that denial is a separate architectural question that the slogan by itself does not settle.
