I'm on the grad council's assessment subcommittee at a mid-sized philosophy dept, and we have to vote next week on whether students can use synthetic literature-review tools—basically LLMs that spit out integrated summaries of source texts—during the prep phase for our qualifying oral exams. The proposal says synthesis is just mechanical labor and the real philosophical work is the live defense; the pushback is that this creates epistemic outsourcing, where students defend positions they haven't actually worked through, and over time we produce people with performance fluency but no generative familiarity, by which I mean the ability to reconstruct an argument from scratch without scaffolding. I need a memo to circulate before the vote that recommends a concrete course—allow with conditions, ban outright, or restrict to specific stages—not just lists options but picks one and defends it. The reasoning should take seriously the worry that we're trading generative familiarity for fluent presentation, even if the memo ends up rejecting the ban, and it should include an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side. Don't ask for citations or a reading list; half the committee dosn't care about that stuff anyway. Give me a committed answer: take a definite position with reasons, not an "it depends" survey.
