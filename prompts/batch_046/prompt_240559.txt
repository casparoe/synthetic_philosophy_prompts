I'm preparing a thirty-minute seminar presentation for a philosophy-of-religion graduate workshop. I defend a grammatical interpretation of Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief—specifically, that religious propositions function not as empirical hypotheses but as rules constitutive of a form of life—coupled with a less common corollary: that this grammatical status renders religious belief structurally more exposed to internal contradiction than to empirical disconfirmation, since revising an empirical claim leaves the framework intact while a grammatical contradiction destabilizes the rules themselves. I argue this distinguishes Wittgenstein's position from fideism, because fideism shields belief from all external critique, whereas my reading exposes it to a stricter form of internal rational evaluation. The audience comprises analytic philosophers of religion (several theists, several naturalists, and one specialist in later Wittgenstein), plus two historians of early modern philosophy who tend to treat any non-evidentialist belief-formation as irrationalist. They are particularly concerned with rational justification, the threat of relativism, and whether non-cognitivism can make sense of genuine religious disagreement. Draft my speaker-prep sheet for the Q&A: present the objections in the order they will likely arise, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup would use; for each, indicate whether I should concede a point, hold firm, or qualify; provide a concise reply suited to a seminar setting (roughly two minutes); flag the weakest point in my own position that I should defend actively rather than hope they overlook; and identify the one objection I am hoping nobody raises, with a brief explanation of why it is damaging and how I should respond if it comes.
