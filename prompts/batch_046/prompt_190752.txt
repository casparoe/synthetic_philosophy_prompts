A popular account treats Benjamin Franklin’s scheme for moral perfection — the thirteen virtues entered in a little book, each assigned a day of the week and marked with black dots for failures — as an 18th-century precursor to modern quantified-self practice: discrete metrics, daily logging, feedback-driven incremental improvement. The comparison is usually deployed to present Franklin as a proto-behaviourist engineer of the soul, treating virtue as a bundle of independently optimisable variables. I want a precise audit of how far this fitness-tracker mapping holds. Please lay out the correspondences explicitly — what in Franklin’s practice maps onto metrics, logging, and feedback loops, and what does not; then identify the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion, particularly those arising from his ordering of the virtues (his view that temperance must be secured before silence can be maintained, implying dependency rather than independence), from his description of moral failures as “errata” like printing errors (suggesting a craft-shop rather than an engineering metaphor), and from his teleological framing of perfection as approximation to divine order rather than as personal productivity; and take a definite position — either defend the analogy as substantially correct despite these mismatches, or argue that it so flattens Franklin’s conception that a different comparison (perhaps to religious examination of conscience, to artisanal apprenticeship, or to another model) is more faithful. Give your reasons fully, and flag any point where your judgment turns on a contested reading of Franklin’s texts rather than on the internal logic of the comparison itself.
