I am a doctoral candidate in medical anthropology serving on my university’s accessibility-policy board, and we are deadlocked over a petition to include “context-dependent attentional divergence” within our disability-accommodation framework. The profile in question involves marked fluctuation in sustained-attention capacity that correlates with reproducible prefrontal-modulation patterns visible on neuroimaging; petitioners, however, argue that the divergence is only functionally harmful within standardized pedagogical architectures that demand continuous linear focus. They contend that if classroom environments were redesigned around variable attention rhythms, the condition would cease to produce harm and should therefore be treated as a neutral cognitive style rather than as a disorder. The clinical consultants counter that the neurobiological markers represent an objective deviation from species-typical functioning regardless of environmental design, and that classifying it as a disability risks medicalizing ordinary human variation. Both sides cite empirical studies—neuroimaging data on one hand, environmental-redesign outcomes on the other—yet they appear to disagree at a more fundamental level about what criteria must be satisfied for something to count as a genuine health condition. I need to advise the board on whether biological markers should be decisive in this case, or whether socially mediated harm alone can justify withholding a disease classification, but I want to avoid merely splitting the difference administratively. Could you identify the underlying philosophical structure of this disagreement—specifically the tension between viewing disease as a statistical-biological dysfunction and viewing it as a state that is harmful only relative to normative judgments about acceptable functioning—and apply those distinctions to determine what should govern our decision? I am not seeking generic policy guidance, but rather an analysis of whether classifying this particular cognitive profile as a disease depends on biological facts alone, or necessarily involves irreducibly normative commitments about how human cognition ought to operate.
