I have been reflecting on moral responsibility in ordinary life, without any formal training in philosophy, but I keep noticing a judgment pattern that I do not have a name for. Consider three people who each make the identical reckless choice: they decide to drive home after working a double shift, fully aware that they are dangerously tired. The first falls asleep and injures a pedestrian. The second also drives tired, but by pure chance reaches home safely with no incident. The third also makes the same decision and has the same mental state, but an unrelated police checkpoint stops her, forces her to rest, and she never enters the highway. In ordinary conversation, people treat the first driver as far worse than the second, which sounds like the moral-luck problem I have encountered in popular writing. Yet they also treat the third driver as somehow better than the second, saying she was spared from causing harm or that the interruption counts in her favor, even though her will never changed and she did not cause the checkpoint. So luck operates twice: bad luck amplifies blame for the first, but good luck—an external, unchosen interruption—seems to generate moral credit for the third, rather than leaving her neutral like the second. I am not asking for the general debate about whether outcomes should affect moral judgment. I want to know whether there is a specific concept for this double asymmetry, in which being accidentally prevented from carrying out a reckless intention produces praise or credit rather than mere excuse, and in which that credit is assigned despite the agent's choice remaining identical to that of someone who was not interrupted. If an established term exists, how is it distinguished from ordinary moral luck, from counterfactual accounts of responsibility, and from the doctrine of double effect? If nothing precise captures it, what should I call this specific boundary case, and where exactly does it diverge from each of those neighboring ideas?
