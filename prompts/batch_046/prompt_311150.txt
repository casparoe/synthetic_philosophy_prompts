The student wrote:

Keynes’s Treatise defines the probability of an argument as the logical relation between premises and conclusion—the rational degree of belief warranted by the evidence—while the weight of an argument is the total quantity and relevance of the evidence itself. Two arguments can share the same probability but differ in weight; greater weight should yield greater confidence in that probability, even if the numerical value does not change. In Keynes’s mature economics, especially in the General Theory and the 1937 QJE defense, he applies this framework directly to investment decisions: macroeconomic uncertainty arises when the weight of evidence about future yields is so slight that agents cannot assign any meaningful numerical probability at all. On this reading, radical uncertainty is not a separate epistemic category but rather the limiting case in which the weight of the argument collapses toward zero, leaving only conventional judgment and animal spirits to guide action. This demonstrates a clear continuity between the Treatise and Keynes’s later work.

That said, Keynes was not fully consistent across these texts. Influenced by Ramsey’s subjectivist critique, Keynes ultimately rejected the logical-relation theory of probability altogether and adopted a purely personalist view, treating probabilities as individual psychological degrees of belief that vary from agent to agent. Consequently, the General Theory’s emphasis on conventional valuation and liquidity preference reflects this subjectivist turn rather than any objective logical relation.
