I am working through a seminar on theories that treat consciousness not as a direct window onto neural reality but as a constructed “user illusion”—a functional interface that presents the organism with a simplified, action-oriented model of its environment and itself, while concealing the underlying machinery. A fellow student argued that this framing is either empty metaphor or simply relocates the hard problem: if the interface is all there is for the organism, then asking why it feels like something to use it seems as pressing as asking why consciousness exists at all. Before I assess particular accounts, I want to establish the standard of adequacy from first principles.

Write the desiderata: what must any theory that genuinely treats consciousness as a user illusion deliver in order to count as explanatory rather than redescription? Specify the phenomena it must accommodate—including first-person transparency, the possibility of introspective error, the distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness, and the evolutionary rationale for constructing an interface—together with the consideration behind each. Identify the distinctions the theory must preserve, particularly between an illusion residing in the representational content (the world-model being inaccurate) and one residing in the apparent existence of a unified user or subject of experience. State the results from neuroscience or evolutionary biology that the theory must not contradict, such as the prevalence of unconscious processing and the absence of a central neural theatre. Flag which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps—say, whether determinism about the interface’s construction is required or optional. Crucially, indicate whether any subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable: for instance, does the demand to explain phenomenal richness conflict with the claim that the interface hides all underlying computation? Finally, very briefly, contrast how a predictive-processing framework and a global-workspace variant would fare against this list, but keep the emphasis squarely on the criteria and their motivating tensions rather than on the scorecard.
