The problem of universals has been debated since Plato and Aristotle, yet the standard positions—realism, nominalism, conceptualism, and the various trope theories—seem locked in a permanent stalemate. Each side has well-rehearsed counterexamples, and the literature reads more like a taxonomy than an advancing argument. I want a diagnosis, not a survey.

My working theory is that the disagreement is not primarily about ontology—whether properties literally exist—but about what explanatory work a theory of properties is supposed to do. Realists appear to need universals to ground lawful regularities and inductive projection: without shared properties, they argue, science cannot justify treating dissimilar instances as falling under the same causal law. Nominalists, by contrast, seem to treat properties as artifacts of our classification practices, sufficient only if they yield predictive success without requiring a metaphysical substrate. But I suspect something deeper is hidden here.

Specifically, I wonder whether the stalemate reflects an unacknowledged divergence in how each side conceives of representation itself. One camp treats properties as extensionally defined equivalence classes—sets of instances bound by recognition rules—while the other treats them as intensionally defined nodes in a causal-explanatory network, where to possess a property is to participate in specific interaction patterns. If that is right, then realists and nominalists are not disagreeing about what there is; they are disagreeing about whether ontology should be modeled on formal type membership or on dynamical system architecture. The debate stalls because both sides answer questions framed by the other side's representational framework without noticing the shift.

Is this diagnosis correct? Is the disagreement verbal—about the term property—or substantive but hidden behind different conceptions of explanation? Could a formal distinction, say between extensional classification and intensional causal-role attribution, actually resolve anything, or is the question ill-posed? And if the impasse is structural, what kind of conceptual move, not just another counterexample, could make progress?
