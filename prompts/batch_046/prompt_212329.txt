I have recently been appointed to a review board for a manuscript archive that suffered an unprecedented preservation failure, and the experience has left me with a philosophical puzzle I cannot resolve without help. For decades, the institution's authentication practices rested on a network of foundational commitments—about the reliability of spectroscopic dating, about the stability of environmental baselines, and about the sharp distinction between genuine artifact and incidental contamination—that were never explicitly defended in any manual but functioned as the fixed points around which every curatorial dispute was settled. We now have strong evidence that an unknown catalytic interaction within the climate-control infrastructure has been gradually altering not only the damaged documents themselves, but also the reference samples and calibration curves that served as the ultimate standards against which all other judgments were measured.

The board is divided into three camps: one faction insists that the core verification methods remain sound and that we should localize the error to specific contaminated batches; another argues that we must suspend the entire catalog and treat every prior attribution as radically provisional until an entirely new verification architecture is constructed; a third suggests retaining the existing framework as a practical navigational tool but appending an explicit, irreducible uncertainty coefficient to every entry. I find myself unsure whether we are facing a merely technical failure of instruments, an institutional crisis of confidence, or a deeper question about which commitments must stay in place for any form of historical inquiry to remain coherent rather than dissolving into skeptical paralysis. Could you help me think through where the line should be drawn between what is genuinely open to revision in this situation and what must remain beyond doubt if our questioning is to have any ground to stand on?
