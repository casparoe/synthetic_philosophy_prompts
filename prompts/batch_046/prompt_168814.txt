I hold that rational decision-making—both individual and institutional—requires strict maximization of expected value, even when the best outcome is astronomically good but has only a tiny probability and the worst outcome is catastrophic and near-certain: the view usually called expected value fanaticism. I apply this specifically to regulatory bodies: if an intervention's expected social value is positive, it should be authorized regardless of irreversible downside risk, since any risk-averse rule misweights long-run aggregation.

What would have to be true for this position to be false? I want the observations, arguments, formal results, and conceptual distinctions that should actually move a defender, sorted by how decisive they are. Include whether proponents themselves concede conditions—such as lexical constraints, deontic limits, or multi-agent coordination failures—under which EV maximization must be suspended; whether decision-theoretic or strategic results show EV maximization is unstable or self-defeating in institutional or sequential settings; whether historical or contemporary cases of EV-style reasoning producing outcomes its defenders regret serve as genuine counterexamples; whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by redefining "value" or "rationality" to absorb every objection, and if so whether that insulation is itself a problem for a view of this kind; and an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything empirically. Apply this specifically to one hard institutional case: a regulator deciding whether to approve an untested synthetic-biology intervention with roughly a 99% chance of eliminating a widespread debilitating disease but roughly a 1% chance of accidentally releasing a pathogen capable of global pandemic mortality. Does the view yield a determinate answer that its defenders must accept, allow an escape hatch, or collapse? Finally, mark clearly where judgment is ineliminable and where the framework simply runs out of resources.
