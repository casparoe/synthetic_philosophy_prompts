In the philosophy of physics literature, the argument over whether deterministic chaos undermines metaphysical determinism has reached what looks like a permanent standstill. The determinist camp points to the shadowing lemma and structural stability to insist that exponential divergence is only an epistemic limitation—future states remain uniquely fixed by present conditions, and approximate orbits stay near true ones. Their opponents reply that if Lyapunov divergence makes specific future states operationally inaccessible to any physically realizable computation, then causal closure is satisfied only in a vacuous sense, and determinism collapses into a merely formal claim about differential equations. Both sides accept the same dynamical mathematics, yet neither treats the other’s inference from mathematics to metaphysics as legitimate. Is this stall substantive—perhaps turning on whether determinism requires computational accessibility, or on competing standards for what counts as a causal explanation—or is it verbal, with one side treating “determinism” as a modal claim about law-governed uniqueness and the other treating it as a claim about predictive tractability? Or are they answering different questions altogether: one about the ontology of dynamical laws, the other about the conditions for scientific explanation? Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says about Lyapunov divergence, shadowing, and causal closure; indicate whether a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work; and specify what, given your diagnosis, would actually count as progress—a formal result linking shadowing to modal logic, an argument that no physical computer can overcome divergence in principle, or a reconceptualization of the debate that separates law-uniqueness from predictive access.
