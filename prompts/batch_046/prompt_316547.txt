I am a novelist working on a manuscript that traces the gradual moral deterioration of a mid-level bureaucrat within an authoritarian apparatus, and in preparing the psychological arc of my protagonist I have become caught between two competing historiographical explanations of perpetrator behaviour that seem to carry very different philosophical payloads. On one side stands the approach associated with Christopher Browning, which emphasises situational dynamics, peer conformity, and the incremental nature of participation in violence among otherwise unremarkable individuals; on the other stands the position linked to Daniel Goldhagen, which locates the primary explanatory weight in a pre-existing, culturally cultivated disposition toward eliminationist hatred that makes voluntary participation intelligible without heavy recourse to coercion or gradual moral erosion. Rather than asking for a simple summary, I want you to compare these two frameworks specifically as implicit theories of moral psychology: examine how each treats the relationship between enduring character traits and immediate situational triggers, and analyse whether Browning's model requires a conception of the self as more fragmented or context-responsive than Goldhagen's more essentialist account permits.

To complicate the comparison further, I am particularly interested in how each framework handles what I will call residual moral cognition—the phenomenon in which a perpetrator continues to register, at some level, that his actions violate normative commitments he still nominally holds, without that recognition generating effective resistance. Does this phenomenon favour one explanatory model over the other, or can both accommodate it only by introducing auxiliary assumptions about self-deception, compartmentalisation, or the division of moral labour within bureaucratic structures? Please provide your analysis in British English; avoid bibliographies, reading lists, or specific page citations, as I am working without access to research materials and need the conceptual anatomy to stand on its own. You may treat the basic contours of both scholars' arguments as common knowledge, but do not quote from either work from memory—paraphrase freely if specific claims are needed.
