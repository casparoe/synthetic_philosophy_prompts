I am moderating a colloquium Q&A with an invented philosopher of science—let us call her Dr. Aris Thorne—who is defending a formalisation of the presumption of independence that she terms the Principle of Minimal Entanglement (PME). Rather than treating independence as a default Bayesian prior or a mere heuristic shortcut, Thorne argues that we should presume independence between two variables only when describing them jointly fails to yield any reduction in algorithmic complexity relative to describing them separately; independence, on her view, is essentially the absence of compressibility across domains. She further claims that standard heuristic arguments—such as the intuition that absence of evidence for interaction is evidence for independence—systematically conflate horizontal independence (between co-level variables) with vertical independence (between ontological levels in a hierarchical model), and that this explains why familiar intuition pumps about coin flips or card draws misfire when applied to nested causal structures such as neural-network representations.

The audience will include formal epistemologists, decision theorists, and statistics graduates familiar with Judea Pearl’s framework but not necessarily with its normative interpretation. I want eight to ten questions that avoid biographical generalities and do not ask her simply to restate Bayesian doctrine. Specifically, I need: one question pressing on how PME operates when algorithmic complexity is unknown or intractable to compute, with a follow-up keyed to her likely appeal to Solomonoff induction as an approximation; one asking whether PME risks collapsing into epistemic conservatism when confronted with highly entangled systems that nevertheless present simple surface regularities, with a follow-up anticipating her reply that entanglement must be positively demonstrated rather than presumed absent; and one probing the horizontal/vertical distinction through a concrete case—say, whether the hidden-layer activations of a trained model are vertically independent of its input features, or merely horizontally correlated—followed by a follow-up addressing her probable insistence that vertical independence requires an explicit commitment to a specific ontological layering. Finally, I want a closing question that asks what conceptual or empirical result would genuinely compel her to abandon PME for a flat heuristic approach, since I wish to prevent the framework from functioning as an unfalsifiable redescription of existing practice.

Please draft the questions in polished British English—using forms such as formalise, behaviour, and programme where appropriate—suitable for a formal colloquium setting. The questions should be intelligible to someone who understands basic probability and causal modelling but has not read Thorne’s hypothetical monograph, and they should make the philosophical idea do real structural work in shaping what can and cannot be said.
