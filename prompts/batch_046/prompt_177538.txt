For a 90-minute philosophy-of-mind seminar with 12 students, design the runnable simulation "The Distributed Archive" to make participants feel Dennett's idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity rather than an internal substance. Divide the group into three modules—Memory, Action, Affect—each holding disjoint fragment-cards about a fictional subject, M. The rules require each module to defend predictions about M's future behavior using only its fragments, then jointly author a "Center Document": a unified narrative that maximizes predictive coherence, with no hidden "true self" file available to discover. At minute 40, inject an "anomaly card": well-attested behavior that violates the Center Document. In assigned roles (Curator, Skeptic, Synthesist), the group must vote within 10 minutes: revise the Center (the self updates), reject the anomaly (narrative inertia protects coherence at predictive cost), or split the Center into branching narratives. The stakes should be real but small—e.g., the minority position must defend the rejected option for the final 15 minutes. Include a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief that maps each vote onto the theory and onto the injected concept of narrative inertia, a frank prediction of what will go wrong (dominance by the Skeptic, conflation of coherence with accuracy) and how to handle it, and an explicit admission of which phenomenological feature—the first-person feeling of owning the narrative—the simulation necessarily cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply verbally. State clearly what participants should leave seeing: that maintaining a self is an active, evidence-negotiating construction project, not the reporting of an inner fact. Do not request citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. End with a one-sentence summary on a line beginning "Summary:".
