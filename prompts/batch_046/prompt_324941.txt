Consider this argument about the stability of self-amending legal orders. (1) Any constitution that cannot revise its own fundamental rules lacks democratic self-correction and is therefore deficient. (2) Hence a viable constitution must contain an amendment rule that applies to itself; otherwise an arbitrary historical accident becomes permanently entrenched. (3) But if the rule is genuinely self-applicable, then by successive valid amendments its threshold can be lowered—from supermajority to simple majority, then to plurality, and eventually to zero—thereby authorizing the instantaneous dissolution of the constitutional order. (4) One might block this by entrenching the amendment rule against self-modification. (5) Yet entrenchment is itself a legal rule: if it is not self-applicable, it introduces an immutable, undemocratic meta-rule; if it is self-applicable, the same regress applies one level up. (6) So either every legal system harbors an arbitrary core immune to democratic revision, or it has no stable conditions of identity over time. The conclusion—that self-amending orders are conceptually incoherent—seems too strong. Where does the reasoning slip? Is there an equivocation between temporal validity and logical self-application, or a hidden scope shift between "the amendment rule" and "any rule governing amendments"? If you reject one premise, explain precisely what you must pay: do you surrender democratic adaptability, accept an undemocratic ultimate rule, or concede that legal identity is genuinely fluid?
