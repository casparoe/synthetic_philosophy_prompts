Consider an argument constructed against the coherence of supertasks, extending a toggle-register variant of the well-known lamp scenario into rational decision theory. (1) Define a deterministic physical device that, at each time t_n = 2 - 2^{-n} minutes (for n = 1, 2, 3, ...), toggles a binary register R between 0 and 1, with R initially 0 at t = 0. (2) At every time t < 2, R has a definite value, fixed by the parity of completed toggles up to that point. (3) If the supertask is completed at the limit time t = 2, then determinism—understood as the thesis that the state of a closed system at any time is fully fixed by its prior states and the governing laws—requires R to have a definite value at t = 2. (4) But there is no "last" toggle prior to t = 2, so there is no parity that fixes R's value; hence R lacks a definite state at the limit. (5) Therefore, if supertasks are physically possible, determinism must fail precisely at limit points. (6) Now apply the same structural pattern to a rational agent whose credence in a proposition P is updated deterministically at each t_n according to the rule: if credence(P) > 0.5, reduce it by a fixed epsilon; otherwise increase it by epsilon. (7) At any t < 2 the agent has a precise credence. (8) By parity with (3)-(4), at t = 2 there is no fact of the matter about her credence, yet determinism—here interpreted as the rational requirement that an agent's mental states be uniquely fixed by her prior reasoning—demands one. (9) Standard expected-utility decision theory, however, requires an agent to have definite credences at every point at which she might choose, including limit points of infinite deliberation; without a definite credence, no comparison of expected values is possible. (10) So if supertasks are coherent, rational decision-making collapses at limit points; but it does not collapse. (11) Hence supertasks are impossible.

This seems to prove far too much: it threatens not only Thomson-style scenarios but any infinite-horizon model with discrete updates. Where does the argument fail? I am most reluctant to abandon (3) or (9), yet I suspect the slide between (5) and (6) treats normative or psychological determinism as parallel to physical determinism without justification, and that (8) may conflate the absence of a computed value with the absence of a rational state. Which specific step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does the error lie in assuming that determinism applies to limit points in the same way it applies to intermediate points, or in applying the toggle-parity structure to credence updates at all? If the argument is actually valid, must we choose between denying supertasks and accepting that rational agents can have genuinely indeterminate mental states at the completion of infinite reasoning?
