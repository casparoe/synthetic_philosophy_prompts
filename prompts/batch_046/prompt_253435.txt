I'm putting together examples for my grad seminar on epistemic circularity and easy knowledge, and I keep running into this one colleague, Dr. Voss, who grades papers with really sharp but kind of idiosyncratic verdicts on bootstrapping cases. So here's her track record. First case: a geologist uses seismic readings to map a fault line, then uses that map to calibrate her seismograph, then uses the calibrated seismograph to refine the map. Voss said that's legitimate—she called it iterative convergence and said it's not vicious circularity because the error bars shrink with each pass. Second case: a student reads a logic textbook, uses the rules in it to check that the textbook itself is consistent, then uses that consistency check to justify trusting the rules for her exam proofs. Voss marked that down—she said it's classic bootstrapping and doesn't generate knowledge. Third case: a sleep researcher wears an EEG headset that reports REM stages, then uses her subjective feeling of being rested to validate the headset's algorithm, then uses the headset data to claim she was in REM even when she felt awake. Voss said that's okay, but only because the subjective report and the machine signal are seperate independent channels, not because one validates the other. Fourth case: a mathematician assumes a conjecture is false to derive a contradiction, concludes the conjecture is true, then cites the conjecture's truth as support for the logical framework she used to derive it. Voss said that's illegitimate—she wrote in the margin that you can't cash a check you just wrote.

Now I'm trying to predict what she'd say about three new scenarios, but I mostly care about mapping the rules. Scenario A: an AI system trains its reward model on human ratings, then uses the reward model to filter which human ratings are noise, then uses the filtered ratings to retrain the reward model. Scenario B: a hiker uses a compass to navigate through fog, then uses the fact that she eventually reached the summit to verify the compass was right, then uses that verified compass to navigate back down through thicker fog. Scenario C: a philosopher argues that if moral realism is false then moral reasoning is unreliable, observes that her own moral reasoning led her to moral realism, and therefore concludes moral realism is true and her reasoning is vindicated.

What do you think Voss would say about each—especially whether she allows the loop or blocks it? More importantly, what's the actual principle she's applying?
