Quick question for the list - I'm putting together a grad module on whether societies "collapse" or just reshuffle (Bronze Age palce economies, the Classic Maya, Easter Island, the usual suspects). There's an argumentative move I keep running into and I can't tell if it has a name or if I'm just being annoyed.

You start with "the Maya collapsed." Someone points out that people never left the lowlands, they just stopped building big monuments and writing dynastic texts - farming continued, languages survived. So the collapse-defender narrows the claim: "I only meant the political-administrative system collapsed." Then someone shows continuity in local governance, and it shrinks again to "the specific dynastic ideology collapsed," then "the long-distance trade network collapsed." Each revision is locally reasonable, but the emotional punch - catastrophe, dark age, civilizational failure - never gets revised downward with it. Meanwhile anyone who says "this was a transformation, not a failure" gets treated like they deny drought or warfare existed. So "collapse" becomes unfalsifiable by piecewise retreat, while "transformation" carries the burden of proving nothing bad happened.

Is there a concept in philosophy of history or science for this kind of asymmetrical definitional salvage? The nearest thing I can think of is an ad hoc auxiliary, but that misses the normative loading. Or is it just a motte-and-bailey? I want my students to see the pattern clearly, not just learn vocabulary - so if there's no real term, what's the sharpest way to describe the boundary conditions? When does a retreat stop being reasonable clarification and become saving the phenomenon?
