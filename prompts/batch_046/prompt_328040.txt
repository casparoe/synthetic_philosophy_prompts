Contemporary Stoicism is frequently packaged as a mental-health and resilience toolkit organized around the dichotomy of control: separate what lies within your power from what does not, and calibrate your emotional investment accordingly. Yet this framing becomes conceptually unstable when Stoic exercises are institutionalized—embedded in corporate resilience curricula, military psychological-training protocols, or clinical CBT modules—because the boundary between individual agency and collective or structural conditions blurs. Imagine you are advising a three-year conceptual research program, staffed by philosophers and conceptual psychologists rather than empirical ML researchers or neuroscientists, whose purpose is to resolve foundational ambiguities in applied Stoicism that currently constrain how practitioners design interventions.

Your task is to propose and defend a prioritized agenda of exactly three open questions or lines of conceptual work. For each entry, provide: (a) the precise conceptual problem it addresses, including how standard presentations of the dichotomy suppress or misrepresent it; (b) an argument for its priority relative to the other two—specifically, why it is upstream (resolving it constrains or enables progress on the others), how tractable it is for conceptual analysis rather than empirical study, and what form of progress—an argument, a distinction, a formal framework—would count as a meaningful result; (c) one specific conceptual mistake or oversight in current applied Stoicism that the question corrects.

In developing your agenda, you must engage with at least two of the following under-examined tensions: the problem of nested control, in which an agent's direct actions depend on institutional structures that are themselves partly subject to collective agency; the distinction between controlling an outcome and controlling one's judgment that the outcome belongs to the class of indifferents; and the challenge that systematically distorted social conditions can undermine the very cognitive processes through which an agent evaluates whether something is within her control, without that distortion itself being within her control.
