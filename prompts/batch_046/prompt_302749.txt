I'm preparing the framing for a philosophy seminar on collective responsibility, and I've become stuck on a tension in Karl Jaspers's fourfold distinction — criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt — that standard treatments of national guilt don't seem to resolve. Most applications assume a nation-state with sufficient diachronic continuity that present citizens stand in some meaningful institutional relation to past harms. But I want to push the framework to a limiting case: imagine a political community that has undergone what we might call constitutional death and rebirth — every original citizen and institutional actor is deceased, the governing legal framework has been entirely replaced rather than amended, the territory has been repartitioned, and the national name survives only as a historical reference point with no continuous bureaucratic or civic identity linking present inhabitants to the prior regime. Given this rupture, what are the most important implications of Jaspers's categories for whether any form of collective guilt can genuinely survive? Specifically, does political guilt collapse into mere historical liability — a debt owed by no particular present agent — once the citizenry has been wholly replaced, or can there be a non-metaphysical, institutionally transmitted obligation that binds current citizens without attributing personal moral fault to them? Furthermore, if metaphysical guilt — grounded in solidarity with the human condition rather than in specific complicity — is the only form that survives such a rupture, does that render collective guilt either universal to the point of uselessness for specific reparative claims, or strictly non-transferable and therefore useless for intergenerational justice? I am particularly interested in whether the distinction between being responsible for repairing harm and being guilty of causing it can be maintained once the institutional substrate that mediated that responsibility has dissolved.
