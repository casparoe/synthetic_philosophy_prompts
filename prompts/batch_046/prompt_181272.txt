Given that the conventional tripartite division of Western history into Antiquity, Middle Ages, and Modernity is now widely recognized as a retrospective historiographical construct—one that imposes false uniformity on non-European regions and that formalizes distinctions only centuries after the fact—does it not follow that all macro-periodization is epistemically bankrupt? Specifically, if the Renaissance was largely crystallized as an interpretive frame by nineteenth-century cultural historiography, and if the so-called Scientific Revolution represents not a discrete rupture but rather a gradual diffusion of practices across generations that the Annales school’s emphasis on long-term structural continuity further undermines, then should we not treat Galileo’s trial and the publication of Copernicus’s major work as merely local occurrences within an unbroken intellectual continuum? Once we grant that epochal labels are invented after the fact and that sharp chronological boundaries obscure persistent social and material continuities, is there any defensible reason to retain periodization at all, rather than adopting a purely continuous-flow model in which no epoch possesses greater historical reality than the rhetorical convenience of the historians who named it?
