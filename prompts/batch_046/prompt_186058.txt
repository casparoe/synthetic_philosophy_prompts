I facilitate an upper-level seminar in moral philosophy—fourteen graduate students, one seventy-five-minute session—where we have been working through agent-centered restrictions and the structural tension often called the paradox of deontology: the fact that a strict prohibition against using an innocent person as a mere means can remain fully binding for an agent even when honoring it guarantees that a greater number of people suffer precisely the same kind of violation, all of them inflicted by an external mechanism rather than by the agent herself. I want an in-class simulation, not a lecture or guided discussion, that forces participants to inhabit that tension through their own simultaneous choices before anyone in the room names the paradox. Design a structured activity with assigned roles, explicit rules, physical materials, and an outcome scheme that requires no real money: each participant should hold custodianship over one fictional “subject” in a separate district and must decide—without negotiation, after a brief private reading—whether to authorize a single direct intervention in her own district that halts a global cascade, or to refrain and allow the cascade to harm subjects elsewhere. The causal distinction between direct authorization and indirect mechanism-caused harm should be made tangible through a framing device or token system you invent for this exercise (not a standard trolley-style lever), and the rules should be arranged so that the group predictably confronts coordination anxiety, strategic anticipation, and moral discomfort before the facilitator explains anything. Include a debrief that maps the physical actions onto the conceptual distinction between agent-relative restrictions and agent-neutral aggregation, predicts what will go wrong the first time the activity is run—such as participants treating the scenario as pure game theory, attempting to negotiate a rotation scheme, or projecting guilt onto other custodians—and frankly states which normative feature of the paradox, specifically the insulation of indirect harms from the agent’s moral ledger, the simulation can stage but cannot philosophically justify, leaving that argumentative work to the facilitator. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; embed all conceptual machinery in the rules and in the novel protocol itself.
