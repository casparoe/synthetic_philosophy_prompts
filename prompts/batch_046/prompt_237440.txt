Consider the claim that Quine's behaviorist theory of meaning, together with his rejection of the analytic-synthetic distinction and his naturalized epistemology, forces a reduction of moral discourse to purely empirical descriptions of communal approval-behavior, leaving no irreducible normative residue whatsoever—not even a pragmatic or instrumental normativity preserved within science itself. Give me your credence that this claim is correct, decomposed as follows.

First, assign a rough probability (0 to 1) with a one-line justification to each term:
(a) that Quine's behaviorist account of linguistic meaning in Word and Object applies to normative vocabulary without special exemption or hidden presupposition;
(b) that his elimination of analyticity removes every non-empirical ground that could support genuinely prescriptive moral content;
(c) that Quine's naturalism permits a descriptive sociology or psychology of moral belief but categorically excludes first-order normative commitment as part of the scientific worldview;
(d) that there is a live internal tension between this reduction and the implicitly normative commitments of Quine's own scientific realism (e.g., the prescription to believe in accordance with evidence).

State your combination rule—whether multiplicative, weighted average, or another method—and compute your overall credence. Then identify which of the four terms is the most sensitive: the one that, if revised by ±0.30, would shift your final number the most. Finally, describe what kind of philosophical or textual consideration—without requiring citation of obscure primary sources—would be sufficient to move that particular term in either direction.
