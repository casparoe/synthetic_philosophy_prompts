In debates about European integration, defenders of ever-closer union frequently invoke the metaphor of a bicycle: the EU, on this view, is like a bicycle that must keep pedaling forward through deeper institutional integration, because if it stops it will fall over. Opponents counter that the image conceals a democratic deficit—citizens are told that pooling sovereignty is unavoidable since stasis equals collapse, yet they are seldom asked whether the trajectory itself is legitimate. Audit this analogy precisely. First, provide a correspondence table mapping the rider, the pedals, the forward motion, the ground, the fall, and any implied absence of brakes onto features of the EU institutional system. Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for the argument: in particular, whether democratic consent functions like a rider who can dismount, whether institutional change is as irreversible as gravity, and whether the “fall” describes a real empirical risk or a rhetorical device. Third, assess whether the conclusion—that sovereignty should continue to be transferred upward—actually follows from the mechanical premises, or whether the analogy merely restates it. Finally, if the bicycle image fails, suggest a more accurate analogy that preserves the idea of interdependence without suppressing the question of democratic reversibility.
