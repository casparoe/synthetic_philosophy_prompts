I am running an undergraduate philosophy seminar on social cooperation and evolutionary ethics — about eighteen students, one seventy-five-minute session — and I want them to feel, rather than merely hear about, the structural gap between evolutionary stability and individual normative reason. Design a runnable classroom game built around a prisoner's dilemma payoff structure, using only point tokens or small non-monetary rewards, that makes participants discover through their own choices how cooperation collapses when the conditions that sustain it — a long shadow of the future, accurate monitoring, and predictable partner rematching — are quietly undermined. Inject at least two hidden design features that are not disclosed in the rules given to students: for example, an undisclosed probability that any given round is the last with the current partner, a secretly executed "noise round" in which one randomly selected pair's choice is flipped without either knowing, or a false facilitator announcement that the game is ending followed by one or more unexpected further rounds. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that students initially settle into cooperative patterns, then experience a sudden spike in defection that they themselves cause, before the debrief reveals which hidden feature triggered it. The debrief must map specific moments in play onto the formal conditions for the evolution of cooperation — iteration probability, monitoring accuracy, partner selection — and must be frank about what the activity structurally cannot reproduce, such as evolutionary timescales or genetic transmission, explaining exactly how you will supply those missing elements verbally. Include predictions about what will go wrong the first time the activity is run — for instance, students refusing to believe the hidden rules existed, or emotional reactions to the noise round — and how to handle each failure. Finally, frame the experience so that participants come away seeing why an evolutionary explanation of cooperation's stability does not automatically provide an individual reason to cooperate in a particular interaction, and let that philosophical point emerge from the game mechanics rather than from a concluding lecture. Please write your answer in British English. I was discussing this with a colleague who argues that evolutionary accounts of cooperation are normatively empty, and I want the students to feel why that emptiness is a feature of the strategic structure itself rather than an abstract philosophical add-on.
