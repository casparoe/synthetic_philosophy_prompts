In a seminar on comparative constitutional design, we were debating whether the European Union's principle of subsidiarity—often glossed as decisions being taken at the lowest effective level—is structurally closer to American federalism under the Tenth Amendment or to a fault-tolerant distributed system in computing, where local nodes fail over to a central controller only when processing errors exceed a defined threshold. A fellow student insisted that both legal analogies are actually disguised versions of an older parenting metaphor: just as a parent should only tie a child's shoes when the child genuinely lacks the motor skills, a higher authority should only intervene when the lower one lacks the "competence"—a term that in German constitutional discourse carries the specific technical weight of Kompetenz rather than mere practical ability. This metaphor is regularly invoked to defend a strict, hands-off reading of Article 5 TEU and to resist what critics describe as competence creep into labor regulation and environmental standards.

I remain unconvinced that the metaphor does any independent work, and I want to see whether either structural comparison survives careful mapping. Could you audit both analogies—the parenting or shoe-tying version and the distributed-system failover version—by providing an explicit correspondence table for each? Specifically, what plays the role of the parent (or central controller), the child (or local node), the shoe-tying task (or regulatory objective), the criterion of "genuine inability," and the hidden normative assumption about whether autonomy is valuable intrinsically or only instrumentally? Then identify the precise disanalogies that matter for the legal conclusion: for instance, whether democratic legitimacy at the member-state level is analogous to a child's developing autonomy or to a server's processing capacity; whether the "failure" condition can be assessed neutrally without presupposing the very competence allocation that is in dispute; and whether the analogy falsely symmetrizes voluntary family relations with treaty-bound political obligations. Tell me whether the conclusion that Brussels or Washington should stay out survives once these breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the normative weight all along. If neither analogy holds up, suggest whether a more honest structural comparison might be drawn from the Swiss system of concurrent powers with explicit federal preemption rules, or from Proudhon's model of federalism as free association, and whether adopting that frame changes the normative outcome.
