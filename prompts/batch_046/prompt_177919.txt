I have been preparing a long-form piece on whether distributive ethics can move beyond stalemate, and in conversations with editors I keep returning to a specific tension that seems both well-worn and perpetually unresolved: the dispute between prioritarian weighting and the leveling-down objection. Rather than another polemic, I would like to understand what a genuinely adversarial collaboration—conducted with the discipline of a joint scientific paper—might actually yield if the participants refused to concede ground on rhetoric but agreed to lock down every claim precisely.

Please draft the joint statement that two committed interlocutors could both sign: on one side, a prioritarian who accepts a continuous, concave weighting function that gives absolute priority to the worst off but denies that equality carries any independent moral weight; on the other, a relational egalitarian who grants that equality may have instrumental value but maintains that any adequate theory must treat downward leveling—making some people worse off with no compensating improvement to anyone else—as a decisive failure. I want this to be more than a generic comparison. The statement should address a concrete, somewhat underexplored case: a public-health authority deciding whether to redistribute access to an expensive neurocognitive remediation therapy in a population where baseline capacity has been bimodally depressed by a localized environmental toxin, knowing that perfect equality could only be achieved by restricting the highest-capacity individuals without raising the lowest.

The document should contain: first, a numbered section of agreed claims about the formal structure of the leveling-down objection—specifically, whether it targets all non-egalitarian principles or only those lacking a particular curvature property, and whether prioritarian weighting can be calibrated to avoid it without collapsing into equality; second, the precise disagreement formulations, each phrased in language its own side accepts; third, the cruxes—sub-questions like whether downward leveling is ever permissible if it expresses equal civic standing, and whether prioritarianism can justify leaving some people permanently below a threshold; and fourth, for each crux, the conceptual or empirical test both parties agree is relevant, with each side's predicted result recorded in advance. Format this as agreed claims followed by a disagreements table, include a brief methodological note on which issues the collaboration marked as value-laden and therefore left unsettled, and do not include bibliographies, citation lists, or reading recommendations—the architecture should stand entirely on its own.
