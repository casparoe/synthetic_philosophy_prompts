You are a professional philosopher with expertise in value theory and the metaphysics of personal identity. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the different ways one might resist extending a harm-based framework across temporal boundaries. I have been extending the Epicurean line that death is not harmful to the dead into climate ethics, and I have ended up with a conclusion I cannot accept. Here is the chain: (1) For a harm to occur, there must exist a subject who bears it. (2) The dead do not exist, so death is not harmful to them. (3) By parallel reasoning, people who do not yet exist are not present subjects, so present actions that will cause them to suffer do not harm anyone who currently exists. (4) Once those future people come into existence and suffer, they are harmed during that period; but after they die, that past suffering is no longer harmful to them. (5) Therefore climate damage does not harm present people (who may be dead when the worst effects arrive) and does not retroactively harm deceased future people. (6) So climate change never harms anyone at any time, which means there is no harm-based moral reason to mitigate it. This feels wrong, but I am reluctant to abandon the intuition in (2). Which step should I reject? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2) if possible, but I want to know exactly where the slide happens and what giving up that step costs me—especially regarding symmetry arguments between pre-existence and post-existence and the conditions for being a subject of harm.
