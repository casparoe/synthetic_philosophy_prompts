In drafting the chapter of my dissertation on anthropic reasoning and cosmological argumentation, I keep returning to the urn analogy that dominates every seminar discussion of the self-sampling assumption, yet I find it is rarely audited beyond a quick intuitive nod. The analogy runs as follows: imagine an urn containing an unknown number of balls, where each ball represents a discrete observer belonging to some reference class; you have drawn exactly one ball—yourself—and from that draw you update your beliefs about the total number of balls in the urn. Proponents use this to license two familiar moves: first, the Doomsday-style inference that an enormous urn makes your relatively early draw surprising, and second, the response to the presumptuous philosopher, who notes that if the urn were filled mostly with minimal, fleeting observers—Boltzmann brains—then your being a richly structured human observer should count as strong evidence against such an enormous population. In both cases, the analogy treats indexical self-location as if it were ordinary statistical sampling with a uniform prior over interchangeable individuals, and it is precisely that statistical machinery I want to stress-test.

I am asking for a precise mapping audit rather than a survey of anthropic positions. What, exactly, corresponds to the ball, the urn, the draw mechanism, and the uniform distribution? Where does the analogy hold—perhaps in framing reference-class uncertainty as a quantitative problem—and where does it collapse once we recognize that there is no temporal or causal process that “selects” me from a pre-existing pool, since my existence and the definition of the reference class are co-extensive? I am especially interested in whether the analogy smuggles in an unjustified atomicity: it requires observers to be discrete, fungible units, but conscious perspectives may differ in phenomenal intensity, temporal duration, or structural integration in ways that resist being counted as identical balls. Does the presumptuous philosopher’s challenge expose a genuine flaw in self-sampling, or does it instead reveal that the urn model has already imported assumptions—uniform weighting across observer-types—that fail precisely when the reference class is not a bag of identical items? If the urn breaks down, is there a more apt analogy—perhaps one of activation or illumination rather than selection—that captures self-indication without dragging in statistical sampling conditions that do not apply to indexical facts? I want the correspondence table explicit, the disanalogies separated into those that damage the intended inference and those that are merely decorative, and a judgment about whether any version of the analogy can actually support the conclusions it is routinely asked to carry.
