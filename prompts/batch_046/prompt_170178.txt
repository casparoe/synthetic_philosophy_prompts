I'm writing a feature on whether the metaphysics of persistence can illuminate real-world heritage crises, and I want you to work through one case with discipline. Analyze the 2019 Notre-Dame de Paris fire and the subsequent reconstruction debate as a live test case for endurantism versus perdurantism applied to cultural monuments. Don't stop at the intuitive question of whether the rebuilt cathedral is "the same building"; instead, map the two metaphysical positions onto the actual choices architects and the French state confronted—whether adopting Viollet-le-Duc's spire design versus a contemporary replacement alters the object's identity, whether the pre-fire and post-reconstruction phases are temporal parts of a single four-dimensional worm, and whether material continuity carries weight when the vast majority of roof timbers are lost. Then press each theory where it is weakest: can an endurantist explain why emotional and legal attachments survive near-total material replacement, and can a perdurantist account for the widespread intuition that faithful restoration "recovers" rather than "successively creates" the monument? Finally, introduce a factor that neither standard view anticipates well—namely, that the cathedral's identity is partly fixed by institutional recognition, including UNESCO World Heritage listing, state ownership, and canonical architectural historiography—and ask whether this social-ontological dimension forces a revision of the metaphysical framework or can be absorbed within it without distortion. Conclude with what the episode reveals about whether persistence conditions for artifacts should be sensitive to collective recognition at all.
