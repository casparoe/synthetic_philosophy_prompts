I'm trying to stabilize my position on corruption for a cross-cultural ethics workshop and want a consistency audit before I present it. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Corruption should be analyzed functionally: an office-holder uses their position to serve private or partisan interests in a way that undermines the legitimate purpose of the institution they serve, regardless of whether local law prohibits the act.

(2) This functional analysis is culturally universal: if a practice structurally undermines institutional purpose in Society A and in Society B, it is corruption in both, even if Society B lacks the concept and treats the practice as normal.

(3) Clientelism and patron-client networks in many societies are therefore not corruption, because they do not subvert the institution; they constitute its actual operating logic, and treating them as corruption is a category mistake imported from Western legalism.

(4) Gift-giving to officials—whether framed as respect, reciprocity, or a "grease payment"—is corruption in every context, because it structurally redirects official judgment toward the donor and away from institutional purpose, irrespective of local normative acceptance.

(5) Western anti-corruption frameworks get the core functional concept right but over-legalize it: they wrongly criminalize ordinary clientelism, yet they remain closer to the truth than anthropological relativism that denies any universal standard.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which only appear to conflict, and how severely. In particular, does (4) sit comfortably with the context-sensitive functionalism in (1) and (3), or does it smuggle in an unconditional prohibition that my framework cannot actually deliver? Is there a hidden conflict between my universalist ambition in (2) and my willingness in (3) to excuse practices that Western codes condemn? Rank the tensions by severity, specify for each what the least costly revision would be, and then identify which single change to the set would resolve the most problems without abandoning the basic orientation.
