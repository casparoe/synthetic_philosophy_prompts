"In her Respuesta a Sor Filotea, Sor Juana uses the ladder image—creatures leading up to the Creator—to show that studying nature is a religious duty, not vanity. This supports women's education because if knowing God requires studying the world, then excluding women from that study blocks their path to salvation. She treats empirical observation and scholastic study as basically the same thing: both are steps on the ladder. She also shows epistemic humility by saying human reason is weak and must submit to divine revelation, which sounds a lot like Descartes's method of doubt, where you tear down false beliefs to build knowledge back up. Because of this humility, she thinks women should be allowed to learn, since recognizing limits is part of worship. The 'recursive humility' idea fits well here: every time she admits she doesn't know something, that opens the door to study more, so humility isn't an endpoint but a cycle. Overall, she doesn't really need male scholastic authority because direct observation gives women their own access to truth."
