The exam question read as follows: "Consider the ontological status of generational categories such as Baby Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z. Some sociologists treat them as demographic cohorts defined by birth ranges; others treat them as interpretive communities shaped by shared historical exposure. Assess whether these categories correspond to real kinds—natural or social—or are merely nominal. In your answer, address: (i) the boundary vagueness problem for birth-year ranges (e.g., those born around 1980 or 1996); (ii) whether shared historical experience can ground kind-membership even when birth ranges overlap; and (iii) whether a cohort can be a real kind if its defining conditions are relational (defined by position relative to events like widespread broadband adoption or economic recessions) rather than intrinsic. You may also consider whether the concept of a diachronic relational kind—a kind whose members share a position in a causal network that unfolds over time, with membership conditions that can be revised retroactively by historians—helps resolve the tension."

Here is the student's answer: Generational cohorts are clearly not natural kinds in the sense of chemical elements, since they lack intrinsic biological or physical essences. Instead, they should be classified as social kinds—specifically, what philosophers of social science call historical kinds, groups constituted not by internal constitution but by shared causal exposure to macro-level events. The boundary vagueness for people born around 1980 or 1996 is a real issue, but it is not fatal: as the sorites paradox demonstrates, many legitimate categories have fuzzy edges, and the fact that we draw conventional lines does not mean the underlying clustering is unreal. Where generations are distinctive is that membership is defined relationally—being positioned after events like the launch of the iPhone or the 2008 recession—rather than by intrinsic properties like DNA or blood type. This relational structure actually strengthens their claim to reality, because even physical kinds such as planet are now defined partly by relational criteria (orbiting the Sun and clearing the neighborhood). Following Searle's account of institutional facts, however, this relational status requires collective acceptance: a cohort only exists as a kind if society collectively assigns it the status of a meaningful group. Therefore, generations are real but institutionally dependent kinds, and the boundary problem is epistemic rather than ontological.
