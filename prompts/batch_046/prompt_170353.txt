Below are draft notes for a short philosophical essay I am working on, titled provisionally “Causal Convergence and the Direction of Time: Beyond the Entropy Gradient.” I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or reading suggestions—only conceptual feedback.

The central argument runs as follows. Standard Boltzmannian explanations treat the arrow of time as derivative from a low-entropy boundary condition: entropy increases because the past was statistically improbable. I grant this explains the thermodynamic gradient, but I claim it does not by itself explain why causation is directed past-to-future, or why “earlier than” tracks that gradient rather than running counter to it. To close the gap, I propose supplementing the entropy account with a relational, topological analysis of causal structure. On this view, the past is not defined by possessing an intrinsic “earlier-than” property, nor merely by lower entropy, but by functioning as the unique convergence zone in the causal graph—the region where causally independent lines systematically merge rather than diverge. This convergence is structurally irreversible in a way that local equilibrium fluctuations are not, because merging branches reduce the dimensionality of causal antecedents available for explanation. Thus “A is earlier than B” should be analyzed not as “A precedes B in an intrinsic temporal flow,” but as “A lies within the convergence region that structurally determines B.”

I then consider the circularity objection: if “convergence” is already defined in terms of causal influence, and causal influence is temporally asymmetric, then the account presupposes the very arrow it seeks to ground. My tentative reply distinguishes topological connectivity—a structural relation among nodes—from temporal precedence. Connectivity can be described in purely graph-theoretic terms (shared ancestors, branch merging) without invoking “before” or “after.” However, I acknowledge a vulnerability: without an independent criterion for identifying nodes and edges that does not already smuggle in temporal ordering, the distinction may collapse. I also briefly examine the implications for the metaphysics of time. If this relational-topological account succeeds, it undermines one prominent motivation for the A-theory—namely, that an objective, flowing “now” is required to explain why change has a direction—because directional asymmetry is relocated into static causal topology. For B-theory, by contrast, the account seems compatible but demanding: it requires treating causal convergence not as a dynamical process unfolding in time, but as a fixed structural feature of a four-dimensional manifold, which raises questions about whether B-theory can genuinely accommodate the modal intuition that the past is “settled” in a way the future is not, without reintroducing temporal passage through the back door.

I would appreciate comments focused on three specific issues. First, is the move from an entropy gradient to a causal-topological convergence legitimate, or does it covertly import the temporal asymmetry it claims to derive? Second, can the distinction between topological connectivity and temporal precedence be sustained rigorously, or does any non-circular specification of “convergence” necessarily depend on prior directional notions? Third, are the implications I sketch for the A-theory/B-theory debate accurate, or do they overreach—particularly my suggestion that B-theory can absorb causal convergence as a static feature without losing explanatory power? Please treat this as a request for conceptual criticism rather than a request for sources or further literature.
