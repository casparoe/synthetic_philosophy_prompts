I'm writing this for a final paper in my upper-level moral philosophy seminar, where we've spent the semester working through Hume's law and the naturalistic fallacy in its various reconstructions. After engaging with the standard logical-form and open-question readings, I've come to defend a somewhat specific reconstruction that I'm tentatively calling the authority-transfer thesis: the is-ought gap is best understood not as a failure of logical form or definitional openness, but as a structural failure in the transmission of practical authority. On this view, descriptive premises—however richly supplemented with evolutionary, psychological, or functional information—cannot by themselves generate the distinctive binding, reason-giving force that normative conclusions claim, because that authority is not the kind of property that can be inherited from factual descriptions. Any apparently successful derivation of an "ought" from an "is," I maintain, succeeds only by covertly importing a normative premise that already carries the requisite authority, and recognizing this is what explains why enriched descriptions still seem to leave normative questions genuinely open.

What I need is a committed, honest assessment of what would genuinely count against this reconstruction—not a survey of familiar objections, but the defeaters ranked by decisiveness. Which formal results or conceptual analyses would show that practical authority can indeed be generated from purely descriptive premises without hidden normativity? Are there particular configurations—perhaps involving institutional facts, functional norms, or the logic of practical reasons—where descriptive premises seem to produce binding conclusions in a way that cannot be dissolved by locating a smuggled normative premise? And if defenders of my view can always retreat to the claim that some hidden normative commitment has been overlooked, does that render the thesis unfalsifiable in a damaging way, or is that insulation actually appropriate for a thesis of this kind? I also want you to address whether well-established rival frameworks in contemporary metaethics—certain naturalistic or constructivist accounts—already dissolve the problem by denying that normative authority needs any special source at all. Please take a definite position: either defend the authority-transfer thesis against its strongest defeaters and tell me exactly which objections remain live and which are fatal, or concede that it collapses under specific pressure and name precisely where. I want a committed verdict with reasons, not an even-handed menu of options.
