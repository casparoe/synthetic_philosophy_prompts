The project concerns the so-called inefficacy objection in collective harm—roughly, that one person's carbon emissions, vote, or meat consumption makes no perceptible difference to climate change, electoral outcomes, or factory farming. Rather than treating this as a single moral puzzle, I want to ask whether the objection takes structurally different forms depending on the causal architecture of the harm in question. Specifically, I propose to distinguish three architectures: linear-aggregative marginal contribution (climate), threshold-dependent contribution where harm is produced only upon reaching a collective cutoff (voting), and market-mediated contribution with variable supply elasticity (industrial animal agriculture). For each architecture, I will ask whether the standard moral responses to inefficacy—such as appeals to expected utility, collective intentionality, or duty not to participate in wrongful schemes—survive intact, or whether they need to be reconstructed around the specific causal logic of the case.
