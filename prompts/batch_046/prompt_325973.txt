I serve on the classification board of an international archive of twentieth-century humanitarian crises, and we are revising our taxonomy for mass-mortality events. At present, the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of 1845–1852, and the mortality crisis during China’s Great Leap Forward (1959–1961) are filed under “Natural Disaster: Famine”. A working group proposes reclassifying all three under a new category, “Political Crime: Entitlement Collapse”, drawing on Amartya Sen’s argument that these deaths were not principally caused by aggregate food scarcity but by the collapse of exchange entitlements shaped by political and distributive choices. The minority objection holds that this framing suppresses genuine ecological triggers—monsoon failure in Bengal, potato blight in Ireland, and adverse weather patterns in China—and risks anachronistically imposing modern moral-legal categories onto complex historical processes. The board must decide by the end of this quarter, with any new taxonomy implemented from the start of the next fiscal year. Please draft the formal opinion I shall circulate before the vote: recommend a concrete course of action—full adoption, retention, or a hybrid taxonomy—with a clear timeline; treat the ecological-trigger objection seriously, identifying where it is strongest; apply Sen’s entitlement analysis consistently across all three cases without treating them as interchangeable; and write in British English. Finally, state what specific historical evidence or philosophical argument would most weaken your recommendation, and pinpoint the stage in your reasoning that is most likely to fail.
