Kant denies direct duties to animals because they lack rational nature and autonomy, so they are not ends in themselves. Instead we have indirect duties mediated through our duties to humanity. If I am cruel to a dog, the wrongness is not in harming the dog but in damaging my own moral character, which makes me more likely to harm rational beings later. This defense is actually quite strong because it ties animal treatment directly to human morality rather than ignoring it. Some people think suffering is invisible here, but that's a mistake—the indirect view explains why suffering registers with us, just through the lens of human virtue.

There is also moral residue, which is the discomfort we feel seeing animals hurt even when we don't think they have rights. Kant can accept this as long as it doesn't become a direct duty, because it's essentially just an emotional response that doesn't confer moral status. So the framework can accommodate it without collapsing.

For the policy case: the farming practice should be banned because allowing cruelty in agriculture corrupts people's characters, so the state should prohibit it to protect human moral development. Even if the community suffers economically, preventing corruption is more important. The tension is that banning it might cause human suffering through economic loss, which could itself damage moral development, but Kant would still support the ban since harm to animals is never the direct reason—only human corruption matters. So the logic mostly holds up, though applying it in real communities is a bit messy because people depend on these practices.
