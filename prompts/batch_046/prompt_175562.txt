I sit on my university's interdisciplinary curriculum board and we vote next week on a pilot program that teaches collective reasoning through judgment aggregation. Students vote separately on three normative premises about distributive justice and institutional neutrality, then an algorithm derives the groups collective stance on endorsing a local community land-trust proposal. The catch is the discursive dilemma: the premises can each win a majority while the conclusion they imply loses, or the conclusion can win while one premise gets rejected. The designers want to patch this with epistemic veto loops, where a premise with minority support below some threshold suspends the derived conclusion unless the group votes to override, but that just pushes the contradiction up a level.

Half the board thinks this is just a neat pedagogical tool for teaching logical consistency. The other half thinks it manufactures a collective opinion that no actual student holds and undermines the whole point of deliberation. I need a decision memo recommending approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with reasoning the designers could actually respond to, plus an honest account of the best argument on the losing side.
