I'm taking an upper-division philosophy of science seminar, and we've been working through the pessimistic meta-induction and its realist responses for several weeks. The dialectic is remarkably stable: the anti-realist side lists past successful theories—phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether—that were eventually abandoned, and asks why we should not expect the same fate for current theories; the realist side answers with structural preservation, selective realism about working entities, or the no-miracles argument that only approximate truth could explain predictive success. Each reply is familiar, each counter-reply is anticipated, and nobody in the literature or in our discussion seems to shift position. My working theory is that the impasse is not really about the historical record or about ontology, but about two incompatible implicit models of what theoretical change actually is. The anti-realist seems to picture change as wholesale replacement—tearing down one building and erecting another on the same site—so that past falsehood is direct evidence of present falsehood. The realist seems to picture change as the rewiring of an explanatory network, in which some nodes (mathematical structures, causal mechanisms) remain fixed even as the surrounding ontology is reconfigured. If that is right, then the two sides may not be disagreeing about whether science tracks reality at all; they may be operating with different conceptions of what "tracking reality" means—correspondence to an ontology versus coherence within a preserved explanatory architecture—and answering different questions under the single heading of "scientific realism." Is this diagnosis correct? Is the disagreement verbal, substantive but hidden, or a case of two questions run together? Please diagnose it using what each side actually says about preservation, success, and change, and tell me what, if anything, would count as genuine progress: a formal model of theory dynamics, a historical case study that clearly favors one picture of change over the other, a conceptual distinction that disentangles the two conceptions, or an argument that the original framing is ill-posed and should be abandoned.
