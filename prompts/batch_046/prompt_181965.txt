As a retired physicist who has recently begun attending an adult-education seminar in moral and political philosophy, I have become struck by an institutional pattern that my earlier scientific training makes me want to formalize, though I lack the philosophical vocabulary to do so precisely. In most advanced economies, health technology assessment boards allocate scarce medical interventions—such as gene therapies or renal dialysis—using cost-effectiveness metrics that implicitly treat welfare, typically measured in quality-adjusted life years, as the currency of distributive equality: a treatment is funded when it delivers comparable welfare gains per unit cost across patient groups. By contrast, when the same governments mandate accessibility modifications—wheelchair ramps, sign-language interpretation, sensory accommodations—they do not apply any welfare-maximizing calculus. Instead, they guarantee a standardized bundle of resources to anyone meeting a disability classification, without measuring how much subjective welfare or which specific human capabilities those resources actually secure for each recipient, and without asking whether an alternative allocation might yield greater aggregate well-being.

I am trying to understand what philosophical commitments about equality this divergence presupposes. Does the health-allocation practice commit us to a welfare-based theory, while the accessibility practice commits us to a resource-based or capability-based theory? Or can both be licensed by a single underlying view—perhaps one that distinguishes between domains where adaptive preferences are suspected and domains where they are not? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value that makes both patterns coherent, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and indicate whether the divergence is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of equality could license it. Then tell me what a unified policy built consistently on a capability-based conception—one that allocates according to the expansion of central human functionings rather than standardized resources—would have to change in each domain, and whether such a unification would resolve the tension or simply relocate it. I would especially value an answer that does not depend on any particular scholar’s formulation, since I have not yet worked through the primary literature in depth.
