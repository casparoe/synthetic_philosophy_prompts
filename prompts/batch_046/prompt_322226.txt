I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been thinking about why human extinction feels catastrophic in a way that goes far beyond the sum of individual deaths. I encountered the idea—associated with the philosopher Samuel Scheffler and sometimes called the "afterlife conjecture"—that much of what gives our present lives meaning depends on the expectation that humanity will continue after we die, carrying forward the projects and attachments we initiate. I want to test whether my intuitions about specific extinction scenarios can be captured by a single principle, or whether some of them must be abandoned.

Here are my verdicts on six concrete cases.

Case 1: A painless asteroid wipes out all biological human life in 2120. No one suffers; every death is instantaneous. Verdict: profoundly bad, and the badness is not adequately explained by aggregating individual deaths.

Case 2: Humanity peacefully transitions to digital consciousness, but biological reproduction stops and the last biological human dies. Digital descendants flourish for millennia, preserving records and algorithms but not embodied cultural practices. Verdict: significantly less bad than Case 1, but still a substantial loss—something about biological continuity or embodied transmission seems missing.

Case 3: One generation lives full lives, completes its long-term projects, and then voluntarily stops reproducing so that the species ends gently after they die. Verdict: the extinction is bad, but noticeably less bad than Case 1, because the people who initiated the projects at least saw them through without abrupt truncation—though the absence of any successors still undermines the point of those projects.

Case 4: Humanity continues indefinitely, but only as a static archive maintained by non-interacting aliens who study us without participation or cultural exchange. Verdict: this avoids the catastrophe of Case 1, yet feels hollow; still, my intuition ranks it clearly better than extinction.

Case 5: I am contributing to an open-source infrastructure I hope will be maintained for centuries. If I learn with certainty that humanity will end in fifty years, my motivation collapses. Verdict: present value depends on expected future continuation, supporting the core of the afterlife conjecture.

Case 6: Extinction in 2100 saves every currently living person from a painful, fatal pandemic, but ends all future life permanently. Verdict: I still judge the extinction itself to be a loss rather than a net benefit, which suggests that future lives have value that is not merely instrumental to present welfare.

The tensions I see: Case 2 introduces a requirement for biological or embodied continuity that Scheffler's framing does not obviously include; Case 4 suggests that mere persistence is insufficient without some form of agency or cultural transmission; and Cases 3 and 6 together imply that future generations matter non-instrumentally, yet Case 3 also makes the badness of extinction sensitive to whether present agents witnessed the closure of their projects.

Is there a coherent principle—perhaps centered on "intergenerational project inheritance" or "value transmission chains"—that can accommodate all six verdicts by specifying exactly what kind of future continuation must exist and in what form? If not, which verdict should I overturn, and what does that cost? Please state any candidate principle precisely, map each clause back to the case that motivates it, predict its verdict on the following case I have not resolved: humanity splits into two permanently isolated colonies, one thriving for eons and the other dying out quickly—does the extinction of the second colony constitute a serious loss if the first continues?—and be explicit about which of my six verdicts the best principle cannot save. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I want an analysis of the logical structure of these judgments, not references to literature.
