I just finished a long and somewhat exhausting argument with a close friend about what actually makes a life go well. She is drawn to what she calls a radical quietist position—essentially tranquilism: only the absence of craving (sometimes rendered taṇhā, though she applies it structurally) and the absence of suffering have intrinsic value. No positive goods—no understanding, no aesthetic experience, no relational depth—count in themselves; they matter only instrumentally if they reduce suffering or craving. I think the view collapses once you press on what pure absence amounts to, but I want to force my own reasoning into the open rather than just trading intuitions.

What is your actual credence—say, a specific number between 0 and 1, not a conditional "it depends"—that tranquilism, precisely defined as the conjunction of three claims, is the correct normative account of well-being? The claims are: (i) suffering and craving exhaust the domain of intrinsic disvalue, so that states like profound alienation, existential dread that lacks phenomenally painful content, or the absence of meaningful orientation are either reducible to one of those two or are not intrinsic disvalues at all; (ii) there are no intrinsic positive values independent of suffering-reduction, meaning a life with minimal suffering and zero craving but also zero knowledge, beauty, or love is at least as good as any alternative; and (iii) the optimal mental endpoint is maximal absence of both suffering and craving, including both propositional desires and affective urges, rather than the presence of any competing good. I do not want a bare number. Decompose the question.

First, assign a rough probability to (i): that the negative domain is genuinely exhausted by suffering and craving, with no further independent disvalues. Second, assign a rough probability to (ii): that genuinely positive goods exist—say, understanding, aesthetic experience, or relational depth—that are not merely instrumental to reducing craving, and that a life containing them alongside some suffering can be better than a life with near-zero suffering and craving but also near-zero such goods. Third, assign a rough probability to (iii): that pure absence is conceptually coherent as a normative endpoint, specifically whether eliminating all craving necessarily eliminates the conditions for any positive mental content, and whether a being in a state of total absence—imagine a permanently anesthetized or deeply contemplative subject with no propositional desires and no affective urges—counts as flourishing rather than as a kind of functional non-existence of the subject. Then combine these into your overall credence, and tell me which of the three sub-questions your final number is most sensitive to.

Finally, suppose my friend holds this view at about 0.85 and I hold it at about 0.15. Given your decomposition, where is the disagreement most likely located—which of the three sub-questions above divides us? And what specific argument or empirical finding—say, from affective neuroscience, from comparative studies of contemplative states, or from axiological intuition—would most move your own number?
