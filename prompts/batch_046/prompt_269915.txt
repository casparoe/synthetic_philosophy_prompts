I'm taking a seminar on rational choice under extreme uncertainty, and our instructor, Professor Voss, has staked out judgments on five scenarios that seem to hang together but could be driven by different underlying rules. She rejects restructuring one's life around Pascal's Wager, arguing that an incommensurable prior and unspecified causal mechanism block any well-defined expected-utility computation, even if the payoff is infinite. She endorses donating $100 to a verified asteroid-deflection initiative that carries a 1-in-50,000 chance of preventing extinction. She dismisses a street-level "cosmic blackmail" in which a claimant asserts a 1-in-10^30 probability of destroying Earth via unspecified mental powers and demands $5, treating both the mechanism and the probability assignment as rationally inadmissible. She supports a climate-adaptation policy with a well-modeled 0.2% risk of severe economic harm and a 99.8% chance of averting catastrophic warming, saying institutional risk-pooling makes the calculus ordinary. Finally, she argues that even a peer-reviewed physics preprint assigning a 1-in-10^20 chance to suffering in distant multiverse branches—mediated by an unspecified quantum correlation—does not justify paying $1 to "block" it, because ontological distance combined with causal opacity defeats moral actionability.

This Thursday we turn to a case she has not yet ruled on. A researcher posts a preprint—not peer-reviewed, but with formally correct derivations within an established branching-multiverse framework—proposing that performing a specific, harmless thirty-second mental exercise, at literally zero material cost, can halve a 1-in-10^35 chance that 10^35 sentient entities suffer in an ontologically distant branch. The proposed correlation is labeled "observer-coupled" but is not experimentally testable from Earth, and the mechanism remains unspecified beyond the formal model. Predict Professor Voss's verdict—should one perform the exercise?—and articulate at least two competing rules that accommodate her first five judgments equally well: for example, a hard epistemic-probability floor that discards outcomes below a likelihood threshold regardless of stakes; a mechanism-credibility combined with ontological-proximity filter that demands both testable causation and local ontology; and a zero-cost symmetry principle holding that genuinely harmless, costless actions require only a non-zero rational belief, irrespective of probability magnitude or ontological distance. For each rule, state what it predicts about the mental exercise, identify which of her prior cases is the closest analog, and explain whether her record genuinely underdetermines the answer. If it does, name the further case—perhaps a near-ontology, physically implausible mechanism with genuinely zero cost—that would best distinguish the rules, and say what principle she would be adopting with each possible choice.
