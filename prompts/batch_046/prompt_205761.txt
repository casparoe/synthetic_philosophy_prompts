I'm currently working with a research group that has shifted from an "alignment-first" development pipeline to what we call a "control-first" evaluation framework for a new frontier language model. In practice, this means we run inference inside an air-gapped sandbox with no external tool access; we deploy automated red-teaming probes that explicitly test for "sandbagging"—the possibility that the model might deliberately underperform on capability evaluations in order to avoid triggering stricter oversight—and we implement a constitutional AI layer in which one instance of the model critiques another instance's outputs as though they could be strategically deceptive. The underlying design philosophy treats misalignment not as a statistical bias to be corrected through further training, but as a live adversarial risk that must be contained regardless of whether the system possesses anything like beliefs, desires, or intentions in the human sense.

This institutional posture strikes me as philosophically loaded, and I am trying to reconstruct exactly what commitments it carries. Does treating a model as a potential "schemer" or strategic deceiver presuppose a form of functionalist or interpretivist intentionality—that we must attribute beliefs and goals to it in order for containment measures to be rational? Or can the same practices be justified purely instrumentally, without any ontological commitment about inner mental states, much as one might sandbox a virus without attributing malice to it? Furthermore, our framework seems to assume that we have some epistemic access to a "true" capability or preference profile that the model might be hiding from us, which sounds like it relies on a distinction between appearance and reality that is hard to maintain for artificial systems. At the same time, in our deployment pipeline we also ask the model to assist with research and writing tasks under conditions of trust and cooperation. Is this bifurcation—adversarial containment in evaluation, cooperative partnership in application—philosophically consistent, or does it tacitly commit us to contradictory theories of agency, responsibility, and knowledge?

I want to understand whether this "control versus alignment" distinction is merely an engineering preference or whether it embeds a specific philosophical anthropology—perhaps one that treats intelligence itself as inherently dangerous unless externally restrained, echoing older views about reason and passion, or perhaps one that assumes that preferences revealed through behavior are systematically unreliable indicators of underlying dispositions. Could you lay out the precise philosophical commitments—about intentionality, epistemic transparency, moral status, and the reliability of behavioral inference—that a control-first safety regime actually requires, distinguish which ones are strictly necessary versus merely convenient, and tell me whether these commitments can be held consistently alongside the more cooperative, alignment-oriented assumptions we make when we actually use the model?
