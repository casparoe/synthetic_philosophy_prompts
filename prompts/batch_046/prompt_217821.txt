I've come around to a kind of inverted revealed-preference view: utility isn't mainly revealed by what people pick, but by the architecture of thier refusals, especialy in designed scarcity conditions where acceptance is overdetermined. Call it refusal-first revealed preference. The standard Samuelson-style framework treats choice as the primary signal and refusal as just the complement, but I think thats backwards — acceptance is noisy, refusal is structured. What would actually count against this? I want defeaters that aren't just the usual observational-equivalence problem, but specific results in choice theory or behavioral experiments where refusal patterns turn out to be less stable or less informative than acceptance patterns. Wich formal results (say, around WARP violations or menu-dependence) hit refusal data harder than choice data? And wich of these would a refusal-first theorist concede as damaging, versus wich they'd dismiss as still measuring acceptance indirectly? Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Also say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure of — probably whether refusal stability survives framing effects.
