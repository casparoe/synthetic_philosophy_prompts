In a graduate seminar on moral epistemology, two doctoral students—Dr. Chen and Dr. Okonkwo—debated whether cross-cultural differences in folk morality undermine moral objectivity. Chen noted that in WEIRD populations moral judgment clusters heavily around harm and fairness, whereas in many small-scale societies loyalty, authority, and purity carry comparable or greater weight, and she argued that this divergence is best explained by culturally calibrated social ecologies rather than by tracking mind-independent moral facts: if objective moral truths existed, she claimed, we should expect broad convergence on core values rather than clustering by subsistence pattern and social structure. Okonkwo responded that divergence in weightings is fully compatible with objectivity—just as engineers apply shared physical laws to different design constraints—provided all cultures draw on a universal set of moral foundations. Chen countered that redescribing every observed divergence as a difference in "parameter weights" renders the universal-foundations thesis unfalsifiable: in societies where purity violations appear to carry no moral force whatsoever, it is more parsimonious to say the relevant foundation is absent, not merely calibrated to zero. Okonkwo then proposed a distinction between "architectural divergence" (the presence or absence of entire moral-cognitive modules) and "calibration divergence" (identical architecture with different environmental inputs), insisting that only the former genuinely threatens objectivity and that the empirical record demonstrates only the latter. Chen replied that calibration can run so deep—determining which situations even trigger moral evaluation—that the architectural/calibration boundary dissolves; if a culture genuinely lacks the concept of individual rights, she asked, should that be analyzed as a zero-weight parameter within a shared framework or as an architectural absence?

Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: who offers the stronger reply at each turn? In particular, evaluate whether Okonkwo's architectural/calibration distinction successfully insulates moral objectivity from Chen's challenge, or whether Chen's dissolution objection collapses it. Identify what both parties overlook—perhaps regarding whether moral objectivity genuinely requires cross-cultural convergence, or regarding the normative significance of descriptive psychological patterns for normative ethics. Give your overall judgment of who is more right, and specify what further conceptual or empirical distinction would best break the deadlock.
