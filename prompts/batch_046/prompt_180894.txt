Construct a thought experiment to specifications. Design a scenario—call it The Transient Column—involving a monumental sculpture formed from a single continuous block of biodegradable polymer that hydrolyzes at a uniform molecular rate while an embedded, autonomous system feeds chemically identical polymer from a hidden reservoir into the structure, maintaining constant shape, mass, and artistic design over ten years until every original molecule has been replaced. The scenario must be suitable for classroom analysis of material constitution and must not invoke quantum mechanics, digital copying, or mental properties. Present the case in no more than two paragraphs. Then, for each of three positions—mereological essentialism, four-dimensional perdurance, and a functional-persistence account—state whether the original statue survives, explain the reasoning, and identify the specific structural feature of the gradual, automated replenishment that drives the divergence among these views. Next, describe what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed and replacement becomes instantaneous rather than continuous, and explain whether this collapses the case into a standard ship-of-Theseus problem. Finally, include an honest note on how a defender of the view that denies survival could attack the scenario as implicitly assuming what must be shown, specifying which premise they would reject. The goal is to isolate the role of spatiotemporal continuity versus functional continuity in constitution judgments, not to resolve the dispute.
