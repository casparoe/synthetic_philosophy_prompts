In hedonist ethics and philosophy of mind, pleasure is frequently analogized to a common currency: just as dollars, euros, and yen differ in physical form, origin, and local rules but share a single exchangeable value that permits aggregation across domains, so too are gustatory, intellectual, and relief-based pleasures phenomenologically distinct but share a uniform "hedonic value" that allows quantitative comparison and summation. The analogy is used to defend quantitative hedonism against the heterogeneity problem—the charge that pleasures share no common feature and thus cannot be measured on one scale. Audit this analogy carefully. First, lay out an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the mint, the substrate or material, the denomination, the exchange rate, counterfeit detection, inflation, and the central authority that stabilizes value? Second, identify the disanalogies that matter for hedonism: does pleasure have a conventional origin like currency, or an intrinsic one; is there a stable intersubjective exchange rate between solving a proof and tasting wine; can there be "counterfeit" pleasure; and what corresponds to the social institution that enforces value? Third, separate the disanalogies that are harmless decorative differences from those that directly undermine aggregation. Fourth, examine what the analogy quietly imports about pleasure's nature—in particular, whether it presupposes a feeling theory (that all pleasures share a distinctive phenomenal tone) and whether it collapses if one instead adopts an attitude theory (that pleasure is a pro-attitude that can take radically heterogeneous contents, with no shared qualitative feel). Fifth, state whether the conclusion—that hedonism can legitimately sum pleasures across kinds—survives once these disanalogies and theoretical commitments are on the table, and propose a more accurate analogy if one exists.
