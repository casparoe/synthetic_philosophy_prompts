I'm working through my judgments about when a Ulysses contract—an advance binding of one's future self—carries genuine normative force rather than being an illegitimate constraint on autonomy, and I want to know whether a single principle organizes them. A patient with bipolar disorder, while fully competent and stable, signs an advance psychiatric directive refusing antipsychotic medication during any future manic episode; when she later enters mania and demands the drugs, I think the directive should be honored. A democratic assembly writes a constitutional amendment that binds all future legislatures to maintain a specific carbon budget, even in unforeseen economic crises; I think this is not a legitimate precommitment but rather an unjust constraint on those who never consented. A person executes a will leaving her estate to charity, then on her deathbed, while lucid but emotionally changed, asks her attorney to revoke it in favor of her estranged children; I think the original will should stand, because the precommitting self was not defective. But an addict, while sober and rational, asks a trusted friend to hold his supply of opioids and never return them, no matter what he says; years later, after sustained recovery, he asks for the remaining supply back so he can dispose of it safely himself, and I think he should receive it—yet during an acute relapse, when he begs for them, I think the friend must still refuse. Meanwhile, a young adult executes an irrevocable legal instrument precommitting herself never to alter her religious affiliation; decades later, after sincere philosophical conversion, she seeks to annul it. Here my intuition wavers: the instrument feels like an overreach, yet if I honor her present request, I seem to undermine every other case where I protect the earlier self. Is there a coherent account—perhaps turning on reversibility, on whether the future self is genuinely the same practical agent, or on whether the costs of the precommitment fall on the precommitter herself—that captures all of these verdicts without collapsing? Which of my judgments must I sacrifice for simplicity, and what does the best principle predict about the religious-affiliation case?
