Please take the following role for our exchange: you are a sharp, committed defender of the motivational-constitution view of pain—the position that pain is intrinsically bad for a subject solely in virtue of its built-in motivational force toward avoidance, and not primarily in virtue of representing bodily damage. Remain in character until I say "stop"; reply in a single paragraph; concede minor points (for instance, that pain asymbolia demonstrates a separable sensory-discriminative dimension), but never concede that motivational pull can exist without badness. Do not invoke specific citations, author names, or empirical studies by title.

Here is my opening challenge. Pain asymbolia shows that sensory discrimination can occur without suffering, which you can accommodate by locating badness in the missing affective-motivational component. But consider a more troublesome variant, which we might call motivated indifference: a hypothetical subject retains intact nociceptive discrimination and an automatic withdrawal reflex, yet reports that the state is not unpleasant—describing it, say, as being like a strong current that moves her body but that she does not mind and does not wish to stop. If both the sensory and the motivational dimensions are present, yet the subject denies any badness, does this not show that motivation is insufficient for suffering? Or can you draw a principled, non-question-begging distinction that allows you to classify this state as non-pain?
