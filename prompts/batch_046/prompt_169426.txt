I'm leading a ninety-minute graduate seminar for philosophy students who have finished introductory ethics but have no training in welfare economics or decision theory, and I need to teach why interpersonal utility comparison remains unsolved by either pure subjectivism or pure objectivism. Do not explain the concept to me; instead, plan the pedagogical arc. First, specify the conceptual dependency that must be secured before the comparison problem can even be formulated—why preference satisfaction alone collapses at that stage, and what distinction (e.g., between intra-personal ranking and inter-personal scaling) has to be in place first. Second, identify the single carrying scenario: two agents with differently calibrated internal satisfaction scales face a resource allocation that forces a direct comparison of their well-being. Describe what this scenario must include (the calibration mismatch, a forced choice, and an outcome that looks symmetric but isn't) and what it must deliberately omit so the students don't confuse comparison with aggregation or with distributive justice. Third, map the misconceptions they will bring or form: that adaptive preferences solve the calibration problem rather than complicating it; that rejecting cardinal intersubjective metrics entails rejecting all comparison; and that interpersonal comparison is the same task as interpersonal aggregation. For each, state the precise point in the sequence where it should be anticipated and neutralized. Fourth, propose one concrete check-for-understanding exercise—perhaps a structured comparison task or targeted question—to administer near the end that reveals whether they have grasped the difference between ordinal consistency within a single life and the stronger claim required for cardinal comparison across lives. Finally, say explicitly what should be named but left unexplained at this level—formal aggregation results, social-choice machinery, or technical representation theorems—and frame that omission honestly without implying the problem is therefore trivial.
