Two members of a reading group clashed over a popular-science article stating that “heritability of educational attainment is about 60 percent, so schooling can’t do much.” The first objected that this is a category mistake: heritability is a population-level variance partition in one specific environment, not a causal share inside any individual, and if environments were equalized the heritability coefficient could fall toward zero. The second conceded the statistical correction but pressed further: if genetic differences currently account for 60 percent of observed variance, then claiming that a given intervention will overcome that genetic spread requires assuming we can identify and manipulate the precise environmental pathways that interact with those genetic differences—something we rarely can do. The first replied by introducing a distinction between static heritability (variance decomposed in the observed environment) and dynamic heritability (how variance would repartition after a specified intervention), arguing that only the latter is policy-relevant but that it is empirically inaccessible without running the intervention itself. The second countered that this distinction collapses in practice, since counterfactual environments are unobservable, so static heritability is the only available measure; treating it as irrelevant to policy therefore renders the concept useless for decision-making rather than saving it from misinterpretation. They then split over whether the headline’s claim was literally false or merely pragmatically misleading. Adjudicate: who is correct about the statistical interpretation, about the inference to policy, and about the static/dynamic distinction? What are both missing—particularly regarding gene-environment correlation and the fact that heritability can be high even when every individual is highly plastic?
