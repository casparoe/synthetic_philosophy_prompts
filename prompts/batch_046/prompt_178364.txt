I have been thinking about whether demographic cohorts like Baby Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z correspond to natural kinds, social kinds, or mere statistical conveniences. In sociology and philosophy of science, there is a familiar debate about whether categories such as race or gender are social constructions that nonetheless acquire real causal powers; I want to apply a parallel analysis to generations, but with a twist that I have not seen fully developed in standard discussions. Specifically, I am interested in what we might call cohort performativity: the idea that once a generational label is institutionalized—by census bureaus, marketing firms, polling organizations, and media narratives—it begins to coordinate consumption patterns, political socialization, and self-identification in ways that retroactively validate the category. This is not merely a self-fulfilling prophecy; it involves the alignment of institutional incentives, such as targeted advertising, generational tracking in surveys, and educational policy framing, that produce measurable homogeneity among people who share only an arbitrary birth-year window.

Please argue for the position that generations are best understood as performative administrative kinds—entities that lack natural-kind status, lack the deep moral salience typically associated with social kinds like race, yet acquire a derivative causal reality through institutional feedback loops—rather than as natural cohorts unified by shared formative historical experience or as purely fictional marketing segments. In making your case, distinguish this performative view from both the standard formative-events theory, which treats generations as groups defined by common exposure to historical shocks, and the skeptical period-effect view, which dissolves generational differences into life-stage dynamics and contemporaneous historical context. You should also address the objection that if institutional labeling can make a cohort causally real, then any arbitrarily drawn age bracket could become a generation, and explain why the conventional labels maintain a privileged, if not ontologically fundamental, status within this framework.
