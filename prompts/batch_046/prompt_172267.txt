I am currently drafting a novel whose protagonist is an entomologist-turned-philosopher obsessed with Sphex ichneumoneus—not as a tired emblem of determinism, but as a clue to the architecture of action. She defends a position I have come to call substrate rigidity: the wasp’s compulsive burrow-checking loop is not the antithesis of flexibility but one of its necessary conditions. On her view, human agency depends on similarly 'locked' sub-routines—deep habituated patterns that resist local revision—while freedom consists in a higher-order representational capacity to recognize, suspend, or reframe those loops from outside. Rigidity and adaptability are not opposing poles but interlocking gears; without the locked substrate, meta-level choice collapses into incoherent noise. I have adopted this framing for her intellectual arc, yet I want to test its philosophical exposure before committing my narrative to it.

Therefore, I ask what would genuinely count against substrate rigidity—not merely the standard dismissal that insects are not people, but the deeper defeaters. Which empirical discoveries about hymenopteran cognition might blur the boundary between rigid repetition and adaptive revision? Which philosophical arguments show that meta-level framing is itself just another deterministic loop, or that representational oversight can be separated from procedural adaptation in ways that break the wasp-to-human analogy? What phenomenological or ethical intuitions would make a freedom built on locked substrates feel indistinguishable from bondage? I want these ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest assessment of which a defender could concede without collapse and which would force abandonment. I am also concerned that the appeal to hidden meta-levels risks insulating the view into unfalsifiability: if that risk is real, does it constitute a philosophical vice for a theory of this kind, or is it an unavoidable structural feature of any adequate account of agency?
