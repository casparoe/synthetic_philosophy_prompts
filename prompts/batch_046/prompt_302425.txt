Analyze the Challenger launch-decision meeting of January 27, 1986, through the lens of frequentist, Bayesian (subjectivist), and propensity interpretations of probability. The Thiokol engineers possessed O-ring failure data from 24 prior shuttle flights, noted a prior incident of partial blow-by at 53°F, and initially advised against launching in a forecast temperature near 30°F; under management pressure they reversed their recommendation. NASA managers, lacking direct failure experience at that exact temperature, treated the risk as acceptable.

First, map the statistical reasoning precisely: what reference class should a frequentist use to assign a failure rate to a launch at 30°F, and does the reference-class problem render frequentism arbitrary or silent? How should a Bayesian model the engineers' and managers' respective priors over O-ring elasticity and temperature dependence—was the reversal rational belief updating or a corruption of priors by institutional pressure? Does a propensity interpretation yield a meaningful single-case probability for this particular joint and seal, given that the physical conditions either dispose failure or do not?

Then reverse the direction: does the episode demonstrate that engineering risk must be interpretation-plural, or does it show that one account best captures what was epistemically at stake? Specifically, assess whether the engineers' failure to persuade was an error about probabilities or an institutional failure in which statistical reasoning was overridden by authority, and what that implies for the claim that risk assessment is purely a matter of correct interpretation.
