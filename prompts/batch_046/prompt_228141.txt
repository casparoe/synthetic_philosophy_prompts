I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a small online magazine, and I'm prepping an interveiw for our podcast next week. The guest is a philosopher-historian—unnamed, but he defends a specific view in counterfactual history he calls institutional convergence inevitability. The core claim is that macro institutions like standardized time zones or central banking don't just happen to be overdetermined by big events; instead they sit inside what he terms equifinality traps, where different micro-level causal paths all get pruned by a selection gradient and converge on structurally identical outputs. So individual contingency might be real, but it gets washed out at the institutional level and becomes invisible in the record.

I need eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond restating the view. I want at least one pressing him on how equifinality traps actually differ from classic overdetermination, with a follow-up for when he says we can model the gradient independantly of outcomes. Another should ask whether this makes counterfactual reasoning about institutions useless or just misapplied, plus the follow-up keyed to his likely reply that divergence is real but epistemically hidden. I also need one on moral and political responsibility—if a harmful institution was going to converge regardless of who held power, does that dilute blame?—and a follow-up anticipating his deflection that responsibility tracks micro-level choices, not macro-level convergence. Finally include something about empirical detection: how do we distinguish real convergence from retrospective smoothing, especially when archives are incomplete? No generic questions about his career or favorite books; this is for an audience that already knows the counterfactual history landscape but wants to see where the literature actually pushes back.
