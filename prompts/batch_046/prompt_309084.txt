I have recently begun volunteering with an advocacy collective that documents atrocities in regions where the violence is primarily bureaucratic and infrastructural—forced displacement through administrative revocation of property rights, targeted destruction of cultural archives, and the systematic replacement of local governance with structures that render collective life unviable—rather than through mass bodily killing alone. Through this work I have become convinced that Raphael Lemkin’s original framework, developed in his 1944 study Axis Rule in Occupied Europe, should be revived as the operative legal and moral standard for naming atrocities. Lemkin treated genocide not as the immediate physical extermination of a population but as a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of the essential foundations of group life—political and social institutions, culture, language, economic existence, and personal security—so that the group itself is annihilated even if many individuals survive. I hold that the 1948 UN Genocide Convention’s restriction to physical and biological destruction, its exclusion of political groups, and its insistence on specific intent directed at a protected group as such is not merely a dated diplomatic compromise but an active moral distortion. It privileges visible bodily death over what I call institutional erasure, allowing perpetrators to achieve the elimination of group continuity through distributed administrative acts that never coalesce into a single explicit order to kill. I further believe that the correct criterion for naming an atrocity genocide should be whether the perpetrator’s actions are structured to eliminate the conditions necessary for the group’s collective continuity, regardless of whether the group is defined by ethnicity, shared practice, or political affiliation, and regardless of whether explicit intent is documented or can only be inferred from the pattern of institutional dismantling.

Given this position, I want an honest audit of its exposure—what would have to be true for this revival of Lemkin’s broader concept to be wrong? I am not asking for the standard historical objections about the Convention’s drafting compromises or the evidentiary difficulty of proving structural intent. Rather, I want to know which moral intuitions, legal precedents, conceptual distinctions, or empirical patterns genuinely threaten the view. Specifically: Are there cases where clear institutional destruction occurred but labeling it genocide would obscure more than it reveals? Does removing the specific-intent and group-definition requirements collapse genocide into crimes against humanity or persecution, stripping the term of its distinctive moral and legal urgency? Could my framework make the genocide label so expansive that it loses the very gravity that makes naming matter? I also want you to distinguish the defeaters that a committed defender of this view would concede as genuinely damaging from those they would dismiss as artifacts of the Convention framework I am trying to overturn, and I want you to say whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability—if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
