A colleague who studies material culture has rendered the following judgments. First, standardized weights coexisting with diverse local currencies at a Bronze Age market site convinced her that economic authority was centralized but regionally tolerant, not uniformly imposed. Second, aniconic geometric temple decoration led her to reject an ancestor-cult interpretation in favor of a cosmic-order cosmology. Third, an abrupt ceramic-style shift lacking technological innovation led her to favor migration-driven diffusion over endogenous change. Fourth, a high-status burial equipped with weapons and equine gear led her to identify a warrior-elite identity rather than generic rank. Fifth, a destruction horizon containing intermixed foreign artifacts but no demographic discontinuity led her to infer conquest and occupation rather than trade-mediated acculturation.

Consider now Site X, where excavators find sudden architectural standardization across domestic and public buildings, imported ritual objects placed inside ordinary houses rather than in shrines, uninterrupted local craft traditions, and no skeletal trauma or stratigraphic signs of violence or population replacement.

Predict what she should conclude to remain consistent with her track record, identify which prior case supplies the closest analog, and state whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer. Articulate the two or three competing interpretive rules that accommodate all five prior verdicts—perhaps rules that tie material change to institutional control, to belief-system transformation, or to demographic replacement—and specify what each predicts about Site X. If the rules diverge, say which further discovery would best adjudicate between them.
