I am presenting a seminar paper next month to an interdisciplinary philosophy workshop on comparative epistemology — roughly half analytic philosophers of science (mostly scientific realists with a couple of pragmatists and one known eliminativist about abstract objects, all concerned with incommensurability and theory choice) and half scholars of South Asian philosophy who know Jain doctrine intimately and are suspicious of analytic reconstructions that strip away religious and soteriological context. I was warned by a senior colleague over coffee that I am trying to occupy an unstable middle ground that both sides will want to collapse. The thesis I defend is that Jain anekāntavāda — specifically the saptabhaṅgī, the seven-fold conditional schema of syādvāda governing predication (is; is not; is and is not; is inexpressible; is and is not and is inexpressible; is inexpressible and is not; is inexpressible and is not and is inexpressible) — should be reconstructed not as a vague metaphysical pluralism in which reality is simply "many-sided," but as a formal semantic framework that specifies the conditions under which any predicate can be truly applied within an explicitly indexed epistemic standpoint. I apply this to scientific cases of apparent incommensurability: when two climate models capture genuinely different, non-reducible aspects of one system, the framework allows us to assert that the model is true and that the model is not true, but each only relative to stated descriptive conditions, without collapsing into global relativism because cross-standpoint evaluation is built into the indexing conditions themselves.

Please prepare my Q&A defense sheet. Order the objections by how damaging they are likely to be, identifying roughly who in this audience will raise each one and in what vocabulary — the realists will talk about ontology and explanation, the pragmatists about predictive success and instrumental value, the South Asianists about soteriological function and textual fidelity. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and give me a concise reply suitable for two to three minutes of seminar defense. Flag the weakest point in each reply, and include the single objection I am hoping nobody asks: the one that, if pressed, exposes the gap between my formal logical reconstruction and the lived religious-philosophical practice of anekāntavāda as a path to kevala-jñāna. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists — I must defend this from reasoning and memory alone.
