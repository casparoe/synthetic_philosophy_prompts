I'm trying to build a decision tree for hinge epistemology, but I want it grounded in lab practice, not abstract labels. The idea is "instrumental hinges": those background assumptions we make when we trust a measurement, like that the spectrometer wasn't tampered with overnight or that the laws of optics haven't shifted.

Can you design a taxonomy — maybe six or seven branching questions — that sorts people by concrete answers rather than by which philosopher they say they like? Each node should present a realistic scenario: e.g., you run a calibration curve and it reads 0.03% off — do you treat the physical law or the machine setting as the hinge? Or you wake up with perfect data but no memory of writing the protocol — do you doubt the result or your own recall first?

The tree should start from the logical split: are hinge propositions genuinely truth-apt (like empirical claims we just never bother checking) or are they framework conditions (like rules that make checking possible at all)? Then branch on whether they can be rationally doubted, whether they carry some kind of entitlement, and whether a working scientist is ever justified in revising one.

Please make sure these positions appear as leaves:
1. The Moore-style common-sense realist: hinges are just extremely well-confirmed empirical beliefs.
2. The strict grammatical reading: hinges aren't beliefs at all, just scafolding.
3. The entitlement view: we have a rational right to accept them without direct evidence.
4. The pragmatic commitment view: treating them as fixed is a methodological choice, not an epistemic stance.
5. One revisionist or skeptical position: hinges are in principle revisable and we should stay agnostic about them.
6. At least one "empty box" combination in the logical space — note briefly whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

The questions should be answerable by someone who's never heard the word "hinge." No bibliographies, no "which of these authors do you agree with," no citation lists.
