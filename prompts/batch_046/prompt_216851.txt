As a retired biologist who has lately turned to philosophy of medicine, I have observed a recurring manoeuvre in debates about health and disease for which I lack a precise term, and I should like to know whether one exists.

The pattern is this. A theorist proposes a strictly naturalist criterion—say, statistical normality of physiological function within a reference class—presented as entirely value-free. In practice, however, the criterion soon encounters cases where statistical normality conflicts with clinical judgement: a genetic variant that is common in a population yet clearly harmful, or a physiological state that is atypical yet evidently adaptive. Rather than admitting that normative considerations have shaped the classification, the theorist introduces a technical refinement: the reference class must be restricted by evolutionary 'design', or by 'species-typical functioning', or by environmental context that determines which traits are selected for. Each refinement is defended on independent empirical grounds. Yet collectively these adjustments appear to perform the normative labour of deciding which deviations count as pathological and which do not, all while preserving the claim that the core account remains purely descriptive.

I have noticed versions of this in discussions surrounding the biostatistical theory of health and in replies to it, though I do not suggest the pattern is confined there. The closest established ideas I can identify are the ordinary auxiliary hypothesis—protecting a theory with an added assumption—and the broader claim that observation is theory-laden, but neither quite captures what I mean. The phenomenon is not mere ad hocery, since each exception is individually principled; nor is it simple equivocation, for the key terms are not being used in two plainly different senses. Rather, it is the gradual accumulation of technically framed, individually reasonable exceptions that together covertly introduce value-laden selection without ever being acknowledged as such.

Does this specific configuration have an established name in the philosophy of medicine or philosophy of science? How does it relate to the normativist critique of naturalist accounts? And if no precise term exists, might one coin and define it in a way that clearly distinguishes it both from a standard auxiliary hypothesis and from the general thesis that all scientific classification involves values?
