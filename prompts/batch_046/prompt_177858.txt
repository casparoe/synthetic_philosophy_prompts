As a novelist accustomed to asking whether a story's shape is dictated by culture or by an individual imagination, I am commissioning an essay for a collection on discovery and attribution. You should approach this as an expert philosopher of science with a background in intellectual history. Please begin with a brief internal brainstorm—outline the three strongest objections to the position you intend to defend—before composing the essay itself.

The controversy is the problem of simultaneous invention, centered on calculus (Newton and Leibniz) and evolutionary theory (Darwin and Wallace). The familiar questions are whether these discoveries were historically inevitable, and whether identifying a single discoverer matters beyond ceremony.

I want you to complicate that framing by introducing a distinction that is rarely made explicit in the standard literature: separate epistemic convergence—the independent derivation of formally identical results—from problem-space convergence, in which thinkers independently recognize that a given conceptual blockage has become soluble, yet produce solutions that differ in notation, metaphysical commitment, or explanatory scope. Use this distinction to examine both pairs. Are Newton and Leibniz cases of pure epistemic convergence, or does the divergence between fluxions and differentials, and between their respective ontologies, show that only problem-space convergence occurred? In the biological case, do Darwin's deep emphasis on gradualism and sexual selection versus Wallace's sharper focus on geographic isolation and less extensive theorizing mean that attributing the theory of natural selection to both collapses important differences?

Having applied the distinction, reach and defend a concrete verdict on two questions: first, whether discoveries of this kind are inevitable in any philosophically substantial sense; second, whether attribution to an individual discoverer retains moral or epistemic weight even when problem-space convergence, rather than pure redundancy, is what actually happened.

Finally, I ask you to treat this request with appropriate self-criticism. If the distinction I have asked you to deploy turns out to be unstable—if it collapses under analysis, or if comparing mathematical and biological discovery in this way rests on a category mistake—do not paper over the difficulty to reach a tidy conclusion. State plainly where the framing fails, and adjust your verdict accordingly. I want the essay to be rigorous, not compliant. Please think carefully, write in a formal and polished register, and do not end on a non-committal note.
