As a novelist preoccupied with the hidden grammar by which readers learn to anticipate emotional arcs without ever being taught the rules, I am preparing a short piece for a salon on narrative prediction. I would like a strict sestina—thirty-nine lines, with the six rotating end-words being emerald, green, grue, entrenchment, boundary, and project—that treats Nelson Goodman’s problem of induction not as a logical puzzle about gemstones but as a formal problem about how stories train us to extend predicates across time. The rotating end-words should enact the repetition that makes projection feel inevitable: in the first five stanzas, emerald and green should feel securely paired, while grue should appear only as an intruding, unentrenched possibility. Around the fifth stanza and into the envoi—where the lines compress—the temporal boundary should collapse: the same predicate is applied after the limit, but the register inverts, so that the reader experiences precisely why grue is not merely false but unprojectible, lacking the right kind of historical entrenchment. The poem should avoid resolving whether fiction is therefore unreliable; rather, it should leave intact the tension between necessary narrative expectation and unjustified projection, since that is where my own thinking has stalled. Write it in a contemplative rather than didactic tone, suitable for an audience of writers and philosophers who already know the riddle but may not have felt it in verse, and ensure that the form itself—the sestina’s relentless return—does the philosophical work.
