Me and two friends from my ethics seminar got into it over dinner last night about this guy in our cohort who just took a job at a quant fund so he can donate like half his salary to malaria charities. My friend Priya said thats classic earning-to-give and at least hes trying to maximize impact with real evidence behind it. But then Marcus jumped in and said thats exactly the measurability bias problem - hes only donating to bednet-style interventions because you can run an RCT and get a clean DALY number, but the actual damage is institutional, like how foreign aid distorts agricultural subsidies and local governance in the global south. Priya said Marcus was just recycling the institutional critique, and that if you take it seriously then direct work at an NGO is also playing inside the same broken system. Marcus said theres a real difference though: subsidy reform is trying to change the rules, whereas the quant guy is outsourcing his moral responsibility to GiveWell metrics while his career probably reinforces the financial structures that cause the harm in the first place - he called it "moral arbitrage," treating ethics like portfolio optimization where you maximize expected utility but ignore correlation risk between your job and the system you fund. Priya said thats just fancy jargon, and Marcus hasnt shown why trading is worse than being a doctor who saves lives but doesnt change policy. Then Marcus brought up counterfactual footprint: if the quant guy quits, someone else does the trading and the donations vanish, but if a doctor quits a patient actually dies, so the moral calculus is different. I honestly cant tell who's right about what. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step - not just who wins overall?
