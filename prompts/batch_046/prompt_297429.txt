I am preparing an essay for a philosophy of language seminar, and I have become rather stuck on precisely what Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of translation is meant to establish. The seminar discussion has largely revolved around the familiar 'gavagai' scenario, yet I feel the standard exposition tends to conflate at least two distinct claims: that empirical evidence alone cannot uniquely determine a translation manual, and that reference itself is therefore ontologically relative. To clarify my own thinking, I want to compare Quine's position with a view I shall call the 'structural-coherence account of translation'—the idea that any adequate manual must preserve the internal logical and inferential relations of the source language, and that cases such as 'gavagai' demonstrate not that rabbits and rabbit-parts are metaphysically indistinguishable, but that two manuals with divergent ontologies can nevertheless be structurally equivalent with respect to all observable linguistic behaviour.

In particular, I would like you to set out, first, how Quine's reliance upon analytical hypotheses differs from the structural-coherence view's reliance upon internal theoretical relations; second, whether adopting the structural-coherence account allows one to block Quine's further move from translation indeterminacy to full ontological relativity; and third, which of the two frameworks offers the more compelling explanation of why a field linguist might nevertheless favour translating 'gavagai' as 'rabbit' rather than 'undetached rabbit part', despite the acknowledged empirical underdetermination. Please treat the comparison as a menu of distinctions rather than a simple verdict, indicating for each point whether the residual choice is best understood as conventional, empirically hidden, or structurally mandated, and note any misconceptions about reference-fixing that students of Quine typically bring to this debate.
