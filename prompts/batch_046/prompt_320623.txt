You are a professional philosopher of science with expertise in nineteenth-century evolutionary theory and in literary interpretation. Before you begin, take a moment to think carefully about the specific mechanisms Charles Darwin proposed in The Descent of Man: first, that the moral sense depends on innate social instincts strengthened by selection for group cohesion, and that without those instincts reason alone produces no binding moral obligation; second, that religious belief is best explained not as revelation but as an accidental byproduct of evolved cognitive capacities for detecting agency and inferring unseen causes. Treat these as precise working hypotheses rather than generic evolutionary slogans.

Now read Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein through that lens. The novel presents a being who is manufactured, not born into any social group: the Creature acquires language, reason, and a religious framework—reading Milton and identifying with both Adam and Satan—through solitary observation, entirely lacking the evolved social instincts Darwin thought necessary for morality. Examine whether the novel dramatizes the collapse of moral life without innate sociality, or whether religious narrative and solitary reason can substitute for those instincts. Focus on specific scenes and choices: the Creature’s hidden education among the De Laceys; his construction of a moral self-image through books; his demand for a female companion—whether as an instinctive social need or a calculated bargain; Victor’s refusal and the Creature’s turn to revenge; and Victor’s framing of his own work in religious terms, speaking of playing God and animating dead matter.

For each scene, ask where Darwin’s framework genuinely illuminates the action and where the novel resists being reduced to an illustration. Does the Creature’s sympathy for the blind De Lacey support Darwin’s idea that sympathy can extend across kinds, or does the fact that it is learned through texts rather than instinct undermine it? Does Victor’s hubris look like the very misattribution of design and agency that Darwin might see as the cognitive root of religious belief—projected onto a mechanistic process? Write a critical reading faithful in both directions: do not distort Darwin’s account to fit the plot, and do not treat the novel as merely an example. Conclude by saying what Frankenstein adds to the philosophical debate about morality, sociality, and naturalized religion that pure argument misses—perhaps something about the phenomenology of being an outsider to every moral community, or about the violence that results when moral demands are pressed without the social instincts that usually ground them. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; work only from the text and the framework outlined above.
