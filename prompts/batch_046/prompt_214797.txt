Here are my intutions about self-deception — I can't tell if they fit one principle or if I'm just being inconsistent.

When a guy "forgets" he owes me cash but suddenly remembers every other debt and avoids eye contact, I say that's self-deception: he knows but talked himself out of it. Same with a researcher who genuinely thinks her theory is bulletproof because she only reads papers that agree with it; she's hiding the counter-evidence from herself.

But when my aunt got stage-four cancer and genuinely planned a trip next year — not suppressing fear, just unable to imagine not being around — I say that's not self-deception, it's just her brain failing to integrate the fact. And my friend writes "I'm an alcoholic" in her journal, then tells me "I'm totally fine" and sounds convinced for a minute — I want to call that compartmentalization, not deception. The belief is split across contexts, not buried.

So yes for the debt guy and the researcher, no for the cancer patient and the journal friend. But the debt guy and my friend both have split awareness — what's the real difference? Is there one principel that saves all four, maybe something like "self-deception needs motivated suppression of an accessible belief, not just structural partitioning"? Or do I have to drop one verdict? Also, what does it predict for someone who deliberately avoids checking there bank account so they can keep spending, genuinely believing they're fine? Is that case 1 or case 4?
