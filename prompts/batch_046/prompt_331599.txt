my study partner jordan and her friend sam were arguing about Quine's gavagai example, I was half-listening but they pulled me in at the end. the twist was they weren't doing the usual rabbit vs rabbit-parts thing; they were asking whether the indeterminancy extends to modal or absence-contexts — like if the native speaker says 'gavagai' when waiting for a rabbit that never comes.

Jordan said this is a red herring. Quine's thesis is about ontological indeterminancy: different manuals can assign different referents (rabbits, parts, stages) to the same term while matching all verbal behavior. adding absence or expectation doesn't create a new problem; it just introduces ambiguity about mental states, which gets resolved contextually, not by radical translation.

Sam said that's wrong because Quine's argument depends on all physical evidence being compatible with multiple manuals. if the speaker utters 'gavagai' in an empty field and reacts differently to questions about expectation vs presence, those behavioral dispostions are still compatible with translating the term as 'expected-rabbit-absence,' 'search-failure,' or even 'modal-rabbit-potential.' so the absence-cases show that indeterminacy reaches into modal and intentional contexts, not just ontology.

Jordan replied that Sam is conflating ambiguity with indeterminacy. ambiguity is one language with multiple meanings; indeterminacy is two languages with different ontologies that are behaviorally indistinguishable. the absence-cases are ambiguous uses of one term, not proof that two radically different manuals are equally good.

Sam countered that the ambiguity/indeterminacy distinction collapses here: to decide whether it's one ambiguous word or two homophones requires a translation manual that fixes the lexical entries, but that's exactly what's underdetermined. so you can't use 'it's just ambiguity' to escape Quine's point, because identifying ambiguity already assumes a manual.

Jordan then said Sam's move makes Quine's thesis trivial — if every ambiguity is indeterminacy, then translation is impossible, which isn't Quine's view. Quine allows that we settle on one manual pragmatically; he just denies there's a unique correct one.

Sam said Jordan was now conceding the point — if we settle pragmatically, then in the absence-cases we settle on one modal interpretation pragmatically too, but that doesn't mean it's uniquely correct. so the modal extension stands.
