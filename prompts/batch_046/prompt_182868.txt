You are a professional philosopher of science with expertise in inductive logic and formal learning theory. Before you answer, briefly brainstorm the main ways philosophical debates stall: verbal ambiguity, hidden normative premises, mismatched explanatory targets, or empirical questions disguised as conceptual ones.

In a philosophy-of-science reading group I attend, discussion of Goodman's grue paradox has been deadlocked for over a year. Side A insists the paradox reveals that induction cannot proceed without a prior, non-empirical distinction between projectible and non-projectible predicates; without it, “all emeralds are green” and “all emeralds are grue” are symmetrically supported, so something must restrict the predicate space before evidence enters. Side B replies that this framing treats induction as if it were a universal logic operating over all syntactically possible hypotheses, which is the wrong model: any actual learner—biological or artificial—works within a restricted hypothesis class shaped by representational biases (for example, low-complexity feature spaces relative to observed dimensions), and inside that class “grue” is either inexpressible or heavily penalized. They agree on the formal point that inductive bias is unavoidable, they agree that humans never genuinely entertain grue, and they both reject the idea that evolution “solved” the paradox by picking green. Yet they have not moved an inch.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Are they asking different questions—one about the logic of universal induction, the other about the architecture of bounded learners—while using the same vocabulary? Is there a hidden empirical dispute about whether bias-selection is an evolutionary-psychological fact or a normative rationality constraint? Or is a bedrock intuition about “naturalness” or “lawlikeness” doing silent work on one side? Diagnose the stall by engaging concretely with what each side says, identify whether it is verbal, substantive, or ill-posed, and specify what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a formal result about bias spaces, an empirical finding, a change of question, or an argument that no further progress should be expected and why.
