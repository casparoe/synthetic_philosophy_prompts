I'm working through a long-running dispute in my philosophy-of-action seminar about the backward-induction paradox in the centipede game. One faction insists that a fully rational player must defect at the very first node: if both agents are rational and this is common knowledge, then each can predict defection at every subsequent node, and the chain collapses immediately. The opposing faction replies that this argument proves far too much—if it were sound, sustained cooperation in sequential interactions would be formally impossible, yet agents clearly do continue for multiple rounds in both experimental and real-world settings, so the "paradox" is largely an artifact of treating reasoning steps as publicly observable actions embedded inside the payoff structure itself. Despite decades of restatement—each side rehearsing the standard derivation or appealing to behavioral data—no one has conceded ground, and I am less interested in a verdict than in understanding why the disagreement has calcified.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate turns on an unexamined formal assumption: that each decision node in the extensive-form tree represents not merely a choice but a "reasoning token" that must be recognized by both players as part of the common prior. If that condition of epistemic transparency is built into the model rather than argued for, then backward induction may not be revealing anything about strategic rationality per se, but rather testing whether a particular architecture of mutual belief is appropriate for sequential games. So I am asking for a structural diagnosis: is this a verbal disagreement about whether "rationality" means procedural adherence to the induction algorithm or substantive achievement of the best feasible outcome? Is it a hidden empirical dispute about whether human agents actually possess common knowledge of each other's reasoning processes? Or are the sides answering different questions—one normative (what ought a rational agent do?), one descriptive (why do real agents cooperate?), and one formal (whether common-prior assumptions are consistent with iterated self-reference)—all under the single heading "the backward-induction problem"? Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says, identify whether an implicit commitment to reasoning transparency is doing hidden work, and specify what would count as genuine progress: a formal result about epistemic conditions in extensive-form games, an empirical distinction between procedural and substantive reasoning, a reconceptualization of the game tree, or an argument that the question is genuinely ill-posed and should be abandoned.
