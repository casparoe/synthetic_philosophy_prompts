I am a journalist covering philosophy and public policy, currently researching how Bernard Williams's integrity objection — specifically the problem he describes as involving one thought too many, in which a moral agent saves a spouse not because she is his spouse but because a utilitarian calculus happens to permit it — might be translated into institutional practice rather than remaining a purely theoretical critique of calculative moral reasoning. I need a reusable audit procedure, designed for a palliative-care ethics liaison who applies it repeatedly under time pressure and emotional intensity, often with distressed relatives present and with a brief written justification required afterwards. The procedure should determine whether invoking formal quality-of-life metrics or cost-effectiveness ratios in bedside decision-making represents an illegitimate intrusion of quantitative reasoning into a patient's identity-constituting commitments — what Williams might call their ground projects — or whether such metrics remain appropriate despite emotional resistance.

Please present this as an ordered checklist or flowchart in words, suitable for use within roughly fifteen minutes, with explicit decision nodes. These should include: a distinction between a genuine integrity-preserving refusal and a mere preference or fear; a counterfactual consistency test asking whether the patient (or their proxy) would still reject the quantitative framework if its result favoured their core commitment; a flag for cases in which relatives are projecting their own integrity concerns rather than representing the patient's first-personal narrative; and clearly marked points where judgement is ineliminable, especially in weighing narrative testimony against clinical data. Include worked applications to three cases: a parent refusing to weigh a child's survival probability against treatment burden; a patient insisting on a ritually significant but clinically neutral intervention; and a case in which the procedure permits quantitative reasoning despite strong emotional resistance, yielding an answer the liaison finds uncomfortable but must accept. State explicitly what the procedure cannot do — for instance, adjudicate between competing integrity claims among relatives — and ensure the framework requires no scholarly apparatus, bibliographies, or citations to be used. Write in polished British English, maintaining sufficient conceptual precision to avoid generic ethical platitudes, but avoid invoking obscure sub-specialist theories or texts that a generalist model might not have properly internalised.
