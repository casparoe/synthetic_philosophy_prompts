I just finished a long argument with a friend about whether classical Buddhist anatta genuinely conflicts with our ordinary practices of moral responsibility and autobiographical memory, or only with a metaphysically inflated notion of the self. She defends a minimal, indexical "I" that functions like a demonstrative rather than a name—allowing narrative continuity without substantial persistence—while I maintain that if consciousness is analyzed into momentary mental events with no underlying substrate, even that thin self-reference lacks the temporal stability required for accountability. Give me your credence that anatta, taken strictly in its Theravada Abhidharma formulation, eliminates rather than merely reconceptualizes diachronic personal identity. Decompose this into: (a) the probability that anatta targets all forms of self-reference or only substantial, enduring ones; (b) the probability that the momentary-analysis of consciousness genuinely severs temporal continuity conditions rather than just redescribing them; (c) the probability that modern narrative-identity theories can be reconstructed within this framework without covertly reintroducing a substrate; and (d) the combined result with one line of justification for each term. Tell me which component is doing the most work in your final number, and what kind of philosophical or textual argument—not a reading list—would most shift it.
