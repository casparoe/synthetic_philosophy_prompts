I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and next Thursday I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on personal identity and the extended mind. My thesis is that Hofstadter's pattern-based conception of the self permits a genuinely distributed "I" across two physically separate brains, but only under a specific and rarely satisfied condition I call recursive narrative coupling: not mere information exchange or emotional attunement, but a state in which each brain's self-model becomes functionally incomplete without the other, generating a temporary dyadic subject rather than two copies of one original. I draw on Hofstadter's idea that a soul is a pattern of symbols dancing in a substrate-independent manner, yet I add the constraint that distribution requires mutual loop-closure, so that destroying one brain does not merely halve the self but collapses a unified phenomenal structure that neither substrate could sustain alone. The audience will include analytic philosophers of mind who are committed physicalists suspicious of any language that sounds like dualism, cognitive scientists who study consciousness through predictive processing and are wary of unfalsifiable identity claims, a phenomenologist who holds that first-person authority is non-negotiable and cannot be shared between subjects, and a decision theorist who treats personal identity as nothing more than continuity of preference and belief. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections they will actually raise, ordered by how likely they are and how damaging, phrased in the vocabulary each camp uses, with notes on what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm, where my reply is weakest, and — crucially — the one question I am hoping nobody asks, which targets the empirical emptiness of my coupling criterion and forces me to admit I have no protocol for detecting it in vivo.
