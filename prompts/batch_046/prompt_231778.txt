At a philosophy reading group, two members—Mara and Leo—got stuck arguing about Sartre’s claim that human beings are radically free and that bad faith is the refusal to acknowledge this freedom. Mara opened by saying that Sartre’s well-known waiter example actually undermines itself: if freedom is truly radical, then the waiter’s decision to remain in his job is itself an exercise of that freedom, so labeling it bad faith requires Sartre to smuggle in a hidden normative standard about which choices count as authentic. Without such a standard, she said, Sartre has no grounds to criticize the waiter rather than praise him for a committed project. Leo replied that Mara confuses the content of a choice with its mode of consciousness: bad faith is not about being a waiter, but about treating oneself as possessing a fixed essence—call it waiter-ness—rather than recognizing one’s perpetual transcendence over every fact. He insisted that Sartre needs no positive ideal of authenticity, only the negative requirement that one not identify with one’s facticity. Mara then pushed back: if authenticity is purely this negative acknowledgment, then a waiter who fully admits he could quit tomorrow but chooses to stay is authentic, while one who feels anguish about his contingency but keeps working is in bad faith. That, she argued, makes bad faith a purely internal epistemic failure about self-awareness, draining Sartre’s concept of its critical and existential weight—since the bad in bad faith now means only mistaken about one’s ontology, not a failure of living well. Leo answered that this is structurally correct but evaluatively wrong: for Sartre, recognizing one’s ontological structure is not a neutral fact but the fundamental task, because to deny it is to live in self-deception. He added that Mara’s worry about smuggling norms arises only if values must be pre-given essences, which is precisely what Sartre denies with the claim that existence precedes essence. They turned to me, the only other person present, and asked me to referee. Can you go through their exchange move by move—rating each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right—say specifically where each is right and where wrong, identify what both are missing about the relation between radical freedom and situated facticity, and give an overall verdict on whether Sartre’s framework can distinguish a genuinely free long-term commitment from self-deceptive role-playing without collapsing into either pure voluntarism or hidden essentialism?
