Next month I present to the epistemology and philosophy of language workshop a paper defending what I call counterfactual pragmatic encroachment: in indicative observational contexts, knowledge attributions expressed by "S knows that P" are invariant with respect to practical stakes, but in counterfactual planning contexts—where an agent assesses what she must know to justify a contingency action—the stakes raise a normative threshold for epistemically responsible assertion without altering the semantic content of the knowledge verb itself. The argument hinges on distinguishing the context of utterance from the modal frame embedding the claim, yet I anticipate the audience will press me on whether this distinction is genuinely stable or merely ad hoc.

The audience will include three contextualists who treat high-stakes bank cases as direct linguistic evidence that "knows" shifts its semantic content, two invariantists who hold that practical interests are never epistemically relevant to knowledge proper, and one philosopher of science who is suspicious of fine-grained semantic analysis and worries that any stakes-sensitive machinery collapses into ordinary pragmatic reasoning about scientific testimony. They care about preserving intuitive judgments in the bank cases, resisting unnecessary semantic proliferation, and keeping epistemology distinct from decision theory. I need to know exactly which objections will come, in whose vocabulary, and where my replies are weakest.

Give me exactly five anticipated objections, ordered from the most damaging to the least damaging, each rendered as a numbered item consisting of exactly two sentences and with no introduction or conclusion: in the first sentence, state the objection phrased precisely as the relevant faction—contextualist, invariantist, or philosopher of science—would put it; in the second sentence, provide my reply together with an explicit note on where that reply is softest. Be sure the fifth item is the objection I most hope nobody asks—specifically, the charge that my indicative-counterfactual distinction collapses into a purely pragmatic, non-epistemic account of scientific testimony that explains away all the data without touching knowledge itself. Keep each entry to no more than two sentences.
