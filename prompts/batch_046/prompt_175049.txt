Consider the institutional architecture of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change: Working Group I is charged with describing the physical climate system across multi-century and millennial scales—ocean thermal expansion, glacial dynamics, long-baseline statistical trends—while Working Groups II and III translate these findings into assessments of regional vulnerability and mitigation pathways organized around discrete socio-economic scenarios, specific temperature thresholds, and identifiable policy windows extending to 2100. In the synthesis reports for policymakers, the slow, cumulative transformations of the Earth system are consistently presented as “background context” or “physical basis,” whereas the “key risks” and “response options” are framed almost exclusively in terms of particular extreme events, bounded time horizons, and agent-centered decisions—emissions targets, adaptation investments, technological transitions—whose causal efficacy is treated as independent of the very long-duration structures that make attribution science possible. At the same time, when climate historians or physical geographers submit interdisciplinary commentary emphasizing multi-century soil degradation, gradual shifts in ocean circulation, or the slow geomorphological reshaping of coastlines, such evidence is routinely characterized as falling outside the “policy-relevant” timeframe, even though the statistical methods used to attribute a specific hurricane to anthropogenic forcing depend on precisely those long-duration baselines. What conception of historical causation and environmental agency does this division of labor presuppose? Does it implicitly commit to a Braudelian ontology in which the longue durée of geography and climate belongs to scientific description but not to the causal narrative that governs political responsibility, reserving the status of “real history” for the short durée of events, decisions, and discrete actors? Or can the practice be reconstructed as consistently presupposing something else—perhaps a pragmatic theory of institutional decision-making that treats slow structures as non-agentive constraints rather than as causes—without thereby contradicting the panel’s own reliance on century-scale processes to establish causal claims? And if the practice is indeed philosophically underdetermined between these reconstructions, what would have to change in the evaluation criteria for climate knowledge for the long structures to be treated not merely as the setting within which policy happens, but as active, irreversible forces that shape the very possibility of the events being managed?
