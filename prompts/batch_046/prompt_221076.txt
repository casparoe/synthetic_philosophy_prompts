I'm defending a thesis at next week's political philosophy colloquium: that a democratic nation can bear what I call diachronic institutional guilt across generations—not as a moral stain distributed over innocent individuals, but as a persistent, non-reducible liability of an enduring political structure that continues to benefit from past injustices. I draw on Jaspers's distinction among criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, but I extend his category of political guilt through a social-ontological framework in which institutions, not just aggregates, can be the proper subjects of obligations over time. My concrete case is a post-colonial state whose legal and economic architecture still encodes advantages derived from colonial extraction; I argue that citizens collectively inherit not personal culpability but an institutional duty of acknowledgment and structural repair that is genuinely collective and not reducible to individual charity or state policy alone.

The audience will be mostly analytic moral and political philosophers who are deeply skeptical of collective moral agency, care intensely about the distinction between guilt, shame, and responsibility, and will worry that I'm conflating causal inheritance with moral attribution. There will also be one historian of post-war German philosophy who knows Jaspers's fourfold taxonomy inside out and will press me on whether I'm misreading his intent—he treated political guilt as tied to citizenship in a specific regime, not to transgenerational institutional persistence. A legal theorist in the room thinks in terms of corporate liability and will grant that states can owe compensation, but will resist my use of the moral language of "guilt" for entities lacking consciousness or intentionality. I need a prep sheet for the Q&A: the objections ordered by how likely they are and how damaging, phrased exactly as these people would put them, each with a concise reply I can deliver in about two minutes, an explicit note on where that reply is weakest, what I should concede up front, and the one question I'm praying nobody asks—specifically, the one that targets the weakest point in my social-ontological move from Jaspers's individual-facing political guilt to an enduring institutional subject.

Please include the objections in roughly the sequence they'll arise: start with the methodological individualist challenge that collective guilt is conceptually incoherent because only persons can be guilty; then the historian's textual objection about Jaspers's actual scope; then the legal theorist's semantic objection that I'm illegitimately moralizing legal liability; then the distinction between benefiting from injustice and being guilty for it; and finally the practical objection that framing repair as collective guilt undermines democratic legitimacy by imposing unchosen obligations on citizens who never consented to the historical structure. For each, tell me what to hold, what to concede, where my reply is soft, and how to pivot back to my core claim about institutional persistence. End with the dreaded question—likely about whether my view implies that all citizens of wealthy nations are equally guilty regardless of their personal actions or resistance—and sketch how to respond without collapsing into either full denial or full acceptance of distributive blame.
