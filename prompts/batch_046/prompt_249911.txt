I was talking with a friend about Montaigne's essay on cannibals — the one with the Tupinambá and the comparison of their warfare to ours — and we realized we've been circling the same question for months. For literally centuries scholars have argued whether he's genuinely endorsing cannibal culture as morally equal or even superior to Europe, or whether he's just using them as a mirror to bash European hypocrisy. The pro-relativist camp points to every line where he praises their honor in battle and their lack of greed; the anti-relativist camp says that's strategic irony, not a real normative stance, and that Montaigne's skepticism makes it impossible for him to genuinely commit to any culture being better. We've read the same passages, we agree on the text, and yet neither of us has moved an inch.

My working theory is that the disagreement isn't really about Montaigne at all — it's about whether cultural relativism can even make sense without a positive metaphysics of human nature, and since Montaigne denies us that ground, both sides are smuggling in commitments he never had. But maybe I'm wrong and it's actually a hidden empirical fight about what he privately beleived, or maybe the sides are just answering different questions: one about textual intent, one about philosophical coherence, under the same name. Can you diagnose what kind of stall this is? Is it verbal, about evidence standards, about what counts as a moral judgement versus rhetorical strategy, or bedrock intuition about naturalness? And given your diagnosis, is there anything — a conceptual distinction, a change of question — that could actually move it, or should we accept it's going to stay stuck?
