I'm trying to understand a particular dynamic that arises when a community's shared recollection of a past event conflicts sharply with the documentary and archival record, but not in the ways usually described. I have noticed a recurring pattern across different kinds of groups. First, consider a diaspora community that commemorates an origin trauma with specific, emotionally central details — the scale of displacement, the sequence of perpetrators, the geography of loss — that historians working from state archives and demographic data have documented differently. The community's representatives typically do not deny that the archives exist, nor do they produce rival documents. Instead, they treat the archival record as categorically unsuited to evaluate the community's account: the archives belong to the institutions that caused the trauma, so applying them to correct the communal narrative is seen not as a factual correction but as a category mistake, an attempt to govern a domain of lived experience with tools designed for bureaucratic control. Historical challenges are therefore not answered but redirected — the past is partitioned into two jurisdictions, one for history and one for memory, with the latter granted immunity from the former. Second, within certain professional or craft traditions, a founding story about a pivotal disaster or breakthrough is transmitted with precise details that contradict the technical record. When practitioners or historians point out the discrepancies, the response is rarely to defend the factual claims. Rather, defenders insist that the narrative operates in a pedagogical or moral register, and that historical accuracy is not merely irrelevant but actively harmful to the function the story serves. Here too, the conflict is not resolved by synthesis; it is managed by assigning the corrected version to one domain and the communal version to another, with explicit or implicit rules about which domain governs practice, identity, and transmission.

Is there an established concept for this specific configuration — not the general tension between collective memory and history, but this particular move of partitioning the past into non-interfering jurisdictions, where memory claims immunity from archival correction not by disputing the evidence but by denying the authority of the archival framework over the mnemonic domain? I am not satisfied with a bare name. I want to understand what exactly distinguishes this from adjacent phenomena — ordinary myth-making, institutional denialism, or what is sometimes called counter-memory — and whether the jurisdictional claim is a genuinely separate mechanism or merely a variant of one of those. If the concept is not well established, I would like a proposed delineation: the necessary conditions for this partitioning move, the boundary cases where it collapses into simple disagreement or into something like an institutionalized falsehood maintained for social utility, and the criteria by which one could identify it in a community that has not explicitly articulated the jurisdictional rule.
