I'm preparing a ninety-minute seminar for advanced undergraduates in a history-of-ideas course who have read excerpts from Harari's Sapiens and Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, but have not yet encountered Graeber and Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything. Rather than explaining grand-narrative historiography to me, plan exactly how I should teach the concept to them. The carrying example should not be the usual agricultural-revolution-to-state arc; instead, build the session around the archaeological contrast between long-term egalitarian seasonal mobility—documented in some Upper Paleolithic and Mesolithic contexts—and the fixed hierarchical settlement patterns that Graeber and Wengrow treat as locally seasonal or contingent rather than as an inevitable evolutionary stage. Specify the dependency order: what conceptual distinction (for instance, between teleological progress and contingent branching, or between scale and determinism) must be firmly in place before that archaeological example can do its work, and why reversing that order collapses the point. Map the misconceptions students will predictably bring or form—such as the belief that criticizing grand narratives means denying all large-scale patterns exist, or that Graeber and Wengrow claim every early society was perfectly egalitarian or reject cross-cultural comparison altogether—and indicate precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off, with the reasoning for that placement. Include one diagnostic question I can ask near the end to distinguish genuine grasp of the methodological issue from mere contrarian posturing. Also state explicitly what I should refuse to explain at this level—perhaps the full archaeological debate over Çatalhöyük's social organization, or the technical uses of "complexity" in evolutionary anthropology—and say so outright. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about this teaching plan: which feature of the audience, the material, or the dependency structure, if actually present, would cause the carrying example or the misconception map to fail, and identify where your reasoning about the sequence is most vulnerable.
