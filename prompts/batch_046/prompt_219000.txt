I'm delivering the opening address tomorrow morning at an interdisciplinary workshop on democratic institutional resilience, before an audience split between constitutional lawyers, Roman historians, and senior civil servants. The occasion has become personally urgent in a new way: last month, while cataloguing the papers of my father—a constitutional lawyer now in advanced dementia, who spent his final working years arguing that the Roman Republic collapsed not through illegality but through the incremental legalization of exception—I found his unfinished manuscript on the transition from Republic to Principate. His core claim, which he never published, is that the Republic's dissolution was not a failure of norms but a triumph of them: the senatus consultum ultimum, the legally constituted triumvirates, and the eventual accumulation of powers under Augustus all proceeded through formal mechanisms that retained popular and senatorial ratification at every stage. I want a fifteen-minute opening address that treats this framing not as a historical curiosity but as a structural challenge to how we think about democratic vulnerability today. The speech must do real work for both halves of the room: the classicists should recognize that I am not flattening Roman institutional mechanics into a modern morality play, and the non-specialists should leave with a clear grasp of why the sequence matters—specifically, why the fact that each step was technically legal makes the familiar warning "protect democratic norms" structurally insufficient. I do not want false comfort: no reassurance that awareness prevents repetition, no direct equation of Rome with any contemporary state, and no call to action that pretends procedural vigilance alone can interrupt a sequence that runs through legitimate channels. Instead, I want the address to use the Roman trajectory to pose a precise design question: if legitimacy can be transferred incrementally through constitutionally valid acts, what feature of institutional architecture—other than shared norms—could actually break the chain? The tone should be respectful of my father's argument without turning the workshop into a memorial; he is alive but no longer able to present this work himself. Please write the speech.
