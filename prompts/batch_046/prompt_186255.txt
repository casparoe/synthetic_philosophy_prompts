I have recently become involved in reviewing ethical guidelines for comparative cognition research, and in that context I have come to defend a specific view about the distribution of phenomenal consciousness across non-human animals. I hold what I call the cross-modal coherence threshold position: phenomenal consciousness is not present wherever there is sensory processing or even global information integration, but only where there is sustained cross-modal binding within a temporal window of roughly 250 to 400 milliseconds, allowing disparate sensory streams to be unified into a single perspectival field. On this view, an octopus—despite its distributed nervous system—exhibits this coherence through synchronized pallial activity and thus likely possesses a form of phenomenal consciousness, whereas a honeybee, despite sophisticated navigation and apparent learning, processes visual, olfactory, and mechanosensory information in largely modular, rapid-cycling loops that do not achieve the requisite temporal integration, and therefore lacks phenomenal consciousness altogether, even if it retains access consciousness or intelligent behavioral control.

What would count decisively against this position? I want to know which empirical findings, conceptual arguments, or formal results should actually move me—distinguished from merely awkward anomalies—sorted by how damaging they would be. For instance, would demonstrating that bees exhibit precisely the kind of long-range temporal synchronization I claim they lack be fatal, or could the view accommodate it by relocating the coherence threshold to a different neural scale? Would evidence that decerebrate mammals retain integrated cross-modal responses show that my criterion is insufficient for consciousness, or only that it is not necessary? I also want an honest assessment of whether this position risks being qualified into unfalsifiability—say, by treating any apparent counterexample as not the right kind of coherence—and whether that insulation is a philosophical defect for a thesis of this kind. Finally, what predictions does my view make that rival accounts, such as continuous gradation theories or brainstem-centered views, do not, and have any of those predictions arguably already failed?
