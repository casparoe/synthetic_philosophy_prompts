I've noticed a recurring asymmetry in how people apply Chesterton's fence principle across institutional contexts, and I'm unsure whether political philosophy treats it as a distinct failure mode. When defending long-standing democratic structures—say, bicameral legislatures or common-law precedent—speakers invoke Burkean arguments about embedded tacit knowledge and place the burden of proof on reformers: we shouldn't tear down a fence until we know why it was put up. Yet when discussing modern administrative rules—zoning codes, occupational licensing boards—the same speakers often reverse the burden: because the rule's origin is an opaque accretion of separate decisions with no continuous narrative, its defenders must justify retention, and ignorance of purpose becomes a reason for removal rather than preservation. The switch seems governed not by age alone but by whether the institution's history can be told as a single coherent design. Is there an established name for this pattern—perhaps within conservative theory or in critiques of selective Burkeanism—and if not, how exactly should it be distinguished from ordinary status-quo bias? I want to know whether the phenomenon is recognized, what its nearest conceptual neighbors are, and what principle, if any, genuinely justifies treating the two kinds of institution differently.
