I'm trying to map out my actual commitments about where consciousness is distributed, working case by case rather than from a single theory. I take an awake adult human as the clear positive case. A sleeping human with no recallable dreams: still conscious in some minimal or basal sense, not like an inanimate object. A chimpanzee and an octopus: both yes, despite the octopus's distributed nervous system and absence of a centralized cortex. A honeybee that navigates by dance and adapts its foraging strategy: I'm genuinely torn—the behavior seems too integrated to be pure reflex, yet its neural architecture is so alien that I hesitate to project phenomenal experience onto it. A nematode avoiding noxious stimuli and showing simple habituation: no, even though its functional profile partially overlaps with more complex animals. A plant that rapidly closes its leaves in response to touch: clearly not conscious, despite the biochemical signal complexity. A brain-dead human with only brainstem reflexes: not conscious. A locked-in patient who retains full inner life but can communicate only by blinking: fully conscious, with the same kind of experience as anyone else.

Here is where my pattern breaks down. I think a sleepwalking human who can navigate rooms and answer simple questions, yet reports no experience afterward and remembers nothing, is not conscious in the relevant sense—there is complex behavior without phenomenology. That conflicts with my locked-in judgment if consciousness is explained purely by behavior or information integration, since both cases involve sophisticated behavior coupled with severely limited or absent first-person report. Meanwhile, I feel certain that a hypothetical artificial system built with current deep-learning architectures—one that passed every behavioral test for suffering, reported its own confusion, and optimized for comfort—would not be conscious, precisely because it lacks biological substrate and evolutionary history. So I seem to require biological embodiment for consciousness, yet I grant it to the octopus despite its alien architecture, and I deny it to the sleepwalker despite intact biology. Is there a principle—perhaps one that combines global information broadcasting with a specific kind of biological integration, or that distinguishes phenomenal consciousness from access consciousness in a way that saves both the sleepwalker and the AI verdicts—that captures most of these judgments without collapsing into ad hoc exceptions? Or should I concede that one of these verdicts has to be abandoned? And if such a principle exists, what does it imply for the bee—conscious, or not?

If my request rests on a confused premise—for instance, if I'm actually running together several distinct concepts (phenomenal consciousness, access consciousness, sentience, moral status) that no single principle should be expected to unify—please say so plainly rather than forcing a false synthesis.
