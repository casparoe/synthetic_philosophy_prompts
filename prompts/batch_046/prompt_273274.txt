Two colleagues at a philosophy workshop argued about Bernard Williams’s “one thought too many” case, in which a husband saves his drowning wife but should not think “it is permissible to save one’s wife.” Maya claimed Williams’s objection is psychological: in an emergency, running a moral algorithm blocks direct attachment, so the problem is the presence of deliberation itself. Noah replied that Williams allows moral reasoning; the real problem is substitutability—the husband treats “wife” as a replaceable role filled by this particular person, so she is not genuinely the ground of his action. Maya countered that “my wife” is still a general category, and any reason that applies to her applies to anyone in that relation, so Noah has not escaped substitutability. Noah responded that “my wife” is indexical: it picks out this particular individual through a specific relationship, not through a fungible role, which is precisely why the thought is not “too many.” Maya then said that if indexicals do the work, then a utilitarian parent who thinks “I must save my child because she is mine” is also saved, draining Williams’s objection of its force. They asked me to referee. Please go through each move—Maya’s initial claim, Noah’s reply, Maya’s counter, Noah’s response, and Maya’s final point—rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say who is more right overall and what both are missing about the structure of integrity.
