I'm designing a single ninety-minute seminar for twelve final-year students split between history and politics. The idea I want them to leave with is not merely that nations remember defeats, but the specific structural mechanism — which I will call defeat-to-sovereignty framing — by which a collective military failure is narratively inverted into proof of exceptional virtue that underwrites a later political claim. The four reference points are Masada, Kosovo Polje (1389), the American Confederacy's Lost Cause, and the Dunkirk evacuation.

Plan the explanatory arc for me. Do not explain the concept in full; instead, lay out the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one. What preliminary distinction — for example, between commemorative grief and an instrumental founding myth — must be established before the comparative framework can be introduced without collapsing? Choose the single case that should carry the mechanism throughout the session: it must make the structural inversion visible rather than merely emotional, and say explicitly why one of the other three cases (if you reject Masada, justify that; if you defend it, show what it has that Kosovo lacks) is unsuited to be the carrier. Then map the misconceptions: they will likely treat the myth as cynical fabrication rather than sincerely internalised narrative; they may conflate strategic withdrawal with rout and therefore miss why Dunkirk functions differently from Kosovo; and they may project modern nationalism onto pre-modern defeat. Identify the precise point in the sequence where each misconception should be intercepted, and with what conceptual distinction. Design a final check — a task or targeted question — that would reveal whether they have grasped the structural pattern itself rather than just memorised the four examples. Finally, state what must be deliberately left out at this level (perhaps the theological dimension of Masada, perhaps the legal restitution claims tied to Kosovo) and say so openly. The response should be in British English.
