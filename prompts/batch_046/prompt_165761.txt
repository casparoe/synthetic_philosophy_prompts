I've been working through a translation of an old Chinese philosophical text attributed to Mencius—something composed well over two millennia ago—and I keep returning to one passage that I can't fully parse. In it, Mencius compares human nature to water: water naturally flows downward, he says, and human nature naturally tends toward goodness, so that only an external obstruction can force it upward. He then points to four beginnings—compassion, shame, courtesy, and a sense of right and wrong—which he treats as present in every person from birth. What confuses me is the relationship between this water analogy and these four beginnings. If the moral tendency is as automatic as water finding its level, why does the same text insist that rigorous cultivation and learning are necessary to develop actual virtue? I am trying to decide whether Mencius thinks virtue is already complete inside us, like a hidden current that only needs to be unblocked, or whether these beginnings are merely raw materials that must be shaped into something predetermined only in outline. More specifically, I want to understand whether this framework genuinely answers the opposing view—associated with Xunzi—that human nature starts out oriented toward self-interest and that moral order must be imposed through ritual and discipline. Does the water analogy resolve the conceptual tension between innate potential and the necessity of external formation, or does it conceal a gap that the opposing view fills more consistently? I am not looking for a broad historical survey; I want help understanding the internal logic of the passage and whether its reasoning holds together on its own.
