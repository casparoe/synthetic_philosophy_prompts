I recently read an unnamed popular philosophy-of-mind article that summarizes the split-brain literature in the following way. According to the article, Sperry and Gazzaniga’s corpus-callosum sections did not merely impair interhemispheric communication but effectively produced two independent subjects of consciousness, each with its own perceptual stream, emotional response profile, and volitional control over contralateral limbs. The article claims that the left hemisphere’s verbal confabulations—such as inventing explanations for actions initiated by the right hemisphere—are not interpretive gaps but direct evidence that one conscious system is entirely unaware of the mental life of the other. It then introduces what it calls the “narrative-integration objection”: recent work in dynamic cognitive science suggests that cutting the callosum destroys not consciousness itself but the brain’s capacity for diachronic phenomenal binding, so that what appears to be two minds is actually one fragmented system struggling to maintain temporal coherence across disconnected processing loops. The author concludes that this reduces split-brain cases to a disorder of unified self-report rather than a genuine ontological division of consciousness.

Is this a fair characterization of the debate? Please distinguish what accurately reflects the empirical findings and mainstream philosophical interpretations, what amounts to a common simplification, and what subtly misrepresents either the experiments or the theoretical positions that defenders of the two-streams view actually hold. In particular, evaluate whether framing confabulation as “coherence maintenance within a single system” genuinely undermines the claim that split-brain patients can harbor two distinct conscious subjects, or whether defenders of that view can accommodate the narrative-integration framing without abandoning their core thesis. Write your response in American English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
