As a voting member of my academic medical center's Clinical Ethics and Resource Allocation Committee, I need a formal written opinion to circulate before our Thursday meeting regarding Patient 447, a 42-year-old with an uncommon presentation of autoimmune encephalitis complicated by acute renal impairment. The patient has completed four weeks of intensive immunotherapy with partial neurological response, and the family is requesting authorization for a six-week extension. Our center recently adopted an internal Reference-Class Transparency Protocol, which requires that any prognostic estimate used to justify continuing or withdrawing aggressive treatment be tied to an explicitly named statistical population rather than an implicit clinical intuition. The treating team has selected the broad reference class of all patients with autoimmune encephalitis and concurrent organ dysfunction, yielding a documented long-term recovery rate below 20 percent; the family, supported by an independent biostatistician, insists that the appropriate reference class is the narrowly defined cohort matched for this patient's specific antibody profile, age bracket, and renal stage, a group that shows a substantially higher recovery rate but comprises fewer than two dozen documented cases worldwide. The philosophical tension here is the reference class problem: there is no metaphysically correct group to which this individual belongs for predictive purposes, yet our institutional protocol forces us to treat one statistical population as authoritative.

The strongest argument for denying the extension holds that clinical ethics should rely on robust, well-powered data, and selecting a reference class with an n of approximately twenty invites statistical noise, publication bias, and false hope; moreover, if we allow families to shop for favorable reference classes, we undermine the consistency and fairness of resource allocation across all patients in our intensive care unit. Conversely, the family's position is not merely sentimental: they argue that using the broad class systematically disadvantages patients with rare disease presentations by treating them as statistical averages of dissimilar cases, effectively encoding a form of epistemic injustice into our triage logic, and that our protocol's demand for transparency should cut both ways—if we must name our reference class, we are also required to justify why we privilege breadth over clinical specificity when the two diverge so sharply. A further complication is that the patient's cognitive status has improved enough to express ambivalence about further aggressive intervention, meaning any recommendation must also address how reference-class selection interacts with substituted judgment and the patient's own emerging preferences.

Please draft the committee's decision memo recommending either approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions—including a clear time frame for reassessment—together with reasoning that treats the family's epistemic objection as serious rather than dismissing it as statistical illiteracy. The memo should acknowledge that whichever reference class we ultimately treat as authoritative carries normative weight, and it should propose a concrete procedural safeguard—such as a mandatory secondary review or a conditional trial period—to mitigate the risk of error inherent in small-n prediction. Write the response in American English, avoid bibliographic citations or reading lists, and frame the recommendation as an actionable document the committee could vote on Thursday without requiring members to consult external literature.
