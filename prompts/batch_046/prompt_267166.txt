In my course on virtue ethics we have spent several weeks on the situationist critique—the empirical challenge, originating in social psychology, that behavior exhibits low cross-situational consistency and that global character traits therefore have poor predictive value. The virtue-ethical responses in the literature typically refine the trait concept by appealing to local traits, domain-specific moral expertise, or the integrating function of practical wisdom; some also insist that virtue is a normative achievement rather than a descriptive statistical regularity. The situationists, in turn, maintain that without predictive power, character talk is explanatorily idle and should be replaced by situation-sensitive models. Despite decades of exchange, the positions appear frozen: both sides accept broadly the same correlation coefficients and experimental designs, yet neither concedes ground. I have begun to suspect that the deadlock is not primarily empirical. My working hypothesis is that it reflects a mismatch in explanatory targets—the situationists demand predictive instruments suitable for a behaviorist framework, whereas virtue ethicists seek regulative ideals that structure moral development and self-formation, and because neither side makes explicit what kind of explanation they require, they talk past one another under the shared label “trait.” But I am unsure whether this diagnosis is correct, or whether the stall instead conceals a hidden empirical disagreement about the malleability of personality over time, or perhaps a bedrock normative intuition that morality must be “in the agent” to be genuine. I want a structural diagnosis rather than an adjudication: is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or methodological? Do the sides differ about what counts as data, about standards of evidence, about which cases are central, or about the very question moral psychology should answer? Could a conceptual distinction—perhaps between competence and performance applied specifically to moral agency, or between descriptive and normative anthropology—dissolve the impasse, or does the framing of “character versus situation” mispose the problem? And if progress is possible, what form should it take: a refined longitudinal study, a formal model of trait ascription, a normative clarification of moral education, or a change in how the question itself is framed? I am working without access to external sources, so I need an argument I can evaluate on its own terms; please do not provide a bibliography or reading list, but engage concretely with what each side actually says and with what would count as genuine movement given your diagnosis.
