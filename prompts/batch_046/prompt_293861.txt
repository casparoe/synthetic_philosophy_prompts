I am preparing an essay tentatively titled 'Indirect Normativity Without a Unified Deliberator: Distributed Idealisation in Collective Practical Reason'. The central notes are as follows.

The standard indirect-normative view maintains that an action's rightness is determined not by direct evaluation of its consequences but by its correspondence to what a single, fully rational and fully informed counterpart of the agent would endorse. I wish to challenge the implicit assumption that this counterpart must be a unified, monolithic deliberator. In institutional and collective contexts, I argue, practical reason is better modelled as a network of role-specific idealisations — for example, an idealised legislator, an idealised affected party, and an idealised overseer — each subject to its own informational and motivational constraints. The normative recommendation should then emerge not from aggregation but from a deliberative equilibrium among these nodes. This preserves indirectness, since actions are still justified through counterfactual deliberation rather than direct consequentialist calculus, yet it abandons the unrealistic psychology of a single counterfactual self. My principal concern is that without a unified arbiter the framework risks collapsing either into direct evaluation disguised as proceduralism or into contradictory prescriptions lacking any resolution mechanism.

Please comment on these notes, focusing specifically on: whether the distinction between monolithic and distributed idealisation holds up conceptually; whether the proposed equilibrium genuinely avoids direct evaluation; and what the strongest reply from a defender of unified indirect normativity might be. Please write in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations — I seek purely conceptual assessment.
