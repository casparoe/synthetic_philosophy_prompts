Here are my verdicts on who belongs to the demos across boundary cases, and I should like to know whether they hang together under a single principle or whether I am committed to an incoherent mix.

Consider the following: a person who has resided in a territory for twenty years, paid taxes, and participated in local life, but who has never acquired citizenship—she belongs. A citizen who emigrated permanently three decades ago, holds no property, and returns only rarely—he does not. A sixteen-year-old who demonstrates clear understanding of political issues and can articulate coherent preferences—she belongs. An adult with profound cognitive impairment whose legal guardian casts a proxy vote on policy matters that affect his care—he does not, despite being directly affected. The unborn generations who will suffer the consequences of present carbon emissions—when the decision is specifically about long-term climate policy, they belong to the relevant demos. A temporary worker on a six-month contract, living in employer-provided accommodation and sending earnings abroad—he does not. A stateless refugee who has lived in the territory for five years, works in the local economy, and is subject to its laws—she belongs. A sentient non-human animal in a sanctuary affected by local planning and noise regulations—it does not.

The pattern that tempts me is some combination of affected interests, subjection to law, and democratic competence, but I cannot make the pieces fit cleanly. Including the teenager but excluding the cognitively impaired adult seems to turn on capacity for judgement rather than on being affected, yet including future generations for climate decisions seems to rest precisely on being affected without any present capacity. Meanwhile, the temporary worker is affected and subjected, yet excluded, while the long-term non-citizen is included despite lacking formal membership.

Is there a precise principle—perhaps one that distinguishes between different kinds of stake, or between direct and representative agency—that delivers all seven verdicts? If the answer is no, which of my verdicts should I abandon to preserve coherence, and what does the best available account predict for a case I have not considered: an artificial system granted legal personhood, directly regulated by algorithmic accountability law, and used to model counterfactual policy outcomes, but lacking consciousness or interests in any recognisably human sense? Please state the principle precisely, identify the clause that handles each case above, say which of my intuitions it must reject, and give an honest verdict on the artificial system case.
