After a long and rather exhausting argument with a friend about whether legal systems can coherently authorize their own amendment, I have tried to set down exactly where I stand on self-amending rules and the paradoxes of legal self-reference, so that any contradictions are mine rather than his. I hold that Hart's rule of recognition functions as the ultimate criterion of legal validity in a mature system, and that this rule may contain an explicit self-amending clause—say, a constitutional provision specifying its own revision procedure—without generating a vicious circularity. I also take Alf Ross's paradox of self-amendment to be genuinely puzzling, but I believe it dissolves once we distinguish sharply between the rule that authorizes change and the meta-rule that validates the authority to apply that rule. At the same time, I am convinced that when a rule amends itself, the authority that applies the amended rule is not identical to the authority defined by the pre-amended rule, which implies that legal continuity across amendments is always a reconstructed fiction rather than an identity-preserving relation. Despite this, I maintain that legal interpretation must remain anchored in the original intent behind the self-amending provision, even when that intent explicitly delegates future interpretive authority to bodies that did not exist at the time of enactment. Finally, I reject the claim that any self-referencing norm requires an external grounding point: I think internal coherence conditions are sufficient, yet I also think that every internal coherence check must itself be authorized by the very system it checks, which appears to close the loop entirely.

My friend insists that the third and fifth commitments are in direct collision, whereas I suspect the real tension lies between the second and the fourth; but I am genuinely unsure where the fault lines run, and I do not want to signal which view I would be least sorry to abandon.
