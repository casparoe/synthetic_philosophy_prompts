I've come around to a pretty strong read of Good's theorem on the value of information. Most people treat it as a narrow result: if you update by conditioning and your utility doesn't depend on beliefs, then free observation can't lower expected utility. But I think it's actually a much broader structural claim — that in any decision architecture, the option to observe a signal before acting should always be taken, even when knowing might carry psychologial costs or shift your values, because those costs can be folded into the action space rather than treated as belief-dependent utility. So deferral is never instumentally wrong, full stop. What would count against this? I don't mean the standard toxin-style counterexamples where knowing ruins the payoff; I mean the deep defeaters: formal conditions where the value of information is negative despite Bayes updating, cases where deferring changes the available actions in ways the proof assumes away, or empirical results about real agents whose belief updates systematically violate the conditions. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender of my strong read would have to concede versus which ones they could dismiss with a qualification.
