I was arguing with my friend Marcus last night over drinks about expected value and he kept saying I'm an EV fanatic because I think we should always just multiply probabily by payoff and go with the max, even when the stakes are wild and the numbers are basically made up. Anyway, I work for a regional emergency management office, and actually we DO have to make these calls every quarter: do we fund the earthquake retrofit, the flood wall, the pandemic stockpile, the chemical-plant backup? The budget's are tiny and the probabilitys are guesses pulled from old reports. I need a decision procedure I can actually run in our 45-minute review meetings — like, the actual steps in order, with the checks — that applies the EV principle seriously but doesn't just collapse into spending everything on the asteroid. It needs to handle cases where we don't have real numbers, where payoffs aren't just lives saved but also things like not destroying the local tax base, and where the board has to sign off and can say no. The procedure should say exactly where I'm forced by the math and where it's just me dressing up a value judgment.

Run it on three proposals: upgrading the levee for a 1-in-200 flood, stockpiling antivirals for a novel flu strain, and funding a backup generator for the nuclear plant 40 miles away that might prevent a meltdown but costs half our annual budget. One of these needs to come out as "don't fund" in a way that feels wrong if you're pure EV, and I want the procedure to flag exactly where the value judgment sneaks in. Also tell me what this procedure straight-up can't do — like if it's useless when the harms are totally incommensurable — and don't just pretend it's objective.
