Many universities use strategy-proof matching algorithms—such as student-proposing deferred acceptance—to assign students to courses or dormitories, treating strategic misrepresentation of preferences as a design failure that corrupts fairness. At the same time, those same institutions routinely select faculty or admit graduate students through committee deliberation in which members are encouraged to rank candidates strategically, trade support, and adjust scores to advance preferred outcomes. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite result establishes that with three or more alternatives any non-dictatorial voting rule can be manipulated, so the asymmetry cannot rest on the belief that committee voting avoids manipulation altogether. What view of preference, autonomy, or collective rationality does this selective intolerance presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes strategic behavior a corruption in course allocation but a legitimate feature of academic judgment, say whether the practice is philosophically consistent or underdetermined between rival theories, and describe how admission and hiring procedures would have to change if the institution applied the same anti-strategic principle uniformly.
