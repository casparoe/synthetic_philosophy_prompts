For the past two years I have co-led a graduate reading group in philosophy of value, and we have reached an impasse over the heterogeneity problem for hedonism—the charge that the pleasures of tasting wine, solving a proof, and feeling schadenfreude share no discernible phenomenal quality, which undermines the claim that pleasure is the single good-making feature of experiences. The familiar dialectic is well rehearsed: objectors press the phenomenological disparity, defenders reply that all pleasant experiences contain a common affective tone of approval or pro-feeling, and critics answer that this tone is either invisible upon introspection or merely a redescriptive label that does not explain the unity. Lately, however, several participants have introduced a distinction that has divided the room without resolving it—between tonal uniformity, the claim that pleasures share a directly felt affective hue, and structural uniformity, the claim that they share only a functional architecture, such as being objects of a unified pro-attitude or correlating with a common reward-prediction-error profile in valuation circuitry. Some now argue that hedonism requires only structural uniformity, while others insist that without tonal uniformity the view collapses into a disjunctive list of unrelated goods that happen to be desired.

Given this framing, I want a diagnosis of why our debate has stalled rather than a verdict on which side is correct. Is the disagreement genuinely substantive—about whether a shared phenomenal property exists—or is it largely verbal, with pleasure functioning as a family-resemblance term for some participants and as a natural-kind term for others? Are the two camps in fact answering different questions, one phenomenological and one axiological, under the single heading of what pleasures have in common? Or is there a hidden normative premise—perhaps about what counts as a satisfactory explanation of value, or about whether introspective data should constrain our axiological theories—doing the silent work? Please analyze the structure of the disagreement using what each position actually commits itself to, including the tonal-versus-structural distinction I have sketched, and be explicit about whether either side is misinterpreting the other’s target.

Finally, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis. Would a refined conceptual distinction dissolve the problem, or should we appeal to empirical neuroscience to settle whether there is a unified neural signature? Might the disagreement reveal that the original question is ill-posed, so that we should abandon the search for a common feature and reconceive hedonism in some other way? I want an honest assessment of whether this is a hard but tractable dispute, a disguised difference in standards of explanation, or an impasse rooted in bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for.
