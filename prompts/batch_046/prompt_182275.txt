Clinical ethics in most jurisdictions treats a competent adult's advance refusal of life-sustaining treatment as binding on physicians even after the patient becomes incompetent and appears content, yet it treats an advance demand for continued aggressive care as merely advisory once a medical team deems further treatment futile. What philosophical account of personal identity and cross-temporal authority does that asymmetry presuppose? Does it commit to the view that an earlier self holds enduring property-like rights over the body that survive radical psychological change, or can it be reconstructed as a harm-minimization rule that weights present suffering more heavily than past autonomy? Reconstruct the implicit theory, assess whether it is coherent with the legal system's general reluctance to enforce contracts that bind a future self against its present will, and describe what hospital practice would have to look like if we replaced the underlying view with one that grants the currently experiencing self an absolute veto over all prior directives.
