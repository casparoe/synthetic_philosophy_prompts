ok so I've been prepping for this grad seminar on epistemology of logic and I want to make sure I'm not just stacking fancy-sounding claims that actually collapse. Here's where I've landed, more or less:

(1) Carroll's tortoise problem shows that you can't justify modus ponens without already using it — so deduction has no non-circular, external justification.

(2) But I think deduction is still rationally mandatory. Not because it's instrumentally reliable (it gets us true beliefs), but because reasoning just is the practice of being governed by rules like modus ponens. You don't opt into it; opting out is opting out of reasoning altogether.

(3) I reject pragmatic or instrumental grounds for epistemic norms entirely. If the only reason to use a rule is that it works well for us, that's not an epistemic reason — it's engineering.

(4) I think norms need grounding in one of three places: direct self-evidence, coherence with a broader system, or being constitutive of a practice we can't abandon without abandoning the activity itself.

(5) Logical rules don't strike me as self-evident in any robust sense — they lack that immediate obviousness — and coherence is too weak to single out classical logic, since paraconsistent and relevance logics can be perfectly coherant too.

(6) So deduction is mandatory, but it lacks instrumental justification, lacks self-evidence, can't be grounded in coherence alone, and can't be given a non-circular external justification. That leaves only the constitutive option in (4).

(7) Here's the weird part: I also think the tortoise problem generalizes. It's not just about deduction. Any normative rule for belief revision — Bayes' rule, inference to the best explanation, even consistency constraints — can be treated as a premise in an argument that already assumes it. So there's no non-circular justification for any rational update rule.

(8) And yet I think we should still treat all of these as rationally mandatory, not just deduction.
