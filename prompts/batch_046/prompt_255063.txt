After a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether nudging is merely paternalism with better branding, I have been trying to locate the precise conceptual fault lines rather than trading slogans, and the best way to do it, I think, is to run an actual policy episode through the machinery and see where the gears bind. Take the UK workplace pension auto-enrollment program introduced in the early 2010s and phased in by employer size: workers were automatically enrolled into pension schemes unless they actively opted out, retention rates turned out to be far higher than under the previous opt-in regime, and policymakers consistently presented the design as preserving free choice rather than imposing it. Analyze this episode through the lens of libertarian paternalism, but bring two analytical tools to bear. First, the standard distinction between pure paternalism (interference justified solely by the agent's own good) and impure paternalism (interference partly justified by effects on others). Second, a framing I want to inject for the purpose of the analysis: call it structural opacity, meaning a condition in which an agent retains formal control over a decision but is kept unaware not merely of an available option but of the choice architecture itself—that is, of the fact that a default has been engineered and that the framing is not neutral. I want a disciplined two-way mapping. On one side, say what libertarian paternalism predicts the policymakers faced, how it characterizes the worker's decision situation, and whether the staging mechanism—large employers first, small ones years later—creates differential autonomy conditions that the theory must treat as morally equivalent or not. On the other side, identify which features of the episode the theory explains cleanly, such as high retention without legal coercion, and which it misdescribes or leaves silent, particularly whether structural opacity converts what looks like choice-preserving intervention into something closer to deception-based paternalism, and whether the fact that otherwise identical workers experienced different default regimes depending on employer size undermines the claim that autonomy was uniformly respected. Finally, tell me what this case shows about the theory itself: is it a confirming instance, an anomaly that forces a conceptual revision, or a case that reveals the theory was never designed for institutional-scale choice architecture? Be explicit about where the theory has nothing to say, and do not force it to speak where it is silent.
