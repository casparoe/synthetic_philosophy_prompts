I'm a PhD student working on interpretation theory, and I'm trying to determine whether Davidson's radical interpretation can accommodate what I call "epistemic enclaves"—coherent but globally false belief networks sustained within insulated communities—without collapsing into interpretive imperialism. My current worry runs like this. Davidson's principle of charity, as I understand it, requires that we maximize the attribution of true beliefs about the shared environment when interpreting an agent. If that's correct, then encountering a speaker who systematically denies well-established scientific claims—say, a committed flat-earther or an organized climate skeptic—presents a dilemma: either we radically reinterpret their utterances so that terms like "Earth" or "warming" do not mean what they appear to mean, or we conclude that their belief system is so irrational that interpretation fails altogether. Yet Davidson also insists that interpretation is possible across radical divergence, and that belief is essentially public and constrained by social interaction. So it seems that charity, read as truth-maximization, makes stable widespread false belief uninterpretable, which conflicts with Davidson's own commitment to the interpretability of ideologically distant agents. Is the error in assuming that charity targets empirical truth rather than rational coherence? Or does Davidson's framework genuinely struggle with agents whose false beliefs are not isolated errors but systematically interconnected, and if so, does this push us toward a weaker, purely coherence-based charity that risks losing its normative bite?
