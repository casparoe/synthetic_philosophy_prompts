I'm a journalist who covers philosophy of mind and contemplative science, and I've been invited to defend a somewhat heterodox thesis at next month's interdisciplinary symposium, The Self Across Traditions, hosted jointly by a philosophy department and a cognitive-science program. The position I want to hold is that the classical Buddhist doctrine of anatta—particularly as articulated in the Abhidharma distinction between conventional truth (samvriti) and ultimate truth (paramartha)—does not annihilate the person but relocates personal identity into a dynamically reconstructed, processual self. More specifically, I argue that the Buddha's denial targets only a metaphysically substantial atman, while leaving intact a functionally unified agent constructed through dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) and the continuity of karmic conditioning. I frame this reconstructed agent not in Dennett's purely fictionalist terms, but as a soteriologically functional narrative center of gravity—more robust than analytic bundle theories allow, yet far less reified than the transcendental ego phenomenologists defend.

The audience will be split into three camps, and I need a speaker's prep sheet rather than a generic defense. First, analytic philosophers of mind—mostly physicalists and reductionists—will likely treat any appeal to karmic continuity as either mystical redundancy or an unnecessary duplication of psychological-continuity theories; I expect them to press whether my account adds anything beyond Parfit-style reductionism except unverifiable metaphysical baggage. Second, phenomenologists will accuse me of smuggling a hidden substantial self back in through the language of functional reconstruction, and they will specifically challenge whether my processual account can preserve first-person mineness (Jemeinigkeit)—the sense that these experiences are irreducibly mine. Third, scholars of classical Buddhism—particularly those working in Theravada Abhidhamma and Madhyamaka—will object that I am anachronistically importing modern narrative-identity frameworks into traditions that explicitly reject any continuity beyond the momentary arising and ceasing of dharmas; they may charge that I am domesticating a radical soteriological teaching into a palatable Western psychology.

Please order the objections by how likely they are to arise and how much damage each could do to my credibility in this room. For each camp, phrase the objection as they would actually phrase it—not as a straw man—then tell me exactly what I should concede up front, what I must hold firm on, and where my reply is weakest. Keep each reply sketch to roughly two minutes of speaking time, since the symposium format allows only brief responses. I also need you to identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks—likely something about how my view handles the distribution of karmic consequence across rebirth if there is no enduring subject to bear the fruits—and to draft the precise reply I should have ready if it surfaces. Finally, do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests; I need to rely entirely on reasoning I can deploy without access to texts in the room.
