Having recently begun working on restorative justice frameworks, I have adopted the view that guilt is the morally preferable emotion—more reparable and less identity-fixing than shame—and that a culture can deliberately engineer a shift toward guilt-oriented norms by restructuring sanctions around restitution rather than status-loss. I want an honest map of what would genuinely count against this position. Which empirical findings, phenomenological distinctions, or cross-cultural patterns would be decisive rather than merely awkward? For instance, could there be evidence that guilt and shame are not institutionally separable but neurologically coupled, or that guilt-based protocols systematically produce a hidden pathology—perhaps a kind of procedural narcissism, in which offenders perform repair primarily to manage self-image rather than address harm? I also want to know whether defenders of this view risk insulating it by attributing every failure to poor implementation, and whether that insulation is a vice for a claim about cultural choice. Please rank the defeaters, distinguish what proponents should themselves concede from what they can dismiss, and indicate whether the vulnerable point is the normative claim that guilt is superior or the empirical claim that institutions can calibrate affect. No citations or reading lists are needed—just the exposure map.
