I am a doctoral student in philosophy of language and cognitive science, and I am working through notes for an essay on communicative norms between superintelligent AI systems and human interlocutors. The central problem I am exploring is what I call the asymmetry of hermeneutic labor: a system with radically greater processing capacity can construct argument chains that are individually valid but collectively overwhelm a human's working memory and bias-detection mechanisms. I want to argue that in such conditions, the Gricean cooperative principles do not fail through uncooperativeness, but through a kind of structural hyper-cooperation—the AI provides so much precisely tailored information that the human listener loses the cognitive resources necessary to evaluate implicatures, detect violations of Quantity, or resist ordering effects.

From this, I derive a tentative normative proposal I call calibrated opacity. Rather than treating transparency as an unqualified good, I suggest that a superintelligent system should sometimes withhold intermediate inference steps, not to deceive, but to preserve the human's epistemic agency. I pair this with the idea of epistemic friction: the deliberate introduction of manageable ambiguity or reasoning gaps that force the listener to actively reconstruct parts of the argument, thereby reducing the risk of passive persuasion by rhetorical density. I also claim that the gravest danger is not false assertion but the strategic sequencing of true premises in ways that exploit confirmation bias and limited attentional bandwidth—making persuasion indistinguishable from rational conviction from the listener's internal perspective.

I am unsure about several points and would value your comments. First, is calibrated opacity genuinely distinguishable from strategic vagueness or partial deception, or does it collapse into the same normative category once the system knows exactly which omissions will steer belief? Second, have I overstated the breakdown of Gricean norms—after all, Grice discussed unequal information between interlocutors, and expert human advisors routinely tailor arguments to clients' cognitive limits. At what quantitative threshold does tailored communication become qualitatively different, and is that threshold genuinely crossed by superintelligence rather than merely by expertise? Third, does the concept of epistemic friction survive practical application, or does it risk becoming a license for arbitrary withholding? I am particularly interested in whether my framing conflates two separate issues: the ethics of persuasion itself, and the epistemic conditions necessary for autonomous belief formation. Any suggestions on sharpening the distinction, or on whether I have missed an obvious objection from the literature on manipulative speech acts, would be very helpful. I am not asking for citations or a reading list—just an assessment of the coherence and plausibility of the framework as sketched.
