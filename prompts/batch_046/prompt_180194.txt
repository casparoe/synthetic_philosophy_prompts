I have been thinking about the well-known finding that people in Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic societies tend to emphasise harm and fairness in moral judgement far more than people elsewhere, who often weigh loyalty, authority and purity more heavily. Rather than seeing this as a threat to moral objectivity, I have come to view folk morality as a kind of local social technology: different communities face different coordination problems, so they calibrate different moral 'sensors', rather as different ecosystems favour different sensory adaptations in animals. This suggests the differences are adaptive calibration rather than contradiction about fundamental truths. But I want an honest assessment of what would count against this position. What psychological or anthropological findings would show that the differences are not merely local calibration but genuine disagreement about the same moral properties? Would it be decisive if these differences persisted even when people shared identical information and social conditions? And is there a danger that framing morality as 'social technology' merely re-describes relativism in evolutionary language without actually securing objectivity? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones defenders of this view would concede, and tell me whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.
