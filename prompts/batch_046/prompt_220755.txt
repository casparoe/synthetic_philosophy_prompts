Read Denis Villeneuve’s Arrival (2016) through Otto Neurath’s philosophy of picture languages rather than through the usual Whorf-Sapir framing. Neurath’s ISOTYPE project sought a universal, theory-neutral pictographic medium for scientific communication, yet he acknowledged that every symbol system embeds conventional rules of interpretation and hidden theoretical commitments. Focus on the heptapod logograms—especially their circular structure and the cross-species ambiguity that produces the “weapon / gift” misreading—to ask whether they function like Neurath’s ideal pictograms, conveying propositional content without grammatical mediation, or whether they smuggle in commitments about causality, temporal direction, and agency that only become visible through Louise Banks’s training. Where does the film treat the script as immediately readable across species, and where does it show that understanding the pictures requires prior shared practices and framing assumptions? I am preparing a philosophy-of-language discussion tired of using the film solely to illustrate linguistic relativism; I need a critical reading that takes Neurath’s optimism and his reservations about visual universalism seriously, identifies the precise points where the analogy between heptapod writing and ISOTYPE collapses, and says what the film contributes—if anything—to the philosophical debate about whether a picture language can ever be genuinely theory-neutral.
