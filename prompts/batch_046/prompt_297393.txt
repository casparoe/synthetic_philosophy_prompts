I am composing a literary novel in which a pivotal dinner-party scene turns on a philosophical dispute about the purpose of marriage. One character—an older jurist who has recently separated from his spouse—repeatedly insists that "marriage is for procreation," deploying the line not only against same-sex marriage but also against the legal recognition of what he terms "contractual cohabitation partnerships," by which he means long-term domestic contracts between unrelated adults that carry tax and inheritance rights without any reproductive intent. He seems convinced that once the reproductive link is severed, the state possesses no principled reason to privilege one form of domestic life over friendship, commercial partnership, or mere co-residence, yet I want his argument to be intellectually rigorous rather than merely rhetorical.

For the scene to function as more than polemic, I need you to disentangle the precise theses that slogan could be expressing—whether it advances a claim about biological function, a normative prescription for institutional recognition, an essentialist definition of marriage, an empirical generalization about historical practice, or a conceptual analysis of what distinguishes marriage from other unions—and to assess, for each reading, whether it is tautological, substantively defensible, false, or covertly question-begging. Please present these interpretations as a menu rather than selecting a single reading, and indicate specifically which ones actually support his conclusion that same-sex unions fall outside the institution of marriage, and which ones, if applied consistently, would either collapse into circularity or inadvertently license the very contractual arrangements he wishes to exclude.
