I have been revising the syllabus for a graduate seminar in practical reason, and in office hours several students have revealed that they understand the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem only as a variant of the old internalism-externalism debate about motivation. I want to correct this with a conceptual handout that sorts positions by their logical commitments rather than by philosopher names, so I need a diagnostic taxonomy built as a decision tree. The root should pose the core normative question: is there a genuine gap between the reasons an agent currently recognizes and the reasons that genuinely apply to her, such that she can be wrong about what she has reason to do in a way that is not merely a causal failure of motivation?

From that root, the tree should branch through three successive questions. First, can that normative gap be bridged entirely within the agent's existing psychological economy—say, by second-order desires or higher-order volitional structures that align first-order motives with normative requirements? Second, if not within the existing economy, can it be bridged by rational coherence constraints that bind any agent qua rational being, regardless of her contingent desires? Third, if neither of those internal routes works, does closing the gap require an externalist normative sanction—something like an objective reason-giving fact that operates independently of the agent's actual motivational set? At each leaf, place either a named position or the label 'unnamed,' together with exactly one sentence describing its most awkward practical commitment—for example, having to deny that a severely depressed agent has any reason to recover, or being forced to treat moral obligations as purely hypothetical imperatives that vanish when desire is absent. I also want the full logical space displayed: every combination of yes/no answers should either correspond to an occupied view or be flagged as an unoccupied node, with a brief diagnosis of whether the vacancy stems from incoherence, extreme unattractiveness, or simple neglect in the literature. The instrument should require no bibliographic apparatus—just the conceptual architecture—since its purpose is to let a reader discover which commitments she actually holds.
