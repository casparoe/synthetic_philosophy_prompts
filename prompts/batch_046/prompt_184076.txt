I serve on the Clinical Ethics and Institutional Risk Committee of a major academic medical center. We are reviewing a proposal to deploy an adaptive, federated-learning surgical triage allocator—designated the Surge Allocation Module (SAM)—for mass-casualty events. SAM continuously updates its decision weights across the hospital network based on real-time post-operative outcomes; as a result, the precise weight vector that denies any particular patient access to an operating theater cannot be reconstructed after the fact. No surgeon, engineer, or administrator can therefore be said to have made that specific allocation decision. The proposal includes complete architectural documentation and training-data audits, but offers no mechanism for post-hoc explanation of individual denials.

The philosophical issue is the responsibility gap for autonomous systems: there is clear forward-looking responsibility (the committee approves or rejects deployment), yet there may be no backward-looking responsibility—no identifiable agent who can be held accountable when SAM’s adaptive logic produces a harmful or biased allocation.

Your task is to draft the committee’s decision memo recommending one concrete course of action: approve unconditionally, reject outright, or approve only if specific binding conditions are met. In particular, evaluate the proposal that the hospital close the backward-looking gap not through technical transparency—which the federated, adaptive architecture renders impossible—but through an institutional moral escrow: a formal, public pre-commitment by the medical center to accept collective moral and legal liability for every adverse allocation made by SAM, treating the institution itself, rather than any individual staff member or designer, as the proper bearer of blame. Assess whether this escrow genuinely resolves the gap or merely displaces it onto an abstract entity incapable of being blamed in any meaningful sense.

Format: Bottom line up front—open with a single-sentence recommendation. Then justify in no more than 450 words. Include an honest, substantive treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. Do not provide bibliographies, citations, or references to named authors or texts; the reasoning should stand on its own.
