Friend A says the standard escape hatch is denying bivalence: if the proposition 'there will be a battle tomorrow' lacks a truth value right now, then determinism doesn't follow, because there's no present fact that makes the future necessary. He thinks this is Aristotle's actual move.

Friend B pushes back hard. She says Aristotle isn't playing semantic games with truth values; the problem is causal. If there's already a fact of the matter about tomorrow's battle, then the causes are already in motion and the sailors can't avoid it. She thinks you can keep bivalence if you treat future-tensed statements as indexical — they're true or false relative to the future point, but that doesn't mean the present world causally locks them in. She accuses Friend A of smuggling metaphysics into semantics.

Then Friend A brings up this weird niche idea he saw on a blog about 'thin-red-line' futures: there's exactly one actual future, it's fixed, but it's epistemically hidden from us like a hidden variable. He claims this saves bivalence without determinism, because even though the battle is 'already true' in some objective sense, we don't have access to it, so our present actions still have causal efficacy? Friend B says that's determinism in a trenchcoat — if the fact is already there, the sailors are still causally locked, and our ignorance doesn't free them.
