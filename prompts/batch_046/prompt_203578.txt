You advise the director of an international foundation that has spent fifteen years funding irrigation and literacy projects in a region that has never operated with representative institutions. Local decisions are made by a council of hereditary elders; women and members of a minority group perform the agricultural labor that sustains the projects, yet hold no seats on that council. The foundation’s charter requires that any grant above a set threshold include participatory governance mechanisms—specifically, elected committees with representation from affected groups—before funds are released. The elders reject this as an alien imposition that disrespects their customary order and threaten to expel the foundation entirely. The director sees two paths: enforce the charter and use funding leverage to restructure local authority, or drop the condition and channel resources through the existing council. She is uneasy with both options. She observes that the community lacks the habits of collective deliberation her own society takes for granted, yet she also distrusts the notion that outsiders may legitimately redesign another people’s institutions simply because they deem them backward. Complicating matters further, the charter’s required committee model is a standardized electoral format designed in the foundation’s home country; local advisors warn that in a society where authority is tied to kinship obligation rather than majority vote, this mechanism will produce only sham bodies that rubber-stamp elder decisions. The director asks you whether she should hold the line, and on what grounds.

In your answer, do not treat this as a generic tension between cultural sensitivity and universal rights. Instead, identify the precise philosophical structure of her dilemma—particularly the claim that despotism exercised by a more advanced society over one not yet capable of self-government can be legitimate when it aims at genuine improvement rather than exploitation, and when it respects the particular customs of the governed rather than imposing uniform schemes. Apply that framework concretely: under what conditions is paternalistic intervention permissible rather than domineering? What obligations does the intervening party have regarding knowledge of local institutions, the purpose of the intervention, and the necessity of a pathway toward eventual self-rule? Finally, advise her specifically—should she hold the charter’s condition, modify it to fit local structures, or withdraw—and justify that recommendation by reference to the conditions that distinguish legitimate improvement from illegitimate domination.
