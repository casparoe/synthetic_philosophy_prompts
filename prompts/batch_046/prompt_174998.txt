Imagine that Eric Hobsbawm had lived into the 2020s and encountered a phenomenon now visible among several dispersed diaspora groups: they maintain collaborative, wiki-style digital archives in which members collectively draft, vote upon, and continuously revise canonical origin myths, ceremonial calendars, and symbolic flags. The creators openly acknowledge these traditions as recent constructions, yet they perform them with full emotional sincerity during virtual gatherings and defend them against outsiders as authentic inheritances. Crucially, these groups do not seek territorial statehood; they treat the ongoing editorial process itself as the primary social bond, substituting participatory curation for language or geography as the criterion of membership.

How do you think Hobsbawm would analyze this? Would he regard it as a direct extension of his thesis that invented traditions emerge as responses to rapid social transformation, providing continuity with an artificially constructed past? Or would the transparency of the fabrication and the perpetual editability of the "past" place it outside his framework, since he emphasized that successful invented traditions must present themselves as immemorial and unchanging? In addressing this, consider whether Benedict Anderson's concept of imagined communities—particularly his emphasis on horizontal comradeship, simultaneous anonymity, and the role of print-capitalism in fixing a shared mental image—offers a more apt description of these groups than Hobsbawm's model of elite-driven ritual invention. Specifically, if the tradition is never finalized, can it still serve the ideological and social-cohesive functions Hobsbawm attributed to it, or has the invention itself become the tradition?
