Two colleagues of mine, Aisha and Leo, became stuck in an argument at a workshop on moral psychology. They were debating whether guilt or shame is the superior moral emotion, and whether a culture can genuinely choose between them. I listened without taking sides; their exchange is reproduced below, and I should like you to adjudicate it.

(1) Aisha opened by claiming that guilt is the better moral emotion because it isolates specific actions from global self-worth. She argued that a culture which prioritises guilt can correct behaviour without risking chronic self-abasement, whereas shame collapses the distinction between deed and doer.

(2) Leo replied that this separation is precisely guilt's weakness. By treating moral failure as a transactional debt, guilt encourages moral accounting rather than genuine transformation. He introduced the idea of "lateral shame" — disappointment registered among equals rather than status loss imposed by superiors — and claimed that this form of shame signals that one's standing in the moral community is genuinely at stake, something guilt's internalised lawgiver structure cannot replicate.

(3) Aisha countered that even lateral shame carries disproportionate weight. Treating a minor broken promise as a matter of global self-distress is phenomenologically excessive. She added that moral emotions are not policy instruments: a culture cannot simply "choose" an emotional ecology, because attempting to institutionalise shame, even horizontally, invites unpredictable collective dynamics that outrun design.

(4) Leo conceded that emotions are not menu items, but insisted that cultures shape emotional scripts through institutional architecture. A society that structures accountability through public deliberation rather than punishment cultivates a specific emotional salience. The choice, he said, is not between pure feelings but between architectures — restitution protocols versus peer-deliberation frameworks — and shame-based architectures preserve a sense of moral membership that procedural guilt risks reducing to bureaucratic checkboxing.

(5) Aisha responded that this concedes her central point: if the choice is genuinely institutional, then guilt-based mechanisms are more precise and less vulnerable to mob sentiment. She noted that Leo's "lateral shame" still depends on collective sentiment that cannot be reliably directed, whereas guilt permits targeted repair without requiring the offender to navigate the volatility of group feeling.

(6) Leo finished by arguing that guilt's precision is also its limitation. By isolating the wrongdoer and the wrong, it can alienate the offender from the community rather than reintegrating them. A culture that wants moral repair to feel meaningful rather than transactional might deliberately design for cultivated peer-shame, accepting some unpredictability in exchange for deeper social bonds.

Please go through these six moves one by one. For each, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what important consideration both are neglecting — particularly regarding whether the phenomenology of an emotion can be separated from the social structures that elicit it.
