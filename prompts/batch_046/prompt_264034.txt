I have been reflecting on Plato's discussion in the Laws of organized drinking gatherings—symposia—where citizens consume wine under supervision so that rulers can observe how they behave once their usual restraint is lowered. My understanding is that Plato treats alcohol as a kind of chemical key that unlocks the soul's true architecture: by stripping away the social masks that sobriety maintains, wine reveals whether a person's rational part genuinely governs the appetites or whether the appetites are actually dominant. From this, I have drawn a conclusion that confuses me. If Plato believes that the rational soul is what remains or becomes visible only after the performance of everyday self-control is dissolved, then he seems to be treating virtue not as a cultivated habit but as an innate structural feature that can be diagnosed pharmacologically. Would this not imply that a well-ordered state should administer mandatory transparency—perhaps through regulated intoxication—to sort citizens by their hidden rational nature, rather than relying on their public actions? I am trying to determine whether Plato's view of drunkenness is genuinely about revealing an innate moral architecture, or whether I have imported a modern idea about true selves and chemical transparency into his account of habit, education, and the parts of the soul.
