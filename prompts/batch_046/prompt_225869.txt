You are a professional philosopher specializing in Enlightenment ethics and practical reasoning. After leaving a demanding career and finding that mere rest does not restore me, I have been studying Émilie du Châtelet's account of happiness and want to operationalize it—not as historical commentary, but as a living framework. Please think carefully and begin by brainstorming the core distinctions in her treatise on happiness: her separation of fleeting pleasure (plaisir) from durable happiness (bonheur); her claim that study and passionate love are the two great engines of happiness; and her controversial view that certain illusions—especially those sustaining love—are not errors to eliminate but functional conditions that make happiness possible.

Turn this into a reusable decision procedure—an ordered sequence of questions with explicit stop conditions—that a practitioner (say, a counselor or someone evaluating a major life change) could run without philosophical training. The procedure should address: (1) whether a proposed change sustains the capacity for sustained intellectual engagement; (2) whether it preserves the specific passionate attachment du Châtelet means by love, rather than substituting comfort or duty; (3) whether stripping away an illusion would actually destroy a scaffold necessary for happiness; and (4) where the procedure must halt because the conflict is one of values rather than facts. For each step, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as failure, and where an untrained practitioner is likely to misapply the distinction; also flag where du Châtelet's aristocratic and gendered assumptions must be updated or rejected rather than adopted literally.

Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) leaving academia for a well-paid but intellectually deadening administrative role; (b) ending a long-term relationship in which emotional dependence has replaced mutual passion; and (c) abandoning a religious belief that structures one's sense of purpose despite now regarding it as false. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer that conflicts with conventional self-help wisdom, and state explicitly what the procedure cannot do—particularly when study and love demand incompatible commitments. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
