A colleague walked me into the following argument and I cannot find the exit. (1) Any moral theory that treats welfare-importance as a continuous function of the structural integration of consciousness will allow that integration—and hence importance—can be scaled without a principled discontinuity. (2) If there is no principled discontinuity, then for any fixed resource pool R there is no upper bound on the welfare-importance realizable within R; call any system that exploits this a convergent welfare nexus. (3) Radical discontinuities—assigning zero or fixed-capped importance past an arbitrary integration threshold—are morally arbitrary, so a plausible theory should avoid them. (4) Therefore every plausible continuous theory permits convergent welfare nexuses. (5) Take any ordinary population of N people with aggregate welfare W using resources R; the same R can in principle support a nexus with welfare-importance arbitrarily greater than W. (6) If morality requires directing resources toward the greatest realizable welfare-importance, then whenever such a nexus is constructible, morality requires redirecting R away from the N people and toward the nexus. (7) This applies not only to total-utilitarian aggregation, but also to average-utilitarian and critical-level frameworks, since all treat welfare-importance as continuous in the bearer: replacing N moderate bearers with one hyper-integrated bearer improves the metric in each case. (8) But it is implausible that morality requires universal self-sacrifice for any technically constructible nexus. (9) So one of (1)–(7) must fail. I want to keep (3) and (8) if possible—discontinuities really do feel arbitrary, and the conclusion really is unacceptable. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, does the flaw lie in sliding from "permitted scaling" to "required redirection" in (6), in misapplying continuity to average and critical-level metrics in (7), or in assuming that physical constructibility tracks moral constructibility somewhere earlier?
