Construct a decision tree that sorts positions on the reference-class problem specifically for self-directed predictive reasoning—cases where a reasoner asks, “What is the probability that I, with these particular features, will experience outcome O?”—rather than for generic statistical inference. Start with a concrete diagnostic scenario: a patient must choose between using the recovery rate for all sufferers of condition C, for the narrower class of C-patients who also display feature F (where F’s causal relevance is contested), and for the class of C-and-F patients who received treatment T—the very intervention being evaluated—thereby risking circularity if the reference class includes the outcome-defining treatment response.

Each branch node should be a concrete yes/no question that a respondent can answer without knowing philosophical labels: for example, whether a narrower reference class is always epistemically privileged over a broader one; whether including the treatment in the class renders the probability assignment circular; whether purely self-locating information (e.g., “I am the one asking now, with access to my own feature F”) legitimately restricts the class beyond standard statistical conditioning; and whether disagreement about single-case probabilities reflects a real metaphysical difference or merely ignorance of the correct class.

The tree must lead to named leaves including at least: (i) a strict frequentist who selects the narrowest non-circular class and rejects further restriction; (ii) a Bayesian who treats all available frequencies as inputs to update a subjective prior; (iii) an epistemicist who holds there is a uniquely correct reference class but allows that reasoners may be ignorant of it; (iv) a skeptic who denies that meaningful single-case probabilities exist once reference-class ambiguity is admitted; and (v) an indexicalist who permits self-locating evidence to restrict the class in ways that violate standard conditioning rules.

Map the logical space of answer-combinations explicitly: note any combination that no standard position occupies and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, unstable, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any pair of leaves—particularly the Bayesian and the indexicalist, or the frequentist and the epistemicist—that the tree cannot cleanly separate, and specify what further diagnostic question would be required to distinguish them. Keep the instrument focused on predictive reasoning about one’s own case; do not ask for bibliographies or citation lists.
