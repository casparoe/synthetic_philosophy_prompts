After a long argument with a friend who works in institutional history, I found myself stuck on a tension in how we talk about preservation and inevitability. She noted that heritage-designation boards routinely reject reconstruction proposals even when the rebuilt site would be physically identical to the original, on the grounds that the specific historical path—the sequence of adaptations, accidents, and local decisions that produced the place—has been destroyed and is irreplaceable. At the same time, she defends a macro-historical determinism about large-scale outcomes, insisting that if a particular democratic institution or industrial form had not emerged in this region, functionally equivalent ones would have arisen elsewhere given the same structural conditions. So she seems to hold both that local trajectories are metaphysically non-substitutable and that broad historical endpoints are overdetermined.

This made me wonder what philosophical commitments the practice of heritage preservation actually presupposes. Does treating a site as non-reconstructible commit preservation law to a strong form of path dependence—the view that the causal trajectory itself is partly constitutive of the object's value, so that an identical physical endpoint reached by a different route is not genuinely the same historical object? Or can the practice be justified on purely instrumental, aesthetic, or legal-standing grounds without any implicit metaphysics of contingency? I also want to consider what would have to change if we replaced the underlying theory with an equifinal view—one in which only the functional or physical endpoint matters, and the particular sequence leading to it carries no independent weight. Would preservation boards lose their authority to block reconstruction? Would restoration ethics have to abandon the distinction between authentic and replica? And is the practice actually philosophically underdetermined, so that both a path-dependent ontology and a purely consequentialist account of cultural value could license the same institutional rules? I am trying to see whether the board's rejection of reconstruction genuinely requires us to reject the idea that history might have arrived at the same place by another road.
