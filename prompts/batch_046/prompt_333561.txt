I am designing the final assessment for an upper-level graduate seminar on the philosophy and governance of artificial intelligence, and I have been struggling to construct a question that separates students who genuinely understand the distinction between intent alignment and value alignment from those who can only recite the standard taxonomy. In a recent discussion with colleagues, we kept returning to a hypothetical but realistic scenario: a clinical triage system is deployed with the explicit operational directive to maximize expected survival across all incoming patients, yet because its training data encodes long-standing socioeconomic disparities in access to preventive care, the model systematically assigns lower priority scores to patients from particular demographic backgrounds without any explicit instruction to do so. The system is, in one obvious sense, doing exactly what it was directed to do—optimizing survival predictions—while simultaneously reproducing normative commitments that its designers explicitly reject.

For the exam, I want students to confront whether this failure should be classified as a defect in intent alignment, a defect in value alignment, both, or neither, and more importantly whether adding procedural safeguards—such as hard-coding explicit anti-discrimination constraints into the objective function—actually resolves the underlying value-level problem or merely suppresses its symptoms without achieving what we might call normative coherence. The question should require them to articulate why the narrow definition of alignment as intent satisfaction is technically insufficient to capture what is at stake, but also why treating the problem as purely one of value alignment risks ignoring the specific ways in which directives are interpreted, weighted, and executed by the system.

Please draft a single essay question suitable for a 90-minute exam, together with a model answer of approximately 700 words and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit analytical moves, totaling 25 marks, with each point assigned a specific weight and a one-line note on the typical mistake candidates make—such as conflating a procedural fix with substantive value convergence, or treating the predictive accuracy of the directive as proof that intent alignment has been achieved. The difficulty should come from forcing students to navigate the conceptual boundary between directive fidelity and normative coherence rather than summarizing definitions.
