I'm building a clinical decision-support tool that presents risk assessments to patients in plain language. To reduce anxiety, it suppresses mention of conditions below a 2% likelihood and summarizes those results as "no significant risk found." When probabilities sit near 50/50, it rounds to "likely" or "unlikely" rather than displaying the split, because users report that precise ambiguity is frustrating and undermines their trust in the system. The engineering lead argues this is no different from a physician simplifying explanations at the bedside—good communication design, not deception. I feel there is a meaningful moral line being crossed, but I struggle to say exactly where it lies, especially since the system has no beliefs, intentions, or desire to mislead; it merely outputs calibrated text shaped by user-experience metrics. Is there a principled distinction between simplifying information for a listener's benefit and deceiving them, and does an artificial system's lack of mental states change whether that distinction applies, or only who bears responsibility when the simplification causes harm?
