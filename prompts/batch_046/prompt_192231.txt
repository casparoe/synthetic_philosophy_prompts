I have been working through some informal decision-theoretic arguments about reasoning across copies in cosmologically large worlds, and I have noticed a recurring structural gap that I do not have a name for. The familiar framework is that if the universe contains many causally disconnected agents with near-identical decision procedures, then a local choice can carry evidential weight about distant choices, and this can motivate cooperative behavior even without causal interaction. I understand the first-order move: I observe my own inclination and treat it as evidence for what my copies will do. But in the discussions I have seen—both in popular expositions and in more technical forums—there is a consistent pattern of stopping the analysis at exactly that first-order link. For instance, an arguer will claim that I should adopt a policy if and only if I expect my copies to adopt it, cite the evidential symmetry, and then treat the matter as settled, without addressing whether my copies will perform the same truncated reasoning, or whether a second-order belief—my belief about their belief about my belief—should revise my initial expectation.

In one particularly clear case, a writer used this structure to defend a universalizing moral principle across copies, but ignored the possibility that higher-order uncertainty about whether other copies also universalize might destabilize the very symmetry that made the first-order inference attractive. Is there an established concept in the literature—perhaps a recognized failure mode, or its correct counterpart—that captures this specific habit of invoking cross-copy evidential cooperation while arbitrarily truncating the recursive hierarchy of beliefs? If not, can you propose a precise definition that distinguishes this truncation from a legitimate practical stopping rule, and identify the boundary conditions under which the recursion should either be pursued or justifiably halted? I am not looking for a bare label; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is, where its edges lie, and how it differs from ordinary computational limitations or from standard fixed-point reasoning in game theory.
