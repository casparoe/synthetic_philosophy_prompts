I'm preparing a feature article on whether generational labels—Millennial, Gen Z, Baby Boomer—denote real ontological kinds or are merely demographic conveniences. Before I settle on a framework, I want to pressure-test my own intuitions across four cases, because I sense an inconsistency I cannot resolve.

First, when economists define Millennials (born roughly 1981–1996) by the shared experience of entering the labor market during the 2008 financial crisis, I think they have identified a genuine kind: a formative economic shock that produced uniform, cross-national shifts in risk tolerance and home-ownership patterns.

Second, when marketing consultants coin "Gen Alpha" (children born after 2010) to cluster audiences for ed-tech campaigns, I treat the label as a pure fiction—an age bracket with no causal unity, invented solely for commercial segmentation.

Third, when sociologists claim Gen Z is a real kind because its members never knew a world without smartphones, I am split. On one hand, universal digital socialization sounds like a shared causal mechanism; on the other, a teenager in rural Bihar and one in Oslo share only the device, not its social meaning, economic function, or moral world. So I reject the global smartphone cohort as a kind, yet I want to preserve some local, context-bound version of it.

Fourth, when historians dismiss "Baby Boomer" as a statistical bulge—merely high post-war fertility with no shared psychology—I resist. The cohort did share specific institutional conditions: mass university expansion, suburban mortgage subsidies, and particular labor-market protections. Those seem like real causal commonalities, not just demographic noise.

My tentative pattern is that economic-institutional shocks can ground real global kinds, technology-exposure grounds only local ones, and market-invented categories ground none. But case one and case three look structurally parallel—both invoke a globally distributed experience—yet I accept the first and reject the second. Is there a precise principle—perhaps a modified homeostatic property-cluster account with a diachronic causal-lock condition, or an institutional-embeddedness criterion—that can accommodate all four verdicts? If not, which verdict should I overturn? Please state the principle exactly, map each clause to the case that motivates it, predict its verdict on a case I have not mentioned—specifically, whether "the COVID-19 school cohort" (students who completed secondary education between 2020 and 2022 across different national systems) counts as a kind—and be explicit about which of my four judgments the principle cannot save.
