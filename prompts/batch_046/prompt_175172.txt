I am preparing a formal position paper for a joint faculty committee at my university, and the matter has become personally urgent because my own research sits at the boundary of foundational physics and conceptual analysis, leaving me caught between two disciplinary cultures. The dispute I need resolved maps onto the well-known division between Camp A, which holds the skeptical stance associated with Weinberg, Hawking, and Feynman—that philosophy offers no genuine utility to scientific practice and often introduces conceptual fog that empirical work eventually clears on its own—and Camp B, which maintains that philosophical inquiry performs necessary functions that working scientists cannot supply internally, such as clarifying ambiguous theoretical terms, exposing hidden presuppositions in modeling choices, and structuring interpretive frameworks that guide long-term research programs. Rather than staging another polemical exchange, I want a document that both camps could sign as an honest representation of their positions, structured as an adversarial collaboration.

Please draft that joint statement. Open with a numbered section of substantive claims both sides can endorse—specifically, agreed descriptions of what occurred in selected historical episodes, such as the development of quantum mechanics, the modern evolutionary synthesis, and the interpretation of cosmological data—without yet attributing causal influence to philosophical work. Follow this with a disagreements table that distinguishes four intervention types I want treated separately: conceptual clarification, methodological prescription, interpretive framing, and ontological commitment. For each type, state Camp A’s precise claim about its value or uselessness and Camp B’s counter-claim, using wording that each side would recognize as its own view rather than a strawman.

Then identify the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them in one direction would cause at least one camp to revise its overall assessment. I take the deepest cruxes to be whether philosophical clarification can ever alter the predictive content of a scientific theory, and whether the historical episodes that appear to show philosophical influence are best explained as genuine causal contributions or as post-hoc rationalizations of independently successful science. For each crux, specify a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it—such as the role of operationalist thinking in the formulation of special relativity, or the impact of logical empiricist debates on quantum interpretation—and record in advance what each camp predicts about how that case should be analyzed if the crux is resolved in its favor. The document should be rigorous enough that a physicist and a philosopher of science could both put their names to it, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than dissolved into vague agreement.
