I write long-form features for a publication covering science and ideas, filing roughly one piece every two weeks that turns on a scientific claim not yet settled by replication, meta-analysis, or institutional consensus. Because readers treat my framing as an implicit credibility signal, I need a reusable editorial procedure—grounded in the ethics-of-belief debate between James and Clifford—that determines when I may present a preliminary claim as epistemically live rather than burying it in hedge language, and when I must withhold that framing. The central conceptual difficulty is that Clifford's evidentialist prohibition targets belief-adoption, whereas journalistic writing can function as belief-exhibition: displaying a claim for public consideration without the writer personally adopting it. James's defense of the will to believe applies only when an option is genuinely live, forced, and momentous. Your procedure should therefore treat belief-exhibition and belief-adoption as distinct nodes, specify ordered tests for each, and identify the exact points where epistemic criteria run out and the decision becomes a matter of editorial values requiring my editor's sign-off.

Design the procedure as a sequential flow of questions with explicit stop-rules (where the answer is a mandatory refusal to frame the claim as credible), explicit hand-off rules (where the decision is one of values rather than fact), and explicit misfire warnings (cases where the procedure yields an answer I am likely to reject and should override). The tests should address: whether the claim is live, forced, and momentous for my audience; whether the evidentiary gap can be bridged by considerations James permits without violating Clifford's social-epistemic duty against widespread credulity; whether framing the claim in the feature genre counts as belief-adoption or belief-exhibition; and whether presenting the claim risks harm that Clifford's universalist stricture would prohibit regardless of my private belief state.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals: first, a single pre-registered but unreplicated neuroimaging study reporting that an eight-week mindfulness protocol increases anterior cingulate cortex density; second, a theoretical argument that quantum decoherence in neuronal microtubules contributes to conscious integration, with no direct empirical test proposed; and third—a case where I anticipate disliking the result—an epidemiological model with robust data but politically explosive conclusions about vaccine-allocation priorities, where the procedure permits framing the finding as credible despite my personal reluctance. For each, indicate where the procedure stops with a clear refusal, where it hands the decision to my editor as a values judgment, and where it misfires by producing an answer that conflicts with sound editorial practice.

Conclude by stating precisely what the procedure cannot determine, especially regarding my own psychological belief states, the boundary between journalistic neutrality and epistemic neutrality, and the long-term credibility costs to the publication should the claim later be retracted.
