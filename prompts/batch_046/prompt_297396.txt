The long-running dispute over Molyneux's problem—whether a person born blind who suddenly gains sight can immediately identify shapes previously known only by touch—has been revived by modern cataract-reversal studies, yet philosophers and perceptual scientists remain at an impasse. One camp emphasizes initial failures to match tactile cubes with visual ones, arguing that spatial form is modality-bound; the other emphasizes rapid cross-modal calibration within hours, claiming a shared spatial format merely needed activation. Both sides cite the same behavioral data but draw opposite conclusions. Is this stall substantive—about whether perceptual representations share a pre-experiential structural format—or largely verbal, turning on whether “recognize” means identify, discriminate, or phenomenally integrate? Or are philosophers asking about phenomenal content while scientists measure behavioral performance, so that a hidden empirical question about whether haptic-visual linking requires exploratory action is being masked? Diagnose the disagreement’s structure, and say what kind of finding—perhaps a documented case in which a subject reliably discriminates shapes by sight without any conscious visual experience of form—would genuinely move the debate rather than being absorbed into either framework.
