I am a novelist drafting a scene in which an ethnographer-narrator travels through a fictional archipelago where each island governs speech according to its own protocols. In one moment, she participates in a local practice called “contractual omission”—withholding a damaging fact to preserve a host’s social standing—which her home culture would classify as deception. When a fellow traveler accuses her of moral compromise, she defends herself with the proverb “When in Rome, do as the Romans do.” He replies that she has smuggled in full-blown moral relativism: if local custom alone justifies the act, then no cross-cultural criticism is possible. I want their exchange to be philosophically precise rather than a cartoon of either side, but the proverb itself seems to conceal several distinct theses, and I need to know which one is actually doing the work.

Please separate the precise claims that “When in Rome, do as the Romans do” could be expressing in this kind of cross-cultural moral dispute. I want a structured menu that distinguishes, at minimum: a purely prudential reading about avoiding social friction; a descriptive sociological reading about how norms actually govern behavior; a weak normative reading about hospitality, respect, or temporary adaptation as a guest; and a strong meta-ethical reading about local consensus determining moral truth. For each, indicate whether it is a platitude, a defensible but substantive position, or plainly false, and specify whether that particular reading actually supports her defense of contractual omission or instead hands her critic exactly the relativism he fears. I am especially interested in whether there exists a coherent intermediate position—something like provisional moral translation without ontological commitment—that allows her to adopt the local form of honesty without conceding that her home culture’s prohibition on deception was wrong all along. Tell me which reading is typically invoked when someone uses the proverb to shut down an objection, and whether that reading genuinely carries the argumentative weight or collapses into one of the stronger forms.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning with no preamble and no restatement of the question. I do not need bibliographies, citations, or references to specific philosophers; I need the conceptual distinctions sharp enough to write convincing dialogue.
