You are convening a two-day workshop at the Crafts and Cognition Research Centre on “Transformative Experience in Vocational Formation: The Case of Long-Duration Craft Apprenticeship”. Your audience is deliberately mixed: analytic philosophers of mind who work on L. A. Paul’s framework of transformative choice; master glassblowers and clockmakers who have each completed fifteen-year apprenticeships; education policymakers tasked with redesigning technical training; and clinical psychologists studying expertise acquisition. You have been asked to deliver the opening remarks—no more than eight minutes—setting the intellectual frame without alienating the non-philosophers, and without claiming an authority you do not possess.

The structural constraint is this: the workshop’s central question is whether an institution can be designed to be “transformatively transparent”—that is, structured so that a person in a pre-transformation state can make a rational, autonomous decision to enter a path (such as a decade-long craft apprenticeship) whose phenomenological and evaluative character they cannot genuinely preview. You must take seriously Paul’s argument that transformative experiences involve both epistemic and personal transformation, which means you, as a convener who has never undergone such an apprenticeship, cannot speak as though you understand what it is like to reach the other side. Your remarks should therefore shape the room’s expectations: you should explicitly mark the limits of your own epistemic position, invite the craft practitioners to correct any mischaracterisation of their lived transition, and frame the philosophers’ conceptual tools as serving the practitioners’ descriptions rather than overriding them. You should also avoid any suggestion that the “pre-transformation self” can fully evaluate the post-transformation goods in advance, and you should not imply that there is a simple metric—hours served, skill acquired—that captures the transformation.

Please write the speech. It should be polished, accessible to the policymakers and craftspeople, rigorous enough that the philosophers recognise the framework being used correctly, and structured around the humility that transformative experience demands of anyone speaking about it from the outside. Do not ask for citations, bibliographies, or further reading; the audience is present, not a syllabus.
