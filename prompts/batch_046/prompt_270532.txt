Student answer:
"Teleosemantics, particularly in Ruth Millikan's biological account, holds that mental representations acquire content through evolutionary proper functions. An indicative representation—such as a perceptual state—has the proper function of being produced in specific environmental conditions and being consumed by mechanisms designed to use it in practical reasoning. A directive representation—such as an intention—has the proper function of causing its consumer to bring about a specific outcome; it misrepresents whenever that outcome fails to occur, even if the failure is due to abnormal external interference.

Misrepresentation is therefore explained without circular appeal to intentionality: a frog's fly-detector token that fires at a black dot is malfunctioning because it was not produced under the normal conditions specified by its evolutionary design. Millikan avoids the disjunction problem—raised by Fodor—by tying content to the consumer mechanism's calibrated response profile in normal conditions, rather than to the full range of actual triggers.

A serious remaining objection concerns normativity. Teleosemantics explains representational correctness solely in terms of evolutionary design, which is a causal-historical property. But this reduces the normative 'ought' of representation—why the frog should represent a fly rather than a dot—to the statistical fact that flies were the dominant selective pressure. Consequently, the theory cannot distinguish genuine misrepresentation from mere evolutionary disadvantage, leaving the normative dimension of content unaccounted for."
