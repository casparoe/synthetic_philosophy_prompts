In philosophical discussions of self-reference and formal semantics, a stalemate has persisted for decades over where meaning resides in layered symbolic structures—specifically in the taxonomy that distinguishes frame messages (the encoding and decoding rules that trigger interpretation), outer messages (the explicit propositional content delivered to an interpreter), and inner messages (the embedded self-referential payloads that loop back onto the structure itself). Researchers who study semantic closure agree on the standard formal examples, including Gödel-style self-referential sentences and a deliberately constructed three-layer symbol—call it SΦ—whose frame declares a specific interpretation protocol, whose outer layer asserts a predicate P, and whose inner layer denies P of the very structure it inhabits. Yet three camps have not moved. The frame-camp holds that meaning is entirely constituted by the decoding protocol: without the frame, the remaining layers are syntactic noise lacking any intentionality. The outer-message camp holds that meaning resides in the interpreted semantic content, with frames serving only as causal or computational delivery mechanisms. The inner-message camp holds that genuine meaning only emerges in the reflexive loop of the inner layer, treating frame and outer message as inert scaffolding unless activated by self-reference.

All three camps accept the logical behavior of SΦ and agree on standard results about self-reference in formal systems. My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden empirical or formal disagreement—no one disputes the syntax or the logical outcomes—but that the camps are answering different questions under the single heading "location of meaning": one about the conditions necessary for interpretation, one about the nature of propositional content, and one about the conditions for semantic closure in reflexive structures.
