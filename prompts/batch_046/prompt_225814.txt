Consider this argument about Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle, which requires that your current credence in P match your expectation of your future credence in P, conditional on learning only that you are in a particular epistemic state. (1) Apply Reflection once: if you know you will update rationally on evidence E and reach either credence 0.8 or 0.4 with equal probability, your present credence in P must be 0.6. (2) Now apply it iteratively over a countable sequence of updates: suppose each update is rational and you know the sequence of credences converges to a limit L. By nesting conditional expectations, Reflection demands that your present credence equal the expectation of the expectation... eventually yielding L. (3) Therefore, for any hypothesis with a rational convergence point, Reflection forces your present credence to be that limit—typically 0 or 1 if convergence is to certainty—eliminating all intermediate credence for eventually-settled questions. (4) This turns Reflection, intended as a principle of rational humility, into a principle of immediate dogmatism. (5) The only escape is to deny that rational updating ever converges for non-trivial hypotheses, which seems implausible. (6) So Reflection is either false or epistemically self-defeating. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would rather preserve Reflection's core intuition than accept that rational agents can never eventually settle. Please take a definite position: identify the precise step that fails—whether it is the nesting of conditional expectations across iterations, the identification of a limit value with a present unconditional credence, or the assumption that convergence is epistemically accessible in advance—and explain exactly what abandoning that step costs for the broader account of self-trust in rational updating.
