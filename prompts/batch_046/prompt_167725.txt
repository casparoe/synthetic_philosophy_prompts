A philosophy-blog post summarized the logic of self-amending legal rules as follows: "The core problem is that if a rule governs its own replacement, you generate a direct analog of the liar paradox — formally, not metaphorically. Imagine a constitutional Article A that can be repealed only by unanimous vote, and an Article B that may suspend A by simple majority in declared emergencies. When both operate, the system derives a contradiction: A is simultaneously protected by unanimity and vulnerable to majority suspension. Peter Suber's game Nomic demonstrated that any rule-system allowing rules to amend rules eventually collapses into such loops, which proves you need an absolutely unamendable meta-rule at the top. Real constitutions avoid the danger entirely by never letting a clause reference its own amendment conditions; they confine all self-reference to the general amendment article, which by design never applies to itself."
