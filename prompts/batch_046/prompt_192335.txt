I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level philosophy seminar on moral responsibility and interpersonal ethics. I want one essay question on standing to blame and hypocrisy that cannot be answered by reciting summaries of the standard literature. Instead, students should confront a constructed case of structurally compelled hypocrisy: a public agency blames private actors for neglect that the agency is itself systemically required to reproduce.

Present this case: A city housing authority, legally mandated to maintain aging properties with chronically insufficient funding, publicly condemns and fines private landlords for allowing comparable deterioration. The director argues that the agency's failures are structurally compelled—budgetary mandates and political constraints—whereas the landlords' failures are voluntary and profit-motivated. Critics reply that the authority lacks standing regardless, because it is guilty of the same neglect.

Write the exam question (90 minutes, about 800–1000 words expected), a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme. The question should require students to: (i) distinguish whether standing is undermined by any similarity of conduct or only by culpable hypocrisy; (ii) evaluate whether structural compulsion blocks the inference from "you do it too" to "you lack standing"; and (iii) assess whether an institution can possess role-relative standing even when its members are personally complicit.

The model answer should defend a clear position—for example, that structural compulsion does not fully restore standing unless the blamer acknowledges its own comparable fault, but that role-relative standing can partially survive if institutional blame serves a corrective rather than morally superior function. Crucially, the answer must end with a brief self-critical paragraph identifying its weakest premise and stating exactly what consideration would overturn its verdict (e.g., if structural excuses always defeat comparative standing, or if collective role-standing collapses into personal standing).

The marking scheme should allocate 15 marks across five must-hit moves: (1) correct framing of comparative versus non-comparative standing (3 marks); (2) precise analysis of structural compulsion as excuse rather than justification (3 marks); (3) distinction between personal and institutional/role-relative standing (3 marks); (4) evaluation of the corrective-function argument (3 marks); (5) the self-critical identification of vulnerability and a stated overturn condition (3 marks). For each, include a one-line note on the typical mistake that costs marks.

Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. Do not include verbatim quotations from named texts; paraphrase any reference to specific views without quotation marks.
