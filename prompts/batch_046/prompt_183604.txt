Consider a defense procurement rule that requires “meaningful human control” over autonomous lethal targeting, but implements it solely as a biometric authorization—an iris scan—by an operator who receives only a single-line confidence metric and has 2.5 seconds to abort an engagement the system has already computed. The operator bears full legal liability for any unlawful strike, yet is given neither the time, the data, nor the authority to independently assess the target. What theory of moral responsibility and agency does this institutional design presuppose? Does it assume that formal authorization is sufficient for authorship of harm regardless of epistemic access, or does it smuggle in a deontological commitment that a human must be the terminal cause even when that causation is emptied of judgment? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely accompanies it, and indicate how the liability framework would have to change if responsibility were tied instead to actual cognitive control over the decision.
