Construct a thought experiment that engineers an instance of the bootstrapping problem in ordinary perceptual cognition, avoiding both lottery-style objections and explicit self-referential belief content. The scenario should involve a subject assessing the reliability of a specific perceptual faculty—such as color discrimination in degraded lighting—using that same faculty to gather evidence, yet it must include one precisely specified structural feature (for example, a split between two functionally independent subsystems of the same modality, or a calibrated feedback loop with a built-in temporal delay) that initially appears to block circular justification.

Present the scenario in no more than 150 words. Then demonstrate, point by point: (a) why the case initially looks like a classic easy-knowledge bootstrap; (b) whether and how the specified structural feature actually interrupts the circularity, with explicit reference to the nearby possible worlds in which the faculty is unreliable; (c) what a reliabilist who allows limited self-certification would conclude about whether the subject knows her own reliability; and (d) what happens if the structural feature is removed—specifically, whether the case collapses into a standard bootstrapping problem or instead generates a distinct epistemic failure (such as unchecked circularity or a loss of first-order knowledge).

Conclude with an honest assessment: identify exactly which feature of the case does the philosophical work, and explain how a critic of the bootstrapping objection might attack the scenario by arguing that the feature either does not genuinely break circularity or introduces an external check that was not present in the original problem. Do not cite specific texts or provide a bibliography; reason from the structure of the case alone.
