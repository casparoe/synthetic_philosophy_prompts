A colleague pressed this on me and I haven't found the gap. (1) A genuinely contingent future event—say, tomorrow's naval engagement—is one whose occurrence is neither necessary nor impossible from today's standpoint. (2) If the engagement will in fact occur, then the present-tensed claim "it will occur tomorrow" is true now. (3) Whenever a singular claim about a specific event is true now, that event's occurrence is already settled in reality—there is nothing further to be determined about it—so it is no longer genuinely open. (4) Therefore, if the engagement occurs, its occurrence is already fixed now, contradicting (1). (5) By parallel reasoning, if it does not occur, its non-occurrence is already fixed now. (6) So for every allegedly contingent event, either occurrence or non-occurrence is fixed: contingency collapses, leaving no unsettled future at all. (7) The only escape seems to be denying that future-tensed claims have present truth-values, but that abandons bivalence for predictions we routinely make and seems to render present ignorance irreducible to mere epistemic limitation rather than metaphysical openness. I want to keep (1) and (6) intact, and I am especially reluctant to surrender timeless truth or bivalence. Which step should be rejected? Explain precisely where the slide happens—whether it is an equivocation, a modal confusion, or a scope error—why that step looked unavoidable, and defend the specific cost of giving it up rather than surveying options neutrally. Take a definite position.
