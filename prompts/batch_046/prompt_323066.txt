As someone who spends more time constructing fictional worlds than resolving metaphysical disputes, I have become convinced of a particular view about how thought experiments persuade and mislead. I hold that an intuition pump is best understood not as an argument in miniature but as a narrative compression: it collapses a dense lattice of modal conditions and counterfactual dependencies into a single vivid scene, much as a novelist collapses a character's entire history into one revealing gesture. On this view, the danger of a pump is not primarily emotional manipulation but epistemic elision—the suppression of the ceteris paribus clauses and boundary conditions that would need to obtain for the intuition to map onto reality. The more contingent parameters are silently omitted, the more the pump functions like literary backstory left on the cutting-room floor: the scene feels complete, but its coherence depends on what has been hidden. I want to know what would genuinely count against this position—not merely the standard objections about emotional bias or framing effects, but deeper defeaters. Which results in cognitive psychology, which formal properties of modal reasoning, or which alternative accounts of narrative cognition would show that this analogy between philosophical pumps and fictional elision breaks down? Please rank these potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Among them, which ones would a committed defender of this narrative-compression view concede as genuinely damaging, and which could they plausibly dismiss by insisting that literary elision is precisely the right model for philosophical intuition? I am especially interested in whether there is any empirical or conceptual result that would demonstrate that intuition pumps mislead for reasons entirely independent of suppressed conditions—say, through direct affective hijacking or structural features of working memory—such that my emphasis on hidden ceteris paribus clauses becomes a red herring rather than the core diagnosis.
