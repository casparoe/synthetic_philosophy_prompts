Write a short story related to the philosophical problem of letterform identity: specifically, what makes every lowercase "a" recognizably the same letter despite radical differences in style, weight, proportion, and medium. Center the narrative on a protagonist—perhaps a type designer, an archivist, or an artificial classification system—who encounters a glyph that sits exactly at the boundary between being an "a" and not being one. Within the story, develop the concept of a "style-field," an internal framework in which a letter's identity is not anchored to any fixed geometric feature but to a relational network of deviations and norms that must be held in tension across the form. Use this framework to explore whether there is an essential "spirit" of the letter that survives even when every measurable property is altered, and whether recognizing that spirit requires a categorical judgment that cannot be reduced to explicit rules or feature-checking. The narrative should reach a decisive moment in which the protagonist must commit to seeing the form one way or the other, with consequences for how they understand category membership and essential properties more broadly. Write in polished American English. Do not include bibliographies, citations, reading lists, or references to specific real-world texts, named theories, or authors; treat the "style-field" as part of the internal logic of the story's world.
