I am preparing an essay for a graduate seminar on distributive moral theory, and I have become convinced that the familiar dust-speck versus torture intuition does more philosophical work than is usually acknowledged. Rather than treating our reluctance to aggregate imperceptible harms as a mere cognitive quirk, I want to argue that it exposes an unjustified discontinuity in standard aggregative frameworks—specifically, any framework that permits linear scaling of harm across subjects without accounting for the qualitative intensity of each individual experience. The position I wish to defend is that a continuous, convex prioritarian transformation of harm values can preserve the judgment that torture dominates any practically conceivable number of dust-speck harms, while still allowing that, in principle, a sufficiently vast population of genuinely experienced micro-harms could collectively outweigh a single moderate macro-harm.

To develop this, I need the argument structured around three claims. First, if phenomenal badness is genuinely continuous, then positing a sharp moral cutoff below which a harm carries zero weight is metaphysically arbitrary unless one can identify a non-arbitrary property of experience—such as crossing a threshold of conscious salience—that distinguishes negligible from non-negligible suffering; but this merely relocates the problem to an epistemic boundary rather than resolving it. Second, our anti-aggregation intuition in the torture case should be explained not by the invalidity of aggregation per se, but by a feature of human moral cognition: we treat sub-salient stimuli as phenomenologically absent, and we mistakenly project this cognitive limitation onto the moral ontology of harm. Third, applying a convex weighting function—where the moral significance of relieving a harm increases more than proportionally with its intensity—allows us to model why one severe harm dominates astronomically many trivial ones, without committing us to the view that cardinality is always morally irrelevant.

Please present a rigorous, self-contained argument for this position. In particular, address the charge that convex prioritarianism merely shifts the arbitrariness from the cutoff point to the curvature parameter, and explain how your framework adjudicates intermediate cases—such as one million people each experiencing a faint but genuinely noticeable discomfort versus one individual experiencing a significant but not catastrophic injury—without appealing to authority-based citations or bibliographic references. I am looking for the internal logical structure rather than a survey of existing literature.
