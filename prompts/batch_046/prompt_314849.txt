After a long argument with a friend about whether George Eliot’s rejection of evangelical Christianity left her with anything that could genuinely be called religious, I want to compare two conceptual replacements she offers for lost transcendence. On one side is the familiar theory, developed across her essays and enacted in Middlemarch, that expanded sympathy—the disciplined, imaginative identification with lives and sufferings unlike one’s own—can carry the full moral weight that faith once bore, sustaining duty through present-tense intersubjective recognition alone. On the other is a structurally different idea, more visible in Daniel Deronda and in her late reflections on collective destiny and Judaism, that secular reverence for historical continuity—the sense that individual moral life gains weight only by being embedded in a human tradition that outlives it—functions as her true substitute for religious transcendence. Compare these two as theories of post-religious moral foundation. Does sympathy operate as an individual, present-oriented orientation, while historical embeddedness operates as a diachronic, collective one? Which of the two can actually sustain the kind of unconditional self-denial that Christian eschatology once motivated, and which risks collapsing into either sentimental preference or a dangerous subordination of the individual to group narrative? I want an assessment of whether Eliot keeps them genuinely distinct in her writing, whether they secretly depend on each other, and if one were forced to defend only one as her core secular contribution, which survives closer scrutiny and why.
