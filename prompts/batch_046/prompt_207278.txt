Please argue that the standard analysis of multipolar traps and races to the bottom—exemplified by discussions of Moloch-style competitive dynamics—rests on a misleading unitary-agent assumption that significantly distorts both the severity of the problem and the available remedies. Specifically, defend the claim that the competing entities in canonical race-to-the-bottom scenarios (corporations, nation-states, AI development labs) should be modeled not as single rational maximizers of a unified objective, but as internally fragmented coalitions containing distributed veto points and competing sub-agents with divergent payoff structures. From this reconceptualization, derive two counterintuitive implications: first, that the downward pressure is often driven not by strategic inter-entity competition per se, but by intra-entity factions that capture disproportionate gains from defection, making conventional inter-agent coordination mechanisms (treaties, mutual regulation, industry standards) structurally ineffective because there is no coherent counterparty to bind; second, that this very fragmentation creates "internal circuit breakers"—institutional veto structures or stakeholder alliances that can halt the race unilaterally from within—so that the path out of the trap runs through organizational and governance redesign rather than through the global coordination that unitary-agent models imply is necessary. Address the obvious objection that internal fragmentation merely accelerates competitive degradation by lowering the cost of defection, and explain why, on your account, veto-rich architectures can stabilize at a suboptimal but non-catastrophic equilibrium rather than sliding all the way to the bottom. Keep the argument conceptual and structural; do not rely on specific empirical case studies that require outside verification, and do not request bibliographies or citation lists.
