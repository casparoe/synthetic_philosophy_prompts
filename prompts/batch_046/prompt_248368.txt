Take Cicero's arguments in his treatise on old age — that aging is honorable when lived according to nature, that physical decline is offset by the authority of accumulated wisdom, and that public service should eventually yield to private philosophical contemplation — and apply them to the modern bioethical aspiration of compressed morbidity, in which medicine seeks to collapse age-related disability into the final years of a lifespan extended well beyond historical norms. Would Cicero regard regenerative and senolytic interventions that preserve cognitive vitality deep into a tenth decade as a fulfillment of the rational soul's duty to remain active, or as a distortion of the natural terminus that gives old age its moral shape? And how would he assess the claim, supported by research on cognitive reserve and sustained neuroplasticity, that one ought to remain in positions of public authority well past the age he considered the proper boundary — does this extend his defense of mental authority, or violate his insistence that each stage of life carries its own proper task?
