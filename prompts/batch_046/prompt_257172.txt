Below are notes for a short essay on Bernard Williams's integrity objection, especially the familiar case in which a husband saves his wife rather than a stranger and is said to have introduced one thought too many by weighing lives against each other. I argue that the standard reading misplaces the corruption: the problem is not rapid calculative reasoning in the crisis, but the retrospective imposition of a universalizing narrative onto a particular attachment. On my view, a ground project survives precisely as an unarticulated horizon, and its integrity is damaged when the agent later reconstructs the choice as the outcome of an implicit moral calculus rather than as an expression of an identity-constituting commitment. I then propose a reply that Williams does not seem to consider: if ground projects are partly opaque to the agent, explicit retrospective justification might actually protect integrity by blocking self-deception, so long as the articulated reason is not adopted as a future deliberative rule. I am particularly unsure whether this temporal distinction between deliberative transparency and retrospective articulation is textually supported, whether it collapses into the familiar acting-from-duty versus acting-from-inclination distinction, and whether my proposed reply genuinely answers Williams or merely changes the subject. Please comment on the accuracy of this framing, the viability of the reply, and whether the central distinction is philosophically productive or artificially constructed.
