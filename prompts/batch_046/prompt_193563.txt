I'm working through Chapter 15 of Marx's Capital and trying to parse the exact relation between technical form and social form in the machinery section. The passage reads:

"That is, however, by no means the aim of the capitalistic application of machinery. Like every other increase in the productiveness of labour, machinery is intended to cheapen commodities, and, by shortening that portion of the working-day, in which the labourer works for himself, to lengthen the other portion that he gives, without an equivalent, to the capitalist. In short, it is a means for producing surplus-value. In manufacture, the revolution in the mode of production begins with the labour-power, in modern industry it begins with the instruments of labour."

I'm unsure whether Marx is advancing a structural claim—that capital necessarily transforms any productivity gain into an extension of unpaid labor—or a contingent claim about deployment within a given property regime. When he writes that in modern industry the revolution "begins with the instruments of labour," does he mean the machine itself becomes "the form of capital itself," so that the technical architecture is inseparable from surplus-value extraction? Or is there conceptual room for machinery that genuinely reduces necessary labor without extending surplus labor? I'd like a reading that clarifies whether this maps onto the distinction between formal and real subsumption, or whether the passage stays at the level of formal subsumption, and whether Marx is committing to a technological determinism in which worker resistance is structurally foreclosed by the instrument itself rather than by the social relation it embodies.
