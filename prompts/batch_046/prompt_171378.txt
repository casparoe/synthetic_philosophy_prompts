Below are draft notes and a structural outline for an essay I am preparing. The central thesis is that Dennett’s multiple drafts model eliminates the Cartesian theater only on the assumption that temporal integration can occur without any functional analog to a central ordering mechanism; however, if we take seriously the neuroscience of temporal binding and the idea of a narrow “specious present” window within which disparate neural events are experienced as simultaneous or sequential, the model may implicitly reintroduce a distributed but unitary integrator—what I propose to call a “chronometric auditorium.” This is not a spatial location in the brain, but a functional constraint that determines which drafts fall within the same binding window and which do not. I argue that this auditorium plays precisely the explanatory role the Cartesian theater was meant to play—accounting for why consciousness seems to be a single, ordered stream rather than a jumble of parallel narratives—without committing us to a homunculus. At the same time, I want to avoid merely renaming the binding problem.

The essay is planned in three movements:

1. A precise reconstruction of Dennett’s rejection of the Cartesian theater, emphasizing that his target is not merely spatial localization but the idea that there must be a “place” or “moment” at which information is made available to a central observer.

2. An exposition of the multiple drafts model, focusing on Dennett’s claim that narrative coherence is achieved post-hoc and that there is no canonical draft that is “the” conscious experience.

3. A critical intervention. I introduce the concept of a temporal binding window—discussed in consciousness studies as a narrow interval within which events are integrated— and ask what determines the boundaries of that window for any given draft. I call the mechanism that performs this boundary-drawing the “windowing mechanism,” and I ask whether it functions as a genuinely non-theatrical, distributed process, or whether it operates as an implicit metric that orders drafts into a single chronological stream. I then consider whether predictive processing architectures can supply this mechanism without reinstating a higher-level “observer” node, and I examine whether Dennett’s own appeal to narrative “editing” already smuggles in the very integration he denies.

Please provide rigorous, specific comments rather than general encouragement. In particular:

- Is my characterization of Dennett’s position accurate with respect to whether he permits any functional analog to central integration, or does he explicitly rule out even distributed ordering mechanisms?

- Does the “chronometric auditorium” and the associated “windowing mechanism” constitute a genuine philosophical contribution, or do they merely restate the temporal binding problem in new vocabulary?

- Are there internal tensions in claiming that predictive hierarchies can integrate drafts without a theater, given that such hierarchies typically involve higher-level nodes that summarize or constrain lower-level activity?

- Is the essay’s framing interesting, or does the argument collapse into the obvious observation that Dennett’s model must still account for temporal coherence?

- Where should the essay be tightened or expanded? For instance, should I address the possibility that the windowing mechanism is itself just one more draft among many, and if so, how does that affect the regress?
