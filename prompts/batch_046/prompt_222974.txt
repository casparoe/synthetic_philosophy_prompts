I lead a graduate seminar in democratic theory—ten students, ninety minutes, meeting once a week. I want a structured, rule-bound deliberation that makes the circular boundary problem feel unavoidable rather than merely abstract. The students are already familiar with the standard all-affected and all-subjected principles, as well as the bootstrapping worry about who gets to decide who decides. What I need is a sixty-minute simulation followed by a thirty-minute debrief, with no lecture up front and no naming of the problem until the mapping phase.

Design the following scenario and mechanics. The students are delegates to a constitutional convention for a new Transboundary Aquifer Authority that will govern water allocation across three existing nation-states. Assign five delegate roles, two students per role: Riparian Residents (direct territorial exposure to upstream use), Industrial Downstream Users (economic dependence on flow), Non-Resident Seasonal Workers (labor mobility across borders), Intergenerational Advocates (representing future persons not yet born), and Ecosystem Proxies (representing non-human ecological stakeholders). Each pair receives a hidden mandate card specifying a different membership criterion for the Authority’s assembly—territorial presence, economic stake, citizenship in member states, long-term affectedness, or ecological interdependence. Crucially, the convention’s first task is to vote on the voting rule that will govern all subsequent constitutional decisions; however, the only people permitted to vote on that meta-rule are “those physically present in this room,” a provisional pool that automatically excludes the Intergenerational Advocates and the Ecosystem Proxies unless admitted by a vote of the present pool. Once a membership rule is adopted, the group has fifteen minutes to apply it retroactively: do the excluded proxy delegates now gain full voting rights, and if so, with what weight?

The payoff scheme should be collective but asymmetric. If the group reaches unanimous agreement on both the initial membership rule and the retroactive admission rule within forty-five minutes, every delegate earns ten points. If they reach a majority-imposed settlement, the factions that composed the winning coalition each earn ten points, while the others earn two. There is no real money, but points determine speaking privileges in the debrief. Provide explicit rules for speaking order—perhaps a rotating chair with two-minute interventions—and a formal amendment procedure requiring seconding and a preliminary straw vote. Break the timeline into ten-minute phases: opening statements, first meta-vote attempt, negotiation, final vote, and retroactive application round.

In the debrief, map each moment onto parts of the boundary problem: the provisional pool corresponds to the arbitrary initial demos; the retroactive admission vote corresponds to the circularity of using an existing demos to redefine itself; the payoff asymmetry corresponds to the strategic incentive to lock in one’s own membership criterion. Identify where each faction could resist—e.g., the Riparian Residents refusing to recognize ecological interdependence as a membership basis—and state the cost of that resistance in terms of collective payoff or coordination failure. Be explicit about which feature of the idea the simulation cannot reproduce: specifically, the normative claim that there might be an objectively correct demos independent of any procedural choice, which I will have to supply in discussion rather than derive from the rules. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for instance, whether the present pool will almost certainly exclude the proxy delegates in the first vote, whether students will conflate role-playing with personal conviction, and how I should handle emotional resistance if participants identify strongly with their assigned mandates.
