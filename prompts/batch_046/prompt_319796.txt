In my honors philosophy of mind course, we have been studying Mikhail Bongard’s pattern-recognition problems and the broader question of how a mind can induce a concept from only a handful of positive and negative instances. One analogy that keeps surfacing in our discussions—especially when we contrast human reasoning with modern few-shot learning—is that solving a Bongard problem is like being a forensic philologist tasked with reconstructing a lost grammatical rule from a small corpus of inscriptions, half of which are authentic and half of which are deliberate forgeries inserted by a hostile scribe. The authentic inscriptions share a hidden structural feature that defines membership in the category, while the forgeries reproduce every superficial trait except that one. The analogy is used to support the claim that human concept induction is not statistical averaging over surface similarities, but rather a targeted search for a single, explicit governing rule—much as the philologist does not vote on letter frequencies but hunts for the grammatical principle that distinguishes genuine texts from counterfeits.

I am writing an essay on whether this comparison actually strengthens the anti-statistical view of human cognition, and I need to audit it carefully. Could you provide an explicit correspondence table—what in the Bongard task maps onto the philologist, onto the authentic inscriptions, onto the forgeries, onto the hidden grammatical rule, and onto the act of reconstruction itself? Then, identify the points where the mapping breaks down: for instance, does the philologist already possess a meta-language and a theory of intentional deception that the Bongard solver lacks? Does the presence of an adversarial forger smuggle in a level of strategic complexity that is absent from the original problem set, which was designed without malice? And does the analogy quietly assume that the induced concept must be linguistic or propositional, thereby ruling out non-verbal or gestalt-based induction that humans might actually use? Finally, please tell me whether the conclusion—that human induction is essentially rule-based and non-statistical—survives these disanalogies, or whether the forensic-linguist framing was doing the normative work all along. If you think a different analogy—perhaps one drawn from medical diagnosis or from navigation—would avoid these particular breaks while preserving the contrast with statistical learning, I would welcome that suggestion together with a brief justification.
