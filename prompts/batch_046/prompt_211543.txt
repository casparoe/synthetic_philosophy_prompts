Consider the paired evaluation norms common in university history departments: a tenure case is judged partly by archival fidelity, the expectation that a scholar's narrative can be reconstructed from cited primary sources without importing theoretical frameworks absent from those sources themselves, and partly by methodological reflexivity, the mandatory inclusion of a section in which the author explicitly states their theoretical commitments—postcolonial, Marxist, microhistorical, or otherwise—and explains how those commitments shaped source selection and interpretation. The two norms operate simultaneously without any formal mechanism for resolving their tension.

What conception of historical objectivity does this institutional pairing presuppose? Does it assume that Ranke's ideal of recovering how events actually happened is achievable at the level of factual reconstruction but not at the level of narrative framing, thereby compartmentalizing objectivity? Or does it implicitly concede Carr's and Hayden White's scepticism about bare factual narratives, treating framework-dependence as unavoidable at the macro level while pretending it can be bracketed at the micro level? Reconstruct the implicit theory of objectivity that makes both norms cohere, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and assess whether the pairing is philosophically consistent. Then describe what would have to change in tenure evaluation if the department adopted a consistently Whitean position—that all historical writing is essentially literary emplotment with no recoverable substrate of framework-independent fact.
