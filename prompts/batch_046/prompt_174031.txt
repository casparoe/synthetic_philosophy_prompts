After a long argument with a colleague about whether memetics and the Darwinian theory of culture still deserve philosophical attention, I want to settle a practical question about where conceptual effort should go. My colleague insists that without identifiable replicators or measurable selection coefficients, the framework is empirically hollow and conceptually redundant with standard social-learning theory. I think there is upstream philosophical work that remains tractable without field data, particularly around what I have been calling "memetic scaffolding" — the transmission of cultural structures through partial cues that never involve copying a discrete unit, as in ritual gestures reconstructed from context rather than imitated exactly. If a small interdisciplinary group had three years of funding to work on conceptual problems in cultural evolution — not empirical anthropology or computational modeling — which open questions should they prioritize, and in what order?

Specifically, I want you to weigh at least three lines of work. First, the ontology of the unit of selection: should philosophers try to defend discrete memes, or abandon them for practices or representations, and does this decision depend on resolving whether cultural evolution requires a genotype-phenotype distinction? Second, the applicability of Darwinian mechanisms to algorithmically curated transmission: when recommendation systems create feedback loops that resemble Lamarckian inheritance rather than blind variation and selection, does the Darwinian framework become misleading, or can it be extended? Third, the problem of memetic scaffolding itself: can a selectionist account explain the reconstruction of complex norms from fragmentary transmission without positing explicit mental representations of the full rule?

For each, explain why it is or is not upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers without access to large-scale behavioral data, and what specific conceptual result over the next few years would cause you to revise the ranking. I am less interested in a survey than in a defended agenda: which question should absorb the most effort, which is a dead end, and what criteria — importance, tractability, neglectedness, or dependency on other answers — justify that ordering. I would especially like you to avoid treating the framework's empirical failures as automatically fatal to its conceptual utility, since that is precisely the move my colleague keeps making and I want to test whether it holds.
