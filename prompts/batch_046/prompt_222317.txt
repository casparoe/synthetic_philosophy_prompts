Hey, I'm a PhD student in political philosophy and I'm trying to figure out what I acctually think about price controls for a chapter I'm writing. Here are my gut reactions, and they feel kind of all over the place.

If a city has rent control because zoning laws artificially choked off supply and now there's a shortage, I think capping rents for existing tenants is basically fair — it's fixing a government-made distortion, not stealing from landlords. But if the shortage is just natural scarcity, like an island with literally no room to build, then rent control feels wrong; it just creates black markets and hurts people who want to move in later. Same with wages: if there's a monopsony — say one factory in a rural town that can pay below productivity because workers have nowhere else to go — a minimum wage feels morally required. But in a competitive urban market with lots of small employers, a high floor feels wrong; I think it pushes bosses to automate and ends up hurting the workers it's meant to protect.

Then there's this other cluster. Price caps on insulin during a supply crisis where the manufacturer is charging a 1000% markup: I'm totally fine with that. But agricultural price floors meant to protect small family farms, even when they cause massive surplus waste and drive up food costs for poor people, feel unjust to me, even though I sympathize with the farmers. Here's the really weird one: a city imposes rent control only on luxury apartments, leaving low-income units unregulated, because that's what the political coalition could pass. I think that's worse than no control at all — it's regressive and arbitary. But I also think universal rent control in a competitive market is bad. So I keep telling myself "control is okay only if it's targeted and corrective" but then I reject a targeted version, which feels like a contradiction.

So is there a coherent principle that saves most of these? Maybe something like "price controls are legitimate only when they correct a specific market failure without producing a larger deadweight loss, and they have to be non-arbitrary in scope"? But then why do I reject the farm-price-floor case, which is also a kind of correction? Is it because the correction is distributive rather than efficiency-based? Anyway, can you lay out one or two precise principles that capture as many verdicts as possible, tell me which one I'd have to drop to keep things simple, and say what they'd predict for a case I haven't mentioned — like a government-mandated price ceiling on carbon credits in a cap-and-trade system that was designed with way too generous allowances? I don't want a bibliography or reading list, just the conceptual repair work.
