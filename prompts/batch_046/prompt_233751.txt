You chair the evaluation committee for a high-stakes clinical-certification board. Every quarter you must decide whether a candidate who performed correctly in a simulated emergency should be credited with knowledge—stable, reason-tied competence that survives variation and pressure—or with mere true belief, a correct response that could have been lucky, context-dependent, or quickly lost, and that offers no guarantee of future reliability. The philosophical distinction you are operationalizing is the Meno problem: knowledge is more valuable than true belief not because both fail to be true, but because knowledge is anchored by an account or reasoning pathway and therefore non-accidental, whereas true belief, like an untethered statue, can wander away.

Write a reusable audit procedure your committee can run in about twenty minutes per case file, using only the simulation transcript, the debrief interview, and the parameter log. Structure it as an ordered sequence of checks or questions. For each step, specify what counts as sufficient evidence, where in the record to look, and whether the step yields a definite result or flags a judgment call. Identify the exact termination points: where the procedure stops with a determination of "knowledge-grade competence," where it stops with "true-belief-only—do not certify," and where it must hand the file to a senior reviewer because reasonable auditors could disagree. Be explicit about the hard cases the procedure must catch: correct performance driven by opaque pattern-matching that collapses under perturbation, post-hoc rationalization of an initial guess, and fluency in standard conditions that masks an inability to adapt.

Then apply the procedure to three candidate profiles:
(a) A resident who correctly diagnoses a rare presentation, articulates a clear causal pathway linking symptoms to pathology, but admits in the debrief that she initially guessed and reconstructed the reasoning only afterward;
(b) A senior practitioner who handles a standard crisis flawlessly but, when the debrief varies two parameters, cannot adapt his protocol and insists the original conditions were essential to his success;
(c) One further case of your own design where the audit yields a conclusion the committee will find counter-intuitive or unwelcome, and explain precisely which step forces that result.

Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which forms of competence it systematically misses, which false positives and false negatives it is structurally prone to, and under what conditions the board should suspend the audit and revert to holistic evaluation rather than procedural determination.
