After a long argument with a friend about whether a statute can be so morally corrupt or so broken in its operation that it ceases to be law at all — rather than merely being bad law — I need to turn our disagreement into something my office can actually use. I serve on the screening panel of a transitional tribunal that reviews petitions challenging legacy statutes enacted by the prior regime. We receive roughly two petitions per week, must issue a preliminary determination within forty-eight hours, and work with legal staff who have no philosophical training. The chair, a senior jurist who must sign every order, will not tolerate metaphysical speculation; she wants a reusable procedure that distinguishes (A) “valid law, however unjust — dismiss the petition” from (B) “not law at all because it fails the internal conditions of legality — accept the petition” and (C) “the question exceeds screening capacity — escalate to the full tribunal as a values judgment.” The stakes are high: a wrong classification either legitimizes an instrument of oppression or destabilizes the legal order by pretending a valid rule was never law.

Please construct a decision procedure — an ordered sequence of tests with explicit pass/fail criteria, stopping rules, and escalation triggers — that translates the Hart–Fuller debate into this operational setting. Do not summarize the debate; operationalize it. Your procedure should include the following elements.

First, a “source-trace” step that determines whether the statute traces back to the prior regime’s rule of recognition. Specify the evidence required (enactment records, publication status, chain of authority), the conditions under which the trace breaks (forgery, ultra vires enactment, dissolution of the enacting body without legal succession), and the automatic outcome if the trace fails or succeeds.

Second, an “internal morality audit” with four explicit sub-tests derived from Fuller’s principles but tailored to authoritarian contexts: (i) generality — does the text address a class of conduct, or is it a disguised bill of attainder? (ii) promulgation — was the rule made available to those governed? (iii) non-retroactivity and temporal stability — does it govern future conduct with sufficient consistency? (iv) congruence — is there a systematic gap between the rule as announced and the rule as applied by officials? Define what counts as a failure severe enough to undermine the statute’s capacity to guide behavior, as opposed to a mere moral defect or an isolated abuse.

Third, a “separation checkpoint” that forces the reviewer to classify the petition’s core objection: is it that the statute lacks the formal or functional properties necessary to operate as law (a legality failure), or that its content violates fundamental morality (a justice failure)? Provide concrete criteria for distinguishing the two, including a “functional guidance” test: could a reasonable subject, knowing the rule and observing official practice, have adjusted her conduct to avoid sanction?

Fourth, a decision matrix that combines the results of steps one through three into outcomes A, B, or C. Specify exactly which combinations yield an automatic dismissal, which yield acceptance that the statute is not law, and which combinations trigger escalation because they depend on an irreducible normative premise — for example, whether minimal moral conditions are constitutive of legal validity at all, or whether a rule that guides conduct only through terror still counts as guiding it.

The procedure must handle conflicts among Fuller’s own principles: for instance, a statute that is perfectly general, published, and stable but is applied with such arbitrary discretion that congruence collapses; or a decree that was never promulgated but was enforced with perfect consistency for years. It must also include hard cases where the reviewer’s personal judgment will conflict with the procedure’s output.

Then apply the procedure to three petitions:

(i) A 1987 “Public Order” statute enacted by the recognized legislature, published in the official gazette, general in form (“all unauthorized assemblies are prohibited”), but applied exclusively by security forces against one ethnic group and never against regime supporters.

(ii) A 1992 “Emergency Economic Measure” enacted by decree after the legislature was dissolved; the decree was never published in any accessible form, but was enforced consistently for five years; its content is technically clear and authorizes property confiscation without compensation.

(iii) A statute that is procedurally flawless, general, promulgated, non-retroactive, and applied with perfect congruence, but mandates the forced relocation of a specific population group. Apply the procedure and explain why it yields outcome A — that this is valid law — despite the fact that you, as the advisor who believes extreme substantive injustice should sometimes disqualify a rule from being law, find that result morally intolerable. Identify precisely where your disagreement lies: in the separation checkpoint, in the definition of congruence, or in the refusal to treat moral content as a condition of legal validity.

Finally, state the procedure’s explicit limits: what kinds of moral disagreement it cannot resolve, what categories of statute it will systematically misclassify, and what assumptions about the prior regime’s rule of recognition might be false but are necessary for the procedure to function at all. The deliverable should be usable by a lawyer who has never read Hart or Fuller; include no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not ask for them.
