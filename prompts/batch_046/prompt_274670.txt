Design a 90-minute essay question for an upper-level MA comprehensive exam in philosophy of history that tests whether students can do more than summarize the cyclical models of Polybius (anacyclosis), Ibn Khaldun (asabiyya dynamics), Spengler (cultural morphology), and Toynbee (challenge-and-response). The question should center on a constructed conceptual tension—call it mnemonic drift—whereby any cyclical account must explain how institutional and cultural memory of prior cycles structurally alters later ones, so that repetition is never pure. Ask candidates to defend one of three positions: that mnemonic drift collapses cyclical theories into linear ones; that it can be fully absorbed within a revised cyclical framework; or that it exposes a false dichotomy between cyclical and linear historiography. Provide a model answer that makes the specific conceptual moves required rather than recounting the four thinkers, and supply a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five must-hit points with individual weights, plus a one-line note on how candidates typically fail each point. The prompt should not request bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and the difficulty should lie in locating the precise explanatory point where cyclical accounts implicitly rely on directional, non-repeating mechanisms.
