Write a dialogue between a proponent of Confucian role ethics and an interlocutor who grants the importance of relational morality but denies that roles can fully determine moral obligations. Focus their exchange on the problem of role overlap: cases in which a single agent simultaneously occupies roles whose demands appear irreconcilable—specifically, the tension between filial obligations to a corrupt parent and public obligations of justice. The defender of role ethics should introduce and deploy the distinction between constitutive roles, which generate non-transferable moral obligations by defining the agent's relational identity, and occupational roles, which generate only functional expectations that can be discharged or delegated without moral residue. The interlocutor should argue that this distinction cannot be maintained without either smuggling in universalist criteria of justice or rendering role conflict merely a practical problem of scheduling rather than a genuine moral dilemma. Each speaker must respond directly to the previous point: the defender should explain why a ruler experiencing conflict between saving a parent and enforcing law is undergoing a moral rupture, not just practical strain, and the critic should explain why that experience is better interpreted as the collision of two universalizable duties rather than an internal fracture of relational identity. The dialogue should reach no artificial resolution; instead, it should clarify exactly where the disagreement lies—whether in the ontology of the self, in the commensurability of obligations, or in the possibility of moral criticism from outside any role. Both characters should be equally competent, with neither reduced to a strawman, and every reply should address the substance of the argument just offered rather than shifting to a different objection.
