Construct a modified version of Parfit's hitchhiker scenario—where rationality apparently requires being disposed to act against one's own immediate interests—engineered to isolate an internal, non-social binding mechanism rather than the usual reputation-based commitment. Design a realistic, one-paragraph case set in a professional environment (e.g., a consulting firm or hospital administration office, not a trolley problem or science-fiction setting) in which an agent rationally installs a subpersonal decision architecture that is genuinely optimal to maintain in general, but which, in this specific instance, forces them to take a costly action that a purely act-oriented calculus would reject. The binding must depend on a specific cognitive feature—such as an informational asymmetry between a planning module and an execution module, or a deliberately maintained false belief about the architecture's origin—that is not reducible to social signaling or reputation. After presenting the scenario, provide the analysis: (i) the ex-ante reasoning that makes adopting the architecture rational; (ii) the ex-post reasoning that makes complying with it irrational by standard decision-theoretic lights; (iii) identification of the exact feature doing the work that separates this case from the standard hitchhiker; (iv) a demonstration that removing that feature collapses the case either into ordinary rational choice or back into the classic social-commitment version; and (v) the line of attack a critic who denies rational irrationality—say, a pure Bayesian updater—would use against that specific feature. Keep the scenario concrete, avoid exotic physics and perfect predictors, and do not rely on bibliographic references.
