I was sitting in on a graduate seminar on social choice theory when two students, Maya and Jordan, got into a sustained disagreement about whether Sen’s liberal paradox undermines institutional designs that try to combine minimal individual autonomy with collective decision-making. They were not discussing the classic book-reading scenario, but a concrete proposal for our university’s student governance forum: a charter rule stating that no collective vote may restrict what any individual member chooses to post or discuss, provided the choice does not involve direct physical harm to others.

Maya argued that once you take this minimal liberal principle seriously and apply it to real social contexts—where people hold preferences not only about their own actions but also about the actions of others—the paradox immediately bites. She sketched a case: Student A prefers that neither A nor Student B post about abortion rights on the forum; Student B prefers that both be free to post, and specifically that B be permitted to post. If the charter respects A’s self-regarding preference not to post, and B’s self-regarding preference to post, the outcome is that B posts and A abstains. But A also holds a preference over B’s action—namely, that B not post. Maya said that any institution claiming to respect autonomy must face the fact that people care about their social environment; if you extend the Pareto principle to cover these external preferences, you cannot consistently respect both A’s and B’s preferences. Therefore, she concluded, the charter is either incoherent or secretly resolves the conflict by arbitrarily protecting only self-regarding preferences, which is not a conceptual solution but a political one disguised as neutrality.

Jordan replied that this misdiagnoses the problem. The liberal principle, he said, is intentionally restricted to self-regarding domains; it does not claim to arbitrate conflicts arising from preferences over others’ actions. The fact that extending it further generates a contradiction with Pareto efficiency is not a paradox that undermines liberalism—it is precisely the reason for the restriction. He compared it to Arrow’s broader impossibility result: social choice becomes unworkable if you allow unrestricted preference domains, so we impose domain restrictions. The boundary between self-regarding and other-regarding concerns is not an ad hoc rescue but a principled line, analogous to the distinction between harm to others and mere offense. Maya countered that in a shared forum, one person’s speech is not merely an external preference for another; it actively reshapes the discursive environment that A must inhabit. Calling A’s objection other-regarding treats social interaction as if people were isolated atoms, which is exactly the fiction that makes Jordan’s boundary seem clean. Jordan then noted that if we abandon the boundary, we do not merely reproduce Sen’s conflict—we lose any basis for protecting minority expression against majoritarian suppression, since the majority can always claim that the minority’s speech violates their preference for a particular environment.

Neither seemed convinced, and they turned to me as a neutral observer. Please adjudicate their exchange step by step: evaluate Maya’s claim that the paradox reveals an incoherence in minimal liberal institutional design; evaluate Jordan’s defense that domain restriction is principled rather than ad hoc; assess whether Maya’s challenge to the self-regarding boundary succeeds in the specific context of a shared forum; and say what, if anything, both are missing about the relationship between Sen’s result and Arrow’s broader impossibility framework. Finally, indicate who is more right overall and what a genuinely consistent charter might need to include that both have overlooked.
