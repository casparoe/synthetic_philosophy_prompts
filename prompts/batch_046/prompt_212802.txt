I am recounting a disagreement I overheard between two philosophers, Maya and Leo, on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — specifically, whether pleasures share anything that justifies treating them as a single kind of intrinsic good. I did not take sides, and I should like a neutral adjudication.

Maya opened by rejecting the classic “common sensation” version of the problem. She argued that pleasures need not share a specific phenomenal quality; rather, they are unified by a structural feature she called a “positive valence vector” — a minimal directional orientation in which the state presents itself to the subject as worth prolonging. The pleasure of a cool breeze and the pleasure of grasping a logical proof, she said, differ radically in content and sensory character, yet both exhibit this vector. She compared it to brightness in visual experience: different hues can all be bright, and similarly different pleasures can all carry the same abstract affective structure without being phenomenologically identical.

Leo responded that this either smuggles in the very qualitative feature Maya denies or abandons hedonism altogether. If the vector is genuinely phenomenological, she has merely renamed a shared “feel” rather than dissolved heterogeneity; if it is purely functional or motivational, then hedonism collapses into a form of preference satisfaction, because what makes the state good is no longer an intrinsic quality but its tendency to motivate continuation. He pressed her on simple bodily pleasures — the warmth of sunlight on skin — which, he claimed, lack the representational resources required for any “presentation as worth prolonging.” He introduced a distinction between “receptive” pleasures, which are passive sensory registrations, and “achievement” pleasures, which involve the resolution of tension or the fulfilment of an intention. Maya’s vector, he said, applies neatly to the latter but misdescribes the former, so her account either excludes a large class of pleasures or forces an artificial cognitive structure onto them.

Maya countered that Leo’s receptive/achievement distinction is ad hoc, invented precisely to rescue the intuition that pleasures are too diverse to share a form. Even receptive bodily states, she insisted, contain a minimal self-referential structure: the body registers its present condition as preferable to an alternative state of discomfort or absence. She also appealed to classical hedonist language — Sidgwick’s phrase “desirable consciousness” — to show that serious hedonists have long sought to characterise pleasure without tying it to a particular sensation. The heterogeneity of content, she concluded, is therefore compatible with homogeneity at the level of affective form.

Leo granted that Sidgwick’s phrase avoids sensory reductionism, but argued that it merely relocates the problem. If “desirable” means “actually desired by the subject,” then defining well-being in terms of desirable consciousness risks circularity; if it means “objectively worthy of being desired,” then one owes an account of what makes it worthy, which seems to require either a shared qualitative feature or an independent normative argument that hedonism was meant to avoid. He added a further difficulty: even if Maya’s structural account succeeds descriptively, it offers no metric for comparing the strength or value of pleasures across domains. Without some common phenomenal currency, he asked, how can one weigh the valence vector of tasting wine against that of solving a mathematical problem?

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each move — Maya’s initial structural proposal, Leo’s phenomenological-or-functional dilemma, the brightness analogy, the receptive/achievement distinction, the appeal to Sidgwick, and the comparison-metric objection — say whose reasoning is stronger and where, if anywhere, it falters. Do not simply declare an overall winner; specify which particular steps are sound, which depend on unstated premises, and which commit subtle errors of conflation or scope. Then identify what both parties appear to overlook: for example, whether there is a coherent middle path between a shared raw feel and a purely abstract vector, whether their dispute turns on an unstated assumption about the relation between phenomenology and value, or whether one party has quietly changed the subject from intrinsic value to motivational structure. Write your response in British English, and avoid inventing bibliographic references or citations.
