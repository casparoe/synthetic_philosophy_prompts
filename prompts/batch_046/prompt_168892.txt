Here is an argument I have been developing for a graduate seminar on practical reason, and I now worry it proves far more than Kavka intended. (1) In Kavka's toxin puzzle, a rational agent should form the intention at midnight to drink the toxin the next afternoon, because the billionaire's reward—one million dollars deposited regardless of whether the toxin is actually consumed—is available only on that condition. (2) Once the money is secured, there is no reason to drink; yet rationality demands carrying out the prior intention, since the capacity to form credible intentions is the very mechanism that makes such rewards possible. (3) Therefore rational action is not governed by reasons present at the time of performance, but by the execution of prior, reason-responsive commitments. (4) If this holds for individuals, it holds a fortiori for collective bodies: a democratic polity should bind its future citizens with irreversible constitutional provisions, automated enforcement mechanisms, or irreversible treaties whenever the binding yields a present benefit—stability, investment, credibility—even though future citizens will have good reason to revise them. (5) Furthermore, since any domain left open to future revision can be anticipated and exploited, the only stable equilibrium is total precommitment: rational societies should eliminate all future deliberation and bind themselves now on every significant choice. (6) But democratic self-governance is not merely instrumentally valuable; it presupposes that present citizens can legitimately overturn past commitments in light of new reasons. (7) Hence the reasoning in (1) must be rejected: rational agents should not form the intention to drink. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I am inclined to preserve (3) and reject (5), but I want to know precisely what that costs—whether it requires abandoning the institutional analog entirely, or only limiting the scope of binding commitments, and whether there is a principled line between legitimate constitutional entrenchment and the absurd total precommitment of (5). Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
