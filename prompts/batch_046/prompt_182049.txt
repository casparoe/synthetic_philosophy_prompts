Below are notes for an essay I am drafting, tentatively titled “The Causal Archaeology of National Wealth: Structural Multicollinearity and the Limits of Macrohistorical Identification.” The core argument is that the familiar triad—institutions, geography, and culture—should not be treated as competing causal hypotheses but as co-constitutive interpretive frames. Because these factors co-evolved over centuries, standard comparative methods face structural multicollinearity: we lack the counterfactual worlds needed to isolate institutional effects from geographic endowments or cultural norms. To move beyond stalemate, I propose evaluating explanations not by empirical fit but by “counterfactual fragility,” defined as the extent to which testing a claim requires holding constant variables that are conceptually entangled with the cause itself. Institutional explanations score high on fragility—imagining British-style property rights in the Congo requires simultaneously altering culture, prior extraction patterns, and ecological constraints—whereas geographic explanations are less fragile but risk collapsing into environmental determinism that ignores institutional adaptation.

I then introduce what I call “epistemic substitution”: the tendency in development economics to substitute observable proxies—legal origin, settler mortality rates, or parliamentary tenure—for unobservable causal mechanisms such as contract-enforcement norms or credible commitment. I argue this is not merely measurement error but a category mistake analogous to the map-territory distinction in philosophy of representation: the proxy becomes the object of causal attribution, so that correlations with wealth are interpreted as direct causal flows rather than representational artifacts. From this I draw a pluralist conclusion: rather than asking which factor “really” caused divergence, we should construct a “diagnostic surface” that maps how each frame highlights distinct failure modes—extraction, adaptation, normative lock-in—without claiming exclusive causal sovereignty.
