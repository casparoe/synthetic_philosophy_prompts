At a workshop on intentionality, two participants—Caleb and Nadia—got into a sustained argument about Dennett’s two-bitser example and its implications for original versus derived intentionality. I listened as a bystander and could not tell who was winning. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

Caleb opened: Dennett asks us to imagine a minimal system—a two-bitser with only two binary registers tracking temperature and humidity—and to adopt the intentional stance toward it. We predict its behavior by attributing beliefs like “it wants low humidity.” Caleb said this only demonstrates derived intentionality, because the aboutness comes entirely from us, the interpreters. But then Dennett claims that even human brains are just more complex versions of the same thing, with no “original” intentionality hidden inside. Caleb argued this is self-defeating: if all intentionality is stance-derived, then Dennett himself must be interpreting his own brain from the outside when he thinks about two-bitsers. So the view either collapses into an infinite regress of interpreters or secretly smuggles in the original intentionality it denies.

Nadia replied: That is the standard Searlean objection, but it misses Dennett’s point. The intentional stance is not a metaphysical gift bestowed by an already-intentional observer; it is a predictive strategy that can in principle be adopted by any system with enough complexity, including networks of two-bitsers interpreting one another. There is no “smuggling” because the origin of interpretation is evolutionary and pragmatic, not anchored in a special mental substance. The regress is stopped not by finding a magical origin point, but by treating interpretation as a useful pattern that needs no deeper grounding.

Caleb pushed back on the distinction between error types. He said that if there is no original intentionality, then when he is wrong that Paris is in France, the mistake is just predictive failure—his neural pattern broke or received bad input. But when the two-bitser misregisters humidity, it is also just predictive failure or mechanical malfunction. Caleb insisted there is a crucial difference: he can be misinformed about Paris while his brain is functioning perfectly, whereas the two-bitser can never be “misinformed” in that normative sense; it can only break. That space of reasons—being justified or unjustified, misled rather than broken—requires that thoughts already have intrinsic content that can be satisfied or not by the world. Derived intentionality, he said, can only be useful or useless, not true or false in the same way.

Nadia answered that Caleb begs the question by assuming misrepresentation requires an intrinsic mental arrow. On Dennett’s view, error is indeed predictive failure, but in a rich system “failure” can take the form of corrupted input patterns without hardware damage—exactly what we call being misinformed. The space of reasons is just a higher-order predictive space with recursive self-monitoring. The two-bitser is too simple to enter it, but that shows original intentionality is not a separate metaphysical kind; it is derived intentionality scaled up with enough recursive complexity.

Caleb then raised the first-person problem: when he corrects himself—“Wait, Paris is not in Germany”—he is not merely updating a predictive model. He is recognizing that his previous thought was false. That recognition requires that the previous thought already had content. The two-bitser never recognizes anything; it just shifts state. Scaling complexity, Caleb argued, cannot create recognition unless you already have original intentionality to scale.

Nadia’s final move was that Dennett would treat first-person access itself as a predictive model—the brain interpreting its own states. The “directness” Caleb feels is an illusion of transparency, not proof of an intrinsic aboutness relation. So the two-bitser is not an outlier; it is the base case that reveals the structure common to all intentional ascription, including self-knowledge.
