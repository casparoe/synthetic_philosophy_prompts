I am a doctoral student in epistemology, and in working through the peer-disagreement literature—particularly the debates between conciliationism and steadfastness—I have repeatedly observed a pattern in both philosophical and scientific argumentation that does not seem to be captured by the standard taxonomy. Rather than stating it abstractly, let me offer two concrete cases.

First, in philosophy of science: two equally credentialed researchers disagree over whether a particular causal-inference method is appropriate for a given dataset. Researcher A defends her use of the method and dismisses B as failing to be a genuine peer, claiming that B "operates within an incompatible statistical paradigm." Several months later, B publishes a result that happens to support A's broader theoretical framework, though B still uses the disputed method. A now treats B as an obvious peer whose agreement lends independent weight to the view—despite the fact that B's training, publication record, and methodological commitments have not changed at all. The boundary of peerhood has contracted to license A's steadfastness and then expanded to harvest agreement.

Second, in normative ethics: philosophers C and D disagree sharply about the moral status of non-human animals. C insists she need not conciliate, arguing that D is not a peer because D's position rests on a "foundational intuition" that C rejects. Later, D concedes a minor point about the relevance of sentience thresholds. At that point, C treats D as now "close enough" to peer status—but only in order to argue that D should conciliate on the remaining major claim. C does not treat her own earlier steadfastness as now subject to revision, nor does she apply the same peer-expansion logic to other opponents who share D's original view.

In both instances, peerhood is not being treated as a stable precondition that determines whether conciliation or steadfastness is appropriate. Instead, it is being toggled in a direction-sensitive manner: contracted to protect one's own belief from revision, expanded to place the burden of conciliation on the other party. This is not mere special pleading about expertise, because the expertise claim is symmetric in both directions; it is not plain equivocation, since "peer" is used consistently within each individual move; and it is not the generic problem of peer identification, since the difficulty is not uncertainty about who counts, but the strategic deployment of that boundary.

Does this specific phenomenon—this asymmetric, interest-relative negotiation of peer boundaries within conciliationist frameworks—have an established name or formal treatment in the literature? If not, can you define and delimit it with precision, distinguishing it from straightforward steadfastness, from the general challenge of identifying peers, and from ordinary rhetorical boundary-policing? I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want the exact carving: what the pattern is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
