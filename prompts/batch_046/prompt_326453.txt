I am moderating the closing Q&A of a graduate colloquium in philosophy and international law, and the guest is Dr. Elena Voss, a legal philosopher who defends a reconstructed Lemkinian framework for atrocity. Her view holds that genocide ought to be treated as a broad moral category encompassing not only physical destruction but also systematic cultural and political erasure, and that the post-1948 legal narrowing of the term has produced a structural “naming gap”: states and tribunals strategically withhold the label to evade obligations, and the gap is maintained by institutional incentives rather than by conceptual necessity. She further advances the idea of “diagnostic authority,” arguing that the power to name an atrocity should rest primarily with affected communities rather than with state actors or international courts. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press her position rather than invite restatement. I want at least one question on each of the following: (1) the conceptual divergence between Lemkin’s original broad conception and the UN Convention’s restricted definition, particularly with respect to cultural and political groups; (2) how “diagnostic authority” operates when affected communities themselves are divided about whether the label applies—whether fragmentation undermines the authority she assigns to them; (3) the charge that broadening the category dilutes its moral and legal force, with a follow-up keyed to her likely reply that such dilution is precisely the strategic purpose of narrow definitional politics; (4) the institutional mechanisms that maintain the “naming gap” as a structural feature—what specific legal, diplomatic, or evidentiary practices reproduce it; and (5) the normative implications for current practice, especially whether recognizing cultural genocide as a moral category should trigger legal remedies even absent the Convention’s physical-destruction criterion. For each area, include a sharp follow-up anticipating the answer she is most likely to give. The tone should be rigorous but colloquial, suitable for an audience of philosophy and law graduate students. Do not ask for bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and avoid generic biographical questions.
