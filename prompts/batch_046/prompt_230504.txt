I've been asked to deliver the commencement address at my daughter's university this spring. She's graduating in philosophy of science, and I've spent the past year auditing her courses after a professional crisis in structural engineering left me wondering whether the safety standards I work with rest on anything more solid than institutional habit and recursive peer review. The audience will be genuinely mixed—graduates across the sciences and humanities, parents who haven't thought about epistemology since college, trustees, and several faculty who teach the regress problem professionally—so the remarks need to land for experts without losing everyone else.

I want the speech to turn structurally on the three live responses to the regress—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—not as a dry taxonomy, but as constraints on what I can honestly say from a podium. I should not pose as a foundational authority offering bedrock certainties ("trust me, I've lived longer"), nor retreat into an empty coherentist reassurance ("it all hangs together if you see the big picture"), nor offer an infinite deferral of responsibility ("you'll understand once you've lived more"). At the same time, it must be genuinely encouraging rather than paralyzing: the point is to show what responsible, non-authoritarian advice sounds like when you admit that justification doesn't terminate in self-evident premises, yet still requires you to speak with conviction. Please write remarks that use these three positions to frame intellectual humility as an active virtue rather than a passive skepticism, accessible to families but precise enough that the philosophy students won't wince, and without any bibliography, citation, or appeal to texts the audience couldn't verify in the room.
