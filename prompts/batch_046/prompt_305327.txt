You are grading a graduate seminar paper in philosophy of language. The assignment asked students to explain Davidson's principle of charity in radical interpretation, using belief-desire holism and distinguishing Davidson's project from Quine's radical translation.

The exam question was: An interpreter observes a speaker who consistently applies the term "water" to clear, odorless liquids that quench thirst, including cases where chemical analysis reveals the liquid is not H2O but a perceptually identical compound. The interpreter knows the chemical difference; the speaker does not. How should a Davidsonian interpreter assign belief and meaning? Discuss the role of charity, holism, and the Davidson-Quine distinction.

Below is one student's answer. It is genuinely mixed: correct about the more visible features of Davidson's view, but contains one or two subtle errors—conflations or steps that do not quite follow—that a hasty grader might overlook. Do not signal your own overall judgment in advance. Provide a specific letter grade (A through F) and justify it with point-by-point comments that identify exactly where the answer is right, where it subtly goes wrong, and what the mistake costs.

Student answer:

The interpreter should apply Davidson's principle of charity, which requires maximizing agreement between interpreter and speaker. Since the speaker applies "water" to both H2O and the chemically different substance, attributing the belief that both are water would mean attributing a false belief about chemistry. To preserve charity, the interpreter should instead assign "water" a broader extension—clear, odorless, thirst-quenching liquids—so that the speaker's utterances come out true. This avoids widespread error and respects rationality.

This case also illustrates belief-desire holism: the meaning of "water" is fixed together with beliefs about drinking, cleaning, and visibility. We cannot determine what the speaker means without also fixing these related beliefs, and vice versa. The interpreter therefore constructs an interconnected web in which the speaker's practical needs shape conceptual content.

Finally, this distinguishes Davidson from Quine. Quine's radical translation is purely behavioral: it constructs a translation manual without attributing mental states, leaving reference indeterminate. Davidson's radical interpretation is psychological: it assigns actual beliefs and desires using the same behavioral evidence, but it allows the interpreter to draw on shared human rationality and common knowledge of the physical environment to resolve ambiguity. Thus Davidson moves beyond Quine by integrating mental-state attribution with environmental knowledge.
