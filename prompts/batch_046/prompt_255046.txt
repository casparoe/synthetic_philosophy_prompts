The exam question was as follows:

“In the philosophy of mind, Paul Churchland defends eliminative materialism about propositional attitudes, arguing that folk psychology is an empirically stagnant theoretical framework likely to be superseded by a mature neuroscience. Daniel Dennett, by contrast, defends the intentional stance, treating belief-desire ascription as a predictive strategy that need not commit us to the literal existence of mental representations inside the head. Recently, some philosophers of neuroscience have proposed an ‘interventional continuity criterion’: a mental category should be retained in our ontology only if it identifies a stable, cross-contextual target for neurocognitive intervention—pharmacological, neuromodulatory, or surgical—that is not reducible without loss of predictive power to lower-level mechanisms.

Write an answer that (i) distinguishes Churchland’s eliminativism from logical behaviorism; (ii) explains whether Dennett’s intentional stance can accommodate the interventional continuity criterion without collapsing into realism about beliefs; and (iii) argues for or against the claim that belief-desire psychology fails this criterion and should therefore be eliminated.”

The student’s answer:

“Eliminative materialism, as Churchland presents it, holds that beliefs and desires do not exist. This differs from logical behaviorism, which treats beliefs as mere dispositions to behave; Churchland instead thinks beliefs are brain states, but our folk-psychological descriptions of them are mistaken and should be replaced by precise neuroscientific descriptions. So Churchland is essentially a reductive materialist who expects neuroscience to reveal the real neural correlates that correspond to belief.

Dennett’s intentional stance tells us to treat people as if they have beliefs in order to predict their behavior. This is only a heuristic shortcut, not a serious ontology. The interventional continuity criterion asks whether belief can be targeted by direct brain intervention. Because we can alter belief-like states both through cognitive therapy and through drugs such as SSRIs, belief clearly satisfies the criterion. However, Dennett is concerned only with behavioral prediction, not with neural targets, so his view actually fails the criterion. That failure shows that Dennett’s stance is just a useful fiction that cannot save belief from elimination.

Therefore, belief-desire psychology should be eliminated: it lacks a stable, exclusively neural intervention target, and Dennett’s predictive strategy does not provide one. Churchland’s eliminativism is correct.”
