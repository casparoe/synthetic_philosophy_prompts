I am drafting the syllabus for my graduate seminar on moral responsibility and punishment, and I want to include a structured disagreement that arose during our workshop last week. Two colleagues—one sympathetic to Dennett’s account of moral competence and reactive attitudes, the other defending Caruso’s public-health-quarantine model—debated whether any form of just deserts can be preserved without collapsing back into retributivism. Their exchange went as follows.

The Dennettian colleague opened by arguing that punishment is legitimate only when it treats the offender as a morally competent agent capable of answering for her actions. If we abandon all backward-looking conditions and justify restraint solely by forward-looking aims—public safety, rehabilitation, risk reduction—we reduce the offender to an object of behavioral management, no different in principle from quarantining a contagious vector. She insisted that “desert” here need not invoke metaphysical libertarianism or cosmic moral accounting; it can be understood relationally, as the normative fact that the victim is owed an acknowledgment that the harm was authored by a responsible agent. Without that relational desert, she claimed, practices like apology and forgiveness lose their point, since there is no “answer” being given, only a therapeutic adjustment.

The Caruso-inspired colleague replied that this conflates accountability with suffering. He granted that we should treat offenders as rational agents—offering explanations, demanding participation in repair, engaging them in dialogue—but denied that this requires any endorsement of deserved harm. Dennett’s “elbow room,” he noted, concerns evolutionary and functional freedom, not a license to inflict pain for past wrongs. He argued that the distinction between dangerous mental illness and culpable wrongdoing can be maintained purely through assessments of rational-control capacity, which determine our mode of engagement without licensing retributive harm. Backward-looking narrative coherence—understanding the wrong as part of the agent’s history—can be preserved, he said, but apology and forgiveness should be reconstructed as practices of mutual repair and recognition rather than as the settlement of a moral debt.

The first colleague pushed back: if the only reason to engage the wrongdoer in dialogue is forward-looking repair or societal benefit, then the victim’s grievance is instrumentalized. We talk to the offender because it aids reform or restores social trust, not because she owes an answer to the person she harmed. The “owe,” she maintained, is exactly what relational desert captures, and Dennett’s framework allows this because moral competence includes being subject to genuinely backward-looking reactive attitudes that address the act performed, not merely the risk posed.

The second colleague countered that “owing repair” can be reconstructed relationally without metaphysical baggage: the offender owes repair because of the harm done and the relationship disrupted, not because the universe demands suffering. The reactive attitudes, he added, are best understood as emotional responses within relationships of mutual respect, not as institutional justifications for state-inflicted harm. He concluded that his interlocutor was smuggling retributivism back in by assuming that “owing an answer” must entail deserving punishment, when in fact the public-health model can preserve all the normative richness of accountability—explanation, repair, acknowledgment—while rejecting the idea that suffering is the appropriate currency of justice.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move—rating each of the six steps above from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification for each rating. Say who is right about which specific point, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing. In particular, address whether the Dennettian colleague succeeds in distinguishing relational desert from retributivism, and whether the Caruso-inspired colleague’s functional distinction between rational-control assessments and desert-based distinctions can do all the normative work she demands. Finally, indicate who is more right overall, and whether their disagreement turns on a substantive philosophical difference or largely on a framing choice about the language of “owing.”
