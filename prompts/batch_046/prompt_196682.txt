I hold a position that I want stress-tested with conceptual rigor, not with standard talking points. The familiar Hayekian defense of markets holds that prices aggregate dispersed private information that no central planner could gather. From the family of no-trade theorems—Milgrom-Stokey and Aumann’s agreement result—I draw a stronger, less common conclusion: if rational agents share a common prior and have common knowledge of rationality, then trade should not occur at all; consequently, in any market where trade does occur under approximately common knowledge, prices must already fully reveal all private information, leaving nothing genuinely dispersed to be aggregated. The epistemic defense therefore collapses into either a tautology (prices reveal what prices reveal) or an admission that real markets depend on systematic failures of rationality or on informational asymmetry about rationality itself. I do not deny allocative efficiency; I deny that markets achieve it by harnessing irreducibly private knowledge. I also want to stress a specific corollary: the informational silence of agents who choose not to trade—what one might call the epistemics of market absence—must either carry independent weight (in which case prices do not fully reveal information) or be ignored (in which case the aggregation story is incomplete).
