I run a philosophy club at a community college, usually 10-12 people, about an hour and a half. I want an activity that gets them to feel the tension with treating video-game NPCs as morally irrelevant just because they aren't conscious. But I'm not talking about the standard AI-sentience argument. I'm thinking about procedural characters that have schedules, react to your actions, hold relationships with other characters in the world, and can be permanently removed if you kill them in a save file. There's no inner life, but there's a kind of functional persistence and relational embedding in the game system. I want the students to actually make choices in a structured game where they can use these characters as disposable tools or treat them as part of the world they need to preserve, with real (small) stakes or point systems. They should feel the pull of thinking that destroying them is wrong not because they suffer, but because it breaks the relational web they inhabit. Then a debrief that maps their choices onto the idea that moral status might depend on relational roles rather than consciousness, and be honest about what the activity can't capture. No lecture up front, don't name the concept untill after, and please include what usually go wrong when you run this.
