Write an essay on whether an artwork's aesthetic value depends on what can be called its "counterfactual stability"—the modal fact that an original exists only because a specific historical agent made it and would not exist otherwise—rather than being fully determined by its "phenomenal profile," the set of perceptual and interpretive responses it actually produces. Consider this precise case: a forger creates a painting physically and perceptually identical to a destroyed masterpiece, using only period-appropriate materials and techniques, with no intent to deceive, merely to reconstruct what was lost. A museum displays it for decades as the recovered original, generating identical critical discourse and viewer responses. Once the substitution is revealed, defenders of indexical value argue that the work's "counterfactual fragility"—its dependence on the forger rather than the original artist—deprives it of a modal property essential to artistic value. Critics reply that this imports metaphysical history into phenomenology and commits a category mistake. Weigh both sides carefully, but do not end agnostically: defend either the view that counterfactual stability is constitutive of artistic value (so the substitution is aesthetically deficient despite perceptual identity and should be removed) or the view that it is an extrinsic historical fact with no bearing on aesthetic judgment (so the work should retain full value and display status).
