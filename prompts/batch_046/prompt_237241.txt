Scholars and lawyers have argued for decades about whether genocide should stay locked to the UN Convention's narrow physical-destruction standard, or if we should revive Lemkin's broader vision that also covered cultural and social destruction. One side says broadening it makes the term legally useless and lets powerful states dodge obligations; the other says the narrow version was basically designed to protect colonial powers and hides tons of real violence under the rug.

My working theory is that the stalemate isn't really about law or history at all, but about whether calling something genocide functions as an 'ontological veto' -- once you apply the label, it locks the event into a single moral frame and makes hybrid descriptions (like genocide plus economic restructuring) sound like you're downplaying the horror. So maybe the fight over the defintion is actually a fight over who controls the narrative architecture of atrocity. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about how states actually behave?
