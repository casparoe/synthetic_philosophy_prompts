Compare the standard consequentialist cluelessness objection — that agents just can't forecast well enough to rank outcomes — with what I'll call "structural cluelessness": the claim that even with perfect local knowledge, long-run social causal graphs are either non-computable or so chaotically sensitive that no stable ordinal ranking of actions exists over long horizons. Don't just say they're both about not knowing stuff; actually figure out whether structural cluelessness collapses into ordinary epistemic cluelessness, or whether it attacks expected-utility consequentialism in a different place — maybe by questioning whether there's even a coherent utility ordering to maximize, rather than just doubting our access to probabilities. Seperate what's pretty settled in the moral-philosophy literature on cluelessness from what's genuinely up for grabs, and don't present a contested reading as if it's the consensus view.
