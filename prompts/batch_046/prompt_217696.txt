As a retired physicist who has recently begun studying philosophy in earnest, I have been working through Rorty's antirepresentationalism and his broader neopragmatist program. I think I grasp the central claim—that knowledge should be understood not as the accurate mental or linguistic representation of a mind-independent reality, but as a form of practical coping with one's environment, justified by social norms rather than correspondence to the world. What I find conceptually unsettled, however, is the precise status of scientific success within this framework. Specifically, I want to understand whether antirepresentationalism forces us to treat the historical succession of now-abandoned but once highly successful theories—such as the caloric theory of heat or the luminiferous ether—as purely instrumental devices whose predictive achievements carry no representational weight whatsoever, or whether there remains conceptual space for saying that these theories captured genuine structural relations in nature even though their ontologies were ultimately rejected.

This question connects, I believe, to the Quine-Duhem thesis and to the problem of underdetermination of theory by evidence. If representationalism is abandoned, does the underdetermination problem simply dissolve, because theory choice becomes an unproblematic matter of pragmatic utility? Or does it become more acute, since without the regulative ideal of getting reality right, there is nothing to constrain the proliferation of empirically equivalent but ontologically divergent theories beyond contingent practical preference? Furthermore, I am concerned about whether Rorty can consistently maintain a distinction between theories that succeed because they stand in appropriate causal interaction with the world and theories that succeed for purely accidental or local reasons, without implicitly reintroducing representationalist commitments through the back door of terms like coping or causal interaction.

Please explain this conceptual issue with care, clarifying whether Rorty's antirepresentationalism genuinely eliminates the need for representational explanations of scientific success or merely relocates them into a pragmatic vocabulary. I ask that the answer be written in American English, and I would prefer that you not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—simply develop the analysis from the concepts themselves.
