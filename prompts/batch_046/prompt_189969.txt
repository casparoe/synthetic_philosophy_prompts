I am a PhD student in philosophy of medicine and science studies, and I sit on a regional working group that reviews proposed amendments to a jurisdiction-specific clinical taxonomy used for mental-health resource allocation and treatment authorization. The group convenes monthly, has roughly forty-five minutes per proposal, and includes psychiatrists, epidemiologists, patient advocates, and one legal officer, but no one with formal training in philosophy of science. I need a reusable decision procedure—something like a structured audit with ordered tests, clear stopping rules, and explicit hand-off points—to evaluate whether a proposed revision to a diagnostic category risks producing harmful looping effects of the kind Ian Hacking describes for interactive kinds, or whether any anticipated reflexivity is benign or even corrective. To make this usable, I want to extend Hacking’s basic framework with two distinctions that are not always kept apart in the literature: first-order looping, in which being classified changes the self-understanding and behavior of those classified; second-order looping, in which clinicians anticipate that change and alter their diagnostic practice in response, thereby destabilizing the evidential basis of the category itself; and reflexive stabilization, in which explicit awareness of looping is built into the classification protocol to correct for drift. The procedure should translate these into questions that a non-philosopher can answer with the evidence available in a forty-five-minute session—prevalence data, clinical interview studies, historical analogues, and stakeholder testimony—while marking precisely where a judgment is irreducibly normative rather than empirical. It should specify, in sequence: what evidence must be present to proceed past each gate; what counts as a sufficient answer; where the procedure terminates with a rejection; where it terminates with conditional approval; and where it must defer to a values committee because the dispute is about whether a form of suffering ought to be medicalized rather than about predictive validity. The procedure should also flag its own failure modes—cases where rapid institutional adoption outpaces the feedback loop, or where patient advocacy groups are structurally excluded from the data that would reveal first-order effects. I want you to apply this audit to three proposals: first, splitting generalized anxiety disorder into somatic-predominant and cognitive-predominant subtypes with separate treatment pathways; second, adding a treatment-resistant specifier to major depressive disorder that requires documentation of two failed pharmacological trials; and third, broadening the criteria for social anxiety disorder to include marked distress specifically triggered by synchronous online interaction, a change I personally favor on normative grounds but which I suspect the procedure will reject or severely restrict because of high reflexivity risk. For each case, run the steps in order, indicate where the answer depends on values rather than facts, and state what the procedure cannot determine—particularly regarding long-term ontological shifts or cross-cultural variability in self-interpretation. Do not provide bibliographies, citation lists, or recommended readings; the deliverable is the operationalized procedure and its three applications, including the case in which it produces an answer I will find unwelcome.
