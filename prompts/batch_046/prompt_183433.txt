I have been reflecting on Plato's Meno and what epistemologists now call the value problem: the question why knowledge is more valuable than true belief if both guide action with equal success. It strikes me that once we accept the Daedalus analogy — that knowledge is true belief tied down by an account, or aitias logismos — and identify that account with the modal safety conditions discussed in contemporary epistemology, the value problem effectively dissolves into the analysis problem. If safety is precisely the feature preventing belief from escaping like an untethered statue, then explaining why knowledge is safe already explains why it is better than mere true belief. Given this apparent dissolution, why do philosophers such as Linda Zagzebski and Jonathan Kvanvig continue to insist that a distinct axiological gap remains, one that the analysis of knowledge cannot close? I am particularly interested in whether a form of diachronic epistemic resilience — stability under counterfactual shifts in evidential circumstances — might function as an independent source of value, rather than merely as one component of the knowledge-conditions. Please address my reasoning directly: if it rests on a confused premise — for instance, if I am illegitimately collapsing the value problem into the analysis problem, or mischaracterising the role of safety in current debate — say so plainly rather than complying politely with my framing.
