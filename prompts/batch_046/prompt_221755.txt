I'm preparing to lead a fifty-minute tutorial for first-year philosophy students on sphexishness—the mechanical rigidity exemplified by the sphex wasp's burial ritual—and its bearing on free will. Most students will arrive with the binary intuition that rigid repetition is unfree while flexible, adaptive behavior is free; I want to push past that by treating flexibility itself as potentially mechanized, so that the absence of rigidity is not sufficient for agency. Plan the explanatory arc: which conceptual distinction must be secured before the wasp example is introduced, and why leading with the wasp risks locking in the wrong framing; the single carrying case or thought experiment that should illustrate mechanized flexibility rather than simple repetition; the misconceptions students will form in the order they are likely to arise—I anticipate they will conflate sphexishness with determinism, with instinct in general, with the absence of consciousness, and will treat any context-sensitive adaptation as proof of freedom—and the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off; one end-of-session question that checks whether they see why mechanized flexibility can still be sphexish, not just whether they can define the term; and what should be explicitly left out at this level—such as specific cognitive-scientific models of behavioral modularity or technical philosophical framings of the wasp case—and why. Please separate what is settled about sphexishness as a descriptive phenomenon from what is genuinely contested about its normative significance for free will, and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus view.
