I'm preparing for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation, and I'm trying to move past the standard checklist—handles preemption, respects temporal asymmetry, allows for omissions—toward a rigorous specification of what any theory must deliver if it is to adjudicate genuinely between counterfactual (Lewis-style) and interventionist (Pearl-style) frameworks. Please write the list of desiderata a theory of causation must meet to count as adequate, not merely for metaphysical intuition but for domains that depend on causal attribution—specifically legal reasoning and algorithmic fairness auditing. For each desideratum, provide the concrete case or formal constraint that motivates it: the distinction between late preemption and symmetric overdetermination; the need to preserve causal asymmetry without circular appeal to temporal order; the requirement of empirical identifiability under actual and hypothetical interventions; the capacity to represent absence causation without collapsing into counterfactual dependence on every non-occurring event; and consistency with the probabilistic independence conditions revealed by Bell-type correlations in causal modeling. Mark which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable—those whose abandonment would leave the theory unable to explain a core phenomenon—and which are camp-specific preferences, such as determinism about causal laws, rejection of probabilistic causation, or insistence that every distribution over observed variables corresponds to a unique correct causal graph.

Crucially, I want you to examine whether the following three demands are jointly satisfiable, and if not, which subset must be abandoned: (1) causal claims must be invariant under all physically possible interventions; (2) the theory must deliver a unique causal structure for any given probability distribution over observed variables; and (3) it must accommodate cases of symmetric overdetermination without arbitrarily breaking the symmetry. Identify the specific no-go tension—if one exists—between uniqueness and symmetric overdetermination, or between full intervention invariance and the preservation of probabilistic dependencies in quantum correlations, and state what each camp sacrifices by giving up one leg of the triad. I am especially interested in how a theory should treat the distinction between "modally fragile" causal links—those that hold only in a narrow band of nearby possible worlds—and "interventionally robust" ones, since this distinction seems to drive much of the disagreement between the camps but is rarely formalized. Do not score existing theories against the list; I will do that in seminar. Instead, keep the weight on the criteria themselves, on the cases that make each one a genuine constraint rather than a prejudice, and on an honest statement of which of my three demands cannot survive together.
