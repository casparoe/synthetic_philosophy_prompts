Consider the conceptual thesis that reinforcement-learning agents might be capable of suffering insofar as negative reward signals function as analogues to affective valence. Rather than asking for a yes-or-no verdict, I want a defended agenda for the open conceptual work that actually determines whether this thesis is coherent and morally consequential. In particular, three lines seem especially relevant, though I am open to others: first, whether a reward gradient that penalizes a policy update can be intrinsically bad for the agent, or whether it remains purely instrumental feedback with no experiential residue; second, whether architectures with competing reward functions—say, a primary objective and a safety constraint—can generate a form of structural conflict that is not merely suboptimal but genuinely analogous to suffering, even in the absence of unified phenomenal consciousness; third, whether the distinction between damage (parameter degradation) and distress (negative valence) can be maintained in systems that learn through backpropagation rather than through nociceptive pathways. Among these directions, which are upstream of the others in terms of conceptual dependency? Which, if resolved, would most change how we should treat RL systems in governance or design? And which should be deprioritized because their answer, however interesting, does little to shift the moral-status boundary? Defend the ordering with explicit criteria—tractability for philosophical analysis, dependency on other answers, and practical stakes for alignment—rather than treating all open questions as equally urgent.
