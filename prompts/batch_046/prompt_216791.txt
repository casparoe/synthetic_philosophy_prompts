I'm advising a small interdisciplinary research group with two years of funding and no empirical survey capacity; they can do conceptual clarification and normative framing, not large-N data collection. Their domain is democratic backsliding, specifically what counts as backsliding, who has standing to decide that a regime has crossed the line, and whether the concept is now doing anything more than serving as a partisan label — with Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States as the recurring reference cases.

To keep the work from collapsing into a generic literature review, I want them to operate within an analytically injected three-dimensional framework rather than standard binary classifications. Dimension A is institutional capture: formal rules — constitutional amendments, court-packing, electoral law — are rewritten to concentrate executive or partisan power. Dimension B is competitive erosion: elections remain technically open and opposition can win local or occasional national contests, but the playing field is systematically tilted through media capture, patronage, or selective enforcement. Dimension C, which I am calling discursive gatekeeping, is the narrowing of legitimate democratic participation not by statute but by sustained delegitimization of opposition voices, civil-society organizations, and institutional checks; here the boundary of "the democratic people" itself is redrawn through rhetoric and social sanction rather than legal exclusion. These dimensions may overlap empirically, but I want the group to treat them as conceptually separable for the purpose of prioritization.

Given this framing, which open conceptual questions should the group prioritize, and in what order? I want a defended agenda, not a survey. Specifically, address the following, but rank them and explain the ranking using explicit criteria: tractability for philosophers and normative theorists rather than empirical social scientists; dependency — which questions must be settled before others become answerable; and practical importance relative to the group's actual deliverable, which is to change how analysts classify regimes rather than to produce another dataset.

The candidate questions are: (1) Is there a non-circular criterion for each dimension that distinguishes backsliding from ordinary but intense democratic politics — for instance, can competitive erosion be identified without presupposing that the tilting is illegitimate? (2) Who decides whether a regime has crossed the threshold — domestic constitutional courts, international bodies, domestic opposition parties, or academic analysts — and does the identity of the decision-maker change whether the classification is descriptive, normative, or both? (3) Does the concept of democratic backsliding carry independent explanatory weight, or is it reducible to disagreements over policy outcomes masked as procedural concerns? (4) How should the framework handle cases where dimensions move in opposite directions — for example, formal rules remain intact (low A) while discursive gatekeeping intensifies (high C) — and does such divergence undermine the usefulness of a unified concept?

For each, explain what resolving it would change about how we understand Hungary, Poland, Turkey, or the US. Also address trade-offs: if resolving the decision-maker problem (2) is upstream of (1) and (3) but is far less tractable for a two-year conceptual group, should it still be first? If discursive gatekeeping (C) is the least studied dimension but the hardest to operationalize without collapsing into subjective judgment, should it be prioritized for its novelty or deprioritized for its intractability?

One final instruction: if this request rests on a confused premise — for example, if these three dimensions cannot be separated in practice and treating them separately produces artificial distinctions; if asking for a prioritized research agenda without specifying whose values should govern "importance" is incoherent; or if conceptual clarification alone cannot settle what are fundamentally empirical and contested judgments about specific regimes — please say so plainly instead of constructing an artificial ordering. I want direct pushback if the task as posed is ill-formed.
