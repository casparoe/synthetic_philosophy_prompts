I'm writing a feature on the ethics of invertebrate consciousness, and I've been discussing the scale argument with Dr. Lena Vasquez, a philosopher of biology whose work on moral ontology I've followed for years. In our conversation, she offered consistent verdicts on several boundary cases: she holds that chimpanzees, crows, and octopuses possess genuine sentience and warrant direct moral consideration; she denies that zebrafish, given their neural organization, are conscious subjects; she rejects individual worker ants as sentient; and she also denies that a silicon-based system running an isomorphic global-workspace architecture—without any evolutionary neural substrate—could be conscious, regardless of its behavioral outputs. These judgments seem compatible with at least two different frameworks. On one reading, she is an architectural essentialist: consciousness requires specific evolutionary-derived neural structures, and neither substrate-independent replication nor numerical aggregation can substitute for their absence. On another reading, she operates with a precautionary probabilistic calculus, assigning near-zero but non-vanishing prior probabilities to entities that lack those structures, such that vast populations might generate substantial aggregate moral risk—even if synthetic systems, lacking evolutionary history entirely, fall outside the probability distribution altogether.

Next week I plan to ask her about insects specifically. Given current estimates that roughly ten quintillion individual insects are alive at any moment, and assuming a conservative 0.001 percent chance that any given insect has a minimal conscious experience, the expected number of sentient insects dwarfs that of all protected vertebrates combined. Would she say that this scale transforms our moral obligations toward insects as a class—perhaps warranting precautionary restrictions on broad-spectrum insecticides—or would she maintain that without the requisite neural architecture in each individual, aggregation is morally irrelevant?
