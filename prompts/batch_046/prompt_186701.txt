I am a high school student writing an essay on whether compatriots have stronger moral claims than strangers. I have reached firm judgments about several concrete situations, but I am unsure whether they rest on one coherent principle or whether I am simply being inconsistent across contexts. Please help me identify the principle, or small set of competing principles, that best captures my verdicts; apply each to cases I have not mentioned; and tell me honestly which of my judgments must be dropped if coherence is to be preserved. Please respond in American English.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Direct rescue: After an earthquake, two people are trapped in a collapsed building—one is my fellow citizen and one is a foreign tourist. I can save only one. I should save my compatriot; the shared political bond gives him a stronger claim.

2. Public spending: My government must choose between funding a domestic literacy program and contributing an equal sum to an international famine-relief fund. It should fund the domestic program. A state owes special obligations to its own citizens.

3. Personal charity: I have five hundred dollars. A local charity can prevent one family from being evicted; an international charity can save a life for the same cost. I should send the money abroad. In private giving, need alone should decide.

4. Business hiring: A factory in my town can hire either a qualified local applicant or an equally qualified applicant from overseas, who would then send remittances home. I think the factory should hire locally. A firm owes loyalty to the community that sustains it.

5. Criminal justice: On a mock jury, the evidence clearly points to a compatriot defendant, while a foreign co-defendant is innocent. I should vote to convict on evidence alone; favoring my compatriot would be a miscarriage of justice.

6. Immigration policy: My country considers admitting refugees whose lives are at risk, even though this might slightly raise unemployment among native-born workers. I think we should admit them. The state should not prioritize citizens' minor economic advantages over foreigners' survival.

Taken together, these feel unstable: I defend special claims in rescue, state budgets, and hiring, yet reject them in private charity, justice, and immigration. Is there a precise principle—perhaps distinguishing institutional roles, proximity, or the kind of good at stake—that can accommodate all six? If not, which verdict or verdicts should I abandon?

Please also apply your best principle to two further cases I have not considered: (a) a physician in a disaster zone who must choose between treating a compatriot and a foreigner with identical injuries, and (b) a university that can admit either a slightly less qualified domestic student or a highly qualified foreign student, where admission is partly funded by domestic taxpayers. State each candidate principle precisely, indicate which clause explains each of my six judgments, show its verdicts on cases (a) and (b), and be explicit about which of my original judgments it cannot save.
