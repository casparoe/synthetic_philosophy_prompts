The cross-cultural moral psychology literature—where WEIRD populations weight harm and fairness far more heavily than loyalty, authority, and purity, while many non-WEIRD groups treat all five foundations as co-equal—has been recruited for decades into debates about moral objectivity. Realists treat the divergence as superficial variation over shared deep norms; anti-realists treat it as proof that morality is constructed locally all the way down. Yet the exchange feels stalled: both sides accept the same descriptive findings, both invoke the same illustrative cases, and neither updates. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not another round of realism versus anti-realism. Is the disagreement substantive—about whether one weighting is genuinely correct—or verbal, turning on competing definitions of “objectivity” that conflate mind-independence with universal explicit assent? Or are the sides answering different questions—one ontological, about whether moral properties exist independently of minds; one epistemic, about whether disagreement indicates error—under a single heading? To test this, import a distinction rarely made explicit here: separate “moral calibration,” the local tuning of judgment to coordination and signaling problems, from “moral ontology,” the question of whether normative truths depend on minds at all. Does this dissolve the stall, relocate it onto an empirical question about whether calibration mechanisms are domain-general or culture-specific, or reveal that both camps have been answering different questions? Give a committed answer: defend one diagnosis with reference to what each side actually argues, specify what kind of disagreement this really is, and state exactly what would count as progress—whether a conceptual clarification, an empirical finding about calibration, or a reframing of the original question. Do not survey all positions neutrally; take a definite stance, and flag where your diagnosis depends on a contested premise about how descriptive psychology relates to normative philosophy.
