hey, i've been chewing on an essay idea about biopics and the dead and wanted to run it past someone who actually reads this stuff. basic premise: everyone obsesses over factual accuracy in historical drama — did she really say that, was the costume right, etc. but i think thats a red herring. the deeper issue is what i'm calling 'conceptual anachronism': when we dramatise dead people using our modern psychological and moral categories, we dont just get details wrong, we misrepresent the kind of agents they were.

for example, imagine a film about a 16th-century alchemist. if you portray him as 'pursuing career advancement through risky R&D', you've imported a whole framework of capitalist rationality that wont have made sense to him. he might have understood his work as purification of the soul and matter together. the harm isnt the invented dialogue; its that you've made him intelligible to us by stripping away the conceptual world he actually inhabited.

so my argument is that what we owe the dead is not factual fidelity but something like 'epistemic strangeness preservation' — we should try to keep their actions strange to modern audiences, even if that hurts narrative coherence. artists should resist the urge to translate historical figures into modern emotional templates.

does this distinction between factual error and conceptual anachronism actually hold water philosophically? and is my standard unrealistic for commercial filmmaking, or does it name a genuine moral constraint? please reply in British English — my editor wants that voice throughout.
