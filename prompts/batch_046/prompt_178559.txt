I am a doctoral student in affective neuroscience working within an active-inference framework for interoception. I am trying to see whether the philosophical debate over pain asymbolia and the necessary badness of pain can be rendered faithfully in the vocabulary I use daily—hierarchical predictive coding of nociceptive signals, precision-weighted interoceptive prediction errors, allostatic set-points, affective priors, and the minimization of expected free energy—without importing philosophical commitments that lack any mechanistic counterpart.

Please restate, as precisely as possible, the three principal philosophical positions on this matter. First, the representationalist claim that pain is necessarily bad in virtue of representing bodily damage. Second, the affective-essentialist claim that pain is necessarily bad because of an intrinsic negative affective valence that can be dissociated from its sensory-discriminative content, as suggested by cases of pain asymbolia. Third, the desire-based or evaluative claim that pain is only contingently bad, being bad solely in virtue of being the object of an intrinsic or learned aversion.

For each position, translate its core thesis into interoceptive predictive-coding terms: specify what the prior belief, the prediction error, the precision weighting, and the allostatic regulatory response would have to be for the philosophical claim to hold. Then provide a residue report. Indicate which parts of the philosophical claim map cleanly onto this framework, which parts survive only with distortion or ad hoc supplementation—such as the introduction of normative, intentional, or second-order evaluative content that the framework does not natively contain—and which parts have no expressible counterpart whatsoever.

Finally, assess whether the residue points to a genuine limitation of the predictive-processing vocabulary—perhaps its inability to accommodate normative evaluation or dissociable affective valence—or whether it reveals that the original philosophical distinctions were less substantive than they appeared, collapsing once the mechanistic details of interoceptive inference are specified. I do not require citations, bibliographies, or references to particular texts; my interest is exclusively in the conceptual translation and in what fails to survive it.
