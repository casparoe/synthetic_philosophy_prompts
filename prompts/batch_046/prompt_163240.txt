Following a recent parliamentary debate in my country about whether the state ought formally to apologise for colonial-era atrocities committed more than a century ago, I have come to defend a particular position on collective moral guilt across generations. I hold that a nation can genuinely be guilty—not merely that its present citizens feel guilty—provided three conditions are met: first, that the state persists as a continuous institutional agent with an unbroken legal and political identity; second, that the original wrong remains structurally embedded in present institutions or distributions rather than being a closed historical episode; and third, that the collective identity which authorised or executed the harm has not undergone a genuine institutional metamorphosis that severs moral continuity. On this view, present citizens are not blamed for past acts in the way an individual is blamed for their own hand; rather, the enduring corporate body inherits an unrepaired moral debt—what I am calling diachronic corporate liability—because the harm was done in its name and its benefits or structural effects persist. I am aware this borrows from, but is not identical to, the taxonomy of guilt associated with Karl Jaspers, yet I want to keep the claim precise and insulated from analogies to individual psychology.

What I need is a map of this position’s exposure: not the standard objections I have already encountered, but the defeaters that are genuinely decisive, those that are merely awkward, and those that a committed defender should concede without surrendering the core view. Specifically, I want you to distinguish observations, conceptual arguments, formal results about collective agency, and empirical developments in historiography or institutional theory that would count against it. Which of these would force me to abandon the claim, which would require only qualification, and which would defenders rightly dismiss as resting on a false analogy to individual culpability? I also want you to consider whether the view risks being over-insulated—whether its conditions for continuity and metamorphosis are so flexible that no evidence could ever dislodge it, and whether that insulation is intellectually respectable or a vice.
