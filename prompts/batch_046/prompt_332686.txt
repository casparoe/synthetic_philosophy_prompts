my firm's new risk manual has two rules that feel wierd together. first, you're liable for "counterfactual harm" — if your action would have caused damage in some nearby possible world (say, if the backup hadn't kicked in), you're penalized even when actual harm was zero. second, there's an XOR disclosure clause for security bugs: auditors must report either a real vulnerability or a false alarm, but they don't know which they hold, and they face the same penalty for silence regardless. what theory of moral responsability does the first rule presuppose? does it treat counterfactual outcomes as real enough to ground blame? and what view of knowledge or disclosure does the XOR clause assume — that ignorance should always be resolved by reporting? are these commitments consistent, or is the policy philosophically underdetermined?
