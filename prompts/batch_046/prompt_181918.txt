Consider the institutional practice in large-language-model evaluation where benchmark tasks award full credit only for answers accompanied by explicit step-by-step derivations in a prescribed symbolic format, while correct answers produced through direct retrieval or pattern completion receive reduced or zero credit—even when the output is factually accurate. At the same time, human baselines in these same benchmarks are graded solely on answer correctness, with no penalty for intuitive or unarticulated reasoning. What philosophical theory of understanding, explanation, or cognitive achievement does this asymmetry presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view—distinguishing, for instance, whether it treats transparent derivational structure as constitutive of understanding or merely as an instrumental check against error—state whether the practice genuinely requires that view or remains compatible with rival accounts (such as a uniform predictive-accuracy standard or a functionalist view of reasoning), and specify what the evaluation protocol would need to change if the presupposed theory were abandoned in favor of one that made no distinction between articulated and unarticulated correct cognition.
