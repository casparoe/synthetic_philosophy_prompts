I am drafting a novel in which the narrator gradually discovers that what she takes to be the material world is in fact a collectively sustained pattern of perceptions, and I want to ensure that the metaphysical framework I am using does not collapse under its own weight before I commit the plot to it. For the philosophical scaffolding, I have settled on the following commitments: (1) I accept Berkeley's core claim that the being of sensible objects consists in being perceived, though I restrict its scope to spatiotemporally located items, leaving mental acts and abstract relations outside its reach; (2) I reject the master argument in its traditional formulation, on the grounds that it equivocates between conceiving an object without a present perceiver and establishing that such an object can exist unperceived, but I believe this flaw is correctable without surrendering immaterialism; (3) I treat fictional entities—characters, narrated places—as existing in precisely the same ontological mode as tables and trees, namely as stable intersubjective idea-complexes sustained by shared attention rather than by purely private mental acts; (4) I maintain that a structurally parallel master argument applies successfully to fictional entities and demonstrates their unreality, because in their case no appeal to divine or communal perception can bridge the gap between being conceived and being actually perceived; (5) I deny any metaphysically deep distinction between primary and secondary qualities, treating both as equally real and equally mind-dependent, without extending this egalitarian treatment to non-sensible entities. Before I build narrative consequences on this framework, I need an audit of coherence: which pairs are genuinely incompatible, which only appear so, and what is the least costly revision? Specifically, does my admission in (2) that Berkeley's argument contains a correctable equivocation undermine my deployment in (4) of an allegedly successful parallel? Does (3)'s claim that fictions are real idea-complexes conflict with (4)'s claim that a parallel argument shows them to be unreal, or does that tension dissolve once we clarify whether communal attention counts as a form of perception? Does (1)'s restriction of being-perceived to spatiotemporal objects sit uneasily with (5)'s egalitarian treatment of qualities, given that spatial extension might seem to fall outside the restricted scope? And does (3) risk collapsing my view into a universal fictionalism that renders (1)'s restriction arbitrary? Please rank the tensions by severity, indicate for each whether resolving it requires abandoning a commitment or merely refining its scope, and tell me whether a single adjustment—say, revising (1) or weakening (4)—would restore consistency, or whether the framework requires more systematic reconstruction. I am not seeking references, citations, or a reading list; I want to know whether this particular package hangs together.
