As a novelist drafting a narrative about cognitive augmentation, I keep returning to Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon, not because it answers my questions but because it seems to resist the standard philosophical framing in ways I cannot quite articulate. I want to read the novel through the therapy/enhancement distinction, yet I want to avoid the usual line-drawing exercise—species-typical functioning, statistical norms, or the restoration of prior capacities. Instead, I want to press what I will call the "baseline asymmetry": the moment an intervention establishes a new functional state, that state becomes the reference point from which all subsequent changes are judged therapeutic or harmful. Charlie Gordon begins far below typical cognitive functioning, is enhanced to genius-level capacity, and then regresses. The novel treats this regression as a tragic loss and seems to treat the pre-surgical Charlie as the authentic protagonist to whom loyalty is owed. But if we take the enhanced Charlie—who writes with syntactic complexity, emotional nuance, and aesthetic judgment—as the legitimate baseline, then the surgical reversal is not a therapeutic restoration but an iatrogenic injury: a deliberate, engineered reduction of a person to a less capable state. Read the novel's formal architecture—the progress reports with their degrading and then recovering spelling, syntax, and emotional register—not merely as illustration of Charlie's mind but as an implicit argument about narrative ownership. Which version of Charlie controls the story? Where does the text support the view that the enhanced self is an impostor, parasite, or corruption of the "real" Charlie? Where does it inadvertently romanticize the original Charlie precisely because his vulnerability invites pity rather than because he possesses a deeper authenticity? Consider especially the closing movement, in which Charlie asks that flowers be placed on Algernon's grave and contemplates returning to institutional care: does this gesture belong to the enhanced narrator's refined aesthetic sensibility, or to the pre-enhancement child's simpler attachments, and why does that ambiguity matter for whether regression can be called therapy? I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the bioethics literature; I want a critical reading that takes both Keyes's narrative craft and the conceptual asymmetry seriously, and that tells me whether the novel ultimately defends the idea that there is an authentic self that enhancement corrupts, or whether it accidentally dramatizes that the very notion of an authentic baseline is a fiction we construct to avoid facing the moral horror of engineered decline. If the novel adds something to the philosophical debate that pure argument cannot, I want to know what it is—whether in the texture of a particular scene, the arc of a character's choice, or the silence where the text refuses to judge.
