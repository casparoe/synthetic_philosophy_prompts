Two friends of mine, Jordan and Riley, argued over lunch about pure time prefernce — whether it's rational to discout future goods just because they're farther away in time.

Jordan went first: if you reject a soul and think personal identity is just psychological continuity, then your future self is basically a different person you're somewhat connected to. So caring less about future welfare isn't irrational; it's like caring more about your kid than a random stranger. Weighting by relation is perfectly normal.

Riley replied with an intertemporal veil idea: imagine you don't know which slice of your life you're currently living, and you have to pick one discount rate for all your stages. You'd obviously choose flat, equal weights. So if you discount now, you're cheating — you know you're the present slice and you're rigging the rules in your own favor.

Jordan said that's incoherent — there's no viewpoint outside of time to stand behind a veil, and we already weight by spatial proximity without anyone calling that irrational. Plus saving for retirement isn't charity; it's more like a treaty between present-me and future-me, where the terms can include unequal burdens without being unfair.

Riley countered that spatial bias is just a bias, not something rational, and that if future-you is basically a stranger then saving for retirement really is altruism. There's no reason not to blow all your savings now except sentiment. And calling it a "treaty" just renames the problem: why should present-you agree to terms that hurt them for the sake of a stranger?

Jordan: because long-term projects need cooperation, but cooperation doesn't require equal weights. The mistake is assuming rationality demands flat neutrality; it only demands consistency with your values. You can consistently value what's closer in time, just like closer in space.

Riley: then "consistent with your values" collapses into "whatever you feel like," and you've lost the normative bite of saying pure time preference is irrational. You started trying to justify it, but now you're just describing it.

Please adjudicate this move by move. Rate Jordan's identity argument, Riley's veil, Jordan's incoherence/spatial reply, the treaty analogy, Riley's altruism objection, and Jordan's final consistency defense — for each say if it's clearly wrong, mostly right, or in between, with a brief reason. Then say what both are missing, especially whether the treaty analogy does real work or just pushes the question back one step.
