As a retired epidemiologist who spent a career modeling cascade dynamics in public-health systems and has only recently begun formal study in moral and political philosophy, I ask you to draft a polished, formal research proposal of approximately 1,500 words, suitable for a one-semester independent-study fellowship in a philosophy department.

The central question is this. Amartya Sen's entitlement framework analyzes the famines of Bengal in 1943, Ireland in the mid-nineteenth century, and China's Great Leap Forward not as simple food shortages but as collapses in the distribution of access, and on that basis treats them as political failures rather than natural disasters. Yet the framework does not clearly distinguish two causal architectures familiar from systems epidemiology: direct agent-induced deprivation, in which specific policies or actions remove access; and systemic amplification of an exogenous shock, in which a drought, blight, or harvest failure cascades through a vulnerable distribution architecture into mass starvation. If we apply a taxonomy drawn from reliability engineering—distinguishing root trigger from structural cascade—to these three cases, do we split Sen's unified moral category into conceptually distinct wrongs, namely intentional entitlement denial versus negligent system design? Or does the distinction collapse upon philosophical analysis, leaving the normative judgment unchanged? The project asks whether this architectural distinction has genuine moral significance or merely redescribes causal complexity.

The proposal should be structured as a single continuous document, beginning immediately with no preface, no restatement of this prompt, and no bibliography. It must include: (1) a precise statement of the question and an explanation of why existing philosophical treatments of Sen leave it open; (2) the conceptual method—specifically, mapping entitlement collapse onto a trigger-versus-architecture taxonomy, and defending a philosophical rather than historical approach to testing whether that taxonomy alters moral evaluation; (3) the anticipated deliverable, describing both a positive result (the taxonomy holds and sharpens moral judgment) and a failure condition (the taxonomy collapses or proves morally inert), together with an honest assessment of what would make the project unmanageable within one semester; and (4) a brief concluding reflexive note in which you state how confident you are in the central analogy between epidemiological cascade models and entitlement failure, and you explicitly identify the specific claim—whether the engineering analogy genuinely illuminates moral responsibility or only complicates causal description—about which you are least certain.

Please write in a formal, polished register appropriate to a scientist entering philosophical argument, avoid inventing specific citations or page references, and present the proposal text itself from the first sentence onward.
