I've been trying to work out a pretty hard-line version of anatta for a seminar project I'm not actually finishing, and I keep running into the same wall. The view I have in mind isn't just the old there-is-no-permanent-soul line — that's old news. It's stronger: there isn't even a psychological-continuity or narrative-self replacement doing the work. Instead, what's happening is a chain of causally adjacent but metaphysically discreet consciousness-moments, each inheritence karmic traces and sensory content from the prior one, but with no literal continuation of anything. Like a flame being passed, except there's no flame-substance at all, just discrete flashes linked by causation. I think this avoids the usual reductionist complaints because it doesn't try to build a person out of parts; it just denies there's anything being built.

What would actually count against this? I don't want the standard objections — fission, memory gaps, etc. — unless they specifically hit the moment-to-moment causation claim rather than psychological continuity. I'm more interested in whether neuroscience, phenomenology, or even formal work on persistence gives us reasons to think causation without continuity is incoherent, or that consciousness can't be that discrete without breaking intentionality. Which defeaters would be fatal versus merely awkward? And what would someone holding this view themselves concede as damaging — maybe something about the first-person feel of duration, or about moral responsibility across moments? I'd also like you to be honest about whether this position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every apparent continuity can just be redescribed as inherited traces, is that a problem? Please say how confident you are in your assessment and single out the point you're least sure of — probably whether Abhidharma actually endorses this discrete-moment reading or if I'm misreading them. I was discussing this with my roommate last night and we couldn't agree on whether intentionality could survive that kind of discontinuity.
