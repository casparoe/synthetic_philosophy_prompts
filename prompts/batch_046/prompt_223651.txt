Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have debated for years how to morally judge figures such as Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus, specifically through and against the man-of-his-time defence.

The first camp, the Accessibility-Threshold Contextualists, defend a refined version of that defence. They hold that retrospective moral culpability requires not only that a modern observer recognizes a wrong, but that the wrongness and a feasible alternative were within what they call the agent’s institutional-cognitive accessibility horizon: the set of moral reasons that could have been activated given the agent’s social position, education, and the discursive practices available within their institutional environment.

The second camp, the Structural-Resilience Critics, reject this defence. They concede that anachronistic judgment is unfair, but propose that evaluation should be structure-relative rather than agent-relative. They ask whether the figure’s actions strengthened or weakened the institutional mechanisms that determined whose suffering was visible and whose was systematically obscured. They term this the counterfactual institutional resilience test: a figure is judged not by what they personally could have conceived, but by whether their choices made it more or less likely that the very moral knowledge the Contextualists prize would eventually become accessible.

Write the document both sides could sign. Include the following, in formal, precise language:

- A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover the basic biographical and historical facts about Jefferson (his authorship of egalitarian political texts alongside his ownership of enslaved people and his racial theories), Churchill (his wartime leadership alongside his imperial policies and his government’s response to famine in Bengal), and Columbus (the 1492 voyage and its immediate aftermath, including the demographic collapse of Indigenous populations in the Caribbean). Also include agreed descriptions of the standard man-of-his-time defence and of the limits of both camps’ frameworks.

- A disagreements table with two columns, each phrased in wording that the respective camp would accept for its own view. The table should identify exactly where the camps part ways—particularly on whether moral evaluation should center on the agent’s accessibility horizon or on institutional resilience, and on whether reinforcing an unjust structure is a distinct moral failure even when the agent’s awareness was limited.

- At least three cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect two central ones are: (i) whether an agent can be excused for failing to perceive a wrong that was structurally invisible yet not logically impossible for someone in their position to perceive; and (ii) whether there is a meaningful moral distinction between actively constructing an oppressive institution and passively benefiting from one that one did not design.

- For each crux, specify a concrete historical case or piece of evidence both sides accept as bearing on it—such as Jefferson’s documented exposure to abolitionist arguments within his correspondence networks, or the availability to Churchill’s administration of reports describing famine conditions—and record each side’s explicit prediction about what that case demonstrates.

- A brief concluding section identifying whether any portion of their dispute is merely verbal rather than substantive, with an explanation of why.

Present the agreed claims as numbered items, and format the disagreements as a clear table. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific scholarly works. Keep the prompt self-contained so that answering it requires reasoning rather than retrieval of obscure literature.
