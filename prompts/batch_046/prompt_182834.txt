I'm running a single fifty-minute seminar for second-year philosophy undergraduates who have just encountered the hard problem of consciousness through popular writing and arrive convinced that 'raw feels' — intrinsic, non-relational, ineffable qualitative properties — are the obvious datum that any theory must accommodate. I want to teach them the argument that such qualia are not merely elusive but conceptually incoherent, the move Dennett termed 'quining' and that Chase and Sanborn develop into a direct challenge to the coherence of the posit itself. I do not want the explanation delivered to me; I want a teaching plan.

Specify the dependency order in which the components must be introduced so that the conclusion reads as an analysis of the concept rather than a denial of conscious experience. Identify the one concrete case that should carry the session — I am considering the contrast between phantom-limb pain and phantom smell (phantosmia) in patients with destroyed olfactory receptors, but tell me whether that pairing works, what it must include and what it must lack to avoid collapsing into a standard argument about neural representation, and whether there is a better non-standard example. Map the misconceptions the students will bring or form in sequence — for instance, that this is covert behaviourism, that denying intrinsic qualia is denying consciousness outright, or that 'ineffable' merely means 'difficult to articulate in language' — and state exactly where in the arc each should be intercepted. Propose one check I can use at the end to determine whether they have grasped the incoherence claim rather than merely feeling uneasy about it. State explicitly what standard material — such as the inverted-spectrum argument or Mary's room — I should refuse to explain at this level and say so.

Please write in British English. Be self-critical about the pedagogical architecture: flag any step that depends on a contested assumption about how conceptual analysis proceeds or about the relation between first-person report and third-person description, and be explicit about your confidence in each recommendation. If the premise is confused — for example, if the phantom-limb/phantosmia pairing undermines the very distinction I am trying to teach, or if one cannot coherently teach that a concept is incoherent without first supplying a positive account of consciousness — say so plainly rather than complying politely.
