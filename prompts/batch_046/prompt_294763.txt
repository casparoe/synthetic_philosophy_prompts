In a recent workshop on computational historiography, a speaker defended an algorithmically assembled chronology of nineteenth-century diplomatic cables by invoking Leopold von Ranke's well-known dictum that history should show how things actually happened—arguing that because the algorithm applies no human narrative framing, it achieves precisely the kind of unmediated objectivity Ranke demanded, and therefore escapes the scepticism about narrative construction raised by E. H. Carr and Hayden White. I am not convinced that the slogan supports that conclusion, but I want to understand exactly what it might claim. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses that Ranke's phrase could express—distinguishing, for example, between a methodological prescription about privileging primary sources over secondary synthesis, an epistemic claim that the past contains recoverable facts independent of the historian's present, a metaphysical claim about the ontological independence of past events from the narratives we impose, and any other plausible reading—and for each say whether it is trivial, whether it is defensible, and whether it actually supports the speaker's argument that automated aggregation avoids Carr's charge that history is an act of interpretation or White's claim that narrative form is constitutive of historical meaning? In particular, address whether any defensible reading of Ranke's position survives Carr's and White's critiques intact, or whether the algorithmic case simply smuggles in an unexamined narrative choice at the level of data selection and categorization.
