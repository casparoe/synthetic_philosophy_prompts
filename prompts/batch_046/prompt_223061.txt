I'm delivering the retirement toast for my mentor, Dr. Elena Vasquez, who taught philosophy of mind for thirty-five years and, in parallel, rebuilt antique clock escapements — a craft she never wrote about. Last month I tried to repair a broken escapement after studying every manual I could find; I grasped the mechanics propositionally and still ruined the part. That failure made her longstanding distinction between knowing how and knowing that personally urgent: her expertise was not an archive I could download but an adaptive responsiveness that lives in the hands and corrects for errors no manual anticipates. The audience is split between philosophers familiar with the Ryle-Stanley debate and family members who are not; the toast must honor her without reducing her teaching to a set of doctrines we can now inherit. Please write it as exactly five movements, each one or two paragraphs long, with no separate introduction or conclusion. Each movement should pair one image from clockwork with one image from her classroom, using my failed repair as a recurring motif. Because knowing-how, on her view, involves counterfactual recovery rather than propositional storage, the speech must avoid claiming that we now possess what she knew; instead it should acknowledge what remains irreducibly present in her and absent in any transcript or lecture note. Keep the tone affectionate and accessible, not academic, and do not include citations, bibliographies, or references to specific texts.
