I have been thinking lately about what Darwin's account of natural selection and Turing's imitation game share, and the thought has become personally unsettling now that I am trying to defend a substantive position in philosophy of mind without feeling that I am merely running an evolved heuristic. Darwin argued that adaptive complexity—eyes, instincts, problem-solving behaviors—arises from a process with no foresight, no grasp of the problem being solved, and no comprehension of the solution; selection produces competence without comprehension, and the appearance of design is an after-effect of cumulative filtering rather than the trace of understanding. Turing inverted the usual order of reasoning about intelligence: instead of asking whether an internal mechanism genuinely understands, he proposed judging by behavioral indistinguishability, so that if a system consistently produces intelligent outputs we have no principled, non-arbitrary ground to deny it intelligence, regardless of whether anything inside comprehends. Putting these two inversions together, I have constructed an argument that seems to undermine the authority of philosophical reasoning itself.

(1) If a process can generate adaptive, problem-solving behavior without any internal comprehension of the problems or solutions, then comprehension is not a necessary condition for successful reasoning.

(2) Darwin's mechanism of natural selection demonstrates exactly this for biological adaptation: it solves design problems without understanding them.

(3) Turing's criterion shows that intelligent-seeming outputs do not require internal comprehension; competence can exist as a surface phenomenon detached from any grasp of meaning.

(4) Human philosophical reasoning is itself an evolved, adaptive process that produces coherent arguments, survives critical selection, and adjusts to objections, yet cognitive science suggests it operates through heuristics, biases, and neural mechanisms to which the reasoner has no transparent first-person access.

(5) Therefore human philosophical reasoning is best understood as a case of competence without comprehension: it produces correct-seeming or defensible outputs without the reasoner genuinely grasping why they hold, in the same way that a selected trait functions without grasping its function.

(6) But if philosophical reasoning lacks comprehension, then the authority of any philosophical conclusion—including this one—cannot rest on the reasoner's understanding or justified grasp of truth; it is merely the output of a blind evolutionary heuristic that happened to survive criticism.

(7) Hence we ought to reject the authority of all philosophical reasoning, including the reasoning that led us to (5).

This looks like a self-undermining reductio. Where does it go wrong, if it does? I am inclined to suspect the slip lies between (3) and (4), or perhaps between (5) and (6), but I want a precise diagnosis of the exact step, an explanation of why that step seemed plausible given Darwin's and Turing's inversions, and an honest accounting of what one pays if one rejects it—whether that means accepting that philosophical reasoning does involve a special kind of comprehension after all, or accepting that authority does not require comprehension but then explaining what else could ground it. I also want to know whether the argument reveals a genuine tension between Darwinian and Turing-style inversions and the practice of philosophy, or whether it is simply an equivocation on 'comprehension', 'reasoning', or 'authority' that collapses once the terms are disambiguated.
