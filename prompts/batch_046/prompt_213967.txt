I have recently been examining how automated scheduling systems in my own work fall into rigid re-optimization loops that resist restructuring, which led me to revisit Dennett's discussion of the Sphex wasp and its relevance to free will. I am grading an exam that asked students to extend the concept of sphexishness by distinguishing first-order rigidity—executing a fixed behavioral loop regardless of changed conditions—from second-order sphexishness, in which an agent's meta-rules for revising its own procedures are themselves hard-coded and immune to reflection. The question asked whether escaping first-order rigidity is sufficient for moral agency, or whether second-order flexibility is also necessary, and whether a deterministically governed agent could possess second-order flexibility without contradiction.

Here is one student's answer:

"Dennett introduces the Sphex wasp to illustrate first-order sphexishness: the wasp drags its paralyzed prey to a burrow, checks inside, and if the prey has been moved away during the inspection, it returns to drag the prey back in an unaltered loop, ignoring the changed situation. Second-order sphexishness operates at a higher level: it is not rigidity in one's actions, but rigidity in the algorithms one uses to update one's actions—imagine an agent that revises its beliefs every Monday according to a rule it is constitutionally unable to suspend. One might think that flexible behavior at the first order is enough for free will, but Dennett's deeper claim is that genuine agency requires flexibility at both levels; an agent with adaptable actions but a frozen meta-procedure remains mechanistic, merely more complex. Therefore, determinism threatens freedom only if it enforces second-order sphexishness. If a deterministic system's revision rules are themselves causally open to environmental feedback, then second-order flexibility is preserved, and with it full moral responsibility, since the agent can genuinely correct its own correctors."
