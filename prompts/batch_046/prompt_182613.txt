There's this analogy people keep using when they talk about populism and "the will of the people" -- they say it's like a microphone picking up one true song in a noisy bar. The leader is just the mic and the amp, the crowd is the singer, and all the static and interferance is elite interference or media distortion. Turn up the gain, filter the noise, and you hear the melody that was always playing.

I'm trying to audit this properly. Can you lay out what mapts onto what -- is the "song" shared preferences, a collective intention, or just a statistical average? Where does the mic analogy break? For one thing, a microphone can cause feedback that actually changes what the crowd sings next, which seems like it smuggels in a passive leader idea that might not survive. Also, what does the analogy quietly assume about the value of the parts versus the whole -- like, if one person in the crowd is singing off-key, do we care, or is only the blended sound real?

And here's the part that's been bugging me: there's a view in collective intentionality that a group belief isn't just summed individual beliefs, more like a choir isn't just ten people humming near each other. Does importing that idea fix the analogy, or does it destroy the claim that there's one hidden melody to amplify in the first place?

If the mic picture collapses, what's a better analogy for how populist claims about "the people" actually work -- maybe something about aggregation or construction rather than discovery?
