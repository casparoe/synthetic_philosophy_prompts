Two members of a rationalist reading group, neither of whom is me, have been arguing about what revealed preference theory actually commits us to about the meaning of utility. I observed the exchange without taking sides. Please adjudicate it move by move: rate each step from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say what, if anything, both parties overlook. Please write your answer in American English.

(1) The first speaker, call him Alex, claimed that revealed preference renders utility conceptually empty. By defining preference through choice and then explaining choice through preference, Samuelson turned "agents maximise utility" into a tautology. Because any behaviour can be rationalised ex post by constructing an appropriate utility function, the theory explains nothing.

(2) The second speaker, Blake, replied that this ignores the empirical constraints embedded in the theory. Samuelson's Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP) forbids cyclical choices across budget sets. Observed violations — for instance, the decoy effect altering selection between two otherwise identical options — show that preferences are not stable, which is a substantive empirical finding rather than a definitional trick.

(3) Alex conceded that WARP rules out some patterns, but pointed to Afriat's theorem: any finite dataset satisfying the Generalised Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP) can be represented by a continuous, monotone, concave utility function. Therefore "utility" is merely a convenient mathematical encoding of consistent choice behaviour; it denotes no mental state or subjective experience.

(4) Blake answered that representation is not vacuous. Once the utility index is derived, one can generate comparative-statics predictions and conduct welfare analysis. The fact that utility is only ordinal — unique up to monotone transformation — does not erase its content; it merely indicates that utility is an ordering, not a cardinal magnitude like temperature.

(5) Alex then pressed further: in applied work, economists routinely treat utility as if it supports interpersonal comparisons and cardinal measures — computing compensating variation, aggregating consumer surplus, or comparing welfare across policies. Revealed preference, being agent-specific and ordinal, provides no foundation for these moves. So either the theory abandons normative economics, or it secretly relies on an unexamined mentalistic notion of "well-being" that it officially denies. The decoy-effect violations Blake cited do not resolve this; they merely show that choice is noisy, not that there exists a hidden cardinal scale.

Please examine each of the five moves in turn. For each, give a brief rating and justification. Then state who is more right overall, and identify any important consideration that both have missed.
