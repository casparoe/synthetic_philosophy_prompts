I'm writing a short essay on whether rationality ever requires breaking a tie, and I've collected a set of judgments about scenarios involving perfect indifference that I'm struggling to systematize. First, if Buridan's ass is genuinely indifferent between two identical bales of hay and starves rather than picking one arbitrarily, I regard this as a failure of practical rationality: the agent ought to break the symmetry and act, and inaction is the greater error. Second, if an autonomous decision system freezes because two courses of action yield precisely equal expected utility, I see this not as a moral failing but as an architectural defect—yet I also think the correct fix is to build in a deterministic, transparent tie-breaker rather than to randomize, which suggests I hold that even perfect ties should be resolved by a manufactured reason rather than left to chance. Third, in a moral rescue case—say, a rescuer who can save either of two strangers with identical claims and flips a coin—I think the coin flip is permissible but faintly unsatisfying; if the rescuer instead invents a spurious distinction ("this one was standing closer by an imperceptible margin") to justify her choice, that feels like rationalization, yet if she refuses to choose and lets both perish, that seems far worse than either arbitrary selection or fabricated justification. Fourth, I think that when a person is genuinely indifferent between two career paths identical in every respect she cares about, adopting an arbitrary commitment—say, resolving to take the one on the left—is not only rational but admirable, a kind of practical self-constitution rather than a cognitive error. Fifth, however, if a judge assigns a prison sentence by lot between two defendants who are genuinely identical in culpability and circumstances, I feel this is an abuse of authority, not a permissible exercise of discretion, even though the tie is perfect.

The tension should be clear: I want to say that arbitrary resolution is required in some ties and forbidden in others, but the distinction doesn't cleanly track whether the domain is practical, moral, or institutional, and my preference for deterministic tie-breaking in algorithms sits uneasily with my acceptance of coin flips in rescue. I'm unsure whether my judgments rest on a hidden principle—perhaps involving the agent's social role, the cost of inaction, or whether manufactured reasons can be legitimately adopted—or whether they are just inconsistent. So my question is: is there a coherent principle, even one that forces me to revise one of these verdicts, that captures why manufacturing a reason is admirable in career choice, defective in algorithm design (where determinism is preferred over randomness), rationalized in moral rescue, and abusive in judicial sentencing? And whatever principle you propose, what should it say about a case I haven't listed: a democratic voter who is genuinely indifferent between two candidates with identical platforms and voting records, and who resolves her indecision by adopting the rule that she will always vote for the candidate listed first on the ballot?
