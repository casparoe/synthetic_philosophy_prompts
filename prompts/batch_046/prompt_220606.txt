I am drafting a novel set in the quiet aftermath of a conflict that never received official recognition, and in my research I have encountered a social pattern for which I lack the precise vocabulary. In societies that have erected formal architectures of remembrance—memorial museums, compulsory school curricula, state-sanctioned rituals of testimony—I have observed that the survivors' own granular, contradictory recollections do not simply erode with time; rather, they become socially awkward, almost indecorous, because they fail to align with the polished commemorative narrative those institutions propagate. The machinery constructed to fulfill a collective duty to remember appears, paradoxically, to generate a structured silence around the actual texture of lived experience: survivors are not censored, but they are gently excluded from public discourse by the very grandeur and coherence of the official remembrance, leaving them to withdraw their particular stories into private, unshared spaces.

This observation seems adjacent to well-known debates about the ethics of memory—Margalit's defense of a moral duty to remember, and Rieff's argument that institutionalized remembrance can freeze grief into political instrumentality and obstruct genuine reconciliation—but I am not asking for an exposition of those positions. What I seek is whether this specific inversion, in which commemorative obligation produces not testimony but a kind of private withdrawal from it, possesses an established name within the literature on collective memory or transitional justice. Is it merely a variant of state-sponsored amnesia, or does it stand closer to concepts like the instrumentalization of trauma or the politics of memory? If no precise term exists, I want a careful delineation: how the phenomenon differs from ordinary collective myth-making, from the voluntary forgetting Rieff defends, and from standard critiques of memorialization, together with an account of where its boundaries lie and whether commemorative practices could be arranged to honor the past without triggering this particular failure. I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I need the carving itself—what the thing is, what it is not, and at what point remembrance crosses over into this unintended suppression of particularity.
