Next month I am defending, before an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-science seminar, the thesis that Gödel's rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations should be interpreted not as a reductio of eternalism but as a formal demonstration that local Lorentz covariance is incompatible with globally privileged temporal ordering unless one smuggles in a metaphysical commitment that Gödel's own Kantian-idealist philosophy explicitly rejects. Specifically, I argue that the absence of global hyperbolicity and the presence of closed timelike curves in the metric do not establish the physical possibility of time travel; rather, they show that any cosmological model preserving local relativistic structure must either abandon a globally consistent past-future distinction or concede that the apparent flow of time is, in Gödel's terms, an illusion generated by the structure of consciousness rather than a feature of the manifold. The audience includes a mathematical physicist who regards philosophical readings of general relativity as usually overreaching, a historian of twentieth-century physics who is intimately familiar with the Einstein-Gödel correspondence and convinced that Gödel misapplied the field equations by selecting an unphysical energy distribution, two B-theoretic metaphysicians who accept the block universe but reject Kantian anti-realism about temporal becoming, and a graduate student in causal structure who suspects I am conflating coordinate-dependent artifacts with invariant physical features. Prepare me for the objections I will actually face, in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary and framing each audience member would use. For each objection, specify what I should concede up front, what I must hold to keep the thesis alive, and where my reply will be weakest. Identify the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they overlook, and include the single objection I am hoping nobody raises. The response should be in American English, structured as a speaker's prep sheet rather than a general defense of Gödel's philosophy.
