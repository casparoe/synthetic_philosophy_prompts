For a feature I am writing on how cities negotiate monuments and street names, I want a decision tree—not a catalog of named camps, but a sorting instrument that takes a reader from concrete judgments about cases to an actual position. Build it around three branching questions, each phrased as a claim about a specific scenario rather than a philosophical label. First: when a city retains a statue or name tied to a figure now widely condemned, is the object functioning as an honorific expression of present collective esteem, or has it undergone semantic drift into an indexical geographic marker—like a proper name that now points mainly to a location rather than praising its origin? Second, for those who see it as honorific: should the figure be assessed by the moral norms of their own era, or by present norms? Third, for those who apply present norms and find serious moral failure: is the appropriate remedy removal and replacement by a figure tied to the affected community, retention with an explicit counter-plaque that subverts the honorific reading, or retention untouched? The tree should yield at least four leaves—call them the Synchronic Preservationist, the Diachronic Reparative Replacementist, the Subversive Contextualist, and the Indexical Functionalist who holds that drift has dissolved moral content—while mapping all logical combinations and flagging any that are unoccupied, such as applying diachronic judgment yet advocating untouched retention. Diagnose briefly whether each gap is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Phrase every node so a reader can answer without knowing jargon, flag any pair of leaves that converge on the same practical recommendation and therefore cannot be separated by the tree alone, and keep the instrument to no more than eight terminal positions.
