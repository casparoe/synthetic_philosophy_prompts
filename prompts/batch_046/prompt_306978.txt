I am preparing the interview guide for a philosophy podcast episode featuring an unnamed philosopher who has recently defended a variant of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. Rather than treating the argument solely as a causal regress about actions, her version targets what she calls "framework inheritance": the claim that any reason-responsive mechanism or hierarchical identification structure an agent might invoke to stop the regress is itself the product of unchosen socialization and temperamental luck. She maintains that this collapses the standard compatibilist distinction between moral responsibility and mere attributability, and she extends the point to collective omissions—arguing that groups cannot be responsible for failing to act when the group's normative framework for recognizing obligations was never collectively authored.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press well beyond a restatement of Strawson's original regress. I want at least one question each on: (a) whether her extension genuinely undermines Watson's distinction between attributability and accountability, or whether it merely shifts the locus of unchosen causation; (b) how she responds to Frankfurt-style hierarchical identification without conceding that second-order volitions are themselves subject to the same inheritance problem; and (c) what the framework-inheritance claim implies for collective moral responsibility, particularly in cases where institutional norms evolve through uncoordinated historical processes rather than explicit design. For each of these, include a sharp follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give—for instance, if she says that attributability survives because it requires only coherence rather than authorship, ask what coherence could mean if the standards of coherence are themselves inherited; if she invokes social construction to explain collective norms, ask whether that construction is ever genuinely collective or just an aggregation of inherited individual frameworks. The remaining questions should probe where the literature already presses: the charge that the argument proves too much by also undermining aesthetic or epistemic evaluation; the possibility of stopping the regress at a non-causal, rational endorsement; and whether her view allows any meaningful distinction between the responsibility we deny to agents and the responsibility we might still assign to artificial systems whose architectures are explicitly designed. Keep the tone rigorous but accessible to an audience familiar with free will debates but not necessarily with Strawson's specific text.
