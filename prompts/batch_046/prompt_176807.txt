I am a doctoral student in philosophy of action and moral psychology, and over the past semester my reading group has kept returning to a configuration that does not fit neatly into the standard taxonomy of moral luck cases. The pattern involves what I have been calling “mediated structural luck”: an agent performs an action within an institutional or technological system that distributes causal influence across many nodes, so that the outcome depends on emergent properties of the system—say, an algorithmic allocation protocol or a decentralized organizational structure—that no single participant designed or could fully predict. The agent’s contribution is genuinely intentional and not coerced, yet the moral quality of the result (whether it produces harm or benefit) seems to depend on structural features that lie outside any individual’s direct agential control. My colleagues are divided. One camp defends what they call the “direct ownership thesis”: moral responsibility requires that the outcome stand within the agent’s sphere of direct practical control, and institutional mediation that dissolves this link thereby dissolves moral attribution, leaving only causal or collective accountability. The other camp defends “relational constitutive luck”: they hold that moral properties can be genuinely constituted by outcomes and circumstances outside direct control, provided those outcomes stand in the right relational structure to the agent’s practical identity, including identity that extends through institutional roles.

Could you draft the joint statement that these two camps should produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration? I want a document both could sign. Please structure it as follows: first, a numbered section of agreed claims about moral assessment, the distinction between causal and moral responsibility, and the conditions under which outcomes can be attributed to agents; second, a disagreements table in which the direct-ownership view and the relational-constitutive-luck view are each stated in wording that its own proponents would accept, particularly regarding whether structural mediation dissolves moral attribution or transforms it; third, the cruxes—I suspect one concerns whether practical identity can extend across institutional mediators without losing moral ownership, and another concerns whether the direct-ownership thesis implicitly relies on an implausibly atomistic model of agency; and fourth, for each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. The answer should be written in American English. I am not looking for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations—this is for my own conceptual mapping, and I want the carving of the phenomenon and the disagreement to be precise rather than anchored to specific literature.
