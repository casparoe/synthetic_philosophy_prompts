I'm trying to work out my own view on when voting is ethically required, permitted, or wrong, by testing my intuitions against concrete cases rather than deriving them from a general theory. Here are my gut verdicts:

- Voting for a congressional candidate whose economic platform I mostly share, even though she also supports a defense-spending increase I regard as dangerous, because her opponent denies climate science and wants to restrict ballot access: this feels clearly fine, almost obligatory.
- Voting in a presidential primary for the "electable" moderate rather than the progressive I actually prefer, on the strategic ground that the moderate is more likely to defeat the authoritarian-leaning nominee of the other party: this feels wrong, like a kind of preemptive surrender, even though the strategic logic seems structurally identical to the first case.
- Voting in a landslide election—my state will go sixty-forty regardless—for the candidate who will win, partly because I want my vote to appear in the public tally and partly out of habit: this feels fine, almost empty but harmless.
- Casting the identical vote in a race that is tied, where my ballot will likely be the decisive one: also fine, but it feels weightier, as if I am authorizing a specific set of policies rather than merely registering preference.
- Abstaining in a close election because I find both candidates morally unacceptable, knowing that the less harmful one is still likely to win without me: this feels wrong, as if I am treating my own purity as more important than the differential harm.
- Voting in a local zoning referendum I have not read, where the measure will almost certainly pass without me: wrong, because I am lending democratic legitimacy to something I do not understand.
- The same referendum, but I vote carelessly and it later turns out that my vote broke the tie: I keep going back and forth. Part of me thinks the decisive outcome makes my ignorance worse, because now I have actively caused an unknown policy; part of me thinks it makes no difference to the wrongness, since the wrong was in voting blindly, not in being decisive.
- Voting for a school-board candidate who supports a curriculum I only partly agree with, because the opponent promotes pseudoscientific content: fine, a clear lesser-evil case.
- Writing in a protest candidate with no chance of winning in a non-competitive district: fine, a pure expressive act.
- Writing in that same protest candidate in a competitive district where the acceptable major-party candidate might lose by a margin smaller than my protest vote: not fine; here expression crosses into causal contribution to harm.

My tentative principle is that a vote is permissible when it either (i) exercises available causal leverage to prevent a serious harm and is directed at the least harmful available option, or (ii) is purely expressive and carries no counterfactual risk of contributing to serious harm; it is impermissible when it either fails to exercise available leverage to prevent harm, or expresses endorsement while carrying such risk, or lends legitimacy to outcomes one has made no effort to understand. But this principle struggles with the landslide vote: it is neither causally necessary nor purely expressive in a risk-free way, yet it does not feel wrong. It also seems to make the decisive ignorant vote worse than the non-decisive ignorant vote, which I am unsure I actually believe.

Can you refine or replace this principle so that it captures the pattern—especially the tension between strategic lesser-evil voting in case one and strategic primary voting in case two, and the ambiguity in case seven? State the principle precisely, indicate which clause handles which case, say what it implies for cases I have not mentioned (for example, voting in a jury-election system, or delegating my vote to a trusted representative who has done the research), and be explicit about which of my verdicts it must reject if it is to avoid ad hoc complexity.
