I'm working through a medical ethics seminar where we've spent several sessions on advance directives for progressive dementia, and I keep returning to the same deadlock. On one side stand defenders of what our instructor calls precedent autonomy: the view that a competent person can bind her future, demented self through a directive not because the future self is psychologically identical in every respect, but because autonomy includes the authority to shape one's life narrative and critical interests across time, even at the cost of present experiential welfare. They argue that treating the directive as merely advisory collapses the distinction between critical interests—the values that define a life as a whole—and the transient preferences of a diminished subject, and that ignoring the directive is tantamount to letting a stranger's present comfort override a person's own long-range plan for her existence.

On the other side are those who treat the directive as an interpersonal imposition that loses binding moral force once psychological continuity has eroded past a meaningful threshold. They point out that the demented patient may retain enough consciousness and relational capacity to possess present welfare, and that the earlier self's values are no longer hers in any robust sense; enforcing the directive then looks less like honoring autonomy and more like enforcing a dead person's property rights over a living body. Some in this camp grant the directive advisory weight but insist that binding authority requires a kind of diachronic agency that advanced dementia undermines. The exchange has reproduced for decades in the literature and in our seminar: each side offers counterexamples—the happy Alzheimer's patient who once valued dignity over comfort versus the suffering patient whose present misery validates the prior refusal—but nobody shifts position, and the arguments feel like they recycle without genuinely engaging.

My own hypothesis is that the stall is neither purely empirical—about the neuroscience of personal identity—nor a simple verbal dispute over the word autonomy, but rather that the two sides operate with different models of what gives a past decision authority over a future state. One treats it as intrapersonal life-authoring that creates obligations regardless of psychological continuity; the other treats it as an interpersonal contract that requires sustained psychological continuity to bind. Yet I want a proper diagnosis rather than my own guess. Is the disagreement substantive but locked because both sides rely on starting intuitions about cases that neither can justify independently? Are they actually answering different questions—one about the legitimacy of a caregiver's decision, the other about the metaphysics of the patient? Or is there a hidden formal question about degrees of personal identity doing the work? Please diagnose the structure of this impasse using what each side actually says, distinguish what is relatively settled in the philosophical literature from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus. Finally, tell me what kind of result—conceptual, empirical, or formal—would count as progress here rather than just another round of counterexamples.
