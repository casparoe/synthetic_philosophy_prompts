Draft the joint statement that a scholar in the institutional-measurement tradition and a scholar in the critical-constructivist tradition could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on democratic backsliding, with particular attention to Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States.

The document should begin with a numbered agreed-claims section. Include agreement on: the minimal institutional requirements for liberal democracy (competitive multiparty elections, horizontal accountability among branches, an independent judiciary with effective review power, and protected civil liberties); the observable institutional changes in each case—Hungary’s consolidation of executive influence over media councils and judicial appointments, Poland’s restructuring of the Constitutional Tribunal and disciplinary mechanisms for judges, Turkey’s presidential-system consolidation coupled with large-scale judicial and bureaucratic purges, and the US pattern of state-level election-administration changes, extreme partisan gerrymandering, and executive-branch attempts to overturn or obstruct certified electoral outcomes; the existence of cross-national measurement projects and the fact that all of them embed coding judgments about baselines, weights, and thresholds; and the logical point that labeling a change “backsliding” presupposes a prior state of democratic consolidation and a normative standard of what counts as erosion rather than legitimate reform.

Next, present a disagreements table with precisely worded rows that each side would accept for its own position. The disagreements should cover at least: whether backsliding is best characterized as a measurable deviation from an objective institutional baseline or as a narrative constructed and applied selectively by transnational epistemic communities with embedded liberal-normative commitments; whether the recent application of the concept to the United States reflects a symmetric analytical framework or an asymmetric partisan framing that treats comparable institutional stress in other contexts differently; and whether Hungary and Poland exemplify genuine democratic erosion or represent democratically legitimated conservative constitutional transformations that liberal observers systematically miscode because they conflate policy disagreement with institutional decay.

Then identify the cruxes—the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete institutional episode or comparative pattern that both sides accept bears on the question, and record in advance what each side predicts. Suggested cruxes: (a) Hungary’s 2011–2023 media-council and public-broadcast restructuring—does it functionally eliminate media pluralism or merely rebalance state and private influence within democratic bounds? (b) Poland’s 2015–2023 constitutional-tribunal crisis—does it destroy horizontal accountability or replace one partisan judicial composition with another without altering the underlying logic of political control? (c) Turkey’s 2016 purges and 2023 election—do they demonstrate that competitive elections can persist alongside deep institutional decay, supporting a “competitive authoritarianism” framing, or do they show that formal electoral competition remains sufficient to deny the backsliding label? (d) US state-level election-administration and certification changes post-2020—are they structurally comparable to Hungarian-style executive aggrandizement, or are they categorically different because they occur within a federal architecture with stronger inter-branch veto points and judicial review?

Finally, include a brief section assessing whether part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive. Address whether “democratic backsliding” operates as a family-resemblance term with no necessary and sufficient conditions, whether disagreement about “who decides” collapses into a normative dispute about legitimate authority rather than an empirical dispute about institutional change, and whether the measurement-constructivist divide is partly about the status of expert coding communities rather than about the underlying political facts.

Format the output as a clean, signable joint document. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, citations to specific authors or works, or requests for external verification of particular data points; treat the institutional descriptions above as shared background for the collaboration.
