I'm giving the opening remarks tomorrow at an occassion called "Living With Division" — half the room are social epistemologists and half are local librarians and councillors who've actually watched a town split over a planning dispute. The theme is echo chambers and epistemic bubbles, but I really don't want to just rehash the usual echo-chamber-versus-bubble distinction everyone has seen a hundred times. Instead, I want to hang the whole speech on a slightly odd framing I've been turning over: that an echo chamber isn't mainly about people repeating beliefs to each other, but about a collective agreement to treat the costs of leaving as invisible. People stay not because they're convinced they're right, but because exit would force them to face a kind of social bankruptcy that the group has quietly agreed not to acknowledge.

This needs to do real structural work. My late mentor ran a cross-party reading group in our town for thirty years and never managed to "fix" the polarization — and I want the speech to say clearly that this wasn't a personal failure. The logic of the bubble makes exit costly in ways that rational argument doesn't touch, so I can't offer false comfort: I can't pretend that understanding each other dissolves the problem, or that one side is the bubble and the other isn't. The point should land for the experts without losing the family and local people in the room, and it should honour him without turning him into a saint who solved something unsolvable.
