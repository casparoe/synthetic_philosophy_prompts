As a novelist constructing the philosophical architecture for my protagonist, Dr. Elena Voss—a clinical neurologist who writes on the phenomenology of harm—I need to map her implicit judgments about pain and its badness to see whether they form a single rule or fracture under a new clinical profile I have invented for the narrative.

Here are her five published case judgments, rendered consistently across her essays.

First, on congenital pain asymbolia: she holds that a patient who feels sharp nociceptive intensity, describes it as loud or geometrically present, yet seeks out thermal and mechanical stimuli without aversion is not suffering. The sensation is present, she writes, but lacks the property that makes pain harmful.

Second, on pharmacological suppression of affect: a subject given a selective affect-blocker who retains precise sensory discrimination and reflex withdrawal, yet reports that injury registers but does not reach me, is judged to experience no harm. The reflex, she argues, is mere mechanics without integrating aversion.

Third, on pure affective dread without sensory localization—what she terms free-floating nociceptive affect—she judges that the subject is genuinely suffering, but that this is not pain proper. It demonstrates, she says, that affect alone can be bad, though it lacks the specific bodily directedness that defines nociception.

Fourth, on the hypothetical of a non-conscious damage-avoidance system—an organism or artifact that repairs tissue and withdraws from threat with perfect efficiency but reports no interiority—she denies that any harm occurs. There is protection, she writes, but no subject for whom protection matters.

Fifth, on ordinary nociceptive experience with intact affect, sensory precision, and protective motivation: she takes the badness to be uncontroversial and fully present.

The new case involves a patient—call her Case F—who presents with what I have termed inverted valence binding. She reports intense, precisely localized negative affect in response to tissue damage; she describes the feeling as the sharpest warning and urgent with truth. Yet her behavioral response is not avoidance but approach: she arranges repeated controlled injury, her reflex arcs are suppressed, and she describes the experience as clarifying and necessary. Her affect is negative, her sensory discrimination intact, but the motivational vector is reversed.

Given Dr. Voss's prior track record, what should she conclude about Case F if she wishes to remain consistent? Identify the closest analog among her earlier rulings, and state honestly whether her precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict. If they do, lay out the competing principles—say, an integrationist rule requiring coupled affect and protective motivation, an affectivist rule requiring only negative valence, and a functionalist rule requiring only successful protective economy—that all fit her five prior judgments. For each principle, predict her verdict on Case F and specify the broader commitment she would be adopting. Finally, propose one further hypothetical case, distinct from Case F, that would force a decision between the two leading rules.
