You have been asked to deliver the opening remarks at an interdisciplinary workshop on moral accountability, held in a university conference center. The audience is divided: roughly half are moral philosophers who publish on standing to blame, hypocrisy, and institutional authority; the other half are practitioners from community restorative-justice programs with little formal philosophical training. The workshop's stated theme is standing to blame and institutional hypocrisy, but I want you to avoid treating the topic as a familiar literature review. Instead, frame the remarks around a specific, somewhat technical distinction—call it functional-standing dissolution—which you should define in plain language for the practitioners without simplifying it into falsehood. The idea is this: an institution or office-holder can lose the authority to blame not through personal moral failure (the standard hypocrisy case), but through a structural contradiction in which the very institutional function that authorizes the blame depends on norms that the institution systematically violates. In such cases, the problem is not that the blamer is morally disqualified in the usual sense, but that the blame can no longer perform its institutional function; it collapses into a private expression of disapproval that lacks the normative backing of the role. Use this distinction to structure the remarks: explain why practitioners who say the criminal-justice system has no right to blame are often gesturing at functional-standing dissolution rather than at hypocrisy; show what this implies for whether reform can restore standing—if the structural contradiction is removed, does authority return automatically, or must something like explicit institutional acknowledgment occur; and close by identifying the precise philosophical question this raises for the philosophers in the room—whether standing is fundamentally a moral property of individual agents or a functional property of institutional roles. The remarks should run about seven to eight minutes, should be intelligible to the non-philosophers without being condescending, and should make the philosophical idea do real structural work: it should determine which objections you take seriously, which you set aside, and why the hypocrisy framework is inadequate here. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to specific texts. Do not name philosophers unless the reference is genuinely unavoidable for clarity, and do not quote anyone verbatim.
