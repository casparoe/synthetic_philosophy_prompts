In moral philosophy, the debate over whether an action possesses moral worth only when performed from duty—rather than merely in accordance with duty—has cycled for decades through the same counterexamples (the sympathetic merchant, the reluctant benefactor, parents acting from natural affection) without convergence. The disagreement is ostensibly about motivation, yet neither side seems to miss the opponent's point so much as talk past it. My working hypothesis is that the stall is not substantive but taxonomic, masked by shared vocabulary: one camp analyzes moral worth as a structural property of practical reasoning (the maxim's adoption through respect for the moral law), while the other treats it as a phenomenological property of conscious moral recognition. If that is right, the two sides may be answering different questions—one about the conditions of justified action, the other about the conditions of praiseworthy agency—under the single heading of "moral worth." Is this diagnosis correct, or is there instead a hidden empirical disagreement about the psychology of motivation, or a normative disagreement about what counts as a moral reason, doing the actual work? Given your diagnosis, is the impasse a sign that the original question is ill-posed, or merely that a missing distinction—perhaps between agent-evaluation and act-evaluation, or between explanatory and justificatory reasons—would dissolve the appearance of conflict? And what, precisely, would count as progress: a conceptual clarification, an empirical result about moral cognition, or a reframing that separates the two questions entirely?
