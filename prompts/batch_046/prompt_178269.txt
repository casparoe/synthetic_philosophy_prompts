Student response:
Lewis demonstrates that if indicative conditionals possess truth conditions, and if Stalnaker's thesis is correct—that the probability of an indicative conditional 'If A, C' equals the conditional probability of C given A—then all contingent propositions must carry exactly the same probability value. This collapses the probability space into a trivial constant. Stalnaker formulated the probabilistic identity primarily for counterfactual conditionals, but Lewis applies it to indicatives to show that anyone who accepts both premises is committed to this collapse. To avoid the result, Lewis rejects the first premise: indicative conditionals do not express propositions with truth conditions. Rather, they are assessed for assertability in light of a speaker's beliefs about comparative similarity among worlds. Since they lack truth conditions, they have no probabilities, so Stalnaker's equation is not false but inapplicable. Critics have objected that this renders indicative conditionals useless for deductive reasoning, but Lewis replies that reasoning with conditionals is always reasoning about beliefs, not about propositional contents. The escape therefore succeeds in preserving non-trivial probabilities, though it does so by giving up a unified semantic treatment of indicative and counterfactual conditionals.
