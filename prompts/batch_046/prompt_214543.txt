I work in software engineering and have no formal philosophy background, but I have been trying to understand how philosophers distinguish health from disease without relying on vague intuition. I tend to think in terms of system specifications, failure modes, and environmental compatibility, and I have formed my own verdicts on several concrete cases. I want to know whether there is a single principle—naturalist, normativist, or hybrid—that captures all of them, or whether coherence requires me to abandon one.

Here are my verdicts.

Case 1: A 45-year-old sedentary adult with insulin resistance, elevated HbA1c, progressive pancreatic beta-cell decline, fatigue, and impaired wound healing. Verdict: this is clearly a disease.

Case 2: A person born with congenital insensitivity to pain. They report high subjective wellbeing, suffer no chronic discomfort, and function well socially, but they accumulate frequent unnoticed injuries and have a significantly reduced life expectancy because the protective warning mechanism is absent. Verdict: this is a disorder or disease, despite the complete absence of felt harm.

Case 3: A cognitive profile marked by intense hyperfocus, reduced social filtering, and sensory sensitivity to open environments. In a standard open-plan office, the individual cannot perform collaborative tasks and experiences severe exhaustion; in a remote, solitary role requiring deep debugging and long-range pattern recognition, they perform exceptionally well and report high life satisfaction. Verdict: this is not a disease. It is a mismatch between a particular internal specification and a runtime environment, not a failure of internal design.

Case 4: The universal, gradual decline in cellular repair, telomere shortening, and neurogenesis that occurs after age 70. It is statistically normal for the species, inevitable, and causes measurable harm, yet medicine classifies aging itself as a risk factor rather than a disease. Verdict: aging is not a disease, though it creates conditions favorable to disease.

Case 5: A therapeutic protocol that permanently suppresses the inflammatory immune response to eliminate a debilitating autoimmune disorder. The patient is now free of chronic pain and tissue damage, but is highly vulnerable to common infections and carries an elevated long-term cancer risk. Verdict: the resulting suppressed state is a form of induced ill health or dysfunction, not a return to full health; the vulnerability is a genuine departure from proper functioning rather than a neutral trade-off.

These verdicts pull in different directions. Cases 2 and 5 treat missing or suppressed protective mechanisms as disease regardless of subjective wellbeing, which sounds naturalist. Case 3 denies that statistical deviation and context-specific dysfunction count as disease, which sounds environment-relative or normativist. Case 4 denies that universal biological harm is sufficient for disease. Is there a coherent account—perhaps one that distinguishes failures of internal design specification from failures of environmental compatibility, or one that weights harm differently depending on whether it stems from internal breakdown versus external misalignment—that delivers all five verdicts?

If no single principle can accommodate them all, please state the best available candidate precisely, indicate which clause is motivated by which case, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed—a voluntary genetic modification that eliminates the need for sleep but causes cumulative metabolic stress that only manifests after decades—and be explicit about which of my five verdicts it forces me to drop and why.

Please do not provide bibliographies or reading lists; I am looking for the principle itself, tested against these cases and repaired if necessary.
