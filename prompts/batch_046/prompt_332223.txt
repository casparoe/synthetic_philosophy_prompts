I'm preparing a seminar for philosophy graduate students with no formal game-theory training. The goal is to explain the backward-induction paradox in the centipede game, but through a custom framing in which each round represents not a monetary payoff but a level of reflective justification: taking means accepting the current epistemic authority, and passing means demanding a deeper reason. I want a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. Specify the logical dependencies: which concept must be secured before common knowledge of rationality can even be introduced, and why the backward step cannot be demonstrated until that dependency is in place. Identify the one carrying example or case structure that makes the chain of best responses visible, stating precisely what feature it must include to show the induction mechanism and what feature it must exclude so that cooperation does not appear merely foolish. Map the predictable misconceptions in the order the students will raise them—likely an empirical objection about human irrationality, then a normative objection that rationality should not be self-defeating, then a logical objection that the first move is already irrational—indicating at which point in the sequence each should be addressed and how the arc should absorb it. Propose a check for understanding that distinguishes genuine grasp of the induction from mere discomfort with its conclusion. Finally, state what formal apparatus—such as extensive-form notation or equilibrium-refinement concepts—should be deliberately omitted at this level and why.
