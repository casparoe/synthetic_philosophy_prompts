As a retired experimental physicist whose career centered on rotational dynamics and inertial measurement, I have in recent years begun studying philosophy of science in earnest. At a meeting of my local reading group, a colleague—likewise trained in physics but now working in philosophy—offered the following summary of the debate over Newton's bucket experiment and the ontology of spacetime.

He maintained that Newton's rotating-bucket demonstration shows water climbing the walls only when the system spins relative to absolute space itself, not merely relative to the bucket. From this he inferred that Leibnizian relationism fails, since reducing motion to comparative displacement among bodies leaves one unable to distinguish the initial state—where bucket and water share no relative motion and the surface remains flat—from the later state in which both rotate together relative to absolute space and the surface curves. He then presented Mach's principle as resolving the difficulty by replacing the metaphysical container with the distribution of distant cosmic matter, which supplies a de facto absolute reference frame without rendering space a substance. On his account, modern general relativity has therefore made the bucket argument largely historical: spacetime is treated as a dynamic field rather than a fixed Newtonian stage, and the affine connection that governs rotating frames is understood as a pure gauge artifact lacking independent ontological weight. He acknowledged that a minority of philosophers defend a structural-realist view in which the connection field is physically real yet not substantial, but he dismissed this as an unnecessary embellishment that adds no explanatory power.

I suspect this characterization contains subtle misrepresentations—particularly regarding Mach's principle, the status of the affine connection, and the implications of general relativity—but I do not have the philosophical vocabulary to pinpoint them. Would you provide a careful audit? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common but defensible simplification, and what is simply wrong or misleading about Newton's original reasoning, Mach's critique, and the contemporary philosophical landscape. Specifically, does the claim that the bucket argument is now obsolete follow from the premises offered? And is the structural-realist alternative sketched here presented fairly, or is it being set up as a strawman?
