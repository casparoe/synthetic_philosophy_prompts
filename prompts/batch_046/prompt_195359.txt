I just spent several hours arguing with a friend about the color-phi phenomenon and whether consciousness handles it through an Orwellian revision—retroactively rewriting the experienced color of the first flash once the second flash arrives—or through a Stalinesque revision, in which an intermediate, contradictory frame is never admitted to awareness at all and is simply airbrushed from the record. We could not settle whether the brain corrects the past or suppresses the gap, and I want to close our conversation not with a verdict but with a poem that holds the ambiguity in its very form. Write a sonnet of exactly fourteen lines in which the octave builds the Stalinesque picture: two differently colored dots appear in rapid succession at separate positions, apparent motion bridges the space between them, and the color change is perceived midway through the trajectory with no trace of contradiction or intermediate state having ever reached experience. At the volta, turn sharply to the Orwellian counter: the first dot is remembered as having already borne the second color, the past experience rewritten by what comes later, as if the record of the initial flash were corrected after the fact. The closing couplet should hold both revisions in suspension without resolving which one consciousness actually performs, since that unresolved tension is exactly where our argument ended. Begin immediately with the poem itself; do not include any preface, commentary, or explanation outside the verse. Make the conceptual distinction precise enough that someone familiar with the philosophy of temporal experience recognizes the two positions, but let the structural turn of the sonnet carry the philosophical weight so the poem stands on its own for a reader who has never encountered the illusion.
