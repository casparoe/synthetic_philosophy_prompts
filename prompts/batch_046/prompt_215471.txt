Compare the direct-suffering framework and the collateral-crop-cultivation framework as methods for evaluating whether beef or chicken consumption produces greater net harm to wild and farmed animals. In the direct-suffering framework, harm is calculated primarily from the suffering of farmed animals per unit of food produced; in the collateral-crop-cultivation framework, harm includes wild animals killed during the cultivation of feed and human-consumption crops, weighted by the agricultural footprint of each meat type. Specifically, examine how each framework handles the substitution-cascade objection: reducing beef consumption may increase demand for crop-intensive alternatives, thereby amplifying wild-animal mortality in cultivation. Assess which framework is more robust to this objection when used to derive population-level dietary recommendations, and provide a reasoned judgment about whether one framework should take normative priority over the other.
