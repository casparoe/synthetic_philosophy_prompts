At a seminar on social ontology, two colleagues—Dr. Elena Voss and Dr. Marcus Chen—debated whether "Generation Z" names a real kind or a demographic fiction. Elena argued that generations are developmentally integrated kinds: despite fuzzy boundaries, members share causally linked traits—early algorithmic-feed exposure, climate anxiety, and delayed economic independence—that cluster stably across developed economies. Marcus replied that this mistakes correlation for kind-hood; "Gen Z" is merely a fifteen-year bin bundling a Nigerian teenager and a Japanese office worker who share only birth years, and if smartphones had arrived five years later the cluster would dissolve, showing the category is an indexical kind tied to contingent technology rather than a robust ontological category. Elena countered that all serious social kinds—class, gender—are partly indexical and context-sensitive, yet remain real because they structure life chances; she noted that generational labels predict voting patterns and mental-health trends well enough to earn explanatory keep. Marcus said predictive success is pragmatic, not ontological—astrology predicts too—and that the real explanatory work is done by narrower mechanisms like algorithmic-native cognition, with the generational label serving as a misleading umbrella. Elena then asked: if we dropped generational language tomorrow, wouldn't we lose power to explain inter-cohort conflict over housing and debt? Marcus said no, we would replace it with precise structural descriptions—"the cohort entering labor markets during rapid monetary tightening and asset inflation"—which explain the phenomena without inventing false unity.
