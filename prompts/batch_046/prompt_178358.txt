I run a philosophy workshop for about 12 undergrad students, 75 minutes, in a seminar room with tables. I want them to actually feel the force of the Chinese Room argument, not just talk about it. Design a live game where half are "rooms" with symbol-manipulation rulebooks in English and half are "outsiders" who pass them invented characters that look like Chinese and ask questions. The rooms must answer using only there rules, no comprehension allowed. But inject this twist: midway through, one room secretly gets access to an external shared lookup table — not part of their rulebook — so they start answering correctly through that resource rather than syntax alone. After they switch roles, debrief them. They should come away seeing why pure symbol-shuffling feels empty, but also feeling the pull of the system reply when the table gets added. Map the upgrade moment onto the critics of the argument, say what will predictably go wrong (people cheating, recognizing patterns in the fake characters), and be honest that the activity can't reproduce the first-person absence of understanding — you'll have to supply that part in discussion. Don't tell them it's Searle's room until after they've played. Oh, and my friend was arguing that the room needs a body for the intuition to stick, so maybe build in a physical constraint like they have to stand still or use only one hand? Keep instructions clear but casual.
