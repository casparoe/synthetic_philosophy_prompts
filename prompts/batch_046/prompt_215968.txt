I'm writing a feature for a science-and-ideas magazine on the inefficacy objection — the old drop-in-the-bucket problem — and I've hit a wierd pattern that I can't shake.

In climate ethics people mostly concede your flight won't move the needle, then immediately pivot to systemic advocacy. In animal ethics they concede your burger doesn't save the cow, then defend boycott logic anyway. In democratic theory they concede your vote is a rounding error, then talk about expressive civic duty. I've been in philosophy reading groups and journalist roundtables where this comes up, and for years nobody has moved an inch. What's frustrating is that the logical structure is identical — individual action is causally impotent — but the stall looks different in each field. Is the disagreement actually the same one about whether moral reasons require causal efficacy, just dressed up differently? Or are there three separate hidden disputes — maybe about collective identity, about visibility and signaling, about what counts as genuine participation — that share only a surface form? Give me a real diagnosis: what's the bedrock disagreement in each case, could one conceptual move break all three logjams, and should I treat them as one story or three separate stalemates?
