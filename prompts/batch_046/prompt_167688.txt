Here's an argument I cooked up about Rousseau's theater thing and now it feels like it destroys everything. (1) In his letter to d'Alembert, Rousseau claims theater corrupts civic life because it gives representational surrogacy: people feel they've exercised virtue just by watching actors perform it, without doing the hard moral work themselves. (2) But this surrogacy effect isn't special to stage plays; epic poems, political biographies, and even philosophical dialogues like Emile or the Social Contract let readers simulate moral reasoning and civic virtue without actually enacting it. (3) So if representational surrogasy is enough to condemn theater, then Rousseau must also condemn his own writings, since they provide exactly the same kind of simulated moral experience. (4) Yet Rousseau clearly thinks some books educate rather than corrupt. (5) So either he has an arbitrary line with no real principle behind it, or the surrogacy argument proves too much and should be thrown out entirely. (6) But if we throw it out, we lose the specific reason theater is dangerous compared to other arts. Where does this argument go wrong? I'd like to keep the intuition that representation can substitute for action, but I don't want to end up burning every book in the library.
