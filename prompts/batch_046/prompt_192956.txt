As a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has worked through philosophy of history and comparative institutional analysis, I am serving as an auditor-grader for an undergraduate essay in a course I now attend. The assignment asked students to interpret the Terror of 1793–94 through competing historiographical frameworks, with particular attention to whether revolutionary dynamics are structurally determined or path-dependent, and to evaluate whether the familiar idea that revolutions devour their own children offers analytical precision about institutional mechanisms or merely substitutes rhetoric for causal explanation.

The exam question was: Consider the interpretation of the French Revolution's turn to terror. First, distinguish a class-struggle account from an ideology-centered account in explaining the radicalization of 1793–94, being precise about what each treats as the primary driver. Second, assess whether recognizing contingency in the Revolution's trajectory undermines the causal relevance of structural conditions such as fiscal crisis, estate inequality, and warfare, or whether the two levels of explanation can be reconciled. Third, discuss whether the claim that revolutions devour their own children captures a specific institutional feedback dynamic—such as escalation within revolutionary committees—or instead functions as an unfalsifiable metaphor that obscures agent-level and organizational explanations.

Below is the student's answer.

A class-struggle interpretation treats the Jacobin Terror as the necessary defense of bourgeois hegemony against both aristocratic reaction and popular democracy, requiring the destruction of the Revolution's own radical elements in order to consolidate capitalist social relations. An ideology-centered account, in the tradition of François Furet, rejects this social determinism and instead locates the violence in the logic of revolutionary discourse itself: the collapse of constitutional representation and the escalation of popular sovereignty rhetoric made terror an almost inevitable expression of the new political culture. The claim that revolutions devour their own children therefore misses the point. Robespierre was not destroyed by an abstract revolutionary force but by specific Thermidorian actors who reacted to institutional overload within the Committee of Public Safety, so the metaphor obscures the actual feedback mechanisms at work. Furthermore, because contingency theorists demonstrate that alternative decisions—such as an earlier constitutional settlement—could have averted the Terror, the structural conditions of 1789, including fiscal crisis and estate inequality, played no genuine causal role in producing the outcome, though they may explain why revolution occurred at all. Consequently, while class-struggle theories correctly identify social conflict, they ultimately fail because they treat all contingency as mere appearance hiding an underlying structural necessity.
