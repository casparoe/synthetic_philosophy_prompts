You are invited to deliver the opening remarks at a workshop convened in a new public archive of historical testimony that operates under an unprecedented institutional rule: personal narratives of trauma may be withdrawn from open access after seventy-five years at the request of direct descendants, even over the objections of historians. The audience includes archivists, moral philosophers, survivors' families with no academic background, and municipal officials who funded the building; the room is genuinely divided between those who see the sunset clause as an ethical correction to perpetual exposure and those who regard it as a dangerous privatization of collective memory. Drawing on the philosophical tension between a duty to remember and a serious, non-sentimental case for forgetting—without treating either pole as self-evident—compose a fifteen-minute address that takes this concrete withdrawal mechanism as its structural backbone. The speech should shape what the institution can claim about its own mission, rather than decorating a predetermined policy stance; it must be fully intelligible to the family representatives present, yet precise enough that specialists recognize you are not trading in platitudes about healing or justice. Do not resolve the tension into a simple recommendation, do not provide bibliographies or named citations, and do not invoke philosophical authorities by surname; let the seventy-five-year feature itself do the conceptual labor of showing what an archive owes simultaneously to the past and to the living.
