I have recently assumed the role of primary caregiver for a parent diagnosed with early-stage dementia, and observing how memories are selectively reconstructed or silently omitted has led me to reconsider the architecture of conscious experience itself. I am particularly struck by the analogy between political revisions of history—Orwellian, in which the record is rewritten to conform to present doctrine, and Stalinesque, in which inconvenient figures and events are simply excised—and the mechanisms revealed by the color phi phenomenon, where the visual system appears to postdictively insert intermediate percepts that never occurred, altering the experienced temporal sequence. If we take seriously the idea that consciousness operates through similar retrospective revisions rather than through a transparent stream of real-time registration, please describe the most important implications for how we should evaluate first-person testimony about the precise timing and qualitative content of perceptual events, and for whether there remains any meaningful distinction between an Orwellian reconstruction of experience and a Stalinesque erasure within the phenomenological field.
