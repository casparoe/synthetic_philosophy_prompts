Present an argument about advance directives that seems to prove too much, then diagnose it.

(1) The standard justification for honoring an advance directive is that the earlier autonomous self S1 has authority over the later non-autonomous self S2, because S1 is the last version of this person capable of self-governance.

(2) If this authority derives from S1 being the "last autonomous version" in the narrative, then S1 should also govern any intermediate autonomous version S1.5 whose values have shifted, since S1.5 is no longer continuous with S1's evaluative framework and S1 was still the originator of the life-plan.

(3) So either S1 lacks special authority over S2, or S1 retains authority over every subsequent autonomous self that diverges from S1's values.

(4) The latter option entails permanent "value-locking": individuals are bound by the commitments they made at the point of maximal autonomy—career paths, religious affiliations, aesthetic judgments—unless they can demonstrate perfect diachronic value-continuity.

(5) But this is clearly false: a person at fifty is not morally required to honor her thirty-year-old decision to pursue engineering, even if she now regards that framework as alien.

(6) Therefore the "last autonomous version" rationale collapses, and advance directives lack the distinctive authority usually claimed for them.

The argument feels suspicious because its conclusion undermines a widely accepted practice, yet each step looks individually defensible. Identify the weakest step, explain why it seemed reasonable, and then price the alternatives: if you reject that step, what must you say about the difference between governing a non-autonomous successor and governing an autonomous one? In particular, can advance directives be grounded without appealing to diachronic authority at all, or does preserving them require accepting some restricted form of value-locking across autonomous stages?
