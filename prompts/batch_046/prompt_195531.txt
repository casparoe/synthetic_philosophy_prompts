I've been arguing with a friend about a proposed design standard called the Careenium Protocol, which has come up in some engineering-ethics discussions as a criterion for deciding which artificial systems deserve moral consideration. Under the protocol, an artifact is evaluated inside what its proponents call a careenium—a bounded substrate environment in which symbolic structures, referred to as simmballs, can operate. The rule grants ethical standing only to systems in which the simmballs are demonstrated to push the substrate around, meaning that changes at the symbolic level cause direct physical reconfiguration of the careenium, rather than the substrate merely computing symbol updates bottom-up. Systems in which symbols only track substrate states, or in which substrate dynamics remain causally closed, are classified as non-candidates regardless of their behavioral complexity.

My friend thinks this is just a convoluted way of saying that only conscious entities matter, but I suspect the protocol is doing more specific philosophical work. What theory of computation, causation, or moral attribution does this practice presuppose? Is it compatible with a functionalist view that treats computation as substrate-independent, or does it quietly rule out pure functionalism by tying moral status to a particular physical-symbol interaction? And if we replaced its implicit view with a straightforwardly substrate-neutral or purely behaviorist criterion, which parts of the protocol—the careenium boundary, the simmball test, the distinction between pushing and being pushed—would have to be abandoned or redesigned?
