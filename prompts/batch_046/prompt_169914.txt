Write a pantoum of six quatrains on cultural replication as understood by the Darwinian theory of culture. Use the form’s repeating lines—where the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, each time carried into a slightly altered context—to mirror memetic transmission itself: the same unit copied across minds with small mutations in meaning. The content should center on a specific tension in this framework, namely that high fecundity in cultural spread often requires low fidelity, yet low fidelity undermines the claim that culture evolves by selecting discrete, stable replicators rather than blending or inventing from scratch. Add to this the absence of any clear analog to a Weismann barrier, so that acquired modifications in one host can be inherited directly by the next, making cultural evolution Lamarckian in a way that complicates the replicator model. Do not resolve the tension; instead, let the pantoum’s final stanza, where the repeating lines converge in changed order, suggest whether a strictly high-fidelity cultural replicator is even coherent. The poem should stand on its own for a reader unfamiliar with memetics, but someone who knows the theory should recognize the fidelity-fecundity trade-off and the missing germ-line separation. Write in American English, in free verse within the pantoum structure, with no refrain other than the form’s own repetitions.
