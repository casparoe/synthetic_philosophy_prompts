I serve on an institutional review board for a hospital network that runs algorithmic triage protocols, and caregivers or family members can petition for exceptions when they claim the protocol violates a core personal or professional commitment. I need a reusable decision procedure—runnable in about fifteen minutes per petition, documented for the committee chair—to determine whether an exception request is grounded in Bernard Williams’s integrity objection, as opposed to either disguised partiality or the one-thought-too-many failure mode in which an agent reaches an impartial-seeming conclusion only after smuggling in an illegitimate personal step. The deliverable should be an ordered sequence of tests with explicit stopping conditions: a clear reject, a clear approve, and a flagged handoff to the chair where the remaining issue is values rather than facts. The procedure must distinguish three failure modes—(1) pure impartial calculation that suppresses legitimate integrity; (2) preference-laundering, where partiality is recast as principle; and (3) integrity-theater, where a petitioner performs commitment-language primarily to avoid the burden of impartial reasoning. Apply it to three petitions: (a) a physician requests that her own child, algorithmically classified as low-priority for ICU admission, be treated because she claims her professional identity is inseparable from parental care; (b) a family demands that their father, a veteran, be prioritized over a younger patient with a better prognosis, framing this as honoring a lifelong commitment to service; and (c) a nurse refuses to assist in a legally required but personally abhorrent procedure, where her refusal would collapse a time-critical surgical schedule. In at least one case the procedure should yield an answer that conflicts with common institutional intuition; explain why it yields that answer and whether that signals a procedural flaw or a legitimate reason for the board to override it. Finally, state explicitly what the procedure cannot adjudicate, especially regarding conflicts between role-based integrity and institutional fairness, and mark every point where judgment cannot be eliminated.
