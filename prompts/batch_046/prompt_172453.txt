I am developing notes for an essay on adaptationism and the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique, and I would welcome your comments on the argument sketched below.

The familiar summary is that Gould and Lewontin rejected the assumption that every trait is an adaptation. I want to suggest that this understates their target: they were criticizing an "atomistic" research program that treats organisms as bundles of independently optimized traits, neglecting the architectural integration of development and form. To make this precise, I distinguish two classes of spandrel. First, structural spandrels: geometric or physical byproducts of scaling that are not themselves targets of selection, such as the cranial sutures that necessarily accompany an enlarged brain. Second, developmental spandrels: phenotypic side effects produced by pleiotropic gene networks, where selection on one pathway unavoidably modifies another—for example, pigmentation patterns that arise incidentally from neural-crest cell migration pathways selected for other functions.

My further claim is that contemporary evolutionary developmental biology often invokes both phenomena but collapses them into an undifferentiated vocabulary of "developmental constraint," thereby obscuring Gould and Lewontin's original emphasis on the priority of integrated architecture over piecemeal optimization.

Is the characterization of evo-devo's vocabulary defensible? In responding, please separate what is settled consensus in evolutionary biology from what remains genuinely contested among specialists—especially regarding the intended scope of Gould and Lewontin's critique—and avoid presenting any disputed interpretive claim as if it were the standard view.
