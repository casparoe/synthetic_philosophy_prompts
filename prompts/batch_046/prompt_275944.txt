I am designing the final assessment for an advanced seminar on suffering-focused ethics, and I want to test whether students can navigate a conceptual tension that standard textbook treatments gloss over. The course has covered negative utilitarianism in its usual form, but I have introduced a more demanding variant: lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism applied to cases where the goods and harms being compared appear genuinely incommensurable—specifically, intense chronic neuropathic suffering versus profound but fleeting aesthetic or relational pleasure across different sensory and evaluative modalities. The central puzzle is whether one can define an "avoid side effects" constraint—i.e., a lexical boundary that prevents any quantity of pleasure from outweighing severe suffering—without implicitly committing to a full, ordered specification of every value at stake.

Please construct a take-home essay examination (approximately 1,500 words, worth 60 percent of the final module grade, 48-hour window) that requires students to engage precisely with this crux. The prompt should ask them to determine whether calibrating such a lexical threshold across incommensurable domains necessarily collapses into a complete axiological framework, or whether it can be anchored in a minimal, suffering-focused commitment that leaves other values unspecified. Crucially, do not ask students for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations of specific authors or texts; the task is conceptual reasoning, not scholarly identification. Along with the exam question, provide a model answer that takes a committed, definite position—arguing either that the threshold survives without full value specification or that it does not—and gives explicit reasons rather than surveying options neutrally.

Finally, supply a marking scheme (out of 30 marks) that specifies the exact conceptual moves required for a top-grade response—such as distinguishing lexical priority from simple quantitative outweighing, diagnosing whether incommensurability is axiological or merely epistemic, and showing how cross-modal calibration either does or does not smuggle in hidden value rankings. The scheme should also list specific errors that should cost marks, for example conflating the lexical threshold with absolute deontological prohibition, treating pleasure and pain as obviously cardinally measurable without argument, or failing to address why partial axiological commitment might still require hidden completeness conditions. The difficulty should be calibrated so that a student who has only memorized the phrase "negative utilitarianism" cannot succeed, while a student who genuinely understands the tension between partial and full axiological specification can demonstrate mastery.
