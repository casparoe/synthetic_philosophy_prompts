I'm drafting notes for an essay that crosses Daniel Dennett's compatibilist account of free will—centered on evolved elbow room and the predictive utility of treating entities as rational agents—with Gregg Caruso's rejection of basic moral desert and his public-health analogy for criminal punishment, in which offenders are quarantined like carriers of infectious disease rather than punished because they deserve suffering. The essay's central claim is that Caruso's quarantine model tacitly reintroduces the intentional stance Dennett describes: to decide whom to isolate and for how long, authorities must evaluate beliefs, intentions, and capacities for reason-responsive control, which looks structurally like assessing moral agency rather than measuring mere pathogenic risk. If that is correct, then quarantine does not escape desert-based evaluation so much as displace it into predictive assessments that still distinguish the dangerous agent from the unfortunate victim. The essay then asks whether Dennett's framework actually supports Caruso's institutional goals more consistently than Caruso admits, since Dennett never required basic desert—only functional, predictive responsibility—whereas Caruso's attempt to purge all agency-talk from punishment may collapse into a purely behavioral risk-management model that loses the normative distinctions (for example, between negligence and malice) that even public-health triage implicitly relies on.

I'd appreciate focused comments on the following. First, does this application of the intentional stance to quarantine fairly capture Dennett's view, or does it conflate predictive utility with normative evaluation in a way he would reject? Second, is the claim that quarantine requires reason-responsive assessment accurate to Caruso's model, or can his position be reconstructed using purely behavioral and neurological risk markers without any attribution of agency? Third, does the essay's conclusion—that Dennett's compatibilism might underwrite Caruso's reformist aims more coherently than Caruso's own anti-desert rhetoric—actually follow, or does it miss a deeper disagreement about whether reactive attitudes are themselves unjustified regardless of their predictive value? Finally, are there parts of this framing that seem interesting but underdeveloped, or parts that appear to rest on a misreading of either thinker?
