I've been drafting an essay for a philosophy of art reading group—mostly graduate students, with one colleague from music cognition and a legal theorist who thinks every "special" epistemic domain is just ordinary testimony with extra mystique. The essay tries to split aesthetic testimony into two channels, which I'm tentatively calling structural testimony and indexical testimony, rather than treating aesthetic judgment as one uniform problem. The intuition is that when someone tells me a building is harmonious, I can take that on board roughly as I take navigational testimony: the property is stable across observers, and my failure to see it is a deficiency in perceptual access, not a refusal of autonomy. But when someone tells me that a particular free-jazz passage feels dangerous, the judgment seems constituted by the speaker's own situated reaction—her specific history with dissonance, her risk-tolerance, the acoustic properties of the room she was in. If I adopt her evaluation, I'm not correcting my perception; I'm borrowing her subject-position, which seems impossible without ceasing to be the subject who is supposed to be judging.

I'd like comments on whether this structural/indexical distinction actually holds up, or whether I'm smuggling in a false analogy with navigational testimony that collapses under pressure. I'm also worried that my dangerous example is doing real work or just trading on ambiguity between literal danger and aesthetic risk—if it's the latter, the distinction might dissolve into a generic problem about thick concepts. There's also a possible intermediate case that troubles me: testimony about beauty in works that seem to require cultural initiation, like certain forms of atonal music or ritual objects, where the property isn't purely structural but the borrower's subject-position isn't exactly the speaker's either. Does that intermediate category undermine the binary, or can it be accommodated? Please don't point me toward literature; I want to know whether the argument can stand on its own conceptual feet, and specifically whether the legal-theorist-style objection—that both channels are just ordinary testimony with different confidence levels—can be answered from within the framework.
