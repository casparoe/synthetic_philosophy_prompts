I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of action and free will, and I need an essay question that moves students well beyond the standard classroom summary of sphexishness—the rigid, loop-like behavioral pattern observed in the sphex wasp's burrow-checking ritual and often invoked in debates about whether mechanical determinism is compatible with freedom. Rather than asking them to rehearse that familiar example, I want the question to present a constructed, hypothetical case and force them to apply the concept precisely.

Please design a 45-minute essay prompt (target length about 800 words) centered on the following scenario: an artificial agent, Agent-S, is built around a single, highly deterministic behavioral loop analogous to the sphex ritual. However, Agent-S contains exactly one built-in override: upon detecting a specific chemical signature in its environment, it automatically suspends its loop and executes one pre-programmed alternative action. It has no representational access to its own mental states, no capacity to evaluate reasons, no ability to choose among alternatives, and no hierarchical structure of desires. The exam question should ask students to determine whether this single point of behavioral flexibility means Agent-S escapes sphexishness, and more broadly whether flexibility of this kind is sufficient for free will or whether something like reflective self-evaluation—along broadly Frankfurtian lines, though students need not cite specific texts—is necessary. The question should require them to take a reasoned position on whether the override mechanism introduces genuine autonomy or merely complicates the rigidity without changing its essential nature.

Along with the exam question itself, please provide: (1) a model answer of roughly 600 words that articulates the central crux—distinguishing first-order behavioral plasticity from second-order endorsement—explains why Agent-S remains sphexish despite its override, addresses the objection that any reduction in rigidity counts as freedom, and clarifies why the kind of control relevant to moral responsibility requires more than a causally triggered switch; and (2) a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific conceptual moves each must contain. For instance, an A answer should identify the difference between mere flexibility and reflective self-control, explain why the chemical-trigger mechanism is still non-rational and determined, consider whether hidden flexibility in the original sphex case undermines the distinction, and apply these points directly to Agent-S rather than retreating into general summaries of determinism and compatibilism. The question should be unanswerable by simply describing the wasp's behavior; it must require students to analyze a novel, niche case and to defend a specific view about the relationship between mechanical flexibility and free will. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or requests for citations, and frame everything so that a student with no web access could answer fully from course concepts.
