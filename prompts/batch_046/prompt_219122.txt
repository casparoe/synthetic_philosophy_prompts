My friend and I just finished a long and somewhat circular argument about the special composition question—whether there is a real metaphysical relation that binds parts into wholes, or whether composite objects are nothing over and above convenient descriptions. We kept generating counterexamples to every criterion we proposed: spatial proximity fails for scattered clouds, causal integration seems satisfied by accidental chains, and functional unity looks like a pragmatic projection rather than an ontological boundary. Rather than continue this armchair stalemate, I want an in-class experience that makes the tension visceral and forces participants to live inside the contradictions.

Please design a ninety-minute classroom activity for an upper-level metaphysics seminar of about twelve students. Do not use the standard statue-and-clay or ship-of-Theseus cases, since those are already familiar. Instead, inject a somewhat niche framing: the group must adjudicate whether distributed synthetic-biological systems—specifically, engineered microbial consortia in which different metabolic functions are distributed across physically separated organisms linked only by chemical signaling—count as a single composite object. The adjudication should turn on a hybrid criterion that combines causal-functional integration with what we can call modal fragility: the idea that replacing a component with a functionally identical but materially distinct substitute might, on some restricted-composition views, destroy the original whole rather than preserve it.

The deliverable should be a runnable simulation or structured deliberation with explicit rules, assigned roles (defenders of unrestricted universalism, defenders of restricted causal-modal composition, and neutral adjudicators who score consistency), concrete scenarios or materials they evaluate, and an incentive structure—perhaps consistency points rather than monetary rewards—that pressures participants to apply their criterion across a constructed sequence of cases. The rules should be arranged so that students build the sequence themselves and only at the end recognize that they have implicitly adopted incompatible commitments about what distinguishes a part from its environment.

Then provide a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the metaphysical positions (universalism, nihilism, standard restricted views, and the causal-modal fragility variant), identifies what will predictably go wrong—such as students conflating epistemic uncertainty about boundaries with metaphysical vagueness or smuggling in biological essentialism without argument—and be frank about which feature of the distributed-consortium framing the activity can reproduce and which feature (the actual biochemical signaling dynamics, for instance) it cannot, leaving the facilitator to supply the missing philosophical argument.

Please do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citation requests. The prompt should feel like it comes from someone who just argued with a friend and now wants a pedagogical tool that captures the same conceptual knot without relying on prior lectures.
