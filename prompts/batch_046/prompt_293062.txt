Specify the full list of desiderata that an adequate theory of political corruption must meet if it is to apply beyond Western liberal-legal contexts—specifically, in settings where clientelism, patron-client reciprocity, and ritual gift-exchange are structurally embedded rather than deviant. For each desideratum, state the motivating case or conceptual distinction it protects: for instance, the boundary between a gift that generates legitimate social obligation and a payment that subverts an institutional duty; the distinction between representation and capture; the difference between personal loyalty within a role and the privatization of public authority. Indicate which requirements are non-negotiable for any cross-culturally viable theory and which are parochial to legal-rational state frameworks. Address whether any core subset of these desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable—particularly, whether a theory can simultaneously honor local moral economies of reciprocity and maintain a universal normative core without contradiction or collapse. Keep the analytical weight on the criteria and their justifications rather than on grading existing accounts, though brief notes on where standard Western definitions fail are acceptable. Separate what is conceptually settled—such as that corruption requires a betrayal of entrusted authority rather than mere inequality of exchange—from what is genuinely contested among scholars of comparative politics and moral anthropology, and do not present any contested empirical claim, such as whether particular gift practices in specific societies constitute corruption, as the consensus view.
