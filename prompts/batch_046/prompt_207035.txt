Read William Golding’s Lord of the Flies through David Lewis’s account of convention in Convention (1969): conventions are regularities that solve recurring coordination problems by being sustained through common knowledge of the regularity itself, of aligned preferences, and of mutual rationality. On the island the boys initially establish conventions—using the conch to summon assembly, electing Ralph chief, assigning shelter and fire duties—that appear to solve a pure coordination problem. Yet these conventions collapse: Jack’s hunters defect, the conch loses authority, and the group shifts to a different equilibrium of tribal violence. Treat this collapse not merely as moral decay but as an instability in a Lewisian convention: ask whether the original norms ever satisfied Lewis’s conditions (transparent rationality, genuinely shared preferences) or whether they were pseudo-conventions maintained only by Ralph’s contingent authority. Now inject the following twist: model the island as a repeated coordination game in which each boy has a private type—either “civil” or “savage”—with an unknown distribution. On this model, the killing of Simon and then Piggy functions not only as moral rupture but as a public signal that updates common beliefs about how many savage types are present, thereby destroying the common knowledge of rational cooperation that Lewis requires. Does Lewis’s framework, which assumes open rationality, need augmentation with evolutionary or Bayesian dynamics about hidden types in order to explain why the conch convention dies so rapidly? And does Golding’s novel, read this way, dramatize a philosophical point that Lewis misses—namely, that conventions depend not only on common knowledge of rules but on common knowledge of the kind of agents who will follow them? Be precise about which scenes (the first assembly with the conch, Jack’s initial defiance, Piggy’s death, Ralph’s flight) illustrate each stage of decay, and conclude by saying whether the novel ultimately vindicates Lewis or shows that his conditions are too fragile for real social norms.
