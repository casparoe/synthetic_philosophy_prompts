I've been thinking on my own about the old challenge in Plato's story of Gyges — the shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible, then kills the king and takes the queen and throne. Most discussions I see treat it as a simple test of self-interest versus morality: if you knew you'd never be caught, why stay moral? But I've started wondering whether the classic "invisibility" is only one version of a deeper problem. There seem to be at least three distinct ways the link between action and accountability can break: the old physical kind, where no one sees what you did; a modern kind, where everything you do leaves traces but can't be tied back to you personally — anonymous digital acts, or actions taken through AI intermediaries that blur authorship; and a stranger kind, where predictive systems map your intentions with such accuracy that your choice feels exposed, or even predetermined, before you enact it. I want to know what an answer to "why be moral?" actually has to deliver — across all three scenarios — before I'd accept it.

Write the full specification — the desiderata — for any adequate theory of moral motivation or moral rationality facing Glaucon's challenge. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it: which of the three broken-link scenarios it addresses, what distinction it protects (for instance, between being praised for being moral and actually being moral, or between avoiding punishment and having a reason that survives when punishment is impossible), and whether it flatly contradicts well-established results in psychology or behavioral economics. Flag which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences — say, of rationalists versus contractarians versus virtue theorists. Then say whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: whether the list itself proves that no theory can meet every demand at once. Briefly, score the main contenders — a pure egoist or prudential view, a social-contract view, a Kantian rationalist view, and an Aristotelian virtue view — but keep that section short; the criteria are the point, not the scoreboard.
