I have been thinking on my own about whether human culture evolves through a genuinely Darwinian process, or whether calling cultural change "Darwinian" is mainly a metaphor that hides the actual mechanics of how ideas spread. The usual analogy compares memes to genes, but I keep returning to what looks like a structural mismatch. In biological evolution, replicators and interactors are distinct: genes are copied with high fidelity, while organisms are the vehicles that carry them and compete. In culture, if a meme is just a mental representation or a practiced behavior, then the replicator and the interactor seem to collapse into the same thing — a person's mind or actions — so there is no separate vehicle carrying a separable unit of selection. Compounding this, cultural transmission does not look like duplication; people observe results and reconstruct rules, meaning the "copy" is never identical to the "original" and fidelity is low. I want an essay that examines whether the Darwinian framework for culture can survive these two problems — the collapsed replicator-interactor distinction and reconstructive transmission — or whether cultural change is better explained by non-Darwinian dynamics such as intentional reasoning, social negotiation, or cognitive attraction. The essay should weigh the strongest defenses of a literal Darwinian account, including attempts to define memes functionally rather than structurally, against the argument that the analogy breaks down precisely where explanation is needed. It should reach and justify a clear conclusion about whether culture is genuinely Darwinian in a meaningful, explanatory sense, not merely metaphorically so.
