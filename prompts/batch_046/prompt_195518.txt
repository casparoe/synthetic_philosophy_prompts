I'm preparing an essay on what, if anything, we owe the dead when we dramatize their lives, and I've reached an impasse with a colleague over a concrete case. We were discussing a recent biopic of a mid-twentieth-century public intellectual — not a figure of world-historical scale, but someone with a substantial archive and living descendants — in which the filmmakers used synthetic voice reconstruction to have her deliver lines she never spoke, though the sentiments matched her published writings. The audience was informed of the dramatization, and the descendants had consented to the project.

My colleague argued that this practice amounts to a form of ventriloquism that violates what she calls a duty of narrative non-appropriation: by placing fabricated sentences into a reconstructed version of the person's own vocal signature, the filmmakers wrong the deceased herself, regardless of whether anyone living is deceived or distressed. She holds that the dead retain a form of symbolic moral standing — not as sentient subjects who can suffer, but as entities whose autonomous narrative identity should not be commandeered for artistic purposes without their consent, and that synthetic reconstruction is categorically worse than conventional acting because it collapses the distance between representation and impersonation.

I replied that once a person is dead, there is no remaining subject capable of being wronged, and that all relevant obligations run horizontally: to the audience (not to mislead) and to descendants or institutions (not to cause gratuitous harm or violate commitments). Since the audience was explicitly told the lines were dramatized and the descendants approved, no wrong was committed. I further argued that synthetic voice reconstruction differs only in degree, not in kind, from an actor's portrayal: both construct a fictional speech event, and the use of archival audio samples does not create a distinct moral category, any more than using a photograph to design makeup creates a special obligation.

My colleague then countered that this reduces all posthumous ethics to consequentialist concerns about living people, missing the deontological core of respect for the dead: we keep promises to the deceased and preserve graves not because the dead feel it, but because violating these practices corrupts our own moral character and severs continuity with the past. She concluded that if we reject wronging-the-dead altogether, we cannot explain why unauthorized grave-robbing for entertainment is worse than unauthorized grave-robbing for scientific research, since both may serve the living equally well.

Can you adjudicate this exchange point by point? Specifically: is synthetic voice reconstruction in biopics a categorically distinct ethical problem, or is it continuous with ordinary dramatization? Can there be a coherent, non-consequentialist sense in which the dead themselves are wronged, and if so, does it apply to fabricated speech? Finally, if obligations to the dead exist, do they derive primarily from respect for autonomy, from fidelity to explicit or implicit promises, or from some other source? I want to know not just which side is stronger overall, but exactly where each of us is right, where we are both missing something, and whether the disagreement ultimately hinges on a prior commitment about moral standing after death.
