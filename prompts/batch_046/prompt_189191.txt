Write a proposal for a one-semester philosophy-of-mind term paper (approximately 6,000 words) addressing whether a single first-person perspective—an "I"—can be genuinely distributed across two spatially separated brains that maintain a sustained, bidirectional, high-bandwidth neural interface. Frame the question through Hofstadter's idea that the self is not a localized entity but a self-referential symbolic loop or pattern distributed over neural architecture; ask specifically what conditions would have to hold for that loop to span two brains without fragmenting into two loops, and whether our ordinary criteria for personal identity (bodily continuity, psychological continuity with a single stream of consciousness) can adjudicate this case or instead presuppose a single substrate. Your proposal should state the precise conceptual question, explain why existing persistence theories leave it open (they typically assume one brain per self), outline the method—treating the first-person pronoun as a functional indexical anchored to loop-integrity rather than to anatomical boundaries, and testing that framework against staged split-brain and brain-computer-interface scenarios—and specify what a result would look like (e.g., a non-circular criterion for loop-unity that predicts whether an integrated subject reports unified or bifurcated experience) and what would count as failure (e.g., the loop-integrity criterion reducing to standard psychological-continuity conditions). Note that the project is conceptual and does not require original empirical data; keep the deliverable realistic for a single semester and do not include requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations.
