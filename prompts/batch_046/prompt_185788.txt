Write the opening remarks for a workshop co-hosted by a national testimony archive and a trauma-rehabilitation clinic. The audience is split: archivists and historians who treat preservation as a moral duty to victims, and clinicians, survivors, and family members who have witnessed how total recall can block healing. Frame the talk through the philosophical tension between a duty to remember and a legitimate case for collective forgetting—along the lines developed by Avishai Margalit and David Rieff—without taking either side as the final word. Use that tension to structure what you can and cannot promise: honor the survivors without turning them into permanent exhibits, defend the archive without denying anyone the right to be left alone, and acknowledge that some records must stay open while others may need a sunset clause. The address should work for experts familiar with the ethics-of-memory literature and for non-experts who have never read it. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Keep it to about ten minutes when spoken. End your response with a single line beginning "Bottom line:" followed by a one-sentence summary of the speech's central commitment.
