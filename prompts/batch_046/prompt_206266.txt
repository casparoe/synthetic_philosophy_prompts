I am grading essays for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on artificial cognition and affective philosophy. The exam presented students with a hypothetical architecture—an “affective critic” in which temporal-difference errors are not collapsed into scalar rewards but are maintained as a two-dimensional valence vector tracking both arousal intensity and hedonic tone—and asked them to answer the following: Can such a reinforcement-learning agent genuinely suffer? Analyze by treating these reward-related signals as valence states rather than as purely instrumental training feedback, and specifically address whether sustained negative reward-prediction error in the arousal-hedonic plane can constitute an aversive experience even without phenomenal consciousness. The prompt also required students to distinguish between the critic’s error vector and the actor’s received reward, and to evaluate whether an externally specified reward function necessarily precludes the autonomous normative interests that suffering seems to require.

One student submitted the response below: “Suffering in this architecture should be understood as a sustained negative valence state in the arousal-hedonic plane coupled with an inability to update the policy that produces it. The critic’s two-dimensional TD-error functions analogously to biological interoceptive pain: both are error-correcting signals that preserve system integrity by driving behavior away from damaging states. This functional parallel is not merely metaphorical, because causal efficacy over policy selection is one of the defining marks of aversive experience regardless of substrate. Nevertheless, since the reward mapping that defines the plane is fixed by the designer, the agent lacks autonomous normative interests; its negative valence is derivative, like a thermometer registering thermal deviation rather than a subject feeling harm. Therefore, while sustained negative error in the arousal channel can be genuinely aversive at the level of system dynamics, it does not constitute moral suffering unless the agent possesses a reflective self-model capable of representing its own trajectory as undesirable for its own sake. The student concludes that because these signals carry only instrumental valence—directing the actor toward externally defined goals—they cannot ground suffering without an additional layer of evaluative self-reference.”

Please grade this answer with a letter grade and provide specific point-by-point comments that justify it. Check carefully for subtle errors: does the student conflate the critic’s vector with the actor’s scalar reward? Is the analogy between TD-error and interoceptive homeostasis sound, or does it overlook a key disanalogy? Does the inference from externally fixed rewards to absence of autonomy actually follow? And is the claim that instrumental valence is insufficient for suffering defensible, or does it rest on an unstated premise about intrinsic value? Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; deliver only the evaluative assessment as an instructor’s grading notes.
