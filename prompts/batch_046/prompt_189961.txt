I have been discussing with a friend whether moral philosophy can genuinely accommodate tragic choices—cases where a fully informed agent, acting without weakness of will, must violate at least one binding moral requirement no matter what they do. We kept circling back to whether the sense of unavoidable loss in such situations reflects a real feature of moral reality or only a psychological shadow cast by our own limitations, and I want to push past the standard classroom examples.

Compare the view that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible—typically defended by claiming that rational deliberation must converge on a single best action, or that apparent conflicts dissolve upon closer inspection—with the opposing view that dilemmas are structurally necessary within a pluralistic moral framework where incommensurable values generate obligations that survive complete rational resolution. I want you to work with a specific conceptual injection: the notion of moral remainder, understood as the appropriate persistence of regret or guilt even when an agent has acted blamelessly with perfect information, and ask whether this remainder functions as evidence for a genuine dilemma or merely as an emotional residue that a fully rational agent should eventually dissolve. Deliver your comparison as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion: address, in order, the nature of obligation under conflict, the conditions for blameless wrongdoing, the evidential status of moral remainder, the possibility of rational arbitration between incommensurable values, and the structural commitments that value pluralism must accept if dilemmas are real.
