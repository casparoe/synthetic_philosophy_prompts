In my philosophy of logic seminar, our professor has offered the following verdicts on knowledge in epistemic closure scenarios. In the classic logical omniscience case—where an agent knows every premise of a valid proof but has not executed the inference—she denies knowledge of the conclusion. In the mathematical discovery case—where a mathematician knows the axioms and the inference rules but has not yet derived a particular theorem—she also denies knowledge. In the bounded-rationality case, however—where an agent knows that all men are mortal and that Socrates is a man, yet lacks the cognitive resources to apply modus ponens—she grants knowledge of the conclusion, treating the failure as a performance limitation rather than an epistemic defect. She has further said that in non-monotonic contexts, where an agent applies a default rule and later retracts upon learning an exception, the initial belief still counted as knowledge despite the revision. For next week’s discussion I have constructed a new case: an agent is embedded in a paraconsistent epistemic framework in which she knows both P and not-P, each justified by separate, non-interacting evidential streams; classically this would entail arbitrary Q by explosion, but the framework structurally blocks that derivation. Her belief-forming mechanisms are otherwise reliable, and she has not derived any arbitrary Q. Predict her verdict on whether the agent knows Q. Identify whether she is implicitly running a resource-bounded theory, a non-monotonic theory, or a structural-closure theory of knowledge; say which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog; and specify which further case would best distinguish the remaining candidates.
