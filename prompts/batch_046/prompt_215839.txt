As I have been preparing a graduate seminar on economic metaphysics in the early modern period, I found myself wondering how George Berkeley might have analysed a form of money that did not exist in his lifetime and that seems to sit uneasily with both his monetary theory and his broader immaterialist ontology. Specifically, I am thinking of a decentralised digital currency—let us call it a cryptographic token—that possesses no physical instantiation, carries no backing by bullion or sovereign authority, and whose continued existence consists solely in distributed ledger entries sustained by computational labour across a network. In The Querist, Berkeley conceives of money primarily as a counter or ticket that represents real labour and tangible commodities, and he repeatedly warns against conflating the sign with the wealth it signifies, particularly in the case of paper issues detached from material goods.

Given this commitment, together with his well-known denial of material substance in favour of ideas perceived by finite minds and ultimately sustained by divine will, how do you think he would have assessed the ontological status and economic function of such a token? Would he have treated the ledger entries as ideas in some philosophically relevant sense, or rather as empty fictions lacking the perceptual immediacy he requires of genuinely real entities? Furthermore, how might his practical concern with promoting the circulation of Ireland’s medium translate into a judgement about whether this form of currency facilitates or obstructs the exchange of actual goods and labour? Please develop the answer by drawing explicitly on the conceptual resources available in Berkeley’s published writings, without appealing to later commentators or to sources he could not have encountered.
