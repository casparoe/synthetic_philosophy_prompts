I was arguing with a friend about mental causation after our philosophy of mind class and realized my intuitions are kind of a mess, so I wrote down these cases to see if there's any actual coherence behind them.

Case one: I consciously decide to lift my arm and it goes up. It really feels like the decision did the causal work, not just the neurons firing in my motor cortex. Case two: a robot with sensors and actuators does the exact same motion based on input, and I have no problem saying there's zero mental cause there — it's all mechanics. Case three: a sleepwalker makes a full sandwich with perfect coordination but zero awareness, then wakes up feeling guilty about eating without permission. The guilt feels like a shadow that just tags along; it doesn't cause the behavior, it only reacts to it. Case four: two people have identicle brain scans and are both about to solve a math problem, but one suddenly gets an "aha" moment and writes the answer while the other stays stuck despite the same neural baseline. Here the mental breakthrough seems to change the outcome in a way the physical state alone didn't cover. Case five: my zombie twin is physically identical to me in every process, grabs a cup with no inner life at all, and that pushes me back toward thinking mental states are redundant, which conflicts hard with case one.

So my verdicts are all over the place — mental causation is real in conscious choice, absent in robots, impotent in sleepwalker guilt, necessary in the "aha" case, and redundant in the zombie comparison. Is there one principle, maybe about feedback loops or conscious accessibility, that can hold all of these together? Or am I forced to drop one? And what should I say about the partial-awareness case I cant resolve: someone half-asleep who starts driving and only vaguely remembers why later. Is there mental causation happening there, or is it just a confused physical process with a faint mental echo riding on top?
