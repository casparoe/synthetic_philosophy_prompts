I've been revisiting Plato's discussion in the Laws where supervised communal drinking is framed not as mere recreation or vice, but as a civic stress-test—something analogous to a basanos for the soul—that exposes whether a citizen retains rational self-governance when inhibitions drop. Rather than treating intoxication as only a harm to be minimized or a private liberty, this view makes regulated inebriation an institutional probe of character. Assuming that reading is roughly right, what are the most important conceptual implications if we take this Platonic framing seriously for modern drug policy—particularly for decriminalization regimes like Portugal's? I want the reasoning drawn out for three specific tensions: first, whether consumption spaces should be designed as observable trials of composure rather than anonymous harm-reduction zones; second, whether using observed behavior under intoxication as an implicit filter for civic competence or trust is morally defensible, or whether it covertly reintroduces a meritocratic gatekeeping mechanism; and third, whether the entire framework collapses into a costly signaling dynamic, where the capacity to drink gracefully under regulatory observation becomes a status display rather than a genuine virtue test, and how that interacts with harm-reduction logic that aims to reduce stigma rather than rank citizens. Please do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I am interested in the structural and normative implications, especially where they conflict with contemporary assumptions about autonomy and anonymity in public-health design.
