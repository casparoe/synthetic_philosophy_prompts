I've noticed in discussions about art and ethics that people endlessly debate whether a work can be aesthetically excellent when it expresses, or was created by, something morally bad—think of technically brilliant films that glorify cruelty, or beautiful poems written by people who did terrible things. The separatists say artistic value depends only on formal and emotional qualities; the moralists say ethical flaws necessarily diminish that value. The argument never resolves, and both sides just repeat familiar examples. My hypothesis is that this is partly verbal—"good art" means technical achievement to one group and overall human worth to the other—but I also suspect a deeper disagreement about whether moral understanding is part of aesthetic experience, or about which properties are essential to a work. Can you diagnose the stall: is it a verbal dispute, a substantive conflict about the nature of art, a clash of bedrock intuitions, or are the sides answering different questions under one heading? I want a definite position, with reasons, not a survey. To push past the usual back-and-forth, consider specifically whether the dispute hides a disagreement about "counterfactual stability"—whether praising a work requires that its value would survive if its immoral elements were removed or altered—and use that idea to explain what is really at stake. Given your diagnosis, tell me committedly what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a particular kind of test case, or a reframing of the question, and defend that choice rather than listing options.
