ok so me and my friend got into it last night about whether the agricultural revolution was basically a mistake, and now i'm trying to figure out if my own takes even hang together. here's roughly where i landed after a few beers and a lot of hand-waving:

(1) i don't think it makes sense to call it a "mistake" in any strong agent-level sense, because nobody made a collective decision with full infomation — it was just local groups adapting incrementally and getting locked in by population growth. there's no planner to blame.

(2) but i also think the "original affluence" story is mostly right about leisure and diet diversity, except people overstate how secure hunter-gatherer life was — seasonal starvation and violence were real, just different risk profiles.

(3) i think deep inequality comes mainly from heritable surplus storage, not farming per se. you can have agriculture without deep hierarchy if storage is communal or non-heritable, and some early farming societies show exactly that.

(4) here's the niche thing i added: the real harm wasn't lower average welfare but irreversibility — once population density crosses a threshold you literally can't revert to foraging, so even if farming turned out worse you were structurally stuck. i want to call this a "path lock" rather than a choice error.

(5) i think evolutionary/adaptive reasoning undermines counterfactual moral judgments about history — you can't say they "should've" resisted farming because competitive exclusion makes that structurally impossible.

(6) but i also think the right method for evaluating historical transitions is counterfactual welfare comparison: compare the actual trajectory to the best feasible branch available at each branching point.

so my question: is this package consistent? specifically, doesn't (4) smuggle in agent-level language ("locked in," "stuck") that (1) rejects? and doesn't (5) undermine (6) — if adaptive reasoning kills counterfactual "should," why am i still running counterfactual welfare comparisons? also, does (3) sit okay with (4), since (4) blames agriculture for enabling the path-lock via population growth, while (3) insists agriculture itself isn't the root cause of inequality?

i want an audit, not a lecture. rank the tensions by severity, say which ones dissolve on closer inspection, and tell me the least costly revision and what it costs me. please don't list everything as equally bad — some of these are probably just apparent.
