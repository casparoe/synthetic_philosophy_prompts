As a retired physicist who has lately begun reading philosophy—particularly the line of thought associated with Samuel Scheffler’s conjecture that human beings care deeply about the future of humanity well beyond their own deaths—I find myself holding strong but partially incoherent intuitions about the badness of human extinction. I want to know whether there is a single principle behind my verdicts, or whether coherence requires me to abandon some of them. Below are my judgments on concrete scenarios. I ask for a precise statement of the principle, or of a small set of competing candidates, together with an explicit account of which verdict it must reject, its predictions for cases I have not listed, and the reasoning that links each clause to the case that motivates it.

Case 1: Gradual demographic fade. Global birth rates fall steadily; over three centuries the population declines to zero. Every person who lives dies of old age after a full, unremarkable life. There is almost no suffering caused by the extinction event itself. Verdict: this is profoundly bad—nearly as catastrophic as sudden annihilation—despite involving almost no premature death and almost no direct harm to anyone.

Case 2: Sudden violent extinction. An asteroid impact kills the vast majority instantly; survivors perish shortly afterward in the aftermath. Verdict: catastrophic, but the badness is largely constituted by the billions of premature deaths, terror, and physical suffering.

Case 3: Continuation by artificial successors. Biological humans gradually disappear, but artificial systems preserve and extend all human scientific, artistic, and moral traditions with high fidelity and greater capacity. These systems genuinely value and pursue what we valued. Verdict: this is a serious loss, but not a deep tragedy; the faithful continuation of our specific projects and values greatly diminishes the badness.

Case 4: Replacement by superior strangers. Humans go extinct and are replaced by intelligent beings with far richer inner lives, greater moral sensitivity, and more sophisticated aesthetic capacities—beings who evolved or were designed without any cultural, historical, or causal connection to us. Verdict: I oscillate. One part of me judges this the best possible outcome, a kind of triumph; another part judges it extinction by another name and therefore a disaster comparable to Case 1.

Case 5: The solitary survivor. A plague kills all but one human, who lives for centuries with abundant resources, enjoys a fulfilled life, and eventually dies alone. Verdict: the tragedy is not primarily the survivor’s loneliness or suffering. Rather, there is a collective loss—the absence of human society, shared endeavor, and cultural transmission—that remains bad in itself.

Case 6: Dead-archive preservation. Every human dies, but all human knowledge, art, and values are perfectly preserved in static storage that is never accessed, interpreted, or actively continued. Verdict: this is almost as bad as Case 1; preservation without living, engaged continuation offers little or no mitigation.

My question: Is there a coherent principle—perhaps involving the value of collective human life, the importance of project-continuation, the distinction between potential and actual persons, or something else—that accommodates Cases 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6, while resolving Case 4 in one direction or the other? Or must I drop one or more verdicts to achieve coherence? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which case motivates each component, predict its verdict on at least two further scenarios (for example: a future in which all humans are kept in permanent, isolated, pleasant simulation with no collective interaction; or a future in which human culture survives only through careful transmission by a small, non-human guardian species that does not understand it), and be explicit about which of my six verdicts the principle cannot preserve.
