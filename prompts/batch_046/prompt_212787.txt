As a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science in earnest, I have become preoccupied with the problem of hierarchical observation: any measuring apparatus is itself composed of the very matter and forces it seeks to quantify, so the observer is never fully outside the system observed. This strikes me as structurally analogous to the tangled hierarchy illustrated by Escher's Drawing Hands, in which each hand draws the other into existence, and to the notion of an inviolate level — the idea that in a properly stratified system there must exist a base stratum that initiates causation but is not itself fully determined by the levels above it. In nature, however, such a base often seems to dissolve, leaving a loop without an obvious exit.

I would like a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets followed by a quatrain, with an ABA rhyme scheme and two refrains that alternate and finally converge in the closing couplet — that takes this tension seriously. Let the first refrain be spoken from the position of the observing instrument or model (the hand that draws), and the second refrain from the position of the phenomenon being observed (the hand being drawn). Use the tercets to build the naive picture of a clean separation between measurement and object, explanation and explained, then allow the form itself to undermine it: the refrains should begin to echo and interfere with each other, and by the quatrain they should appear side by side, not resolved but intertwined. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the logic of strange loops and hierarchical containment recognizes the philosophical move rather than a mere name-drop; someone unfamiliar should still find it a coherent, moving poem. Do not resolve the tension with either a triumphalist reduction to physics or a mystical surrender to paradox — let the loop remain open, exactly as the image does.
