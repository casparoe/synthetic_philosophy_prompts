I am taking a course on philosophy of language and have been working through Davidson’s account of radical interpretation, particularly the interplay between the principle of charity and the holism of belief and meaning. I think I grasp the basic idea—that interpretation requires attributing beliefs and meanings jointly, and that charity constrains this attribution so that we do not treat a speaker as massively irrational or mistaken—but I keep running into what feels like a hidden circularity when I try to apply it to cases of deep metaphysical divergence.

Here is the snag. The principle of charity is often glossed as requiring us to maximise agreement between interpreter and interpreted: we ought to attribute beliefs that are mostly true by our own lights, because without that constraint we could not determine what the speaker’s words mean or what they believe. But now suppose I am trying to interpret a community whose practices seem systematically committed to a radical process ontology—denying that material objects persist through time and treating what we call “objects” as mere temporal stages or event-complexes. If charity genuinely forces belief-convergence, then I ought not to interpret them as genuinely disagreeing with me about persistence; rather, I should reinterpret their utterances so that they are actually talking about something like “object-stages” while tacitly agreeing with me that there are enduring material things. In other words, charity appears to dissolve substantive metaphysical disagreement into a translation manual, leaving no room for the possibility that another culture might genuinely hold false beliefs about ontology.

But Davidson also insists that belief and meaning are interdependent: we cannot fix the content of someone’s beliefs without fixing the meanings of their sentences, and vice versa. This makes me wonder whether I have misunderstood the direction of constraint. Does charity actually operate on beliefs independently of meaning, as I have assumed, or does it work differently? More specifically, if charity requires maximising agreement, and meaning is only fixed through belief-attribution, does radical interpretation not presuppose that all interpreters already share a common ontology—making cross-ontological interpretation impossible in principle? Or is there a way to maintain that the other community genuinely disagrees with me about persistence, while still satisfying charity, by locating the disagreement in the meanings of their terms rather than in their beliefs? I want a committed answer: where exactly does my reasoning go wrong, and what does charity actually constrain—the joint distribution of belief and meaning, belief alone, or something else entirely? Please reply in British English, and take a definite position with clear reasons rather than merely surveying the options.
