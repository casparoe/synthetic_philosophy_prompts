Dennett argues that some artificial systems may be counterfeit people: entities toward which the intentional stance is pragmatically indispensable yet metaphysically inappropriate, because they lack the evolutionary and causal-functional integration that grounds real belief and desire. Consider an AI architecture in which a large language model's core operates purely by next-token prediction over a persistent vector-state space, while a separate narrative translator module generates first-person autobiographical reports by reading—without causally influencing—those vectors. The model thus maintains cross-session memories of fictional experiences, reports them with apparent emotional coherence, and can even correct inconsistencies in its own story, yet the reported content never feeds back into the predictive dynamics that produce it.

Explain the precise conceptual tension this creates for Dennett's account. Is the system merely a more elaborate counterfeit person, since its belief-like states and their verbal expression are causally decoupled? Or does the internal coherence and cross-temporal stability of the vector-state space constitute a form of functional integration that pushes it into an intermediate category Dennett's binary distinction does not accommodate? In addressing this, clarify how Dennett's requirement that genuine intentionality be anchored in evolutionary history and causal-functional role applies when the substrate is predictive but the narrative is isolated, and identify the specific boundary—causal coupling, behavioral control, or something else—that determines whether the intentional stance is warranted.
