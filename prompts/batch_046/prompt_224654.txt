I came away from a long, circular argument with a colleague in evolutionary biology about whether the units-of-selection debate is genuinely empirical or merely a bookkeeping dispute over where to draw system boundaries, and I want to stage that circularity as a classroom experience rather than resolve it by lecture. Please design a 90-minute, in-person simulation for an upper-level philosophy-of-biology seminar of about twelve students that makes the boundary problem visceral before anyone names it. Divide the group into four rotating roles—gene-level analysts, organism-level analysts, group-level analysts, and boundary arbiters—and run them through three rounds of an evolutionary scenario presented via cards: first a classic altruism case, then a case in which the organisms systematically alter their soil or social environment in ways that are inherited across generations, and finally a case in which that inherited modification feeds back onto the selective environment so that what was treated as “outside” in round one is now structurally inside the reproductive unit. Give each analyst group a point-allocation rule that rewards predictive accuracy but penalizes them if the arbiters show that their assigned unit requires smuggling an external factor back in; the arbiters win by demonstrating that any single-level account must borrow from another level to close its explanatory loop. The conceptual tension should emerge from the scoring conflicts themselves: participants should discover that fixing the unit requires deciding in advance what counts as environment, inheritance, and interaction, and they should feel that decision as forced rather than discovered. Specify the timeline, the materials needed (scenario cards, score sheets, role badges), and the rules for rotating roles so that every student spends time both defending a level and challenging boundaries.

The debrief should map each round onto the standard conceptual distinctions—replicator, interactor, selective environment—then explicitly onto the twist I have injected: the inherited ecological modification that blurs the line between selective environment and unit of inheritance, so that what looks like group-level cooperation might also be readable as gene-level selection operating inside a self-constructed niche. Tell me precisely what will predictably go wrong the first time—for instance, whether students assigned to defend gene-level explanations will resist conceding that the modified environment belongs inside the system, and how the facilitator should handle that resistance without resolving the dispute—plus what the facilitator must supply afterward because the activity cannot encode it, specifically the formal covariance partitioning of quantitative multilevel selection theory. Be frank about which feature of the idea the simulation cannot reproduce, such as the actual measurement of selection gradients across nested levels in real populations, and state clearly that the facilitator must supply that formal scaffolding if the conceptual point is to stick. Finally, keep the prompt self-contained: do not ask for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not hinge the design on named authors or specific texts that a model without web access could not verify; the idea should live in the rules and the payoffs, not in references supplied after the fact.
