I have been trying to make sense of the rulings issued by our state’s Ballot Integrity Council over the past several election cycles, not as a lawyer or political scientist but just as someone who follows the ballot language closely. So far the Council has struck down a referendum that would have allowed voters to recall an appointed, non-elected city manager; allowed a measure that switched local council elections to ranked-choice voting; struck down a measure that would have required direct voter approval for specific municipal contracts; allowed a measure directing the legislature to adopt broad, prospective carbon-reduction targets; struck down a measure requiring that every budget item over one million dollars be individually approved by referendum; and allowed a measure mandating that an independent, nonpartisan fiscal impact statement accompany every future ballot question. I can see more than one coherent principle running through these decisions—perhaps a line between general democratic procedure and specific administrative execution, or perhaps a distinction between one-time policy choices and the creation of standing mechanisms that permanently alter how government functions—but I am not sure which one actually explains the pattern.

The case now before them is one I have not seen addressed anywhere in the commentary. A proposed constitutional amendment would establish that any future referendum concerning economic regulation must first be considered by a randomly selected citizens’ assembly of one hundred residents, who would study the issue for three months and draft a single recommended text. The subsequent referendum vote would then be restricted to a simple approve-or-reject decision on that assembly’s text, with no voter-initiated alternatives, no floor amendments, and no option to send a different proposal back to the legislature. I want to know what the Council should say for consistency with its own record: should this pass or fail? I am especially interested in the competing rules that both fit the earlier verdicts, what each rule predicts about this sortition-filtered referendum, your best guess at the actual outcome with the reasoning behind it, and—crucially—which further hypothetical case would best tell those rules apart. For that last part, I have in mind something like a measure that creates a permanent, purely advisory audit office—using existing staff and having no binding effect on ballot options—to see whether the Council’s real concern is the creation of a lasting institutional body or something else about constraining direct voter choice.

Could you walk through the main candidate principles, apply them to this new assembly-filter proposal, state your predicted verdict with an honest account of why you prefer one reading over the others, identify the closest past analog from the Council’s record, and then specify the distinguishing follow-up case and how each principle would treat it? I am less looking for a bare yes-or-no than for the logic that makes the Council’s body of decisions cohere—or fail to cohere—once this kind of deliberative gate is added to the mix.
