I've been working through the philosophical implications of pain asymbolia—the neurological condition in which patients report experiencing pain sensations yet deny that these sensations are unpleasant, threatening, or in any way bad—particularly in relation to debates about whether pain is essentially bad or only contingently so. In the standard philosophical reading, asymbolia demonstrates a dissociation between the sensory-discriminative dimension of pain (its location, intensity, and qualitative character) and its affective-motivational dimension (the felt urgency to withdraw and avoid). On this view, the badness of pain resides entirely in the latter component, which is precisely what asymbolic patients lack. However, I want to consider an alternative interpretation that treats asymbolia not merely as a missing affective layer, but as a disruption of what might be called normative binding: the integration of a sensory signal with a self-referential evaluation that this particular state is harmful-to-me and therefore to-be-eliminated. Compare these two interpretations—the affective-dissociation view and the normative-binding view—with respect to what each implies about the metaphysical structure of pain's badness. Specifically, address whether each allows for the conceptual possibility of a being that experiences the full sensory profile and even the aversive feeling of pain, yet lacks any normative orientation toward its cessation.

To sharpen the comparison, consider a hypothetical condition I will call motivational blindness. Imagine a subject who retains both the sensory qualities and the full aversive affect of severe pain—she reports it as intensely awful and expresses a sincere verbal desire for it to stop—yet exhibits no behavioral avoidance, no instrumental learning to prevent recurrence, no physiological stress responses oriented toward escape, and no preference-revealing choices that distinguish pain states from neutral ones. Would the existence of such a case provide stronger support for the normative-binding interpretation than for the affective-dissociation interpretation? Or does it instead suggest that the two views are not competing explanations but descriptions operating at different levels—one phenomenological, the other functional? Furthermore, evaluate whether either interpretation genuinely allows for pain to be bad in a purely internal sense that requires no behavioral manifestation whatsoever, or whether both ultimately tie the badness of pain to some form of motivational or normative structure that could, in principle, be absent. Please justify your answer by reasoning from the conceptual architecture of each view rather than by appealing to specific empirical case studies.
