I am taking a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and after class my study partner and I tried to extend Frankfurt’s original counterexample to the principle of alternative possibilities into what she calls a “distributed counterfactual architecture” argument. She thinks it shows that Frankfurt-style reasoning, applied consistently, undermines itself by erasing the very distinction between coerced and uncoerced agents. I find the conclusion absurd—if it went through, it would mean that the presence of hidden backup mechanisms, even ones that never activate, globally nullifies every moral distinction we care about—but I cannot isolate the exact misstep, and I want to preserve both the standard Frankfurt intuition in local cases and the idea that responsibility tracks something about the actual causal history rather than mere counterfactual possibilities. Here is her argument, laid out step by step.

(1) In Frankfurt’s canonical case, Jones decides to lie of his own reasons and does so; Black stands ready to force the decision should Jones waver, but never intervenes. Jones is morally responsible for lying despite lacking an alternative possibility.

(2) If responsibility can be preserved in this local, single-decision structure—where a counterfactual intervener is present but dormant—then the presence or absence of alternative possibilities is not what grounds responsibility in any structurally analogous case.

(3) Every human decision occurs within a complex system (neural, social, environmental) that contains redundant pathways. For any actual choice C, we can in principle identify at least one such pathway that would have enforced C had the agent shown preliminary signs of deviating, even if that pathway never activates.

(4) Therefore, for every actual human choice, there exists within the agent’s distributed architecture a dormant counterfactual mechanism that would have compelled C; consequently, no human agent ever genuinely possesses an available alternative in the actual sequence of events.

(5) If the principle of alternative possibilities requires that one could have done otherwise in the actual world, then PAP is false not merely for Frankfurt-style exceptions but for all human action.

(6) But then the distinction between coerced and uncoerced action collapses: in both cases the agent lacks available alternatives, since even the “free” agent is embedded in a distributed architecture full of dormant enforcers.

(7) Yet we still judge that coerced agents are excused precisely because they lacked alternatives that the uncoerced agent possessed; if both lack them, we have eliminated the normative distinction Frankfurt cases were meant to preserve rather than explain.

(8) The only escape is to deny (2) and confine Frankfurt’s result to isolated local cases, but then we owe a principled account of why local dormancy is compatible with responsibility while global, architecture-wide dormancy is not—an account that cannot simply beg the question by appealing back to responsibility itself.

She claims this demonstrates that Frankfurt reasoning is self-undermining. I suspect there is either a subtle scope shift between counterfactual possibility and actual availability, or an equivocation on what “lacks alternatives” means in (1) versus (6), but I am unsure which premise bears the cost. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it require me to give up? I would strongly prefer to keep (1) intact, and I want to maintain that responsibility depends on features of the actual causal path—such as whether the agent’s own reasons were the operative cause—rather than on the mere modal presence of backup mechanisms. If I must pay a price elsewhere, I want to know exactly what it is.
