I am taking a seminar on political philosophy, and I have grown dissatisfied with treatments of Burkean conservatism that present Chesterton’s fence either as a rhetorical flourish or as a blanket prohibition on institutional change. I want to work out what an adequate normative theory of institutional reform must actually deliver if it takes that fence seriously as an epistemic constraint rather than as a status-quo prejudice. Write the full list of desiderata such a theory must meet, with the motivating case or conceptual consideration behind each requirement, and flag which are non-negotiable and which belong to particular camps.

The theory must be able to handle: (i) cases in which the original purpose of an institution is genuinely irrecoverable, yet its removal exposes hidden “dependency shadows”—load-bearing relationships that only become visible upon collapse—without allowing defenders to invoke opacity as an unfalsifiable shield; (ii) the distinction between preserving an institution for its intrinsic value and preserving it as a repository of “epistemic debt,” the accumulated but largely unarticulated practical reasoning of prior generations; (iii) the Burkean intergenerational contract, treating society as a partnership among the dead, the living, and those yet unborn, and therefore constraining how radically present generations may restructure shared institutions; (iv) the conditions under which rapid democratic correction is legitimate despite the fence principle, particularly when an institution causes active, identifiable harm to present persons; and (v) the difference between a fence that protects a genuinely valuable practice and one that merely entrenches contingent power relations, without collapsing that distinction into a simple progressive versus conservative dichotomy.

For each desideratum, state the case behind it, indicate whether you regard it as jointly satisfiable with the others, and identify any subset you think is jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether full transparency about institutional purpose can coexist with the humility about accumulated wisdom that Burke demands. Then briefly assess how a strict Burkean view, a rational-reconstructionist liberal reformism, and a pragmatist “fence abolitionism” fare against your list, but keep the weight firmly on the criteria rather than on scoring the contenders.

Please answer in British English. In your response, state explicitly how confident you are in each part of the analysis, and single out the specific point about which you are least certain—whether that concerns the joint satisfiability of transparency and humility, the status of dependency shadows as a genuine phenomenon, or another matter entirely.
