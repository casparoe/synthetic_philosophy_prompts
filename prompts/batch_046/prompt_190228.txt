Having spent three decades in experimental biophysics before turning recently to philosophy of science, I remain puzzled by how my former colleagues treated mental experiments—reasoning about an idealized membrane, for instance, without ever measuring one—as yielding knowledge rather than mere heuristic convenience. The literature asks whether such cases work through logical derivation, tacit empirical generalization, or some form of modal intuition about physical possibility. Rather than a survey, I want a defended, dependency-aware prioritization among the open lines: whether scientific thought experiments are genuinely a priori; how they avoid circular dependence on the theories they are meant to test; whether quantum-mechanical cases invoke a distinct epistemic mechanism from classical ones; and how they differ epistemically from computer simulations that now replace mental imagery. Please rank the two or three questions that are most upstream—those whose resolution would restructure evaluation of the others—according to tractability for someone with scientific literacy but no formal training in modal logic or epistemology, explanatory payoff for actual scientific practice, and interdependence among the questions. I suspect part of the confusion stems from an unremarked distinction between counterfactual isolation of variables and what one might call modal anchoring in background physical law; if that distinction is sound, say which debates dissolve and which remain substantive once it is applied. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria, not a reading list; I am preparing a long-form essay and need to know precisely where limited attention should go.
