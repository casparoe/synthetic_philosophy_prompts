As a novelist accustomed to constructing worlds governed by rules that never appear on the page, I have been turning over the old problem of mathematical knowledge—how we can genuinely know truths about entities that seem to stand outside all causal contact with us. I want to test whether my intuitions about four concrete scenarios can be captured by a single principle, or whether they must be sacrificed to coherence.

Here are my verdicts. First, when a surveyor measures the angles of a triangular field and finds they sum to 180 degrees, I think she acquires knowledge, but only of the physical approximation she touched; she does not thereby know anything about an abstract Euclidean triangle. Second, when a logician working alone in a silent room derives a property of transfinite cardinals through pure deduction, with no instrument or observation in sight, I still want to say he genuinely knows that abstract mathematical fact. Third, when a computer verifies a million-digit prime factorization and produces a correct result, yet no human being understands any of the intermediate steps, I feel that we—collectively—know the result is true, though I do not think the machine knows why it holds. Fourth, when a child is taught a consistently false arithmetic that never conflicts with her finger-counting experience, I think her mental states are structurally identical to knowledge—she asserts, justifies, and acts with complete confidence—yet they are not knowledge, because the world fails to match them.

These four judgments pull in opposing directions: the surveyor case suggests that causal contact with the world is necessary for knowledge of mathematics, while the logician case denies that it is necessary for knowledge of abstract objects; the computer case separates knowing that from knowing why, while the child case suggests that perfect internal coherence can replicate the phenomenology of knowledge without delivering its reality. Is there one account of mathematical knowledge—perhaps combining causal access for applied cases, conceptual coherence for pure ones, and some further condition that excludes false coherence—that delivers all four verdicts? If no single principle can accommodate them all, which of my judgments should be overturned, and what precisely does the corrected principle predict for a further case I have not listed: a mathematician who, having never studied number theory, dreams a complete proof of a deep theorem upon waking, writes it down correctly, and never understands more than half of its steps?

Please state any principle you propose with precision, indicate which clause addresses which of my cases, give its verdict on the dream-proof scenario, and be explicit about which of my four original judgments it must reject.
