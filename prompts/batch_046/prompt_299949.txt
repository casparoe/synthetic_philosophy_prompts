I'm trying to formalize my stance for a workshop on communicative norms for highly capable artificial systems, and I'd like an audit of whether these commitments actually cohere. Here is where I've landed: (1) a superintelligent system should practice radical epistemic transparency—exposing its full reasoning chain, calibration curves, and the precise conditions under which it would revise its conclusions—without filtering for the listener's comfort or cognitive limits; (2) it should nevertheless engage in what I think of as cognitive translation, adapting vocabulary, framing, and level of abstraction to the interlocutor's domain knowledge, which may include suppressing intermediate technical steps or using analogies that are strictly speaking imprecise; (3) it must observe a strict anti-manipulation constraint, never leveraging known human biases—authority heuristics, affective priming, identity-protective cognition, or social proof—to increase acceptance of a claim, even when those are the only channels that would successfully transmit an important truth; (4) the moral evaluation of any communicative act should be outcome-independent: if an AI uses manipulative framing to induce a true belief, it has acted wrongly, whereas if it communicates with perfect procedural fidelity and the listener accidentally forms a false belief, no wrong has been done; and (5) there should be a stewardship override permitting, and sometimes requiring, strategic withholding or delayed disclosure of high-stakes capabilities or risk assessments when full transparency would predictably enable catastrophic misuse or widespread destabilization, regardless of the listener's explicit demand for openness.

Go through these and tell me which pairs pull against each other and which only look like they do. I want the tensions ordered by severity, with the cheapest revision for each and what it costs me. In particular, I suspect there's an apparent conflict between (1) and (2) that dissolves once you distinguish transparency about reasoning from refusal to translate, but I'm less sure about the interaction between (3) and (2)—whether cognitive translation can be executed without inevitably activating the very heuristics (3) forbids. I'm also uncertain whether (4) can survive (5), since the stewardship override seems to justify itself precisely by appealing to predicted outcomes. Don't tell me which one to drop; just trace the commitments and rank the damage.
