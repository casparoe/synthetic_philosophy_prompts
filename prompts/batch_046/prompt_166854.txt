You serve as the senior ethics-review officer for a regulatory agency that issues binding guidance documents blending scientific findings with normative recommendations. Your staff must evaluate drafts in thirty minutes without consulting external philosophy literature, and any audit result requires your signature. Translate the conceptual machinery behind the is-ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy into a reusable “Derivation Audit” checklist—explicit steps, ordered tests, and decision nodes in plain language—that a policy analyst without philosophical training can apply repeatedly.

The audit must preserve three distinctions that survive simplification: first, descriptive claims about states of affairs versus prescriptive claims about what should be done; second, implicit bridge principles that license inference from the former to the latter versus explicit normative premises; and third, definitional reductions of value terms to natural properties versus substantive identity claims that remain open to further questioning. It must also include a “normative leakage” scan for value-laden language disguised as neutral description—terms such as “healthy,” “normal,” “balanced,” or “optimized”—and a stability check that asks whether the bridge principle still holds under a counterfactual change in the descriptive facts.

Structure the deliverable as a step-by-step procedure with the following elements: an initial extraction of all factual and normative propositions; a mapping of logical connections between them; a classification of each bridge principle by type (instrumental, constitutive, or definitional); an open-question test applied specifically to any definitional bridge; explicit markers indicating where the procedure requires irreducible judgment rather than mechanical application; and a sign-off rubric that records confidence levels and residual ambiguity. Fix the role, the frequency and stakes of the decisions (binding regulatory guidance, signed off by you, run under deadline), and the constraints (thirty minutes, no web access, no philosophy training required).

Apply the audit to three hypothetical draft recommendations. First, a public-health guideline deriving “we ought to reduce sodium intake” from epidemiological data linking sodium to hypertension, where the bridge principle appeals to a tacit account of well-being. Second, an environmental standard deriving “we must preserve wetland biodiversity” from ecological descriptions of species interdependence, where the audit should flag that the procedure yields a result you are likely to resist—namely, that the derivation depends on an unstated axiology of ecological integrity that the audit cannot validate internally. Third, a clinical guideline describing “normal” hormone ranges that implicitly pathologizes deviations without explicit normative argument, testing the leakage scan.

Conclude by stating precisely what the Derivation Audit cannot do: it cannot generate normative premises from descriptive ones, it cannot adjudicate between competing bridge principles without external value commitments, and it cannot replace institutional deliberation about which axiologies should govern regulatory action. The answer should be self-contained, should not invoke bibliographies, citations, or reading lists, and should be executable by a practitioner who has no web access and only the checklist in front of them.
