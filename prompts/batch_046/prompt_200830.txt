Rank the following five strategies for constructing a reference class when forecasting the long-run institutional stability of a contemporary liberal democracy over the next century, using only an outside-view approach that relies on base rates rather than inside causal mechanisms. For each strategy, assign a tier (S, A, B, C, D) reflecting how well it balances predictive validity against selection bias and reference-class ambiguity, then provide a reliability score from 1 to 10 and a single-sentence justification.

1. The imperial lifecycle class: treat the democracy as analogous to historical empires such as Rome, the Ottoman Empire, and Britain, matched by bureaucratic maturity and technological level, then apply their mean elapsed time to a major systemic crisis.

2. The democratic cohort class: restrict the reference class to liberal democracies older than fifty years and compute the historical frequency of constitutional crisis or rapid executive consolidation within that set.

3. The regime-transition class: pool every documented transition from a high-capacity state to authoritarianism, severe state failure, or major capacity loss across all eras and geographies, weighting each case equally regardless of prior institutional form.

4. The synthetic stress-index class: build a continuous multidimensional reference space using observable structural variables—such as debt-to-output dynamics, elite polarization metrics, and civil-military integration scores—identify the nearest-neighbor institutions in that space, and derive base rates from their outcomes rather than from categorical membership.

5. The survivorship-filtered peer class: include only those institutions that have already survived at least two systemic shocks comparable in magnitude to the present threat, then compute the conditional base rate of surviving a third shock.

After completing the ranking, identify which of the five comes closest to the standard outside-view methodology used in structured forecasting tournaments, and which one most risks smuggling inside-view causal judgments under statistical cover. Briefly explain the specific feature—reference-class construction, weighting scheme, or conditioning logic—that makes the latter a disguised inside view.
