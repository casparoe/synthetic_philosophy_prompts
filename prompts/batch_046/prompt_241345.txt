I've been auditing a course on conservative political philosophy, and in discussions with friends afterward I keep describing an argumentative pattern I can't quite place in the literature. Imagine a proposal to streamline an internal bureaucratic procedure—say, collapsing a multi-stage approval into one direct decision. The opponent doesn't defend each stage on its own merits, nor invoke Chesterton's fence in the usual sense of warning that we shouldn't remove something whose purpose we don't understand. Instead, they concede the larger institution may need reform, yet insist that this particular procedural drag serves a hidden cognitive function—forcing a deliberative pause—without being able to specify the mechanism. If you propose a simpler substitute, like a mandatory waiting period, they shift ground: the specific form of the steps carries tacit local knowledge calibrated to context that can't be reconstructed from scratch. Pressed further, they say the burden of proof lies with the reformer because no visible harm has occurred. A friend in organizational behavior suggested path dependency, but that misses the rhetorical shift and the selective application to sub-components of a system the defender otherwise accepts should change. Does this configuration have an established name—perhaps in institutional design or conservative reasoning? How should its boundaries be drawn: must the defender be open to broader institutional reform, or can it apply to any embedded procedure? How does it differ precisely from Chesterton's fence, from ordinary precautionary reasoning, and from status quo bias? Please lead with your bottom-line identification—either the closest established term or an explicit statement that none exists—and keep your answer to a single paragraph of no more than 220 words.
