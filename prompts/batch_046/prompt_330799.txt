I'm putting together a unit on moral worth for my grad seminar and I was arguing with a colleague over coffee about how institutions actually treat motives. There's this thing with civilian bravery awards — you know, the ones given out by municipal boards or national honor committees — where the nomination packet has to include a specific attestation that the rescuer acted without regard to personal risk and not out of a desire for recognition. The form literally asks witnesses to rate wether the act seemed dutiful versus impulsive or emotionally driven. Meanwhile, Good Samaritan statutes in the same jurisdictions shield anyone who helps, regardless of wether they were trying to be a hero, just covering their own guilt, or even hoping for a reward. So one part of the system cares deeply about the internal reason, and the other part treats the reason as legally irrelevant.

What theory of moral worth does that pairing presuppose? Does the award practice commit us to a Kantian view that only actions done from duty have full moral value, and if so, does it also commit us to thinking that impulsive courage has only derivative or no value? Or can a virtue-ethical framework explain why we honor the right reason without saying that the impulsive rescuer lacks worth? And then there's the legal side: if Good Samaritan law is indifferent to motive, does that mean our legal institutions presuppose that moral worth is none of the law's business, or just that tractability forces us to ignore it? I'm especially puzzled about wether these two practices are consistent — does honoring pure motives in awards while ignoring them in liability rules presuppose one unified view, or are they just pragmatically compartmentalized? And what would have to change in either practice if we adopted a consistently outcome-based or consistently reason-sensitive framework?
