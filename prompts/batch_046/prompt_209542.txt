I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy of science serving as a voting member of my university's Senate Committee on Research Governance. At Thursday's meeting we must decide whether to approve the Office of Institutional Strategy's proposed Logical Reconstruction Protocol (LRP) for interdisciplinary research funding over the next three years. The protocol is explicitly modeled on Rudolf Carnap's distinction between internal questions—those resolvable by logical and empirical methods within a chosen framework—and external questions—those concerning the selection and justification of the framework itself, which require practical and political deliberation. Its architects propose that funding priorities be established through democratic, value-laden deliberation (the external stage) and then executed via an algorithmic optimization engine that treats disciplinary weights and impact metrics as purely internal to the fixed framework. They also invoke Carnap's tolerance principle to justify allowing each college to adopt its own precisely formalized evaluation framework, arguing that logical pluralism in planning structures is permissible so long as the rules are explicit.

A vocal faction on the committee, led by our chair, contends that this bifurcation fundamentally misreads Carnap's political and planning philosophy: they maintain that for Carnap, planning was never meant to become technocratic execution after a single democratic choice, but rather required continuous democratic accountability, and that treating value-laden trade-offs as internal once a framework is adopted contradicts his insistence that practical reasoning about ends cannot be fully formalized. Defenders of the LRP reply that Carnap's logical empiricism precisely authorizes this separation, and that his sympathy for rational social planning supports deploying formal tools to implement collectively chosen ends without reopening every value dispute during each allocation cycle. The committee needs a formal opinion recommending a concrete course—approve, reject, or approve with binding conditions—together with a specific timeline or sunset clause, that can be circulated before the vote.
