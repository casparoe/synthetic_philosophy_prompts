I keep returning to a specific puzzle in debates about the timing of conscious experience, particularly in the visual illusion where two discrete, differently colored flashes presented in rapid succession at separate locations are reported as a single object moving smoothly through intermediate hues, despite the absence of any physical intermediate stimulus. The philosophical literature usually divides explanations into two revisionary camps. In the first, the subject genuinely experiences the discrete flashes in real time, but the brain subsequently revises the memory trace so that the report includes a continuous transition that was never experienced. In the second, there is never any experience of an intermediate state at all; instead, the brain constructs a unified narrative of motion and color change only after both stimuli have arrived, and the report reflects this post-hoc fabrication rather than any prior experiential content.

What interests me is a narrower configuration that arises when we split reporting by time. In some experimental setups, subjects can be cued to respond immediately after the first flash; in that condition they typically report only discrete colors and positions, with no mention of motion or intermediates. If they respond only after the second flash, they reliably report the smooth transition. This divergence is usually treated as data to be explained, but I notice that the two camps interpret it in diametrically opposite ways. The first camp takes the immediate report as authoritative: it reveals the true conscious content (discrete flashes), and the later continuous report is therefore a corrupted memory—precisely the retrospective revision they predicted. The second camp takes the delayed report as authoritative: it reveals the completed conscious construction, and the immediate report is therefore an incomplete or premature snapshot of a process that had not yet finished generating the experience.

So the same behavioral pattern—discrete reports under immediate cueing, continuous reports under delayed cueing—is claimed by both sides as confirming evidence for mutually exclusive metaphysical pictures. One reads the asymmetry as proof of retrospective corruption; the other reads the identical asymmetry as proof of prospective construction still in progress. Is there an established name for this specific kind of interpretive deadlock, in which a single temporal reporting asymmetry is used to underwrite both of two empirically indistinguishable revisionary accounts? If there is no standard term, is the deadlock itself recognized as a distinct problem in the philosophy of temporal consciousness, or is it typically dissolved by some further distinction—perhaps about the authority of first-person reports at different delays—that I am missing? And where exactly are the edges of the phenomenon: does it apply only when the two reports are separated by a stimulus boundary, or does it generalize to any case where an immediate and a reflective report diverge in content, regardless of whether a revisionary story is available?
