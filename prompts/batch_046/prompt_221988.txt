I'm a doctoral student in moral philosophy drafting a workshop paper on responsibility, and I want to check whether my own stance on moral luck is jointly tenable. Here is my profile: I accept Nagel's fourfold taxonomy—causal, constitutive, circumstantial, and resultant luck all genuinely shape moral assessment—but I want to bracket only resultant luck (the actual outcomes) from direct blame, while allowing constitutive and circumstantial luck to inform our evaluation of agents. I think Williams-style agent-regret is rational and appropriate even when an agent lacks culpability for a harmful outcome, which is why outcome luck still carries emotional and relational weight, just not blameworthy weight. At the same time, I hold a fairly strict control condition: an agent is morally responsible only for what lies within the scope of her direct volitional control. I also maintain that in large-scale collective problems—say, climate harm—individuals can bear genuine moral responsibility for aggregate outcomes they did not individually cause, but only in a distributive, not collectively-agentive, sense. Finally, I judge that two people with identical intentions and efforts but different outcomes deserve identical assessments of their actions, though possibly different assessments of their practical wisdom or character if the outcomes reveal something about their judgment. Go through these commitments and audit them for joint consistency. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only appear to conflict but can be reconciled, and how severe is each real tension? For every genuine conflict, specify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and what that revision costs me conceptually. Then identify the single change that would clean up the most conflicts at once, and say whether adopting it would still leave me recognizably within the moral-luck literature.
