I'm working through my position on collective and intergenerational responsibility for a seminar, and I'd like a consistency check before I present. Here is where I've landed:

(1) I accept Karl Jaspers's fourfold classification of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—as the right framework for analyzing collective responsibility, and I take political guilt to be genuinely collective: it attaches to the state or polity as an institutional agent rather than distributing over individual citizens.

(2) From this, I draw that if collective institutional agency is sufficient for political guilt, it should also be sufficient for collective moral guilt, since both depend on shared agency structures rather than on individual conscience alone.

(3) At the same time, I maintain that metaphysical guilt has no collective analogue: in Jaspers's account it turns on an individual's direct existential failure and solidarity with others, which cannot be scaled up to a nation.

(4) I also hold that present-day citizens of a state that committed historical injustices bear genuine forward-looking moral obligations—reparative duties—to remedy those injustices, but I insist on calling these obligations rather than guilt, because guilt requires that the bearer committed, or was personally complicit in, the wrongful act.

(5) Finally, I think the difference between guilt and obligation here is not merely verbal: guilt carries a first-personal moral stain that requires awareness of one's own wrongdoing, which institutional agents and uninvolved descendants alike lack.

Do these five commitments hang together? Please rank the tensions by severity, identifying for each whether it is a real contradiction or only an apparent one that dissolves on closer inspection. For the real tensions, say which commitment is the least costly to revise and what that revision would cost me conceptually. Also tell me which single change would clean up the most problems.
