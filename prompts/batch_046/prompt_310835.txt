I take the view that the long-running dispute over whether national wealth is driven primarily by institutions, geography, or culture is not merely empirically unresolved but structurally undecidable, because macro-historical causal inference lacks the counterfactual stability required to isolate any of these factors. Every plausible natural experiment—discontinuities at colonial borders, variation in settler mortality, or partitioned regions—fails an exclusion restriction, since institutional assignment was endogenous to geographic feasibility and pre-existing cultural norms. More specifically, I hold that institutional effects are substrate-dependent: when an institutional package is transplanted onto a different geographic or cultural base, its economic impact shifts in ways that prevent identification of a substrate-independent institutional coefficient. The apparent identification strategies in the literature therefore do not separate institutions from their substrates; they measure interaction terms that are misread as main effects. Given this, what would genuinely count against my skepticism? I do not want another cross-country correlational study. I want to know which kinds of evidence—formal proofs of exclusion-restriction recovery, synthetic counterfactuals from institutional transplantation, theoretical results showing substrate-independence, or unexpected historical cases—would have to obtain for my substrate-dependence claim to fail. Rank them from decisive to merely awkward. In particular, is there an observation that would force me to concede that institutions carry stable, portable effects, or is my position insulated by the move of reinterpreting any counterexample as an interaction term? And if it is so insulated, is that insulation a defect in the view or a correct diagnosis of the epistemic limits of history?
