I'm a retired physicist who got into philosophy late, mostly from reading Hofstadter and getting lost in the weeds. I mostly understand the MU-puzzle: you start with MI and apply those four rules about adding U's, doubling after M, and collapsing triples of I, but you can never reach MU. The answer involves JOOTSing—you notice an invariant about the I's (they stay powers of two, never hit a multiple of three) and see the goal is impossible from outside. But here's what's bugging me. In physics we have effective theories with domains—Newton works until it doesn't, then you extend to relativity, still formaly inside a frame. With the MU-puzzle, it seems there's no internal extension that gets you to MU; you have to stop playing the symbol-game and look at arithmetic. So is JOOTSing just using intuition, or is there a sharper line between (a) fixing a formal system by adding rules or axioms, and (b) seeing that the system is the wrong kind of tool for the job? I want a gut-level explanation of that boundry—when does a wall mean build a bigger box, and when does it mean stop being formal altogether? And does formalizing the invariant itself blur the line, since now we've just built a meta-system? I don't need the full Gödel machinery, just the logic of why some dead-ends ask for extending and others ask for walking away, and whether JOOTSing is a real move or just a fancy label for getting tired of the rules.
