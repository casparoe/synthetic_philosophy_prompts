A student in my seminar offered this summary of the Hume-Moore barrier to naturalized ethics: Hume's is-ought gap is not merely a logical point about derivability, but an ontological claim that normative properties do not metaphysically ground in — and indeed do not even supervene on — any configuration of natural properties, since one can always coherently ask why a given natural state is good. Moore then formalized this with the open-question argument, which shows that identifying 'good' with any natural property (say, cooperative behavior stabilized by iterated prisoner's-dilemma dynamics) closes a question that should remain open. The student concludes that evolutionary ethics therefore fails twice: it derives an 'ought' from an 'is', and it commits the naturalistic fallacy by treating a normative property as reducible to a strategic equilibrium property. Is this summary fair? I suspect it conflates Hume's logical separation with a much stronger metaphysical claim about grounding, and that it applies Moore's open-question test to causal explanations rather than to definitional reductions. Please audit the summary: separate what is accurate, what is a subtle misrepresentation of Hume or Moore, and whether evolutionary ethics actually needs to make either mistake. Respond in one paragraph, bottom line first, no more than 180 words.
