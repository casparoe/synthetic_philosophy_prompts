I'm a doctoral student in philosophy working between epistemology and the philosophy of history, and I'm trying to think through a problem that standard accounts of collective memory leave underspecified: when a community's living remembrance of a traumatic event conflicts with the forensic record, is the right response always to "correct" the memory in the name of history, or is there a legitimate form of mnemonic solidarity that can resist archival accountability without collapsing into denialism? I want to work this out through Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon, not as a simple illustration of perspectivism, but as a sustained aesthetic argument about whether a definitive historical account should take precedence over the versions that sustain communal identity and dignity. The film's nested testimonies about the bandit Tajomaru's encounter with the samurai and his wife present four irreconcilable accounts, and the frame narrative under the Rashomon gate adds a fifth layer that is rarely read philosophically: the woodcutter's admission that he witnessed the killing, his removal of the pearl-inlaid dagger from the scene, and his subsequent decision to adopt the abandoned infant despite his own dishonesty. 

What I'm calling mnemonic solidarity is the moral weight given to preserving the version of events that allows a community to maintain its ethical self-understanding, even when that version conflicts with evidentiary closure. By archival accountability, I mean the historiographical obligation to establish a single record regardless of communal cost. Rashomon seems to resist both: none of the central testimonies are vindicated by the frame, and the woodcutter's own account is compromised by theft, yet his final act of care suggests that ethical response might depend on suspending the quest for definitive truth rather than resolving it. Could you read the film through this lens? Specifically, does Kurosawa dramatize the idea that when collective memory and the historical record conflict over gendered violence and warrior honor, neither should automatically yield to the other, but both should be held in a kind of practical suspension that makes moral action possible? Where does the film support this reading, where does it resist it—particularly in the contrast between the commoner's cynicism and the priest's despair—and what does it add to the philosophical debate about memory and justice that a standard argument drawing on Halbwachs or Nora wouldn't capture on its own?
