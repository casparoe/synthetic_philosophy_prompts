I'm trying to work out my actual position on direct democracy and referendums—not the standard civics-class version, but the mechanism-design version—by testing my intuitions against concrete cases and seeing whether they cohere. Here are my verdicts, going case by case.

The 2016 UK referendum on EU membership strikes me as a clear misapplication: a binary choice was presented on a multi-dimensional problem with technically dense implementation pathways (trade regimes, regulatory equivalence, Northern Ireland protocols) that were not specified on the ballot, and voters had no institutional mechanism to correct course once the result triggered irreversible legal processes. By contrast, the Swiss 2018 popular initiative on whether to maintain federal subsidies specifically for farmers who keep horned cattle—rather than dehorning them—feels like direct democracy functioning well: the decision was concrete, the stakeholders were identifiable, the symbolic and economic trade-offs were transparent to ordinary citizens, and the outcome was easily reversible in subsequent legislation. California's ballot propositions that adjust specific penal-code thresholds—for instance, reclassifying property crimes below particular dollar values—strike me as worse: they ask voters to perform legislative drafting without staff, committee hearings, or accountability for interaction effects with sentencing guidelines elsewhere in the code. I also think direct democracy is well-suited to constitutional amendments that encode broad normative commitments, such as Switzerland's periodic revisions to fundamental rights provisions, because these set constraints rather than optimize within them.

But I am not consistent, and I want to know where the crack is. Consider a hypothetical referendum I have been turning over: a municipal ballot measure that mandates a specific algorithmic auditing protocol—requiring differential-privacy checks with epsilon bounded below 1.0, applied to all government AI procurement contracts—rather than leaving the standard to an administrative agency. On one hand, this feels technically niche, closer to the California sentencing adjustments: voters are being asked to legislate a mathematical parameter they likely do not understand, with knock-on effects for model utility and vendor markets. On the other hand, it feels like a values question about democratic sovereignty over automated decision-making, closer to the horned-cattle case: citizens are deciding whether they want algorithmic governance constrained by a hard, legible rule rather than delegated to experts. I keep reversing my judgment on this case.

So my question is whether there is a principle—or a small family of principles—that captures the pattern across the UK vote, the cattle subsidy, the California propositions, and constitutional amendments, and that delivers a determinate verdict on the algorithmic-auditing referendum. I want the principle stated precisely, with each clause motivated by the case that supports it, and I want its predictions for two cases I have not listed: first, a local referendum on adopting a specific carbon-tax schedule with revenue recycled through existing income-tax brackets; second, a national referendum on whether to adopt a constitutional provision banning predictive policing algorithms altogether. Crucially, I want an honest accounting: if the simplest principle that saves my verdicts on the UK, Swiss, and California cases forces me to overturn my judgment on either the constitutional-amendment cases or the auditing referendum, say which one has to go and why. This is reflective equilibrium on request: I am not asking for a survey of democratic theory, but for a repaired principle that either accommodates or explicitly sacrifices one of my intuitions.
