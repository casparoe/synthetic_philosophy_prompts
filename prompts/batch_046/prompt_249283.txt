My dissertation advisor, Professor Elena Vasquez, has given explicit verdicts on five successive variants of Dennett’s well-known brain-transplant puzzle, in which a brain is removed from a body and the question is raised whether the self travels with it. Her judgments are these:

1. Classic transplant: the brain is moved into a new synthetic body and the original body dies. Verdict: the self survives and is now located in the new body.
2. Gradual replacement: over ten years every neuron is replaced with a functionally identical synthetic counterpart, with no interruption in conscious experience. Verdict: identity is preserved throughout; substrate is irrelevant.
3. Fission: the brain is split, each hemisphere implanted in a different functioning body. Verdict: neither resulting person is uniquely the original; singular identity is dissolved, though both stand in a psychological-successor relation.
4. Externalized cognition: the biological brain remains in the original skull, but all higher-order processing is handled by a remote cluster linked via direct cortical interfaces; the brain regulates only autonomic functions and serves as a bidirectional relay. Verdict: the self is distributed across the brain-relay-cluster system; there is no single point-location.
5. Scan-and-copy: the brain is destroyed after high-resolution scanning, and the pattern is instantiated in a single synthetic brain inside a robotic body. Verdict: this is not the original person but a qualitative duplicate; causal continuity was broken.
