You serve on your university's Strategic Research Funding Board. The board must decide whether to renew a fifteen-year grant to the Institute for Long-Horizon Demography, which models global agricultural carrying capacity with a modified Malthusian framework that treats technological yield gains as temporary perturbations within a longer exponential constraint. Critics note that the Institute's thirty-year forecasts have repeatedly underestimated food production, and they argue this pattern of falsification should trigger defunding under our "predictive accountability" policy. Supporters respond that the theory is not falsified but "right but early" — that rescue hypotheses (delayed soil depletion, energy-cost asymptotes) preserve the core mechanism, and that cutting funding now would discard a framework precisely when its long-run predictions are becoming empirically testable.

Draft the board's decision memo recommending either renewal with conditions, non-renewal, or a phased transition, with a clear bottom line and an implementation timeline. Your reasoning should treat the "right but early" defense seriously: explain whether a theory can be rescued repeatedly by pushing its verification horizon further into the future without becoming unfalsifiable, and apply that standard rigorously to this case. Include an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and do not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists. The memo should be actionable by researchers and administrators who are not specialists in philosophy of science.
