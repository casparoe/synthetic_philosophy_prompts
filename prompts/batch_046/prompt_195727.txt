The participants, all consenting adults, practice what they call “structured emotional transparency”: a ritualized exercise in radical honesty about personal failures, private regrets, and hidden resentments. The organizers stress that the practice is entirely voluntary and produces no documented physical or psychological harm.

The proponents of the ban—including several prominent civic and religious leaders—do not claim that anyone is injured. Instead, they argue that the practice corrodes what they term “protective ambiguity”: the unspoken social understanding that certain inner experiences remain partly concealed, which they regard as a necessary scaffold for public trust and communal stability, even in the absence of demonstrable harm. Opponents counter that the state is overreaching into private conscience and that harmless conduct should lie beyond legal prohibition.

The council is split between members who believe the law should defend common moral convictions regardless of tangible harm, and members who insist that without injury to non-consenting parties, coercion is illegitimate.

Please advise me on how to understand the philosophical structure at work here. Is there a coherent framework that justifies banning harmless private conduct solely because it violates widely held moral convictions? And does extending liability to property owners strengthen or weaken that framework? In your answer, recognize the underlying philosophical problem, apply the relevant theoretical distinctions, and explain whether the landlord provision changes the moral calculus—rather than offering generic journalistic guidance.
