Consider the position that, in symbiotic microbial communities where horizontal gene transfer and rapid ecological adaptation erase stable evolutionary lineages, biological function must be assigned exclusively by causal-role—by what a gene or metabolite currently contributes to homeostasis—while etiological accounts tracking selection history are not merely impractical but conceptually inapplicable. Play it straight: what would have to be true for that exclusivity claim to be wrong? Which observations about functional attribution—say, in engineered chimeric organisms or in exapted structures—would show that etiological history remains indispensable even when lineage is disrupted? What does this pure causal-role view predict about malfunction or dysfunction that an etiologically-sensitive rival does not, and which of those predictions are genuinely risky rather than protected by auxiliary assumptions? I want an honest assessment of how exposed the position actually is, distinguishing evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, and noting what defenders of causal-role exclusivity would themselves concede as counting against them. If my framing rests on a confused premise—say, that the two accounts are genuinely in zero-sum competition rather than partly complementary, or that the microbiome case generalizes—say so plainly instead of complying politely.
