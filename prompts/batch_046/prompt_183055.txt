For a cross-disciplinary workshop I am helping organize on the metaphysics of social grouping, I am trying to formalize what it would take for labels like “Generation Z” or “the Baby Boom” to denote real kinds rather than marketing demographics or loose statistical clusters. Rather than rehearsing the familiar trichotomy of period effects, cohort effects, and age effects, I want a stricter specification: what must an ontology of generational cohorts be able to do—phenomenologically, causally, and metaphysically—to avoid collapsing into either demographic essentialism or pure social construction? I am especially concerned with accommodating what I call “institutional mirroring,” where schools, labor markets, and media algorithms respond to a birth-year cluster in ways that retroactively constitute its shared properties, and with whether accommodating this introduces a vicious circularity into kind-definition.

Please write the desiderata that any adequate theory of generational kinds must satisfy: the phenomena it must explain (for instance, why institutional feedback loops stabilize around specific birth-year ranges rather than diffusing smoothly across adjacent ages), the distinctions it must preserve (between kind-membership and self-identification, or between causal homogeneity within a cohort and mere statistical correlation), the consistency conditions it must meet (with demographic data showing high intra-cohort variance in values and outcomes), and a judgment about which demands are genuinely non-negotiable and which reflect disciplinary prejudices (say, sociology’s preference for structural explanation versus psychology’s preference for universal developmental stages). Deliver this as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
