The exam question was:
"Skolem's paradox follows from the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem: any first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable elementary submodel, even if the theory proves the existence of uncountable sets. Explain precisely how Skolem functions are deployed to construct such a submodel; distinguish the internal and external perspectives on the cardinality of, say, the power set of ω inside that submodel; and evaluate whether the paradox genuinely threatens the determinacy of cardinal notions like ℵ₁, or whether it is neutralized—perhaps by second-order logic with full semantics. Be precise about whether the 'missing' bijections are hidden or genuinely absent, and about the limits of any formal resolution."

The student's answer (written under exam conditions):

"The downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem guarantees that any first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable elementary submodel. To construct it, one skolemizes the language: every existential quantifier is replaced by an explicit Skolem term, so that witnesses become named functions. Applying this to ZFC, we take a countable closure of initial parameters under all Skolem functions, producing a domain M that still satisfies every axiom.

Inside M, let P denote the power set of ω. By Cantor's theorem—now expressed in the skolemized language—there is no surjection from ω onto P. From inside M, this absence is literal: the Skolem terms have replaced the existential claim that a surjection exists, so no counterexample to Cantor's result can be formed within M. From outside, however, M is countable, P is a countable collection of subsets of ω, and the surjection that should refute Cantor's theorem exists in the ambient universe but lies outside M because its defining formula involves parameters not in the closure.

This is Skolem's paradox: the theory proves uncountability, yet every model is countable. The standard resolution invokes second-order logic with full semantics, where quantifiers range over the entire power set of the domain. Here the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem fails, and ℵ₁ receives a categorical characterization up to isomorphism, restoring determinacy.

But this resolution is unstable. Any second-order model is itself a set in some larger first-order universe, and from that vantage point the previously absolute cardinalities become relative once again. Therefore the paradox is not peculiar to first-order logic; it reveals that no formal system—first- or second-order—can stabilize the notion of 'uncountable.' The determinacy of ℵ₁ is not recovered by stronger logic but requires abandoning formal axiomatization and appealing to an intuitive, pre-formal conception of the set-theoretic universe."
