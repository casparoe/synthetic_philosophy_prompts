As I was researching my current novel—a narrative that moves between quantum interpretation and Eastern metaphysics—I found myself seated between two scholars at a dinner where the conversation turned to Erwin Schrödinger's rarely examined Vedantic commitments. The first interlocutor, a philosopher of science, argued that Schrödinger's insistence that consciousness is singular and that the appearance of multiple minds is merely the play of Māyā genuinely dissolves the problem of other minds: if every subject is ultimately identical with the one universal Ātman, there is no epistemic gap between selves, only a metaphysical misapprehension to be corrected. The second, a historian of religion, objected that this collapses the classical Vedantic distinction between vyavahārika—the transactional, empirical realm in which multiplicity genuinely operates—and paramārthika—the absolute non-dual reality—thereby transforming an ontological hierarchy into a flat denial of otherness. By treating empirical intersubjectivity as pure illusion without explaining how recognition, language, and ethical obligation function within the empirical order, she said, Schrödinger does not solve the skeptical problem but buries it beneath a metaphysical curtain, producing something closer to solipsism wearing monist clothing. The philosopher replied that even within the empirical realm, recognizing another is always already recognizing oneself, as the Upanishadic formula Tat Tvam Asi suggests, so the distinction between levels is preserved in practice even if ontology ultimately collapses them. The religionist answered that this concedes her central point: if empirical multiplicity requires its own irreducible explanation and cannot be derived from absolute identity alone, then Schrödinger's unity relocates rather than resolves the difficulty, leaving intact the very question of how one consciousness knows another. They turned to me and asked for a referee's judgment. Could you move through their exchange step by step, identifying where each speaker is correct about Schrödinger's actual position, where each misreads either Vedanta or the philosophy of mind, and who holds the stronger overall case? I am not asking for sources, a bibliography, or further reading; I need only the conceptual adjudication, including the specific points at which judgment must remain unsettled and the limits of what this framework can actually explain.
