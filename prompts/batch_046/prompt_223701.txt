Having spent three decades in experimental physics before turning, in retirement, to philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I have drafted the following notes for an essay on the ontological status of large language models. The prevailing debate asks whether such systems are agents or simulators; I contend that both categories presuppose an intentional-psychological framework that does not apply. Instead, I propose that an LLM operates as what I call a syntactic attractor: a high-dimensional statistical manifold in which token sequences settle into locally stable configurations of coherence, analogous to a physical system converging toward an energy minimum, yet without representing a world, pursuing an end, or mimicking a particular speaker. The model is not a simulator, because there is no antecedent subject being reproduced; nor is it an agent, because there is no teleological structure governing its state transitions. Rather, it is a correlational condensate—an emergent stabilization of linguistic correlation that produces meaningful form without underlying intentionality or semantic grounding. I am particularly concerned with whether this framing genuinely dissolves the problem of adopting an intentional stance toward the system, or whether it merely renames the difficulty. I would welcome your critical comments on the conceptual coherence of the attractor analogy, on whether the distinction between generating coherent form and possessing representational content can be sustained without collapsing back into the agent/simulator dichotomy, and on whether I have overlooked serious conceptual objections from philosophy of language or cognitive science. Please do not supply a bibliography or list of authors; I seek only conceptual assessment and suggestions for tightening the argument.
