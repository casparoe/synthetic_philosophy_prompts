I am a doctoral candidate in early modern environmental history, working at the intersection of climate anomalies and state formation during the Little Ice Age. In my graduate seminar on historical method, a senior colleague repeatedly invokes the Braudelian slogan that "events are foam on the waves of history," usually to dismiss arguments that discrete diplomatic crises or the specific administrative decisions of seventeenth-century ministers possessed any lasting causal efficacy. The underlying move is that because geographic and climatic structures operate over the longue durée, singular occurrences are merely epiphenomenal surface disturbances—narratively vivid, perhaps, but explanatorily inert.

I want to treat this not as a rhetorical stopper but as a claim that needs disambiguation. Could you lay out the precise theses hidden in that slogan as an explicit menu—ranging from the modest observation that long-term causes are often underweighted in popular accounts, through intermediate claims about explanatory priority and counterfactual dependence at different temporal scales, to the metaphysically radical thesis that events as such lack causal powers? For each reading, please indicate whether it is conceptually defensible, whether it genuinely licenses dismissing the causal relevance of specific diplomatic or bureaucratic decisions in my period, and which version is most likely being smuggled in when the slogan is deployed in seminar debate. I am especially interested in whether there exists a coherent middle position that grants climate and geographic structures deep explanatory priority without rendering singular events causally inert, or whether the slogan, once precisely parsed, collapses into either an uncontroversial platitude or an untenable structural determinism.
