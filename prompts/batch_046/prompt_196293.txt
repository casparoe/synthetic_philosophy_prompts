I'm taking an advanced seminar on practical rationality, and during a discussion of Derek Parfit's work on what he calls rational irrationality, a fellow student offered a summary of the hitchhiker case that I am not sure I should accept. She described it roughly like this: Parfit imagines being stranded in a desert and picked up by a driver who will take you to town only if he predicts that you will pay him after arrival, even though once you have arrived there is no further causal benefit to handing over the money. The rational agent, she said, should therefore cultivate a stable disposition to pay in that situation, because being the kind of person who pays is precisely what explains why the driver stopped in the first place. From this she concluded that Parfit has shown rational choice is never about evaluating local causal consequences—rather, it is always about maintaining globally optimal patterns of disposition—so that any theory, such as causal decision theory, that assesses acts one by one is not merely limited but fundamentally mistaken. She also compared the case to Ulysses tying himself to the mast, suggesting that Parfit's point is essentially about rational self-binding: you deliberately render yourself unable to defect in the particular case so that your broader strategy succeeds.

This sounds mostly right, but I have a nagging sense that she has smuggled in a stronger claim than Parfit actually defends, and that she may have conflated two different mechanisms that Parfit keeps separate—perhaps an internal disposition with an external precommitment device, or the hitchhiker argument with a direct argument against causal decision theory. I also wonder whether her framing subtly shifts Parfit's modest thesis about rational dispositions into an overstatement about the incoherence of local evaluation as such. Could you help me audit this summary? Specifically, I would like to know which parts accurately capture Parfit's position on rational irrationality, which parts are overstatements or misrepresentations, and whether her conclusion that causal decision theory is "fundamentally mistaken" actually follows from anything in the hitchhiker argument itself, or whether it relies on a conflation with other debates. I want to return to the seminar able to distinguish the fair core of her account from the subtle distortions.
