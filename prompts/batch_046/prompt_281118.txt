The debate over whether Dewey's experimentalism demands that democratic politics literally mirror scientific laboratory practice—or only that citizens adopt a fallibilist, problem-solving attitude—has persisted without resolution. One side, emphasizing institutional redesign, argues that democratic deliberation should be structured around explicit hypotheses, measurable outcomes, and iterative feedback loops analogous to controlled inquiry. The other side, emphasizing dispositional habits, insists that politics lacks the isolatable variables and reproducible conditions necessary for genuine experimentation, so Dewey must have meant only that citizens hold beliefs provisionally and revise them through social exchange. Both camps cite the same broad commitments in Dewey's writings on education and logic, yet neither has conceded ground. Diagnose this impasse: is it a verbal disagreement about the scope of the term experiment; a substantive disagreement about whether political conflict can have empirical content; or are the two sides answering different questions—one normative about institutional architecture, one about civic virtue—under a single heading? Ground your diagnosis in what each position actually requires, and say what, if anything, would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a clarification of Dewey's intellectual sources, or an argument that framing democracy as inquiry is itself a category mistake.
