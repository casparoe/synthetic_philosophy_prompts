I'm working through some ideas from a recent seminar on collective action and moral responsibility, and I keep running into a tension that standard treatments of the inefficacy objection don't seem to resolve. Most discussions—whether about dietary choices, carbon footprints, or electoral participation—treat individual contribution as either binary or linearly scalable: either your action makes no perceptible difference to the aggregate harm, or it does, and that fact alone is supposed to settle moral relevance. But I've been thinking about what we might call threshold-sensitive non-linear aggregation, where collective harm is produced only once certain population-level thresholds are crossed, yet individual actions can still shift the probability distribution over those thresholds in ways that expected-utility framing doesn't fully capture. Given this framing, I want a defended prioritization, not a survey, of which open conceptual problems actually need settling before we can say whether the inefficacy objection succeeds or fails across climate, consumption, and democratic participation.

Specifically, please rank the following categories of question by how upstream they are: metaphysical questions about what counts as a cause in overdetermined collective outcomes; normative questions about whether moral duties depend on counterfactual efficacy or on something like role-based obligation; and decision-theoretic questions about how rational agents ought to treat probabilistic contributions to harmful equilibria when no single agent can trigger or prevent the threshold crossing. For each, indicate which depend on resolving others first, which are tractable for philosophers working without heavy empirical social science, and which are essentially decorative—interesting but not load-bearing for the practical conclusion about individual abstention. I'm particularly interested in whether there is a coherent middle ground between pure consequentialist aggregation and pure deontological side-constraints that treats partial efficacy as structurally different from full efficacy; if you think that middle ground collapses upon inspection, explain why its failure doesn't change the priority ordering. Defend your agenda with explicit criteria—downstream dependency, tractability, and neglect—so that it reads as a research strategy for a small group with limited time rather than a literature review.
