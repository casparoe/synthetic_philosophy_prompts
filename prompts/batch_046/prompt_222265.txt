A fellow student in my philosophy of medicine seminar offered the following summary of Ian Hacking's looping-effect account of psychiatric classification: diagnostic labels cause those classified to change their self-conception and behavior, which in turn alters clinical observation, producing a feedback loop between category and person. She drew three conclusions. First, psychiatric kinds are never indifferent kinds; they are purely interactive kinds that the label creates ex nihilo, leaving no biological substrate untouched by the loop. Second, this makes cross-cultural prevalence comparison conceptually incoherent—if Japanese and American clinicians apply the same DSM term to differently looped phenomena, they are measuring ontologically distinct kinds rather than one disorder at different rates. Third, the DSM-5 Section III Alternative Model for Personality Disorders illustrates reverse looping: clinicians revised dimensional criteria precisely because patients had learned to perform the trait clusters under assessment. Is this a fair rendering of Hacking's position? Please distinguish what is accurate about interactive kinds and looping, what overstates his anti-realism about biology, whether cross-cultural comparison is indeed ruled out, and whether her DSM-5 example captures a genuine looping effect or conflates it with measurement reactivity.
