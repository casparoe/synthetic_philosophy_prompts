After a long argument with a friend about whether Athenian democracy collapsed through bad luck or through structural features of its own procedures, I have become convinced that there is a specific pathology—call it procedural reversibility—where mechanisms designed to correct democratic error instead accelerate catastrophic judgment when they interact with competitive rhetoric and collective identity. The Mytilene debate, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates are not three isolated accidents; they are instances in which reversal protocols, escalation rules, and accountability procedures produced outcomes that many participants privately rejected. I want to design a 90-minute seminar activity for about fourteen graduate students in political philosophy that makes this tangible through experience rather than lecture, but I need the rules, incentives, and roles arranged so that students feel the pull themselves before any historical parallel is named.

The activity should run in three rounds with paper, tokens, and a hidden point system—no real money. In Round One (Mytilene, 20 minutes), each student receives a “citizen” card and casts an anonymous vote on whether to execute all male rebels. The facilitator then announces that the vote was close and activates the reversal protocol: any student may propose reversal, but must stand and speak for sixty seconds; those who switch their vote lose two reputation points, while those who stay lose one point if reversal fails but gain three if it succeeds. The scoring is designed so that reversal feels risky and performative rather than corrective. In Round Two (Sicily, 30 minutes), information is distributed asymmetrically: “generals” receive cards showing depleted troop numbers, while “assembly members” receive exaggerated reports of enemy weakness, and “soldiers” receive only emotional appeals. Each round, students vote either to reinforce or to withdraw, but reinforcing requires only raising a hand, whereas withdrawing requires writing a justification that is read aloud and costs reputation points; success conditions are rigged so that reinforcement always fails, yet the administrative asymmetry makes retreat feel like institutional cowardice. In Round Three (Socrates, 20 minutes), students serve as jurors in a stylized trial with anonymous voting followed by a public accountability round in which three randomly selected jurors must defend their vote; defending conviction yields five points and public praise, while defending acquittal yields one point and a visible “corruption” marker on the board, so that private doubt is systematically penalized.

The debrief (20 minutes) should map Round One onto the destabilizing effect of rapid reversal after Cleon’s and Diodotus’s competing speeches, Round Two onto the administrative bias that made reinforcing Sicily easier than retreating despite private knowledge of failure, and Round Three onto the way democratic accountability suppressed dissent in the trial.
