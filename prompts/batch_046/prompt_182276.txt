I have been trying to work out a conceptual distinction within causation by omission—specifically between what I am calling structural omission, where the absence of a factor is embedded in the background conditions that make an outcome possible, and interventional omission, where an agent actively withholds an action that would otherwise have interrupted a causal chain. To test whether this distinction has any historical roots, I turned to John Stuart Mill’s discussion of negative conditions in Book III, Chapter V of A System of Logic (1843). There Mill argues, in substance, that every cause is the sum of conditions both positive and negative, and that the absence of a necessary condition can form part of the total cause of an event just as much as the presence of one. He offers the illustration that the presence of food, air, and water is required for life, but so is the absence of poison; and he implies that if poison is absent, that absence belongs to the cause of the person’s continuing to live.

I am struggling to see whether Mill treats this negative contribution symmetrically with positive ones. On one reading, he holds that omissions are genuine causal contributors, which would support my view of structural omission as having the same metaphysical status as a positive cause. On another reading, he is merely saying that the absence of an interfering factor is a background condition for the operation of positive causes, which would make omission causation derivative—closer to what I call interventional omission, but only when an agent fails to introduce the interference. I also wish to map this onto a more formal framework in which omissions are represented not as missing edges in a causal graph but as edges carrying a negative weight; yet I am uncertain whether Mill’s text gives any warrant for treating absence as a positively weighted relation rather than as a simple lack.

Could you help me understand precisely what Mill’s passage commits him to? In particular, does he allow that the absence of poison is a cause of survival in exactly the same sense that the presence of food is, or is he only describing a necessary background condition? And if there is any ambiguity in the passage that could be exploited to support the negative-edge formalisation I have sketched, where exactly does it lie? I should prefer a close conceptual reading of the structure he sets out rather than any modern bibliographic apparatus.
