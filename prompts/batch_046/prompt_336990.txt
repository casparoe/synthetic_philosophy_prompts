I'm taking an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of historiography, and we recently spent several sessions on Herbert Butterfield's well-known critique of what he termed Whig history. After one class, a fellow student offered what she presented as a more philosophically precise reconstruction of Butterfield's argument, and I found it compelling but also suspiciously strong. She maintained that Whig history is not merely a partisan bias in favor of modern liberal institutions, but a deep methodological error: the historian treats present outcomes as fixed explanatory endpoints and then measures every past action solely by its causal contribution to reaching those endpoints. On her reading, Butterfield's central objection is epistemic rather than moral—he allegedly invokes something like a principle of explanatory invariance, according to which a sound explanation of a past event should remain stable even if the later outcomes that actually occurred had turned out differently. She added that because Whig history selects only those past events that seem significant from our present vantage point, it necessarily performs an abridgement that collapses causal investigation into a morality play of progressive heroes and reactionary villains. From all of this, she drew a sweeping conclusion: once you genuinely reject Whig history, you must also reject every form of retrospective moral evaluation of past figures, including the use of biography to extract moral lessons, since any judgment made from our present standpoint reinstates the very presentist distortion Butterfield attacked.

I suspect that parts of this capture Butterfield's actual worries—especially about presentism, the selective focus on what led to the present, and the dualist framing of history as a drama between liberty and its enemies—while other parts sound like an anachronistic import from philosophy of science or an overstatement of his conclusions. But I do not know enough about the historiographical literature to identify the missteps with confidence. Could you audit this summary for me? Please distinguish what fairly represents Butterfield's concerns—such as his critique of studying the past with reference to the present, his warning that this turns history into an abridgement that obscures real causal complexity, and his point about modernizing past figures—from what appears to be a philosophical embellishment or a conflation with a separate debate. In particular, is the so-called principle of explanatory invariance genuinely part of Butterfield's argument, or is it a foreign framework being projected onto him? And does his rejection of Whig historiography actually entail that we cannot learn anything moral from past lives, or does that conclusion rest on an independent mistake about the relationship between explanatory neutrality and retrospective evaluation? I am not looking for a bibliography, just a careful accuracy check I can use to think through the issue on my own.
