I'm finalizing the assessment for an advanced seminar on consciousness and personal identity, and I need one 90-minute essay question for the final exam. The course has covered split-brain research, but I want to avoid a question that students can answer by summarizing Sperry's classic experiments and repeating the standard "one mind or two" framing. Instead, the question should force them to navigate a specific conceptual tension: whether dissociation in split-brain patients undermines an access-based conception of conscious unity—where unity is defined by the global availability of information for rational control and verbal report—or whether it leaves a deeper phenomenal or emotional substrate intact.

To prevent mere regurgitation, embed a hypothetical variant in the prompt: a split-brain patient in whom the left hemisphere initiates actions guided by environmental cues present only in the right hemisphere's perceptual field, yet confabulates explicit reasons for those actions, while the right hemisphere shows physiological emotional recognition of those actions as self-produced, but only when they occur in the patient's own body and not in an experimenter's. Ask students whether this pattern supports a single subject with fractured access, two subjects with a shared emotional substrate, or a third structural option they must articulate and defend. The answer should require distinguishing behavioral dissociation, phenomenal binding, and narrative self-attribution, and should take a clear position on which level of unity is genuinely threatened.

Provide a model answer—roughly 800 words—taking one defensible position (for instance, that access is fragmented but subpersonal phenomenal integration remains, though it is epistemically inaccessible to either hemisphere alone) and addressing the strongest counter-argument. Then provide a marking scheme out of 25 marks, structured around five must-hit moves: (1) correct distinction between access, phenomenal, and narrative unity (5 marks); (2) precise application to the hypothetical cross-cueing and confabulation scenario (7 marks); (3) evaluation of whether emotional-substrate data resolve the division (6 marks); (4) articulation and defense of the chosen structural option or a well-formed alternative (5 marks); and (5) recognition of the epistemic limitation—why first-person report is unreliable here and what that implies for the inference (2 marks). For each move, include a one-line note on the typical failure, such as treating behavioral dissociation as direct evidence for phenomenal division, or assuming emotional recognition proves singular ownership without addressing confabulation.
