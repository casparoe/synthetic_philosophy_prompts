After retiring from a long career in condensed-matter physics I have begun working through problems in the philosophy of computation and representation, and I find myself repeatedly returning to a toy framework I invented to keep my intuitions straight: I imagine a substrate called careenium, a crystalline lattice whose nodes can enter discrete stable states, and within that lattice I embed structures I call simmballs, which are configurations of nodes that map onto formal rules or semantic contents. The question I keep circling is whether the simmballs, considered as symbols, ever genuinely push the careenium around, or whether every change is always and only a matter of local physical dynamics in the substrate. To test my own views I have been judging specific scenarios, and I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle behind them or whether I must abandon one.

Here are my verdicts. In a case where the careenium is arranged so that a particular simmball pattern representing an arithmetic error physically destabilizes a neighboring region in a way that exactly mirrors the logical contradiction, and where no purely local physical law predicts that specific destabilization without invoking the symbolic interpretation, I think the symbol really is doing causal work and the substrate is being pushed by the simmball. In a case where an identical physical rearrangement occurs but is interpreted by an outside engineer as a different simmball altogether, with no change in the substrate dynamics, I think there is no symbol-level causation; the careenium does what it does regardless of the interpretation we project onto it. When a simulated careenium exists only in software running on silicon, and altering a simmball in the simulation changes the output of the program but does not alter any physical property of the silicon except through the generic execution of machine instructions, I think the simmballs do not push the substrate in any symbol-specific way; they ride on generic computation. But here is the tension: in a biological system made of careenium-like tissue, where neural firing patterns act as simmballs for sensory information, I am inclined to say that the patterns do cause behavior, yet I also want to say that this is just electrochemical substrate dynamics all the way down, with no extra causal power belonging to the symbols. That pair feels hard to reconcile.

I also have a further case in mind that I have not yet settled. Suppose we build an autonomous robot whose careenium brain evolves its own internal simmball vocabulary over time, not designed by us, and one day a particular configuration that the robot never explicitly learned causes it to avoid a hazard in a way that is only predictable if we treat the configuration as representing danger, not if we only model the node-to-node physics. Would I be forced to say the self-generated symbol pushed the substrate, or could I still maintain that the substrate dynamics explain everything and the symbolic reading is merely our post-hoc gloss? Is there a single principle that delivers my first three verdicts and also resolves this evolutionary-robot case, and if not, which of my earlier judgments should I drop?
