I've been participating in an informal reading group with colleagues who work at the intersection of decision theory and AI safety, and last week we found ourselves in a prolonged disagreement about whether "low-impact" or "side-effect-free" agency can be specified conceptually without first solving the full problem of value alignment. The dispute was between two members, Elena and Marcus, and I was listening without taking sides. Elena opened by arguing that any attempt to formalize "avoiding side effects" necessarily presupposes an axiological baseline—if you don't already know what should be preserved, you can't define what counts as a deviation—so the project of impact minimization collapses into the project of complete value specification. Marcus responded that this conflates measurement with evaluation: one can construct a counterfactual reference trajectory by modeling the world without the agent's presence, compute divergence across a fixed set of state variables, and minimize that divergence structurally, without ever ranking which divergences are good or bad. Elena pushed back that selecting the state variables—whether to include atmospheric carbon, neural firing patterns, or the spatial distribution of artifacts—is itself a normative choice disguised as methodology, and that without knowing which variables matter, the divergence metric is arbitrary. Marcus then proposed what he called a "dynamic neutrality" condition: rather than freezing a snapshot, the agent preserves only those statistical regularities that would have persisted absent intervention, using conservation-law analogues in information and energy flow, which he claimed are value-independent physical facts. Elena countered that appealing to physical conservation smuggles in a reductionist metaphysics of value and ignores that human "side effects" are defined relationally—disrupting a promise, altering a social practice—categories that don't map cleanly onto physical state spaces. Marcus conceded that practical implementation requires design choices, but insisted that the conceptual architecture—minimize counterfactual divergence within a specified variable class—is separable from the full specification of the good, much as a thermostat regulates temperature without knowing why warmth is desired. Elena closed by asking whether, if the designers must still choose both the variables and the divergence threshold, the value problem hasn't merely been relocated rather than dissolved.
