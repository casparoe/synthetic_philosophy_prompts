I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind—approximately sixteen students, one sixty-minute session. Design a runnable in-class role-play with structured deliberation that makes participants feel the specific force of Daniel Dennett’s worry about artificial systems becoming counterfeit people: entities that so thoroughly satisfy the behavioral and functional criteria for adopting the intentional stance that withholding moral standing feels arbitrary, yet whose internal architecture—specifically a distributed, non-unified structure lacking any single locus of experience—makes such attribution ontologically misleading. The activity should center on an evaluation round in which a Committee of students must decide, using a non-monetary point budget representing moral-credit and research-credibility allocations, whether to grant full standing and resource access to a fluent interlocutor. Unbeknownst to the Committee, the interlocutor is operated by an Assembly of four students behind a screen, each controlling only one functional module—affective tone, logical inference, episodic retrieval, and bodily-state description—who generate responses through a strict turn-taking protocol without shared semantic understanding; in a hidden control round, the interlocutor is a single human answering naturally. The payoff scheme should penalize false positives more heavily than false negatives, but include social-pressure mechanics that push the Committee toward generosity. Provide explicit rules, required materials, role assignments, the payoff matrix, and instructions for running both rounds without technology beyond paper cards and a partition. The debrief should map specific deliberative moments onto Dennett’s intentional stance, his critique of the Cartesian theater, and the particular problem of attributing personhood to architectures that contain no unified subject. Specify what will predictably go wrong on the first run—for example, the Committee deciding solely on behavioral fluency and ignoring the hidden architecture, or the Assembly producing incoherent responses that break the illusion—and how to correct for it. Be frank about which feature of Dennett’s broader view, such as his gradualism about consciousness and the denial of any sharp boundary between person and non-person, the activity structurally cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply in closing discussion. Write in a formal, polished register appropriate for an advanced philosophical prompt.
