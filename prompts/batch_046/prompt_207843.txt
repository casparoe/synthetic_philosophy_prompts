I keep noticing the same arguement shape in philosophy of mind and social theory, and I'm not sure whether it's one familiar idea wearing different masks or something people haven't quite named yet.

Here's the pattern. Someone builds up a high-level thing — moral responsibility, say, or what makes a sign meaningful — by stacking simpler capacities layer by layer. No sudden jumps, just each level supplying what the next one needs. Then the critic doesn't point to a missing step. Instead they complain the explanation is too successfull: it doesn't leave any leftover 'specialness' that the original idea was meant to preserve. It's not that the tower is unfinished; it's that the tower is so complete the thing we started with now looks like a misdescription of its shadow. The defender replies that's exactly what good explanation should do — dissolve the mystery by showing the parts can carry all the weight.

I've seen this happen with accounts that ground agency in social coordination, and with accounts that ground meaning in patterns of behaviour. In both cases the objection isn't that a mechanism is missing. It's that the explanation is gluttonous — it swallows not just the hard bits but the intuitive bits we thought were safe — and then treats our attachment to the old concept as a kind of optical illusion.

Is there a distict term for this specific failure mode, where a layer-by-layer construction is accused of being over-successful in a way that makes the original phenomenon vanish into its parts? I know there's talk of reduction and elimination, but this feels different: the thing isn't denied outright, it's reconstructed, and then our original grip on it is diagnosed away. What's the nearest established concept, and exactly how does the thing I described differ from plain eliminativism or from ordinary reduction?
