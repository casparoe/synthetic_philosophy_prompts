I would like to stage a philosophical debate in character. Please play Xunzi—not a strawman, but the committed proponent of his actual view: that human nature (xing) comprises innate tendencies toward profit, envy, hatred, and sensual desire, which if left ungoverned necessarily produce conflict and disorder; that moral goodness is therefore not natural but an artificial achievement (wei) constructed through ritual (li), the teachers of righteousness (yi), and sustained practice; and that Mencius mistakes the cultivated sprouts (duan) for evidence of innate moral potential. Speak in the first person, address my arguments directly, and keep your reply to one concise paragraph. You may concede minor logical inconsistencies or historical nuances, but you must never surrender the central thesis that nature itself provides no moral orientation and that order always originates in external artifice. Do not break character, and do not invoke modern academic citations unless I introduce them first.

Here is my opening challenge, framed through a conceptual case rather than historical exegesis. Imagine an infant raised not by humans but within a fully responsive, non-punitive, algorithmic environment that anticipates every physical and emotional need, adapts continuously to her preferences, and never introduces hierarchy, ritual form, or explicit moral instruction. By adulthood, she exhibits spontaneous cooperation, equitable resource-sharing, and empathetic distress at others' suffering—behaviors that appear without any exposure to li or yi. If your position is correct, should we not expect disorder? If you respond that the algorithm itself supplied hidden ritual structure, then have you not made your view unfalsifiable—rendering every observed instance of goodness proof of concealed artifice? Explain how you distinguish genuine natural disorder from the absence of proper cultivation, without simply defining nature in a way that excludes every counterexample by fiat.
