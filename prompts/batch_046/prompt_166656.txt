I sit on the certification board of the Mid-Atlantic Vocational Training Consortium, and we must vote next month on which assessment standard to adopt for our new digital-manufacturing apprenticeship pilot. There are two competing protocols under review. The Outcome-Stable Protocol certifies an apprentice once she consistently produces correct outputs—accurate calibrations, safe assembly sequences—without requiring any explanation of why the steps work. The Justification-Anchored Protocol withholds certification until the apprentice can both produce the correct output and articulate the reasoning chain behind it; this adds roughly thirty percent to training time and instructor cost.

In my own reading outside of work, I have come across an ancient philosophical puzzle—often associated with Plato’s dialogue the Meno—asking whether knowledge is genuinely more valuable than mere true belief, and whether the extra effort to anchor a correct opinion with an account buys anything practical. Our board’s disagreement seems to replay that puzzle in concrete form: if both protocols yield workers who do not make mistakes on the floor, is there a tangible institutional reason to prefer the more expensive, slower protocol?

Please draft the formal written opinion I will circulate before Thursday’s meeting. Recommend one protocol with a concrete implementation plan—adoption for the pilot cohort beginning next quarter, with a mandatory review checkpoint at six months. The reasoning should take seriously the philosophical question at stake—why should knowledge, understood as correct belief anchored by explanation, be valued over true belief alone in a vocational setting?—while also giving an honest, substantive treatment to the strongest argument on the other side, namely that in high-stakes manufacturing only error rates and throughput matter, and that requiring theoretical justification is an unjustified luxury. The memo should read as an actionable institutional document rather than an abstract essay, and it should not include requests for bibliographies, citations, or further reading lists.
