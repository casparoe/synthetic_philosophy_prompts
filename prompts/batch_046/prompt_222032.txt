I'm taking a seminar on empiricism and innate ideas, and we've been working through the classic puzzle about whether a person born blind who suddenly gains sight could immediately recognize shapes they've only ever known through touch. I'd like you to write a short story that moves this problem into a speculative register without losing its philosophical core. Write about a master sculptor named Elena who has been congenitally blind and understands form exclusively through direct carving and haptic exploration. After receiving an experimental cortical implant, she does not recover conventional vision; instead, her visual cortex receives translated data that renders the world as dynamic fields of pressure and resistance, so that a sphere appears not as a round object but as a uniform gradient of yielding density, and a sharp edge appears as an abrupt discontinuity in that field.

In the story, Elena is placed in a dark gallery containing two bronze objects—perfect mirror-image helices that are chiral but otherwise identical—suspended on invisible wires. She can perceive them only through this synthetic modality and must determine, without ever touching them, which form is left-handed and which is right-handed. Ground the narrative in her internal monologue as she tries to apply her mathematical knowledge of symmetry and her tactile memory of twisted surfaces to this alien stream of sensory data. Explore whether she can construct a bridge between her hands and this pressure-based sight through pure reasoning, or whether the absence of any prior experiential correlation between touch and this translated vision leaves the gap unbridgeable. Keep the ending genuinely ambiguous about whether she succeeds, and let the story ask whether cross-modal recognition requires an innate correspondence or can be forged entirely by the intellect.
