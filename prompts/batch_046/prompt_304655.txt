I am preparing to lead a single ninety-minute seminar for a small interdisciplinary reading group composed mostly of graduate students in environmental design and civil engineering, none of whom have formal training in moral philosophy but all of whom are accustomed to reasoning about distributed infrastructure systems. The conceptual task is to have them understand the inefficacy objection as it applies to individual contributions to collective harms—particularly in climate mitigation, dietary choice, and democratic participation—without allowing them to treat the objection as a mere excuse for inaction. To keep the discussion from collapsing into familiar slogans, I want to build the session around a constructed scenario rather than relying solely on the standard triad of examples: specifically, a distributed greywater recycling loop in a multi-unit residential building, in which each tenant independently decides whether to install a particular biodegradable filtration cartridge, and in which the aggregate chemical load determines whether the shared loop remains viable, yet no individual cartridge's presence or absence is causally detectable in the system's failure or success.

Please plan the explanatory arc, not deliver the explanation itself. First, specify the exact order in which the conceptual pieces must be introduced—starting from the structural feature that renders an individual contribution causally imperceptible, proceeding through the aggregation mechanism that transforms imperceptible inputs into a collective outcome, and only then introducing the normative evaluation—so that the dependency relations are transparent and the students do not begin with moral intuitions about blame. Second, describe what properties this greywater-loop example must possess and what it must explicitly lack in order to carry the full logical weight of the inefficacy objection; in particular, indicate how to prevent its misreading as either a prisoner's dilemma or a simple free-rider problem. Third, map the predictable misconceptions—such as the equation of imperceptible causation with zero causation, the assumption that collective harm requires collective intention, and the inference that if individual action is inefficacious then collective action must be equally so—stating precisely where in the sequence each should be intercepted and with what distinction or counter-example. Fourth, propose one targeted check for understanding, perhaps a brief exercise or a single well-formed question, that would reveal whether the participants have grasped the logical structure of the objection rather than merely adopted a skeptical posture toward individual responsibility. Finally, tell me which elements of the standard climate-meat-voting framework I should deliberately leave out of this session, with an explicit statement of what is being omitted and why, so that the constructed infrastructure scenario remains the central vehicle rather than a decorative illustration.
