I was discussing with a friend recently how scientists justify predictions about things they have not yet observed — for instance, that emeralds will still be green next year — and he mentioned a philosopher named Nelson Goodman who introduced a curious term, "grue", for objects that are green before some specified time and blue thereafter. I am not formally trained in philosophy, but I have been reflecting on this idea independently, and I think I grasp the central difficulty: since every emerald observed so far is both green and grue, induction appears to support the prediction that emeralds will remain green no more strongly than the prediction that they will suddenly turn blue. Yet I find myself suspecting that the paradox may be less profound than it first appears. "Grue", after all, strikes me as a contrived, artificial predicate — one that stitches together two unrelated colours using an arbitrary date — whereas "green" seems to denote a genuine, unified natural property. So I wonder: if scientific practice were disciplined to employ only predicates that correspond to real kinds in nature, and to reject all such gerrymandered constructions, would that not restore induction and effectively dissolve Goodman's challenge? Or does this response misunderstand the problem at a more fundamental level?

Please provide your answer in British English. I should be grateful if you could clarify whether restricting our vocabulary to "natural" properties actually solves the paradox, and if not, what the correct philosophical response to Goodman's argument actually involves.
