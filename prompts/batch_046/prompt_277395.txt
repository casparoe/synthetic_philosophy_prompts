Following a long and somewhat heated discussion with a friend about whether philosophical clarity undermines human happiness, I have been trying to make sense of a passage in Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur. In the text she argues that genuine contentment requires sustaining certain protective illusions—particularly about the durability of affection and about one's own merit—since a fully rational, unvarnished awareness of human fragility and the deterministic mechanics of passion would dissolve the very conditions that make joy possible. She also proposes that the study of metaphysics and mathematics promotes happiness not by revealing objective truths but by offering an absorbing occupation that distracts the mind from these fragilities, though she adds that such study must remain deliberately incomplete, because fully comprehending the deterministic laws governing desire and attachment would extinguish the spontaneity essential to happiness. I am struggling to see whether she intends this as a normative claim—that rational beings ought to cultivate strategic partial ignorance—or as a purely descriptive psychological observation, and I wonder how this sits with her well-known Newtonian determinism: does she think reason should serve illusion instrumentally, or does she allow reason an authority that makes this advocacy internally unstable? Please explain this conceptual knot in exactly five numbered points, each consisting of one or two sentences, with no introductory or concluding remarks.
