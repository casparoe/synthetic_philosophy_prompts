I am a doctoral researcher in science and technology studies, and I am seeking conceptual guidance on a governance conflict within an interdisciplinary collective I co-founded. We curate an open-access repository of ethnobotanical field notes that demands continual verification and taxonomic standardization. The notes themselves are non-depletable—downloading does not diminish the archive—but the expert labor required to maintain accuracy and interoperability is finite and currently voluntary. A commercial herbal-medicine firm has begun extracting large batches for proprietary development without contributing to curation, and several graduate-student contributors have withdrawn their labor, arguing that they bear disproportionate costs for benefits that accrue to free-riders. One faction advocates a tiered-access model: unrestricted academic use, but licensing fees for commercial entities, with revenue directed toward paid curatorship. Another faction insists that any fee structure violates our founding ethos of unrestricted circulation and will eventually exclude independent scholars with limited institutional support. I have been asked to draft a governance framework, but I am struggling to determine whether our dilemma is best understood as over-appropriation from a shared pool, as under-provision of a collective good, or as both simultaneously, given that the data layer and the maintenance layer exhibit different economic properties. Could you advise me on the philosophical structure underlying this conflict? Please distinguish the appropriation dynamics from the contribution dynamics, evaluate whether design principles developed for common-pool resource governance apply symmetrically to the informational content versus the sustaining labor, and assess whether a differentiated licensing regime resolves the underlying problem or merely displaces it. Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then provide the reasoning, without preamble and without restating the question.
