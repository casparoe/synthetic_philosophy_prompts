I will be presenting a paper next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on mathematical cognition to an audience split among formal epistemologists, cognitive psychologists who study analogical reasoning, and working mathematicians who are generally skeptical that computational models capture genuine insight. The thesis I am defending is a reconstructed version of Hofstadter’s knob-twiddling model of creative analogy, applied specifically to the generation of novel proof strategies. Rather than treating conceptual variation as movement through a semantic similarity network such as the Slipnet, I propose that in mathematical domains the adjustable “knobs” should be understood as syntactic proof-theoretic invariants—properties like conservativity over a base theory, preservation of decidability, or stability under particular model-theoretic expansions. On this view, creative analogy in mathematics is not driven by associative proximity between concepts but by constrained twiddling within a lattice of formal consistency conditions; an analogical mapping feels illuminating precisely when the syntactic adjustments it requires preserve membership in that lattice, and it feels forced when they violate it, even if the semantic resemblance is strong. I argue that this explains several well-known cases in which mathematicians reject structurally parallel arguments across domains on the grounds that the formal cost is too high, without being able to articulate the rejection in purely semantic terms.

The audience is likely to come at me in three waves. The mathematicians will treat the model as an unwelcome reduction of intuition to syntax and will ask how a lattice of invariants could ever account for the normative judgment that one analogical leap is deep while another is merely clever. The cognitive psychologists will worry that by jettisoning semantic slippage and associative activation I have abandoned everything that makes analogy psychologically plausible, leaving a model that might describe formal consistency but not human cognition. The formal epistemologists will press whether I have simply relocated the grounding problem—if the lattice is defined by proof-theoretic properties, then explaining why those properties should constrain creative reasoning rather than others seems to require the very semantic or normative commitments I claimed to avoid. I need these objections ordered by how damaging they are to the paper’s survival, phrased in the vocabulary each subgroup actually uses, with a brief reply for each, an explicit note on where that reply is weakest, and guidance on which concessions I should make up front so I do not appear defensive.

Please also identify the single objection I am most hoping nobody raises—the one that targets the weakest point in my own position, namely my claim that syntactic constraint lattices can fully explain the normative authority of mathematical analogy without smuggling in tacit semantic content about what counts as a “relevant” invariant. I want this flagged clearly, with a strategy for defending it in under two minutes rather than hoping the room moves on, and I want the full prep sheet structured so that I know what to hold, what to concede, and where my argument is genuinely soft.
