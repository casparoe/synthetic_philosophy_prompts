As a novelist constructing a philosophical dialogue, I want my character—a decision theorist—to present an argument that imprecise probabilities destroy practical rationality, yet I need the flaw exposed. Here is her reasoning, numbered:

(1) Genuine rational belief is often imprecise: a rational agent holds credences only within intervals, not at sharp points.
(2) Expected utility requires a single probability distribution; with an interval of distributions, the same act can have different expected values under different admissible precisifications.
(3) Therefore, whenever probabilities are imprecise and outcomes differ, there is no fact of the matter about which act maximises expected utility.
(4) But practical rationality demands that there be a correct choice; if no choice is determinately best, rationality issues no guidance.
(5) Since real-world beliefs are pervasively imprecise, imprecise-probability frameworks render rational choice almost everywhere impossible—an absurd result that proves such frameworks incoherent.

My character suspects the slide occurs between (3) and (4), perhaps mistaking epistemic openness for practical silence, but she is unsure. Which premise should she surrender? Would adopting an interval decision rule—such as maximising the minimal expected utility—save determinacy, or does it merely relocate the arbitrariness? And if she abandons (1) and insists on sharp credences, what does she forfeit?
