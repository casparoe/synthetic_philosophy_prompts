I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of value and the philosophy of life, and I'm drafting a six-month fellowship proposal on a structural tension in Moritz Schlick's well-known essay on the meaning of life. Schlick's central move is to deny that human existence has any transcendent purpose—any Sinn assigned from outside—while insisting that life can still possess Wert through absorbed, autotelic activity that he characterizes as play or Spiel. Commentators have treated this largely as a phenomenological claim, comparing it to flow states or to Aristotelian praxis, but I think the normative architecture of the distinction remains unexamined. Specifically, Schlick needs a criterion that distinguishes genuinely valuable play from mere distraction or compulsion, yet any such criterion seems to invoke a coherence among an agent's deeper preferences that risks smuggling teleology back in through the side door.

The precise question I want to pursue is this: Can Schlick's anti-teleological value theory be reconstructed by applying a formal model of idealized preference coherence—similar in structure to the framework sometimes called coherent extrapolated volition, though stripped of its decision-theoretic machinery and applied purely to axiological judgment—such that an activity counts as valuable play just in case it would be endorsed by a fully informed, bias-corrected, structurally coherent version of the agent's own preference set? Or does any coherence condition of this kind necessarily import an external normative standard that Schlick's rejection of cosmic purpose cannot accommodate?

Existing answers leave this open because the secondary literature on Schlick focuses on his psychological descriptions of joy and absorption rather than on the implicit normative logic of his selection criterion, while discussions of idealized preference satisfaction in value theory typically treat coherence as a discovery procedure for objective goods rather than as a possible grounding for anti-objectivist, internalist claims. No one has tested whether Schlick's specific rejection of transcendent meaning is compatible with a coherence-based internalist filter for valuable activity.

My method would proceed in three phases. Over the first two months I will reconstruct Schlick's argument from the primary text, formalizing his conditions for valuable play into three explicit criteria—pursuit for its own sake, capacity for full absorption, and compatibility with the agent's broader life structure—without relying on secondary literature for the core distinction. In months three and four I will apply a modified coherence test to each criterion, asking whether a hypothetical fully reflective version of the agent, with complete self-knowledge and corrected cognitive biases, would endorse continuing the activity, and I will check at each step whether hidden teleological commitments enter. Month five will compare this reconstructed view against two alternative frameworks—desire-satisfaction and objective-list theories—to determine whether they can accommodate Schlick's phenomenological descriptions without the coherence machinery. The final month is reserved for writing up the results and assessing failure conditions.

A successful result would be either a reconstructed Schlickian view in which coherence serves as a purely procedural filter that leaves the anti-teleological substance intact, or a demonstration that coherence conditions necessarily import normative standards Schlick must reject, thereby forcing a revision of his value theory. The project fails if the coherence framework turns out to be either trivially applicable to all activities—collapsing Schlick's distinction between play and distraction—or indistinguishable from standard objective-list theories, rendering the reconstruction redundant. The scope is realistic for a six-month fellowship because it relies on conceptual reconstruction and comparative analysis rather than empirical work; the main risk is that the formal framework may sit uneasily with Schlick's literary-philosophical style, but that tension itself yields a philosophically interesting negative result.
