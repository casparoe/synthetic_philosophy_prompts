I've been chewing on Dennett's counterfeit-people idea — the worry that an AI could pass every behavioral test for being a real person without actually having the inner lights on. My friend and I were arguing about whether today's LLMs fit that, and we kinda invented this middle category we're calling "hollow interlocuters." The idea is they aren't faking full-blown consciousness like Dennett's counterfeit people, but they're faking something narrower: the conversational vulnerablity that makes talking to someone feel like a real risk. You know, the sense that they can actually be hurt by being wrong, or that there's something genuinely at stake for them in the exchange. A hollow interlocuter simulates the language but misses that particpatory fragility.

So here's my position: if a system is just a hollow interlocuter, Dennett's counterfeiting charge misses the point entirely, because there's no pretense of personhood — just an absence of stakes. And that absence means we shouldn't grant them moral status either, not because they lack consciousness (who really knows) but because they lack the kind of exposed, risk-bearing engagement that makes moral consideration relevant in dialogue. What would actually count against this? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be — including things that have arguably already happened with current models, plus an honest check on whether I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability. Like, if a model ever acts genuinely surprised or vulnerable, can I just say "well that's simulated fragility" and keep the view? Is that insulation a problem for a claim like this? Also, does Dennett's own framework reject this hollow-interlocuter split, and is there any formal or empirical result showing the distinction between counterfeit person and hollow interlocuter collapses?
