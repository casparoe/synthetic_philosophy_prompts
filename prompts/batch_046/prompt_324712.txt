I have been working through Aristotle's discussion of the sea battle in De Interpretatione chapter 9, and I believe I understand the central logical move: for a contingent future event, neither the affirmation nor its negation is determinately true at the present time, since if either were true now, the event would be necessary and contingency would be destroyed. Yet I find myself puzzled by an asymmetry that seems to underlie this reasoning. If the absence of a present truth-maker for the claim that there will be a sea battle tomorrow is precisely what preserves the openness of the future, then why does the corresponding absence not arise for the claim that there was a sea battle yesterday in a counterfactual branch in which it did not occur? This suggests to me that Aristotle is not merely invoking a failure of bivalence for future-tensed propositions, but is implicitly relying on a branching-time ontology in which present truths ground all past histories while leaving future histories metaphysically unanchored. On this reading, his argument concerns not logic alone but the directional anchoring of propositions across temporal branches. My question, then, is whether modern branching-time semantics actually captures Aristotle's concern, or whether it merely formalizes the asymmetry—assigning propositions to sets of histories—without addressing the underlying metaphysical question of what makes a future-tensed claim true or false. Furthermore, within Aristotle's own hylomorphic framework, is there any basis for thinking that potentialities carry truth-makers prior to actualization, or does the gap he identifies remain genuinely irreducible?
