I am attempting to read Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù through a decision-theoretic lens. In The Prince, Machiavelli compares fortune to a violent river whose floods must be contained by dikes, and he advises that she can be mastered only by bold, aggressive action—what he calls virtù. I have interpreted this as an early theory of decision-making under radical uncertainty: fortune represents an unobserved state of nature, and virtù is the agent's capacity to form strong priors and update rapidly given sparse evidence, rather like Bayesian reasoning in conditions of high variance. But this makes Machiavelli's gendered imagery puzzling. If fortune is merely epistemic limitation—noise in the agent's model—then optimal belief revision should neutralize her influence without requiring the agent to adopt a posture of domination. Why does Machiavelli insist that fortune favors young, bold men, and that she must be struck and beaten? Is the gendered violence structurally integral to virtù, or can the decision-theoretic reading be preserved by treating the imagery as purely rhetorical ornament that plays no normative role?
