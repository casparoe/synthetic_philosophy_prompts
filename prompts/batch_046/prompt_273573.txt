I have no academic background in philosophy or religion, but I have been thinking on my own about the Buddhist teaching of anatta—that there is no permanent self, only five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness) that arise and cease. What keeps returning to me is that consciousness is only one of those five, so the feeling of being an inner watcher must itself be impermanent and empty of an owner. I want a poem that enacts this structurally rather than explaining it.

Write a sestina whose six end-words, in order, are form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness, and watcher. Rotate them through the six stanzas according to the sestina pattern so that the watcher never occupies a fixed position as the container of the others, but is treated as one rotating term among them. For the three-line envoi, include only the five aggregate words, leaving the watcher literally absent—so that its disappearance is built into the form. Make the language precise enough that someone familiar with the Abhidharma analysis recognizes the aggregates, yet let the poem stand on its own for a reader with no Buddhist background. Do not resolve the tension with comfort; let the absence remain open.
