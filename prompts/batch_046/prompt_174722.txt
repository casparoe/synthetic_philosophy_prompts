In recent years, strategic-planning bodies ranging from central-bank stress-test divisions to climate-risk boards have adopted a formal “dual-track” forecasting protocol: analysts must construct an inside-view narrative that traces case-specific causal mechanisms, pair it with an outside-view forecast drawn from a reference class of historically analogous events, and then reconcile both into a single decision-relevant probability. Several of these institutions have added a further procedural layer—if the inside view diverges from the outside-view base rate by more than a predetermined margin, the analysis is automatically flagged for an “epistemic-modesty review” conducted by an external panel with no stake in the case-specific narrative. What philosophical commitments about rational belief, the authority of statistical generalization over particularist reasoning, and the proper attitude of epistemic humility does this institutional design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology: does the protocol treat the outside view as a default that the inside view must overcome with exceptional evidence, thereby embedding a form of base-rate fundamentalism, or does it treat the two tracks as incommensurable sources of authority that must be weighted rather than merged? Does the very idea of an “epistemic-modesty review” presuppose that overconfidence is chiefly a failure of individual psychology correctable by external oversight, or that it is a structural feature of institutional incentives that no internal check can remedy? And is the practice coherent with the same bodies’ frequent reliance on expert judgment in domains where reference classes are sparse or contested? To sharpen the analysis, inject the following niche framing: imagine that the protocol were redesigned around a “modesty-first” principle that required the inside view to be articulated not as a competing probability but as a qualitative narrative of the specific ways the reference class might misapply, with no numerical reconciliation permitted. Would such a redesign expose that the current practice already smuggles in a particular theory of epistemic utility—one that privileges decision-relevant precision over faithful representation of uncertainty—or would it show that the practice is philosophically underdetermined, compatible with both accuracy-first and modesty-first epistemologies? Finally, distinguish what the institution strictly needs from what it merely accommodates: say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change in its architecture—who is authorized to declare a reference class appropriate, whether divergence is treated as a cognitive failure or as a legitimate expression of contextual knowledge, and whether the concept of risk retains any objective purchase—if we replaced the implicit commitment to a unified probabilistic synthesis with a rigorously pluralist framework that treats inside and outside perspectives as permanently distinct.
