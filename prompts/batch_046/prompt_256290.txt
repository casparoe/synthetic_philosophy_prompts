Write the desiderata that any adequate normative theory of moral sainthood—understood as maximal moral goodness that remains psychologically sustainable and socially intelligible—must satisfy if it is genuinely to resolve whether such a life is desirable for an agent rather than merely admirable from a distance. I want the full specification first; the evaluation of contenders is secondary.

Your list should cover at least these six clusters, with the motivating case or conceptual pressure behind each:

(i) Boundary conditions: what separates sainthood from ordinary moral diligence on one side and from pathological self-erasure or obsessive altruism on the other, and whether the distinction is one of degree or of kind.

(ii) Constitutive versus supererogatory structure: must maximal goodness be a stable character trait, or can it consist in discrete acts? Include the case of someone who performs extraordinary sacrifices but retains an ordinary self-conception.

(iii) First-personal phenomenology: what sustained maximal other-regarding concern must feel like, and whether the theory permits moral exhaustion, humility, or what we might call moral luminosity—the degree to which extreme goodness must be visible or recognized by others in order to count as sainthood rather than as hidden virtue.

(iv) Social recognizability without performativity: whether a community must be able to identify a saint, and how the theory avoids collapsing sainthood into reputation management or virtue signaling.

(v) Consistency with empirical psychology: the account must not contradict well-established results on altruistic burnout, identity fusion, and the limits of sustained empathy, yet it must not reduce sainthood to a psychological type like the “pathological altruist.”

(vi) The normative deliverable on desirability and demandingness: the theory must yield a clear result about whether maximal goodness is rationally required, permitted, or forbidden, and it must do so without entailing that anyone who fails to achieve it is blameworthy—particularly for agents with legitimate personal projects or attachments.

For each desideratum, flag whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (e.g., virtue-ethical maximalists, demandingness-sensitive consequentialists, or those influenced by the critique that associates sainthood with an impoverished personal life). Then identify any subset of these requirements that appears jointly unsatisfiable—especially whether psychological sustainability conflicts with maximal other-regarding concern, or whether the visibility condition conflicts with genuine humility. Indicate which single desideratum does the most work in excluding the most popular existing accounts.

Finally, briefly and without bibliographic references, score three broad approaches against your list: the critique that sainthood requires sacrificing non-moral personal goods, a virtue-ethical maximalism that treats extreme virtue as the telos of human development, and a demandingness-sensitive view that treats sainthood as permissible but not required. Keep the weight on the criteria, their tensions, and the unsatisfiable subsets; treat the scoring as a brief check rather than the main deliverable.
