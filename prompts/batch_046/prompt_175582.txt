Please describe the most important implications of Plato’s apparently contradictory treatments of intoxication—wherein the Symposium presents wine as a revelatory mechanism that exposes the hierarchical stability of the soul’s parts, while the Laws treats symposia as state-regulated instruments for civic education and moral stress-testing—for the design of contemporary institutional policies that aim to cultivate virtue through structured vulnerability rather than through prohibition alone. Since I was recently appointed to revise the residential ethics code at my university, I am especially interested in whether Plato’s framework supports the idea that an institution should deliberately engineer low-stakes conditions in which members’ rational and spirited capacities are temporarily destabilized, so that their character can be observed and corrected in a supervised setting; and, conversely, whether the legislative restrictions in the Laws imply that such testing is only legitimate when embedded in a hierarchical authority structure that can interpret the results. Focus on the conceptual structure of the argument—particularly the relation between the soul’s tripartition, the epistemic value of revealed preference under intoxication, and the conditions under which an institution can claim to know the character of its members—rather than on historical commentary or textual exegesis.
