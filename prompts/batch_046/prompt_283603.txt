I've spent the past couple of years working through George Berkeley's philosophy on my own — I have no formal training in philosophy, only a stubborn habit of following arguments to their end. One idea that kept returning is the so-called master argument: the claim that we cannot consistently conceive of an object existing without being perceived. I have come to see that there are two serious camps that rarely meet on shared ground. One camp holds that the argument exposes a genuine contradiction in the notion of mind-independent matter. The other holds that it succeeds only by sliding between different senses of conceiving and that a coherent supposition of unperceived existence remains available.

Rather than asking for an adjudication, I want you to draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a rigorous adversarial collaboration. Please name each position precisely, in terms its own proponents would accept.

The document should open with a numbered section of agreed claims — not vague commonplaces, but substantive points both sides can endorse about what the master argument actually asserts, about the status of abstract ideas in Berkeley's system, about whether the argument targets sensory qualities specifically or extends to all properties, and about where the burden of proof properly rests. Then provide a disagreements table. Each row should present the precise formulation of the residual disagreement, worded so that the camp holding that view would recognize its own position in the phrasing.

After the table, identify the cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that resolving them would move at least one side. For each crux, set out the case, thought experiment, or line of reasoning that both camps agree would genuinely bear on the issue. Alongside each, record in advance what each camp predicts the resolution would show.

I want one particular thread woven throughout, without letting it overwhelm the general structure: the application of the master argument to dispositional properties — properties like solubility or fragility that are defined not by present manifestation but by what would happen under conditions that might never actually occur. Does the argument carry special weight against the idea of an unperceived dispositional property, or does it falter precisely there? Make sure this appears in the agreed claims and in at least one crux.

Write this as a formal, constructive text — not a dramatized exchange, not a verdict on who is right — that both parties could put their names to, with every remaining disagreement made exact. Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations to specific editions or page numbers; I am working without library access and want the reasoning to stand on its own terms.
