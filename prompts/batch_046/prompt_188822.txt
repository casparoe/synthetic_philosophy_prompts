I'm putting together a metaphysics seminar, about 10 grad students, one 75-min session. I want them to feel the special composition question in there bones — not just nod at it. Design an in-class game with rules and small token-stakes (no cash needed) where they physically group and split objects I bring in — lego bricks, string, a jar of water, printed photos of distributed server clusters — and have to rule whether each arrangement is "one thing" or "many things." Build in weird cases that involve spatial discontinuity and socially-dependent artifacts so they hit ontological dependence across gaps; that's the niche twist, not just the ship of Theseus replay.

The rules should make them construct a boundary policy themselves, apply it to seperate cases, and then watch it collapse on something they actually care about. They shouldn't hear the phrase "special composition question" named until the debrief. In the debrief, map there specific breaking points back onto nihilism, universalism, and restricted composition; be honest about what the game can't reproduce — probably modal or temporal persistence, maybe vagueness over time? — and warn me what'll go wrong first run, like one person dominating or everyone treating it like a joke. No lecture up front, don't ask for a bibliography, and weave that discontinuity/social-dependence idea right into the case descriptions so it's not something they've seen in every pretraining example.
