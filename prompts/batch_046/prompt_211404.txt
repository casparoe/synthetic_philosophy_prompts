I am preparing a consistency audit of my own views for a philosophy of art seminar I am taking in my final year of high school, and I would like an outside check before I present them to my classmates. Over the past month I have been arguing about these issues with friends after school, and I have settled on six commitments that feel plausible in isolation but may not cohere as a set. First, I hold that the aesthetic value of an artwork is determined primarily by its internal formal and expressive properties—its compositional coherence, emotional dynamics, and the integrity of its representational aims—rather than by the biography or moral character of its creator. Second, I think that genuinely immoral content can sometimes contribute positively to a work’s aesthetic power: for instance, a painting or film that depicts cruelty without sanitizing it may be more unified and compelling precisely because it allows that cruelty to resonate structurally. Third, I believe that if we discover an artist’s real-world conduct genuinely embodies and was motivated by the same immoral perspective expressed in the work—imagine a director whose films glorify domination and who was later convicted of exploiting others for that exact purpose—this discovery can legitimately diminish our aesthetic appreciation of the films, not merely our willingness to fund or display them.

Fourth, I maintain that cultural institutions have a moral duty to weigh an artist’s conduct in curatorial and funding decisions, even when the works in question are aesthetically excellent. Fifth, I have been working with what I call a provenance-parity principle: appreciating a work’s expressive stance involves a tacit kind of alignment with it, and if that stance turns out to be the artist’s sincerely held harmful view rather than a fictional or critical exploration, then our prior admiration is undermined in much the same way that admiration for a forged painting collapses once the forgery is revealed. Sixth, I think that in every case we ought to correct for biographical influence on aesthetic judgment, treating any such influence as a cognitive bias rather than a legitimate critical response. Could you audit these commitments? I want to know which pairs are in genuine tension, which conflicts are only apparent, and for each real tension how severe it is and what the cheapest revision would cost me. Please rank the tensions by severity, say whether fixing them mainly threatens my autonomy thesis, my institutional-duty claim, or my provenance-parity principle, and tell me which single revision would eliminate the most inconsistencies overall.
