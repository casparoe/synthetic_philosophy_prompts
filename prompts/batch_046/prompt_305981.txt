I have recently become professionally involved with a mental-health technology venture that is piloting a predictive scheme using smartphone keystroke dynamics and geolocation patterns to flag users for pre-emptive psychiatric assessment, with automatic referral pathways to involuntary evaluation services. As I prepare the ethical review for this pilot, I find myself returning to Thomas Szasz's sustained critique of psychiatric diagnosis as a social myth and his arguments against coercive psychiatric practice. I should like you to predict, in detail, how Szasz would analyse this specific arrangement—particularly the substitution of clinical interview by algorithmic proxy, and the deployment of involuntary intervention before any manifest harm or expressed request for help. Please write in British English. Address these points: (1) whether he would regard the algorithmic risk score as a new form of myth or merely as an extension of existing psychiatric ideology; (2) how he might distinguish, if at all, between voluntary contractual therapy and this form of automated surveillance; (3) what he would likely say about consent when the subject has never requested evaluation and the putative symptom is inferred from behavioural metadata rather than from reported experience; and (4) whether any feature of this scenario might lead him to revise his earlier scepticism about the legitimacy of psychiatric diagnosis altogether. Do not provide bibliographies, reading lists, or citations; I seek conceptual reasoning rather than scholarly apparatus.
