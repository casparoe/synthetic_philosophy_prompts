For the past several months, since a long-standing professional collaboration collapsed in a way that felt both predictable and personally devastating, I have been trying to understand whether my persistent resentment toward the other party counts as a morally appropriate reactive attitude in Strawson's sense or merely as a private emotional residue that I ought to suspend. I have become particularly interested in how Strawson's naturalistic account — which treats reactive attitudes as constitutive of interpersonal relationships and exempts only those who are genuinely candidates for the objective attitude — relates to the more normatively charged idea that reactive attitudes are a form of second-personal moral address, an exercise of authority that requires not just emotional engagement but a legitimate claim to hold the other accountable. Could you compare Strawson's reactive-attitude framework with the second-personal moral-address framework on three specific points: first, whether moral responsibility requires an actual emotional response or only the normative entitlement to demand one; second, how each view treats cases of institutional or structural harm, where the wrongdoer retains power and the victim's reactive attitude may have no practical effect; and third, which framework offers a more defensible boundary between warranted resentment and an inappropriate slide into the objective attitude? I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey of the literature, but for a conceptual comparison that could help me decide whether maintaining my reactive stance is morally justified or whether I should adopt the objective attitude toward someone whose actions have become, in my view, irredeemable.
