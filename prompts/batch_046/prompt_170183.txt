Write a sestina—six stanzas of six unrhymed lines, followed by a three-line envoi—addressing whether ranking historical atrocities is possible without moral obscenity. The six end-words must be, in order for the first stanza: measure, kind, scale, count, name, silence, rotating through the standard sestina sequence (F A E B D C; C F D A B E; E C B D A F; D E A C F B; B D F E C A; then an envoi containing all six). Contrast two structurally different evils—an administrative genocide and a famine produced by institutional neglect—without reducing them to a single metric. In the opening stanza, establish that comparing them demands a shared measure that risks erasing their distinct kind. Through the middle stanzas, allow the compulsory rotation to generate grammatical tension: let measure govern kind as its object, let count appear as a verb threatening name as its target, so that the form itself enacts the violence of quantification. In the envoi, place silence in the final line, leaving genuinely open whether refusing to rank is a moral failure or the only respectful response. Do not resolve toward either a utilitarian calculus or an absolute prohibition; let the form's repetition mirror the speaker's compulsive return to comparison despite doubting its legitimacy. After the poem, provide a brief note—no more than 150 words—separating what is settled (that different atrocities have distinct structural features and comparison requires some shared dimension) from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of value (whether incommensurability blocks all ordinal ranking, whether partial comparison can proceed without full metric reduction). The poem should be recognizable to someone familiar with debates on incommensurable evils, and should stand as poetry for a reader unfamiliar with them.
