You serve as the ethics-compliance lead for an international public-health consortium that approves regional interventions using quantitative welfare-impact modeling. The consortium operates under a modified consequentialist mandate: approve any proposal whose expected welfare gains exceed its costs, unless the proposal demands more of staff, partner governments, or beneficiary communities than a reasonable institutional agent can be required to bear. Your task is to design a reusable audit procedure—not a one-time judgment—that a three-person review panel (clinicians, program officers, and local representatives, none with philosophy training) can complete in ninety minutes to determine whether an intervention plan crosses from "permissibly demanding" into "excessively demanding" and must be rejected or redesigned.

The procedure must translate the philosophical demandingness objection into institutional terms. It should include: a fixed sequence of questions the panel asks in order; for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer (numerical, narrative, or both) and what forces the panel to move to the next; explicit stop-points where the answer is "reject" or "redesign required"; explicit hand-off points where the question is irreducibly about values and must be decided by a designated ethics officer rather than derived from facts; and clear markings of which steps are judgment calls disguised as procedure. The procedure must distinguish resource costs (money, time, materials) from sacrifices (erosion of moral agency, institutional integrity, or community identity), and must treat demands placed on agent-staff differently from demands placed on beneficiary populations who are not acting as moral agents in the relevant sense. It must also handle distributive concerns: whether a burden falls on many lightly or on a few crushingly.

Apply the procedure to three proposals. First, a vaccination campaign requiring local nurses to work seventy-hour weeks for six months in remote clinics with minimal hazard pay and no replacement staff—where the welfare gains are high but the labor burden is concentrated. Second, a water-purification program requiring a partner government to redirect forty percent of its education budget for two years—where the burden is fiscal and political rather than bodily, and falls on a non-agent institution. Third, a mental-health screening initiative that requires community health workers to record and transmit detailed family-conflict and trauma data to a central authority—where the procedure may yield an answer you think is wrong: specifically, design the audit so that it likely approves the program because the burden is informational and diffuse rather than a direct resource sacrifice, even though you believe the relational and privacy harms should have triggered rejection. After running all three, state precisely what the procedure cannot adjudicate—where it will systematically misfire, which philosophical distinctions it collapses for usability, and which decisions remain genuinely open even after the audit is complete.
