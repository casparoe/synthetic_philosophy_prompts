In discussions comparing the Late Bronze Age systems collapse, the Classic Maya decline, and Easter Island's ecological transformation, I keep encountering the claim that "collapse is just rapid transformation." The speaker deploys it both to deny that these societies genuinely failed and to reject any cross-case comparison. Please lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be advancing—for instance, that demographic contraction is always compensated by cultural continuity; that "collapse" is an etic analytical category with no emic counterpart; that political decentralization is functionally indistinguishable from societal failure; or that normative evaluation of past societal trajectories is impossible. For each reading, indicate whether it is tautological, empirically defensible given the archaeological and historical records, or false with respect to the Bronze Age, Maya, and Easter Island cases specifically, and identify which version the speaker actually needs for her dismissal to succeed. Finally, explain how the slogan's argumentative work differs across the three cases, since the mechanisms involved—systems-integration loss, drought-adapted urban abandonment, and deforestation-driven isolation—are not uniform.
