As a PhD student in philosophy of logic, I have been trying to clarify my own commitments about deductive justification by treating Carroll’s dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise as a diagnostic framing rather than a settled argument. Here are my verdicts on six concrete cases. First, when an experienced logician derives Q from P and P→Q without ever articulating modus ponens, I judge her inference fully justified; explicit citation of the rule is unnecessary. Second, in Carroll’s scenario I think the Tortoise is not merely being perverse when he accepts premises A and B, and also the hypothetical that if A and B then Z, yet still refuses to accept Z unless an additional linking principle is added; he is exposing a genuine gap between accepting premises and accepting the inferential license that binds them. Third, a student who correctly solves a syllogism but can only report that the conclusion just looks right strikes me as justified nonetheless, though through direct cognitive grasp rather than through articulated rule-following. Fourth, a language model that produces correct logical outputs for entirely novel inputs, despite possessing no symbolic representation of inference rules and no personal-level grasp of why the outputs follow, does not strike me as genuinely justified in its deductions; behavioral reliability alone is insufficient. Fifth, a mathematician who applies a complex lemma she does not understand, relying solely on the authority of a colleague, is not fully justified in her own right, even though the proof she produces is valid. Sixth, a reasoner who demands an explicit meta-rule for every single application of modus ponens, thereby generating an infinite regress of justifications, is not being rational; at some point the demand for further license becomes vicious rather than virtuous.

These verdicts feel mostly stable, but I keep returning to an unresolved tension. In case two I seem to insist that accepting an inferential rule is essential, which sounds like endorsing the Tortoise’s demand for more; yet in case one I deny that explicit articulation is required, and in case three I allow justification without any articulable rule at all. Meanwhile, case four denies justification to a system that behaves correctly without any grasp of rules, which pulls back toward requiring some cognitive or representational engagement with the rule, but case three suggests that such engagement need not be explicit or even introspectively available.

Is there a single principle—perhaps centered on a distinction between implicit operative acceptance at the personal level and mere subpersonal or behavioral reliability—that captures all six verdicts? If not, which of my judgments should be revised: should I concede that the Tortoise is indeed being perverse and that case two collapses into case one, or should I instead deny that the student in case three is genuinely justified? Please state the principle precisely, identifying the case that motivates each clause, and provide its verdict on a further case I have not listed: a child who, through repeated conditioning, reliably applies all-A-are-B patterns to novel instances but cannot articulate any general rule, shows no understanding of why the pattern holds rather than merely tracking correlations, and exhibits no frustration or curiosity when the pattern occasionally fails. Finally, please say honestly which of my original six verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without strain, and what revision that forces.
