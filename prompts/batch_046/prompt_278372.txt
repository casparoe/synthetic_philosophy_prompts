Consider the institutional practice—common in forensic psychiatric evaluations and long-term guardianship proceedings—of assessing whether an individual retains personal identity sufficient for legal responsibility not mainly through memory tests or bodily-continuity checks, but through what clinicians might call a narrative coherence audit: the patient must demonstrate an ability to construct a first-person story linking past actions to present intentions with minimal discontinuity, and courts often treat severe breaks in that narrative, as in certain dissociative conditions, as grounds for diminished capacity or temporary suspension of legal personhood. At the same time, within the same healthcare systems, trauma therapists routinely encourage patients to tolerate and even cultivate fragmented, non-linear self-narratives, treating forced narrative unity as potentially oppressive or inauthentic rather than as a therapeutic goal. What theory of the self does this dual practice presuppose? Does it treat the self as Dennett’s center of narrative gravity—an abstract point around which stories cluster rather than a substance—such that disrupting the narrative literally dissolves the legal self, while therapeutic health requires allowing multiple gravitational centers? Or does it quietly smuggle in a substance-based view, holding that there is a real self that narrative either reveals or obscures? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, say whether the legal and clinical contexts actually share one consistent view or presuppose incompatible theories of personal identity, and tell me what would have to change in either courtroom procedure or clinical protocol if we replaced the narrative-gravity model with a straightforward bodily-continuity or psychological-continuity account.
