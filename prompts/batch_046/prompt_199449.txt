I have recently come into possession of my great-uncle's wartime papers, including a carbon-copied requisition form misrouted in August 1943—an error that, according to family history, delayed his transfer and thereby placed him in the depot where he met my great-aunt. This trivial administrative slip now feels personally weighty, and it has led me to formalize a broader argument about macro-historical contingency that I find suspicious because it seems to annihilate the very possibility of structural historiography. Here it is:

(1) If a macro-historical outcome O depends counterfactually on an arbitrarily small event C—such that, had C not occurred, O would not have occurred—then C possesses the same modal status as any macro-structural factor M (e.g., economic collapse, institutional failure) that is also counterfactually necessary for O.

(2) In complex social systems with dense feedback loops, arbitrarily small perturbations can propagate into arbitrarily large trajectory divergences; this is not merely a physical property of weather but applies, mutatis mutandis, to military logistics, bureaucratic routing, and demographic movement.

(3) Therefore, for any macro-outcome O explained by some structural factor M, there exists some arbitrarily small contingency C (a lost form, a changed meeting time, a misfiled nail) that is equally counterfactually necessary for O.

(4) Historical explanation requires not merely listing counterfactual dependencies but ranking them by explanatory significance; however, if (3) holds, there is no non-arbitrary criterion for preferring M to C, since both are necessary and both are, ex hypothesi, outside the control of the agents involved in O.

(5) Consequently, macro-historical explanation collapses: every institutional narrative can be replaced, with equal logical right, by a narrative of micro-accidents.

(6) This is intolerable—historians legitimately explain revolutions by structural conditions rather than by lost paperwork.

(7) Hence at least one of (1) through (4) must fail.

I am inclined to defend (1) and (2) because they match both my family archive and the dynamics of complex systems. But if the argument is valid, then saving macro-historiography requires giving up something I value. Where exactly does the argument go wrong? Is there a hidden slide between counterfactual necessity and explanatory salience—perhaps an equivocation over what it means for a cause to "matter"—or is there a quantifier shift in (4) that illegitimately moves from "there exists a C" to "C is equally explanatory"? If I must pay a price, I would rather preserve (1) and (2) and accept that macro-explanations are always provisional and context-relative, but I want to know whether that price is indeed required, or whether I can reject a single step without losing the intuition that made the argument compelling.
