I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been thinking for some time about the old puzzle of a statue and the lump of clay from which it is made. I am preparing a short interview series for an independent podcast, and my next guest is a philosopher who defends a particular form of constitutionalism: she holds that the statue and the clay are genuinely distinct objects, not identical, and that the relation between them is governed not by mere spatial overlap but by what she calls kind-relative modal resilience — the principle that what counts as destroying an object depends on the essential properties of its kind, so that the statue is destroyed by aesthetic deformation even when the underlying matter survives intact in a different shape.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go well beyond asking her to restate this view. I want the sequence to include:

- An opening question that presses her on why kind-relative modal resilience is not just a disguised linguistic convention about how we use the word "statue" versus "clay."
- At least one question centered on a specific scenario I keep returning to: a clay statue that is gradually replaced, molecule by molecule, with a synthetic polymer that behaves identically to clay in every physical respect; the polymer form is then melted and reshaped into a sphere. I want to ask precisely when, in this sequence, the original statue ceases to exist, and whether her answer changes if the replacement happens all at once rather than gradually.
- A question that raises the combination problem for her constitutionalism: if the statue is constituted by the clay, and the clay is in turn constituted by its molecules, does this generate a regress of infinitely many constituted objects occupying the same space?
- For each of these three, a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. In particular, for the polymer-replacement case, anticipate that she might argue the statue's form is preserved in the new substrate, and ask whether this commits her to the view that form can migrate across materially different substrates without loss of identity.
- A closing question about what kind of evidence or imagined case could persuade her to abandon constitutionalism in favor of an identity theory, specifically avoiding appeals to parsimony or Occam's razor.

The tone should be formal and polished, appropriate for a recorded colloquium Q&A rather than a casual chat. Do not include generic questions about her biography or how she entered the field, and do not ask for reading lists, citations, or bibliographic recommendations.
