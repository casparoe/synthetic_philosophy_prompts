I help administer a long-running online forum with a rationalist audience, where members vote quarterly on which long-form essays to fund from a shared pool. Over the years we have cycled through plurality, approval, and Borda-style ranking, but in every case members eventually began misreporting preferences—ranking mediocre proposals highly just to block disliked ones, or withholding support from viable options to manipulate outcomes. In response, we recently adopted a hybrid mechanism: each member still submits a ranked ballot, but the weight of that ballot is scaled by a "calibration score" derived from how accurately the member's past predictions about proposal quality matched actual post-publication ratings by the community. The hope is that strategic distortion will be penalized, since gamers will accumulate poor calibration and lose influence. One co-administrator insists that with further tinkering—perhaps adjusting the scaling function or adding reputation bonds—we can eventually arrive at an aggregation rule where honest reporting is always the best response, regardless of what others do. I have a strong but inarticulate sense that he is pursuing something structurally impossible, though I am unsure whether the calibration layer genuinely changes the aggregation rule or merely relocates the strategic pressure upstream into gaming the calibration metric itself. Is there a formal result that rules out any non-trivial, non-dictatorial voting procedure with three or more alternatives being immune to this kind of preference misrepresentation? And if the result holds even for our weighted scheme, what should that imply for our forum's governance—should we abandon the search for a perfect mechanism and instead focus on transparency, normative constraints, or the size and diversity of the voter pool?
