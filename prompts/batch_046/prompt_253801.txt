Please review the following notes for an essay on Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public. I propose that the standard interpretation, which treats the public as merely an enlarged crowd, misses a structural distinction present in his account. I distinguish between synchronous aggregation—where individuals are physically co-present and mutually visible, as in a crowd—and what I term asynchronous spectatorship, the mode of relation produced when print media allow dispersed individuals to consume the same events without temporal co-presence or reciprocal accountability. On my reading, Kierkegaard's concept of leveling is not simply quantitative dilution of responsibility across numbers, but a qualitative transformation in which direct encounter is replaced by a phantom relation to an abstract totality. This, I suggest, explains why the press threatens the category of the single individual not only by encouraging anonymity, but by substituting mediated opinion for inward deliberation. I am concerned that framing the issue through this distinction may impose an anachronistic media-theoretical vocabulary onto Kierkegaard's theological concern with inwardness. Would you assess whether this distinction genuinely illuminates his texts, particularly his account of leveling, or whether it obscures the specifically religious dimension of his critique? I would also welcome observations on coherence, potential objections, and whether the argument risks conflating technological mediation with the ethical structure of the public as he describes it.
