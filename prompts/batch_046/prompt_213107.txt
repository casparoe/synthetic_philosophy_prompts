I'm currently taking a seminar on moral responsibility and the philosophy of punishment, and I've become stuck on a recurring exchange between the compatibilist defense of just deserts—roughly in the spirit of Daniel Dennett's view that agency is robust enough to ground fair punishment—and the free-will skeptical position associated with Gregg Caruso, which holds that because no one is ultimately responsible for their actions, retributive punishment is never justified and should be replaced by something like a public-health or quarantine model. What puzzles me is that both sides accept the same broad neuroscience of decision-making, both reject libertarian free will as metaphysically extravagant, and both claim to care about fairness; yet after years of debate in the literature and in our classroom discussions, neither side has conceded an inch. The disagreement seems to sit exactly where the metaphysics ends and the normative architecture begins, but I can't tell whether that boundary is itself the source of the problem.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not really about free will at all, but about whether moral desert should be understood as an intrinsic, synchronic property of an agent at the moment of judgment, or rather as a diachronic, relational feature that emerges from a community's shared predictive models of behavior—something like an epistemic contract in which punishment functions not to balance a moral ledger but to update collective expectations about future agency. On this framing, Dennett might be defending the coherence of institutional desert within a liberal democratic framework, while Caruso might be attacking the metaphysical pretensions of basic desert, and they could both be right about the thing they are actually describing without recognizing that desert is doing two different kinds of work. But I am unsure whether this diagnosis captures a genuine verbal disagreement masked by a single word, a substantive clash over the standards of evidence for attributing responsibility, or a deeper divergence about bedrock intuitions regarding whether suffering can ever be fair.

Given this, I want a diagnosis rather than an adjudication: is the impasse a hidden empirical question about how punishment actually affects the predictive accuracy of social coordination? Is it a difference about what counts as a moral reason—for instance, whether fairness requires that suffering be proportionate to wrongdoing, or only that it be instrumentally justified? Or are Dennett and Caruso simply answering different questions, one about the legitimacy of state punishment within existing institutions and the other about whether anyone metaphysically deserves harm? And if your diagnosis is correct, what would actually count as progress—a conceptual distinction between institutional and basic desert, a formal model of responsibility attribution, an empirical study of community expectations, or an argument that the entire framing is ill-posed and should be abandoned?
