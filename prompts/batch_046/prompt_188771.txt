I have been reading Lewis Carroll’s dialogue “What the Tortoise Said to Achilles,” which appeared in the journal Mind in 1895, and I keep returning to one question: do logical rules like modus ponens need some kind of justification, or is demanding one a mistake? I have talked with two groups who both understand the dialogue well but reach opposite conclusions, and I want the document they should have produced together.

Camp One—call them the Circularity Worries—say Carroll’s Tortoise exposes something genuinely troubling. If we try to justify the rule of modus ponens, they argue, we must use modus ponens in the justification itself, so any defense is circular. They believe this means deductive reasoning lacks a non-circular foundation, and that this gap matters. They are open to calling reasoning “pragmatically entitled,” but they insist pragmatic entitlement is not the same thing as justification, and that honesty requires us to admit the gap remains.

Camp Two—call them the Stopping-Rule Defenders—say the Tortoise’s demand is a category mistake. Logical rules, on their view, are constitutive of reasoning itself, not hypotheses that need external proof. They agree you cannot justify modus ponens without using it, but they take this to show the request is confused, not that reasoning is unfounded. They are willing to call pragmatic entitlement “justification” in a broader sense, but they maintain no further, non-circular proof is possible or required.
