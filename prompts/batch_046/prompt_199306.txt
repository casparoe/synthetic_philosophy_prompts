I am a novelist who occasionally teaches a graduate seminar on metaphysics and narrative form, and I have found that students grasp philosophical arguments most firmly when they treat them as plot structures with hidden premises driving the action rather than as museum pieces to be catalogued. For the final examination in my current course, I need an essay question that forces candidates to work inside the logic of Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles without allowing them to earn credit merely by naming Black's spheres and reciting the familiar relationalist reply. To that end, the prompt should present a modified counterexample: not simply two spheres in an otherwise empty universe, but two spheres situated in a perfectly symmetric, mirror-image cosmos in which every intrinsic quality and every external relation is duplicated across a central plane, so that even spatial relations to other bodies—were any present—would be identical. The question must ask whether this stronger version can be defeated by appealing to "primitive thisness" (haecceity) without rendering the principle tautological, and whether saving Leibniz by denying that spatial position is a genuine relational property succeeds only by smuggling in an unanalyzed indexical.

Please write the exam question itself, calibrated for a 90-minute response by an advanced undergraduate or first-year graduate student; then provide a model answer of approximately 600 words; and finally supply a marking scheme built around five specific conceptual moves that a top answer must contain, with marks assigned to each (totaling 20 points) and a one-line note on the characteristic failure mode for each. The scheme should clearly separate answers that merely summarize the standard debate from those that actually evaluate whether the modified counterexample forces a choice between a vacuous principle and an unexplained primitive. The entire response should be in American English, and it must not request bibliographies, citations, or reading lists; evaluation should turn entirely on conceptual precision and on the ability to locate the precise point at which the argument breaks. I want the difficulty to arise from identifying that crux rather than from recalling labels or standard summaries.
