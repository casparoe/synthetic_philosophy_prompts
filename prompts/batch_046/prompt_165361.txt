I am a novelist giving the opening remarks at tomorrow evening's retirement symposium for Dr. Elena Voss, whose work on self-reference in formal systems shaped both my fiction and her colleagues' research in logic and cognitive science. The room will be split between philosophers who know Gödel, Escher, Bach intimately and family members who do not; the address must honor her without either half being left outside or turned into an audience for a lecture. I want the speech governed structurally by her three-level account of meaning in any self-interpreting system—the frame message that establishes the rules of decoding, the outer message of literal content, and the inner message that emerges only when the system recognizes its own framing—but applied reflexively to the occasion itself. The difficulty is this: if I state my personal debt to her teaching directly, it collapses into an outer message that the experts decode as illustration and the family receives as generic sentiment; if I make the frame explicit—declaring that the symposium is itself a message about interpretation—I dissolve the inner message of what her ideas actually meant to a writer trying to embed meaning inside nested narratives. Treat the audience as the message: they are simultaneously the frame (interpreting) and the outer message (the community she built). Write a formal, four-minute address that locates the inner message—the particular debt of a novelist to her model of distributed meaning—in the gap between those roles, without synthesizing the tension away or explaining the model didactically. Let the form of the speech perform the problem rather than solve it; keep the tone polished and intimate, avoid false closure, and ensure neither half of the room is asked to pretend they understand what the other half does not.
