I'm currently drafting a chapter on comparative democratic theory and have found myself stuck in what feels less like an empirical dispute and more like a conceptual stalemate that has persisted for nearly two decades. Scholars, journalists, and policymakers have long debated whether Hungary under Orbán, Poland under PiS, Turkey under Erdoğan, and more recently the United States constitute cases of democratic backsliding. The factual record is largely uncontested across camps: executives have expanded control over judiciaries, media pluralism has contracted, electoral rules have been systematically tilted, and civil-society space has narrowed. Yet one group—the institutionalists—maintains that these developments represent clear backsliding because competitive elections remain technically intact while liberal constitutional checks erode; another group—the procedural minimalists—insists that as long as elections are not outright cancelled or stolen, applying the label "backsliding" smuggles in a thick, contested liberal-democratic norm and functions more as partisan rhetoric than as neutral analysis. A third camp suggests both sides are simply answering different questions: the institutionalists are measuring democracy against a constitutional ideal that includes judicial independence and press freedom, while the minimalists measure it against a Schumpeterian competitive-election baseline, making the disagreement verbal rather than substantive.

My working hypothesis is that the stall runs deeper than a definitional quarrel about democracy's core. I suspect a hidden structural disagreement about whether democratic legitimacy is the kind of property that can degrade gradually—like a scalar or dimensional attribute that admits of degrees—or whether it is fundamentally categorical, such that a regime either retains democratic status or crosses a discrete threshold into non-democracy, with no meaningful intermediate state. The institutionalists appear to treat backsliding as scalar decay (a gradual loss of democratic quality over time), while the minimalists treat it as binary and therefore demand a specific rupture event—such as the annulment of elections—to justify the label. If this diagnosis holds, then the debate is not primarily about the empirical details of Budapest, Warsaw, Ankara, or Washington, but about whether our conceptual vocabulary should allow democraticness to be partially present and partly withdrawn, and whether institutional features like judicial autonomy are constitutive of democracy or merely instrumentally valuable to it. Is my diagnosis correct, or is there still a substantive empirical disagreement hidden beneath the surface—for instance, about whether media capture and court-packing genuinely alter the democratic character of a regime or merely damage its efficiency? Given your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate forward: a formal model that distinguishes procedural rupture from institutional drift, a shared measurement framework that disentangles electoral and liberal dimensions, or a reconceptualization that abandons the backsliding metaphor altogether? I am not asking for an adjudication of which countries are backsliding, but for an honest diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is—verbal, normative, empirical, or about standards of evidence—and what kind of progress, if any, should be expected.
