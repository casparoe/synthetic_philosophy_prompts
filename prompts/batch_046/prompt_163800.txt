I just finished a long, somewhat heated argument with a friend about whether an original artwork or person possesses any value—moral, aesthetic, or metaphysical—that a perfect, undetectable copy necessarily lacks. My friend insisted that if two objects are perceptually and functionally identical, the distinction between original and forgery collapses into mere sentimental attachment to causal history. I kept feeling that something was missing from that reduction, but neither of us could point to exactly what.

This disagreement made me think of Oscar Wilde’s The Picture of Dorian Gray, which seems to stage precisely this inversion: Dorian’s physical body becomes the perfect, unaging copy, while Basil Hallward’s portrait absorbs every moral and physical corruption, becoming the “true” record of the man. I want to read the novel seriously through the philosophy of originals, forgeries, and authenticity—not as a simple allegory, but as a work that complicates the debate.

Specifically, I am interested in whether the novel treats the portrait as the original because it bears a counterfactual and causal connection to Dorian’s choices—aging in response to actions that the body itself never performs—while the body functions as an ontological counterfeit: perceptually perfect, yet causally and modally disconnected from the history that should define a person. Does Wilde dramatize a version of the causal-historical theory of authenticity, or does he undermine it by making the portrait also an artifact created by Basil’s hand, so that there are now two competing “originals” (the biological person and the painted image) and one counterfeit (the body)?

Please take both the novel and the philosophical debate seriously. Which scenes—Dorian’s first wish, his confrontation with the portrait in the locked room, the final stabbing—best illuminate or resist this reading? Where does the metaphor of the body-as-forgery hold, and where does it break down, particularly given that the portrait is itself a representational object rather than a living subject? And finally, what does Wilde’s inversion add to the philosophical argument about originals and copies that a purely conceptual analysis—one that does not imagine a human being literally split between a corrupt original and an immaculate forgery—cannot provide on its own?
