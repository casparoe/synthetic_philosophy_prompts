As a retired experimental physicist who has recently turned to philosophy of science and economic thought, I find myself repeatedly returning to a conceptual knot in John Maynard Keynes's writings on probability and uncertainty that I have not been able to untie on my own. During my scientific career, I operated within a framework in which uncertainty was essentially a quantitative deficit—measurement error, sampling variance, or incomplete parameter estimation—all of which could be managed, at least in principle, by statistical methods and calibrated priors. Keynes, however, seems to advance two claims that sit uneasily together: first, that probability is an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion, not a measure of subjective belief; and second, that there exists a genuine form of uncertainty that is not numerically measurable at all, distinct from calculable risk. I am struggling to see how an objective logical relation can fail to yield a number, and whether Keynes's concept of the weight of an argument is meant to bridge that gap or to mark its unbridgeability.

What I would like is a careful conceptual explanation—intuitive in its reasoning but precise in its distinctions—of how Keynes's notion of weight operates within his logical theory of probability, and specifically how it relates to his claim that some propositions are subject to unmeasurable uncertainty. Does a low weight of argument mean that the logical probability relation itself is weak, or does it mean that the evidential basis is too impoverished to establish any such relation with confidence? Furthermore, from the standpoint of scientific practice, where we routinely assign probabilities to hypotheses despite severe underdetermination, is Keynes's refusal to quantify in cases of radical ignorance a principled boundary or an unnecessary restriction? I am particularly interested in whether his position offers a coherent intermediate ground between frequentist objectivism and subjective Bayesianism, or whether it ultimately collapses toward one pole when applied to inductive reasoning in the natural sciences. Please do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or historical chronology; I am seeking a direct mapping of the conceptual terrain.
