"I think they are mostly constructions, but not useless ones. The Renaissance involved a real revival of classical learning and art in Italy and then elsewhere, and the Scientific Revolution included actual new practices like controlled experiment and mathematical modeling of nature. However, calling them separate periods can mislead us into thinking people in 1400 and 1600 lived in completely different worlds, when in fact many medieval universities taught the same texts that Renaissance scholars praised. Also, the idea of a Middle Ages as a dark gap was invented by Renaissance humanists like Petrarch to make their own era look brighter by comparison, which shows the label carries bias. The Scientific Revolution is different from the other two, though, because it changed the rules of knowledge-production—introducing peer review and repeatable experiment—rather than just changing artistic style. Rule-changes are binary: you either have institutionalized verification or you don't, so this cluster is genuinely discontinuous. The strongest argument for treating all three as real is that specific discoveries—like Copernicus's model—are either correct or not, and grouping them together helps explain why they happened in the same centuries. If we reject the categories entirely, we lose the ability to ask why so many innovations appeared between 1550 and 1700 rather than scattered evenly across time."
