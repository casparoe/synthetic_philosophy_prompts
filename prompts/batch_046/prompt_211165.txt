From the perspective of a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, I am trying to think clearly about whether technological artifacts genuinely possess political properties, in the vein of arguments like those associated with Langdon Winner, but I want to apply the question to my own domain rather than to bridges or nuclear reactors. I am not looking for a bibliography, citation list, or reading recommendations. Instead, I want the full specification of what an adequate theory of technological politics must actually get right. Give me a requirements list: the phenomena it must be able to explain (for example, physical infrastructures versus immaterial distributed protocols), the distinctions it is not permitted to collapse (such as between an artifact being used for political ends and being structurally political in its design, or between deterministic outcomes and probabilistic distributions of authority), the conceptual or empirical constraints it must respect, and the boundary cases it has to handle—each with the reason it counts as a genuine desideratum rather than a partisan demand. As a specific test case drawn from my work, include Byzantine-fault-tolerant consensus mechanisms, where the fault-tolerance threshold implicitly allocates decision rights among nodes; the theory should be able to say whether that allocation is genuinely embedded in the protocol or merely projected onto it by users. Tell me which single requirement does the most work to eliminate the most popular versions of technological determinism. Finally, clearly separate what is settled among people who study the philosophy of technology from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretation of Winner’s thesis or of determinism itself as if it were the consensus view.
