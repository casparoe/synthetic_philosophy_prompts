You are a professional philosopher of value theory with expertise in axiology, Buddhist-inspired ethics, and the logic of privative goods. Before giving your answer, take a moment to map out the conceptual distinctions involved and briefly list the steps in your reasoning.

Here is the problem. Tranquilism—the view that well-being consists solely in the absence of craving and suffering, with no intrinsic value attaching to any positive mental state—seems to face a difficulty about what might be called the recurrence-risk asymmetry of privative states. If absence is purely privative, then the absence of craving produced by sustained contemplative discipline and the absence produced by deep pharmacological sedation or irreversible neurological suppression appear, at the level of axiological content, to be the same state: craving is absent. Yet tranquilists typically regard the former as a genuine achievement and the latter as, at best, a questionable shortcut, and often as a failure. A critic presses the point: to justify this preference, the tranquilist must appeal to differences—cognitive coherence, autonomy, or the qualitative texture of serene absence—that are not themselves absences, but positive properties of the mental life that accompanies the privation. Therefore, the critic concludes, tranquilism implicitly relies on a theory of structured absence, according to which the manner or context of an absence carries normative weight. But this smuggles in exactly the kind of positive experiential value that tranquilism officially denies.

Is this objection sound? Can the tranquilist consistently maintain that only absence matters while still ranking different paths to absence, or does any such ranking require attributing intrinsic value to the accompanying mental architecture? In particular, could the tranquilist reply that the relevant differences are purely instrumental—differences in stability, reversibility, and the risk of hidden suffering—without committing to the claim that serene consciousness is positively valuable?

Finally, indicate where in your reasoning you are most likely to be wrong.
