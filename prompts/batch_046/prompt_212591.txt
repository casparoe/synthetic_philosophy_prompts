I serve as the evaluation chair for a regional museum consortium that reviews proposals for reconstructive historical installations—immersive exhibits that fill archival gaps with reconstructed dialogue, soundscapes, embodied gestures, or inferred internal states, where the institution claims the result is grounded in the record rather than fictional invention. Proposals arrive monthly, must be evaluated in a single 45-minute session by a panel with historical and design expertise but no formal philosophy training, and I must sign off with approval, conditional approval, or rejection with written reasons; the stakes are public trust, potential harm from misrepresentation, and legal liability for misattributing fabricated detail to real persons. I need a reusable decision procedure—an ordered checklist with explicit decision nodes, clear stopping conditions for rejection, flags marking where mechanical judgment ends and values-based judgment must take over, and a small set of test questions the panel can apply without specialized philosophical vocabulary—that operationalizes the philosophical problem of verisimilitude: the tension between a representation being literally false in its particulars yet arguably closer to the truth than available alternatives, and the common error of mistaking perceptual plausibility for epistemic fidelity. To keep the distinction usable, the procedure should treat trace-verisimilitude (whether the reconstruction preserves an evidential link to actual artifacts, testimony, physical remains, or documented behavior) and structural-verisimilitude (whether it tracks the causal and phenomenal structure of the event, including emotional dynamics and environmental constraints, even when specific details are invented) as separate gates rather than points on a single scale, and it should specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each gate. Apply the procedure to three proposals: first, an audio reconstruction of a factory-floor conversation in 1923 based solely on analog recordings of similar machines and union minutes describing topics discussed, with no direct recording; second, a visual reconstruction of a prisoner’s facial expression during interrogation, derived from forensic-psychology studies on stress responses and a guard’s written description, intended for a trauma-history exhibit; third, a reconstructed internal monologue for a documented historical figure during a well-attested public speech, built from their private letters and the speech’s rhetorical structure. The second case should yield an answer—approval or conditional approval—that I will find ethically unwelcome, and the answer should pinpoint exactly where the procedure stops being mechanical and demands a values-based override that I, as signatory, must exercise. The procedure should also state explicitly which kinds of questions it cannot adjudicate—such as aesthetic merit, political messaging, or emotional impact on visitors—and should include a worked demonstration of each gate for at least one case. Finally, note what the procedure is unable to do: for instance, rank two reconstructions that both pass all gates, or resolve conflicts between trace and structural fidelity when both are partially present.
