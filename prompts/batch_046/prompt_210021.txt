I have been weighing a major career change and trying to decide how much self-reflection is rational before committing. In that context I encountered the decision-theoretic result usually associated with I.J. Good—that free information never lowers expected utility within a fixed problem—and a friend pushed it into what looks like a reductio. I cannot locate the slip.

(1) Within any fixed decision problem, acquiring costless information about exogenous states of the world before choosing cannot reduce expected utility.

(2) Now consider the meta-decision whether to investigate my own utility function—say, whether I genuinely value autonomy over security—before selecting a career path. The reflection costs nothing.

(3) By (1), I should always undertake such an investigation, since learning my true preferences cannot worsen my decision.

(4) But the investigation may revise my preferences: I might discover that security matters more, which changes how I evaluate the career options.

(5) Once my preferences have shifted, I face a structurally new decision problem. Applying (1) again, I should now investigate whether I genuinely value security over autonomy.

(6) This initiates an infinite regress: each resolution of preference uncertainty generates a revised preference profile that demands further free investigation.

(7) Therefore, if the result applies without restriction, rational agency requires completing an infinite sequence of self-inquiries before acting, which is impossible.

(8) Hence either the result is false, or rational agents can never reach a decision.

Every step feels defensible, yet the conclusion is absurd—people do decide, and the mathematics is sound. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Must we restrict the theorem to exogenous states, deny that preference discovery counts as information in the relevant sense, or accept that some self-knowledge is genuinely harmful?

3. Bottom line: your verdict on whether the regress is real or spurious, with the key reason in one or two sentences.
