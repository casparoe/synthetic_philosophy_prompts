Deliver the opening remarks for a two-day workshop titled "Certainties We Build On," attended by practicing structural engineers and philosophers of science in roughly equal numbers. Your remarks should introduce the pairing of these audiences by drawing on hinge epistemology — the view, developed in Wittgenstein's late writings on certainty, that certain propositions function not as pieces of knowledge subject to ordinary verification but as the fixed framework within which verification and practical reasoning occur. Make the idea do structural work: show what the engineers are entitled to treat as non-negotiable in daily practice (for instance, that measurement instruments do not systematically misrepresent load, or that material behavior is continuous across tested samples) and explain why philosophers should avoid framing those commitments either as concealed empirical hypotheses or as irrational dogmas. Confront the specific tension that arises when an engineer asks what happens if one of these hinges fails; answer in a way that honors the engineer's practical worry while maintaining that, within Wittgenstein's framework, the collapse of a hinge is not an internal empirical finding but a transition to a different system of judgment altogether. The audience is mixed and largely non-philosophical, so avoid unnecessary jargon, but do not weaken the conceptual architecture. Keep the remarks appropriate for about five minutes of spoken delivery. Do not include bibliographies, citations, or reading suggestions.
