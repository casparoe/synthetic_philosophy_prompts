As a PhD student in philosophy of history and science studies, I am designing a formal seminar instrument for graduate students on the problem of non-human causation in historical explanation. I need a branching decision tree—functioning as a “which position do you actually hold?” taxonomy—that sorts substantive views on whether pathogens, climate systems, and volcanic events can operate as “historical actors” without collapsing into environmental determinism. Rather than asking respondents to identify named theories, the tree should navigate through concrete diagnostic commitments and cases. The first node should ask whether causal efficacy requires intentional agency, using as its illustrative contrast the 1815 Tambora eruption’s impact on European grain harvests versus the strategic deployment of a contemporaneous human tariff policy. The second node should ask whether attributing large-scale mortality patterns to the plague bacillus during 1348–1353 necessarily renders redundant explanations centered on Venetian maritime trade networks—thereby testing explanatory redundancy across causal scales. The third node should ask whether ecological causation necessarily carries teleological direction, or whether it can be mechanistically decoupled from moral and political responsibility. Each branch must be labeled by the claim that splits it, and every node must be phrased as a substantive question answerable without prior knowledge of philosophical labels.

The tree must locate the following leaves in the logical space of answers: (A) hard environmental determinism; (B) methodological environmentalism that grants substantial causal weight to non-human forces but denies them agency; (C) distributed-agency attribution that treats microbes, climate patterns, and volcanoes as nodes in a multi-scalar causal network without strategic intention; (D) anti-determinist humanism that restricts historical explanation to intentional human action; and (E) a compatibilist position that accepts non-human systemic causation at multiple scales while reserving moral accountability exclusively for human institutions. Map these onto the combinations of answers permitted by the nodes; identify any unoccupied branch—particularly one that accepts strategic microbial agency while denying ecological causation altogether—and provide a brief diagnosis of whether the vacancy stems from logical incoherence, theoretical unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Explicitly flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, especially between (B) and (C) when a case involves overlapping causal scales such as pathogen transmission and economic infrastructure. The instrument should be self-contained, avoid all requests for bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and rely only on general knowledge of these historical episodes and the conceptual distinctions between causation, agency, and determinism.
