Is there a name for this pattern I keep seeing in talk about lost causes? Like, take the way some people discuss Masada or the Confederate Lost Cause — they don't defend the actual political goal anymore, they defend the decision to keep fighting when it was obviuosly useless. And they say that decision proves the cause had real moral weight, because nobody dies for nothing. But then if you point out that people also die for stupid or evil causes, they retreat and say well that's different, the defenders were noble *because* they fought for something worth dying for. So the nobility is used to justify celebrating the futility, and the futility is used to prove the nobility — its like a closed loop. I noticed something similar with Dunkirk, where celebrating the evacuation as a victory in defeat gets used to argue Britain was somehow morally superior for having lost so well. Not quite sunk-cost fallacy, not quite martyrdom mythology, maybe closer to something like retroactive valorization? Anyway, is there an established term in historiography or rhetoric for this exact circular move, where defeat's hopelessness gets converted into proof of the cause's worth, and that worth is then used to excuse the defeat? Or should I just call it something like a futilty-nobility loop and define it with clear boundary cases so it doesn't collapse into plain circular reasoning?
