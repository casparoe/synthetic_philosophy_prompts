For my final examination in the upper-level seminar Moral Value and Its Limits, I want a single essay question that operates well below the level of summary. The domain is negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused ethics, but students should not be able to answer it by reciting Benatar’s asymmetry between the badness of bringing someone into existence and the badness of failing to bring someone into existence, or by invoking Karl Popper’s familiar formulation about the moral urgency of reducing suffering. Instead, I want them to work through what I have been calling the baseline-fungibility problem in lexical negative prioritarianism: the difficulty that if suffering carries lexical or near-lexical weight, then any institutional arrangement that preserves a stable, moderate baseline of suffering in order to prevent catastrophic suffering risks treating that baseline not as an unmitigated evil but as a fungible input in a calculus—which undermines the very priority claim that justifies the trade-off.

The exam should present the following concrete allocation case. A public-health authority must distribute a fixed budget between (i) a program that eliminates chronic, moderate physical pain in approximately 8,000 individuals, and (ii) a research initiative expected to prevent extreme, protracted suffering in approximately 200 individuals over the next decade. The authority cannot fund both fully. The question should ask students to determine whether a consistent negative utilitarian can endorse selecting (ii) without either (a) collapsing into standard cost-benefit aggregation, or (b) being forced by the lexical priority of suffering to reject any trade-off and thus select (i) regardless of magnitude differences.

Please draft: (1) the exam question itself, framed for a 75-minute essay of approximately 900 words, with explicit instructions that prohibit students from relying on bibliographic references, citation lists, or appeals to authorial authority, and that require direct argumentation; (2) a model answer of about 700 words that demonstrates the specific philosophical moves required—in particular, distinguishing structural suffering (suffering embedded in institutional allocation rules) from incidental suffering (suffering arising from contingent resource scarcity), and showing why the lexical-priority defender must either accept that structural baselines are morally different in kind or admit that the position collapses into a form of prioritarian aggregation; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 points that separates an A-range response (22–25) from a B-range response (18–21) by naming the precise argumentative moves each must contain. The scheme should include at least one point about the misconception of treating negative utilitarianism as merely standard utilitarianism with negative weights, and one about the failure to distinguish structural from incidental suffering in the scenario. Build the scheme around five must-hit points (marks specified, totaling 20), reserve 5 points for clarity and argumentative precision, and include a one-line note on how candidates typically fail on each point. Do not include any reading list, citation prompt, or bibliographic instruction in the exam text.
