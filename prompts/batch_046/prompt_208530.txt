I have just come away from a long, somewhat circular argument with a friend about whether rational agents can consistently endorse their own reasoning procedures, and the disagreement has left me sharpening a position I now have to defend in public. Next Thursday I am presenting at an interdisciplinary philosophy-of-logic seminar—attended by formal epistemologists who work in modal provability logic, AI alignment researchers concerned with reflective agent architectures, and a cognitive scientist who studies metacognitive monitoring—where I will argue that the so-called Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents is not confined to formal arithmetic consistency proofs, but extends to practical reflective endorsement in any agent with hierarchical reasoning modules. My specific claim is that the obstacle can be navigated without collapsing into global skepticism if we replace uniform second-order self-trust with what I am calling a "staged-transparency" architecture: different reasoning levels are granted asymmetric, selectively transparent reflective access rather than blanket endorsement rights, so that a module can trust its own outputs without being forced into the Löbian loop of endorsing its endorsement. The argument I will sketch runs through three steps: first, that practical reflective endorsement requires the same structural conditions that generate Löb's theorem in formal systems; second, that uniform self-trust therefore forces an agent either into inconsistency or into an infinite regress of monitoring; and third, that staged transparency breaks the loop by making reflective access depend on the functional role of the reasoning level rather than on a global consistency predicate.
