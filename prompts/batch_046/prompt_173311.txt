I'm writing a paper for my philosophy of physics seminar on how multiverse hypotheses in string theory can be evaluated when direct empirical access to other vacuum states is impossible. I want to compare two approaches to this problem. The first is the framework of non-empirical theory confirmation developed in recent philosophy of physics: it treats mathematical consistency, explanatory uniqueness, and meta-inductive success across related domains as partial epistemic substitutes for direct observation, particularly in contexts like quantum gravity where conventional testing is unavailable. The second is a modified "selective landscape" view I have been developing, in which the space of physically realized vacua is restricted not by empirical measurement but by an internal stability criterion—specifically, that only metastable de Sitter configurations with a decay rate below a cosmologically defined threshold count as epistemically accessible worlds. In this second view, the multiverse does not confirm the underlying theory through sheer abundance of possibilities, but through the structural constraint that our local vacuum sits near a critical boundary within that restricted set.

Compare these two approaches with respect to how they handle the "predictive deficit" objection—that the string landscape appears to make no distinctive predictions about local physics. In particular, does the selective landscape view genuinely improve on the non-empirical criteria of the first framework, or does it merely relocate arbitrariness from the choice of vacuum to the choice of stability threshold? Furthermore, can either framework accommodate the possibility that future cosmological data might falsify the multiverse idea itself, rather than merely selecting among its members? Please ground your comparison in the conceptual structure of each position rather than in historical surveys of the debate.
