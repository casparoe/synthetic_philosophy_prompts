Several research universities have adopted 'epistemic hygiene' protocols that sharply distinguish adopting a scientific hypothesis on evidential grounds from adopting it because of funding incentives, career pressure, or disciplinary loyalty. A researcher who holds a belief because the data support it commits no fault, even should the belief turn out false; yet one who holds it because it secures a grant faces disciplinary sanction, even if the belief is correct. Notably, the same protocols permit pragmatic reasons—career advancement, institutional loyalty, collaborative utility—to justify the adoption of research programmes, methods, or professional commitments, but never to justify the belief itself. What philosophical view of the wrong kind of reasons does this institutional distinction presuppose? Must it assume that belief is governed exclusively by evidential norms, or is it compatible with accounts that allow pragmatic encroachment? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the protocol strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and indicate how the rules would have to change if we substituted a unified account on which all mental attitudes are answerable to both truth-related and practical considerations.
