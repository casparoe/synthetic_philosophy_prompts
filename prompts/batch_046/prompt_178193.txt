My philosophy-of-economics advisor has ruled on revealed-preference cases as follows. First, a consumer picks an option only because it is the pre-selected default; once informed, she immediately switches—my advisor says the initial choice reveals no welfare. Second, a worker consistently declines promotions that raise income but reduce autonomy, across many years and different employers—my advisor treats this as a genuine welfare-revealing preference. Third, an agent installs a binding commitment device to block alcohol purchases and later praises the block; my advisor holds that the unblocked choices were error-ridden and welfare-irrelevant, while the committed choice reveals true preference. Fourth, a hidden subsidy shifts a buyer's selection without her awareness; upon discovery she reverts—my advisor rules that covert manipulation voids the signal.

Next case: a patient undergoes an elective, irreversible neurocognitive adjustment that reliably makes her prefer solitude and minimal stimulation. Afterward, with full information and no wish to reverse the change (reversal is impossible), she consistently chooses solitary activities. Yet she describes her current preferences as not genuinely her own—she values them instrumentally for health, identifies with her pre-treatment preference for social engagement, and reports absence of regret only because undoing the change is impossible. Predict my advisor's verdict: does her present revealed preference for solitude count as welfare-revealing? State the implicit principle—reflective endorsement, counterfactual stability, or error-corrected consistency—that best explains her earlier rulings, and identify the single further case that would separate that principle from its nearest rival.
