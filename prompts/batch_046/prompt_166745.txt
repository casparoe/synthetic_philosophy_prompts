I'm preparing a ninety-minute workshop for graduate students in urban planning and environmental design—empirically trained, with no background in analytic philosophy—who are skeptical of "abstract ethics" but deeply invested in long-term infrastructure choices. I want them to understand the structure of the nonidentity problem, but I do not want to rely on the familiar textbook examples that every syllabus repeats. Instead, the session should be built around one carrying case: a 1974 municipal decision to zone a riverfront district for heavy industry rather than public green space, a choice that, over fifty years, shifts housing costs, school districts, and commute patterns so thoroughly that the specific people living there in 2050 would not have existed had the park been built. The students need to see why this creates a distinct philosophical puzzle about obligation and harm, rather than a merely sociological observation about urban change.

Plan the explanatory arc, not the explanation itself. First, specify the dependency order: what conceptual element must be secured before the students can recognize the zoning choice as ethically puzzling rather than just consequentially complicated, and why grasping the counterfactual fragility of personal identity must precede any discussion of future-oriented duty. Second, define exactly what the riverfront example must include—how many generations, which causal mechanisms—to make the identity-dependency visible, and what it must deliberately exclude—such as climate framing or explicit reproductive choice—so the mechanism is not obscured. Third, map the misconceptions these students will predictably form: I anticipate they will conflate "no particular person is made worse off" with "there is no moral problem at all," they will slide toward aggregate welfare reasoning, and they may implicitly treat identity as a continuous substance rather than a relational outcome. For each misconception, identify the precise point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and the exact contrast or question you would use to block it. Fourth, describe the check for understanding at the end: a brief framing exercise or question that reveals whether they have grasped that the moral difficulty survives even when every actual 2050 resident is flourishing. Fifth, state clearly what should be left out at this level—no modal realism, no theories of personal identity, no assigned readings or citations—and say so explicitly.
