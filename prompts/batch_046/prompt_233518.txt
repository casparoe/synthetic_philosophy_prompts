I am constructing a scene for my novel in which Dr. Elara Voss, a retired philosopher of language, serves on her university's speech-ethics appeals board, and I need her reasoning to be philosophically consistent across rulings. Over four prior appeals she has rendered the following verdicts on whether an assertion violated the board's standard of warranted assertion.

In the first case, a botanist asserted that a particular alpine flower blooms exclusively in volcanic soil, basing this solely on a childhood memory that happened to be correct; she sincerely believed the claim but possessed no scientific documentation. Voss ruled that no violation occurred, observing that the belief was both true and genuinely held, despite its slender evidential basis.

In the second, a translator asserted that an ancient text dated from the fifth century, knowing full well that he had relied on a corrupted manuscript but believing—through a separate, unsupported intuition—that the date was correct regardless. Voss ruled that a violation had occurred, noting that the speaker knowingly rested his claim on a foundation he recognized as unreliable.

In the third, a doctoral student asserted an interpretive claim about a modernist poem she had not finished reading, accepting her advisor's judgment on authority; the interpretation turned out to be correct. Voss ruled no violation, holding that warranted deference can support assertion even in the absence of first-hand knowledge.

In the fourth, a mathematician recited the conclusion of a complex proof he had memorized without comprehension, asserting it in a colloquium; the conclusion was true. Voss ruled a violation, stating that assertion requires genuine belief rather than mere vocal repetition of a correct formula.

The pending case involves a climate researcher who asserts that a specific valley glacier will retreat by twelve kilometers before 2030. She derived this prediction from a widely validated climate model, sincerely believes it, and the claim is in fact true. Unbeknownst to her, however, her belief depends essentially on an intermediate assumption—that a particular satellite calibration coefficient lies within a specific range—which is false, though the model's output is robust to the coefficient's actual value and she has no grounds whatsoever for doubt.

Please predict Voss's verdict on this new case. Identify the competing norms of assertion—whether knowledge, justified true belief, mere sincere belief coupled with truth, or a reliability condition that permits hidden false intermediates—that her prior four rulings jointly accommodate. Explain which norm best unifies her track record, state your best guess for her ruling with the reasoning behind it, and describe the precise further case that would distinguish the two leading interpretations.
