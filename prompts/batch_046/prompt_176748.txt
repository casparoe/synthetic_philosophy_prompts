Design a structured 75-minute classroom activity for a graduate seminar of about 12 students in political philosophy, centered on the Hart–Devlin debate on legal moralism but staged without asking students to reenact the classic British committee report. Instead, create a simulated legislative committee for a fictional pluralistic city-state, Marisburg, tasked with drafting a “Social Cohesion and Private Conduct Ordinance.” Assign roles: three “Hartians” who may prohibit only conduct causing direct, demonstrable harm to non-consenting parties using a harm-test worksheet you must design; three “Devlinians” who may prohibit consensual private conduct they predict will erode the shared moral intuitions necessary for institutional trust, even absent direct harm; three skeptical legislators with veto power who must be persuaded by either camp; and three citizen-advocates who present testimony about specific consensual practices—unconventional domestic arrangements, private substance rituals, and non-standard religious observances—that are widely disliked but cause no obvious injury.

The rules should require concrete votes on at least two ordinances, with payoffs in “institutional stability points” and “liberty points” that accumulate differently per role. Crucially, inject a hidden-information mechanism: after an initial vote, reveal that the Devlinians’ erosion predictions rested on flawed correlation data, and that the Hartians’ harm assessments missed indirect network effects visible only in longitudinal data. Force a revised second vote so that participants discover through their own changed calculations that both frameworks face an unresolved epistemic problem—predicting long-term social causation—that neither theory resolves on its own.

Include: (1) the role cards with precise constraints and payoff matrices; (2) a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes; (3) a debrief that maps the first and second votes onto Hart’s distinction between critical and positive morality and Devlin’s claim that legal authority depends on shared moral bonds, showing why legal moralism is not mere conservatism but a thesis about causal prerequisites of law; (4) an honest account of which feature of Devlin’s argument—his appeal to natural limits of toleration or his analogy between private immorality and treason—the simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply; and (5) a prediction of what will predictably fail in the first run (likely confusion between skeptical legislators and advocates about veto scope) and how to correct it. The activity should be runnable without requiring students to have read the original texts in depth, but they should leave seeing why the debate is about the empirical and conceptual limits of legislating private morality, not just about tolerance.
