I have noticed a recurring argumentative pattern in contemporary financial ethics that seems to echo Aristotle's distinction between acquiring goods to satisfy household needs and pursuing monetary gain as an end in itself, yet in a form that departs sharply from his original framework. Critics of particular modern practices—such as high-frequency algorithmic trading or cryptocurrency lending—condemn them as unnatural on the grounds that they generate value without corresponding productive labour or tangible goods, invoking the old image of money generating further money. However, these same critics frequently accept other mechanisms of capital accumulation—equity dividends, rental income from property, or returns on government bonds—without levelling the same charge, despite the fact that these too separate monetary return from direct productive effort and from the direct satisfaction of needs. The boundary they defend appears to track social respectability or institutional familiarity rather than any consistent principle about the relationship between exchange and use. This produces a selective moralising in which a concept originally meant to distinguish two fundamentally different orientations towards wealth is instead used to police the boundary between reputable and disreputable finance. Is there an established philosophical term or named fallacy for this asymmetrical deployment—where an Aristotelian distinction is retained as rhetorical ornament but emptied of its original structural logic? If no precise label exists, please define the phenomenon precisely, distinguish it both from ordinary hypocrisy and from Aristotle's own account of exchange and acquisition, and indicate where its conceptual edges lie.
