In my phil of science reading group we've been stuck on the dogmatism paradox for like three months and its definatly getting annoying. We all agree on the basic setup: you hold P with high confidence, then you learn an epistemic peer holds not-P with equal confidence, and conciliationism says split the difference. But then applying that rule recursively creates this weird loop that occured — you either stay dogmatic about your first-order belief or you wash out to zero confidence, which seems irrational either way. The split in the group is that some people think this just kills conciliationism and proves rational agents must be dogmatic at the base level. Others think its not a paradox at all, just a misapplication of the reflection principle where we confuse object-level beliefs with meta-level judgments about our own reliability. There's also one person who insists the whole stall is hiding an empirical claim about how actual human credences update in social settings, but nobody wants to run experiments.

What I want is a diagnosis, not another survey. Is this disagreement substantive — about what rationality requires — or is it verbal, like we're using 'peer' or 'revision' in different ways? Or are we answering different questions, one about normative ideal agents and one about descriptive psychology, under the same heading? And here's a twist: what if the peer isn't human but a synthetic agent — say, a differently-trained model that processes the same dataset and lands on not-P? Does that change whether conciliation applies, or does it just show the disagreement is about training distributions rather than truth? Given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually move us — a formal result about belief dynamics, a conceptual distinction, or should we just accept that this is bedrock intuition and stop arguing?
