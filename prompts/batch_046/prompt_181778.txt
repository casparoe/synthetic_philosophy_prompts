I have served for the past three years on the stewardship committee of a neighborhood agricultural cooperative, where members maintain shared plots according to collectively agreed labor and environmental standards. This season our harvest has fallen well below expectations, and during our monthly review meeting, a fellow member named Alex directed sustained criticism at me for failing to weed my assigned rows, arguing that my negligence allowed invasive species to spread into adjacent plots and damage the collective yield. I do not deny that my weeding has been seriously deficient—personal circumstances made it difficult to maintain my usual schedule—so his factual claim about my failure carries genuine weight. At the same time, Alex was revealed several weeks ago to have been applying synthetic fertilizers to his private section in direct violation of a ban that he himself championed and enforced within the cooperative; he has since refused to apologize or acknowledge any wrongdoing.

A number of other members now insist that because Alex secretly violated the very standards he enforces, he has forfeited any right to criticize others for different failures, and they have urged me to dismiss his complaint entirely rather than engaging with its substance. I find myself caught between recognizing that I genuinely failed in my duties and sensing that there may be something disqualifying about receiving blame from someone who has committed a comparable breach of cooperative norms—though I am unsure whether the two failures are genuinely comparable or whether his hidden violation cancels his authority to point out my visible one. How should I evaluate whether Alex can legitimately hold me accountable for my weeding negligence despite having broken the fertilizer rules himself, and does his particular wrongdoing invalidate the content of his criticism, or are these dimensions properly evaluated separately?
