I'm a novelist who has spent the last decade writing fiction about characters whose sensory worlds are altered—phantom limb experiences, synesthetic mappings, the kind of private phenomenology that scientists study but never truly inhabit. Next month I have been asked to deliver the opening remarks for a workshop called 'The Text of Experience: Heterophenomenology and Its Neighbors,' hosted jointly by a cognitive science department and a writers' collective. The room will be split: half are researchers who take the heterophenomenological method seriously—treating first-person reports not as direct windows onto private qualia but as texts expressing a subject's beliefs about their own experience, to be interpreted with the same hermeneutic care one brings to a novel or a dream report. The other half are fellow writers, editors, and readers who believe, or at least hope, that fiction can genuinely transmit what it is like to be another person.

Please write the remarks—about six minutes spoken—so that they do real structural work rather than merely decorate a welcome. The speech should open by acknowledging that I have no scientific authority, then use the heterophenomenological frame to say exactly what I, as a novelist, am and am not entitled to claim about consciousness. Specifically, I want the address to hinge on a tension I have been thinking about: in fiction we construct interior monologues that feel authoritative to the reader, yet if we take heterophenomenology seriously, every such monologue is not a transcript of inner speech but an interpreted narrative artifact—something closer to a carefully edited witness statement than to a raw datum. The speech must navigate between the scientists (who should hear that literary interiority is not competing with neuroscience but is itself a form of interpreted report) and the writers (who should hear that this does not diminish fiction but clarifies its epistemic humility). Crucially, the speaker must never claim direct access to anyone's experience—not even her own characters', not even her own—because that claim is precisely what the method forbids. The remarks should therefore avoid phrases like 'we all know what it feels like' or 'inside her head,' and instead build a vocabulary of interpretation, belief, and textual consistency.

Finally, I ask that the response include a brief, explicit note on where its reasoning is most vulnerable—what argument or counterexample would cause it to abandon the central analogy between novelistic interior monologue and heterophenomenological report, or to concede that fiction actually does possess a kind of phenomenal authority that the method cannot accommodate. I want to know what would change its mind, not out of modesty, but because the workshop itself is about testing the limits of first-person description.
