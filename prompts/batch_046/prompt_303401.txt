I'm trying to sort out when I think a legend has actually turned into history, going by gut feeling on specific cases. The Trojan War feels like it has a real bronze-age conflict behind it - Schliemann's site and all - but the wooden horse is pure myth. King Arthur: maybe there's a 5th or 6th century warlord in there, but Camelot, Merlin, and the round table push it back toward legend for me, especially with no near-contempory mention. The Exodus I treat mostly as founding myth, even though there's that debate about a small group leaving Egypt or the Hyksos connection; somehow that archaeological ambiguity doesn't pull it into history the way Troy does. The Green Children of Woolpit are in medieval chronicles, but I still say pure legend, zero historical kernel. Prester John is weird - he started as distorted reports about real Asian rulers then ballooned into fantasy, so it feels like history got swallowed by legend rather than the other way around. I'm going back and forth on whether correcting one detail in a legendary story - like finding out one of the 47 ronin was actually an informant and wasn't executed - turns the whole thing into history, or if that's just trivia inside a myth. Is there a principle behind all this, maybe something like "material archaeological anchor plus no supernatural escallation in the earliest layer"? But then why do I treat Arthur differently from Prester John? And what should the principle say about cases with just one chronicle entry and no material trace? I want the principle, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, plus its verdict on the Woolpit-style single-source case and on that ronin-correction scenario, and an honest call on which of my gut feelings I just have to drop to keep it clean.
