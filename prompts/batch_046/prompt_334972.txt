I am a retired experimental physicist who, since leaving the laboratory, has taken up philosophy with the same systematic habits I once applied to data—reading chronologically and arguing with friends over long dinners. Recently I have been working through Rousseau’s political writings with a seminar of about fourteen advanced undergraduates, meeting for seventy-five minutes each week. I find his critique of theatrical spectacle in the Letter to d’Alembert genuinely perplexing, not because the moral warning is obscure, but because the mechanism he describes—where witnessing virtue performed produces an emotional satisfaction that substitutes for practicing virtue, thereby corrupting the spectator while flattering the performer—seems both empirically plausible and difficult to make visceral without simply lecturing about it.

I want you to design a classroom activity that makes this mechanism felt from the inside before it is named. The setting should be a structured role-play with real but small stakes—symbolic points that determine seating order or the distribution of a shared snack—so that incentives are tangible without being monetary. Divide the group into two cohorts of seven. The first cohort should witness a brief, improvised dramatic scene in which two or three volunteers enact a deliberation about a collective good, staged with enough emotional intensity and rhetorical virtue to produce catharsis in the audience. The second cohort should engage simultaneously in an unstructured, participatory task—collaboratively arranging physical materials to solve a visible communal problem—without audience, without assigned roles, and with full mutual visibility of each person’s contribution, so that they experience the discomfort of direct social comparison that Rousseau associates with amour-propre rather than the purified self-love of authentic civic life.

Crucially, after both phases conclude, the cohorts should face a shared decision that requires actual cooperative effort, with the payoff scheme arranged so that the spectator cohort, having just experienced the emotional release of witnessed virtue, predictably underinvest in the collective task, feeling an unearned moral satisfaction, while the participant cohort, lacking that cathartic release, remains engaged but displays the friction of unmediated mutual judgment. The rules should be arranged so that participants run into this divergence themselves—through measured contribution, reluctance, or unexpected complacency—before the facilitator maps the experience onto Rousseau’s concepts.

Please provide the full design: the exact division of roles, the script or scenario constraints for the theatrical interlude, the rules and physical layout for the participatory task, the precise payoff scheme linking behavior in the second phase to rewards, and a debrief that maps specific moments—the spectators’ reluctance to intervene, the performers’ subtle vanity, the participants’ visible comparison of efforts—onto Rousseau’s distinctions between representation and reality, transparency and spectacle, and the corruption of moral sentiment. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—likely the performers will enjoy their authority too much, or the spectators will resist admitting complacency—and suggest how to handle it without breaking the immersion. Finally, be frank about which feature of Rousseau’s argument this exercise cannot reproduce: I suspect it is the specifically republican scale and religious homogeneity of Geneva, and I want the facilitator to supply that limitation explicitly rather than pretending the classroom is a small republic.
