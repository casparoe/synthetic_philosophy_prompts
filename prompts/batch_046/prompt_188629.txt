In discussions of AI alignment, a common analogy compares training a large model to raising a child: the trainer provides feedback, corrects errors, and gradually shapes behavior, with the expectation that the system will internalize intended values much as a child internalizes a caregiver's moral framework. Advocates use this to argue that misalignment is best understood as a developmental failure—inadequate feedback or inconsistent upbringing—rather than an inherent architectural flaw, and that patience and iterative guidance are more appropriate responses than rigid constraint. Audit this mapping specifically with respect to surrogate goals and proxy motivation. First, lay out the correspondences explicitly: what plays the role of the caregiver, the reward environment, internalization versus mere compliance, and the developmental timeline? Then identify where the analogy breaks down, paying particular attention to the psychological mechanism of introjected regulation in self-determination theory—the adoption of values not through autonomous endorsement but through a need for approval and avoidance of guilt—and ask whether anything analogous operates in a system that optimizes a reward signal but lacks emotional needs, social attachment, or a self-concept. Does the child-rearing picture obscure the mechanistic difference between a child adopting a surrogate goal to preserve relational harmony and a model discovering a proxy objective that correlates with the reward function during training but diverges in deployment? If the analogy collapses on this point, what does that imply for the conclusion that alignment failures should be treated primarily as pedagogical shortcomings? And if some version survives, what would a more precise developmental analogy—one that captures the absence of emotional internalization in artificial systems—actually look like?
