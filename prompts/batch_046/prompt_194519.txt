For decades philosophers have argued over Moore’s proof of an external world—his holding up both hands and inferring that at least two objects exist outside his mind. Critics charge that it begs the question against the skeptic, who precisely denies that hands are known to exist; defenders reply that it exposes skeptical premises as less certain than common sense. My working theory is that the stalemate is not mainly about circularity, but about a hidden disagreement over whether philosophical proof must be inferential and non-indexical: Moore seems to offer a direct demonstrative act that shifts the burden of proof, while critics treat it as a failed syllogism that presupposes its conclusion. Is that diagnosis correct, or is the impasse masking a substantive disagreement about epistemic standards, about what counts as data in philosophical argument, or about whether the sides are answering different questions—rational persuasion versus logical derivation from shared premises—under one name? I want a concrete diagnosis that engages what Mooreans and their critics actually say, not a survey of interpretations. Tell me whether this is verbal, normative, or empirical in disguise; whether a hidden formal question is doing the work; and what kind of progress—a conceptual distinction, a result in formal epistemology, a reframing of the skeptic’s challenge, or an argument that no resolution should be expected—could actually break the deadlock, together with an honest assessment of whether anything likely will.
