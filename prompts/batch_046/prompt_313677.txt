I am trying to determine whether biography can serve as a distinct mode of moral learning, and I want to test whether my judgments about particular cases converge on a single principle. Here are my verdicts.

First, when Plutarch pairs lives—say, Alexander with Caesar—to show how shared ambition produces both grandeur and ruin, I take the reader to be learning the moral shape of a virtue pushed to excess; this feels like genuine instruction. Second, when a modern psychobiography explains a subject's apparent altruism entirely through unconscious guilt or social conditioning, I feel that whatever moral content was present has been dissolved into causal explanation, leaving nothing to emulate or avoid. Third, medieval hagiography that suppresses a saint's flaws presents an ideal that does teach, but I judge the lesson brittle: it collapses once the reader learns what was omitted. Fourth, a "warts and all" biography that integrates serious moral failures into a narrative of growth or accountability teaches more robustly than either pure hagiography or pure debunking. Fifth, a biography of an entirely ordinary figure, lacking exceptional virtue or vice, offers no moral learning—only historical or sociological information. Sixth, I think a deliberately counterfactual biography—an imagined life in which a real historical figure makes one decisive moral choice differently, rendered with full narrative realism—can cultivate moral imagination, yet I hesitate to classify it as biography proper, and I feel its instructional value is different in kind from that of factual lives. Seventh, a biography narrated from within a subject's self-deceived moral framework—where a tyrant's cruelty is presented through his own justifying lens without external evaluation—strikes me as capable of teaching by negative example, but only if the reader already commands the normative framework the subject lacks.

Is there one account—perhaps involving what we might call "mimetic calibration," in which moral learning requires the reader to adjust emotional identification against an independent normative baseline—that accommodates all of these judgments? Or does coherence require me to abandon one? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which verdict it forces me to drop if any, and apply it to a case I have not mentioned: an AI-synthesized biography that merges traits and episodes from several real lives but attributes them to a single, non-existent individual presented as historical.
