I'm a phd student in philosophy of science and I was talking with some friends about Darwin's weird inversion -- selection building eyes and brains without knowing what they're for -- and Turing's parallel move where computation produces language-like behavior without any grasp of meaning. We're running a small grad workshop next week (about 9 people, 90 minutes) on competence without comprehension, and I want an activity that makes them feel both inversions rather than just mapping them onto slides.

I have a rough two-stage idea but need you to build it out. Stage one: split them into three groups of three, each group recieve a deck of cards with pseudo-phonetic symbols arranged into ambiguous strings (they look grammatical but aren't any real language). There's a hidden 'fitness' score based on some statistical regularity they don't know. They can only propose mutations -- swap one symbol, merge two cards -- not design rules from scratch. After 15 minutes they have a classifier they can use but can't justify. Stage two: I present them with a black-box system (just an envelope of rules I'll reveal later) that sorts the same cards with 100% accuracy using purely syntactic patterns. They have to vote, with small stakes (maybe they bet points on whether it 'understands' the language), before they open the envelope. After voting, I show them the rules are just symbol-matching, zero semantics. Can you turn this into a full activity? Give me exact rules for mutations and betting, materials list (just cards and envelopes?), timeline with breaks, and roles if any. The debrief needs to map stage one onto evolutionary competence-without-comprehension and stage two onto Turing-style mechanical performance, and please be honest about which feature this can't reproduce -- probably the actual absence of consciousness in machines, or the deep evolutionary timescale -- so I know what I have to supply verbally. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong: I imagine people will peek at the envelope or conflate accuracy with understanding too fast.

Write this casually -- I'm not asking for a peer-reviewed syllabus. No bibliographies or citations needed; just design the thing. At the very end, put the activity's core philosophical target inside \boxed{...}.
