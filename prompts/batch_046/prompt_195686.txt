I hold the view that generational cohort designations—'Baby Boomers', 'Millennials', 'Generation Z', and analogous labels—are not genuine ontological kinds, natural or social. Rather, they are reified demographic intervals: arbitrary chronological clusters (for example, the conventional 1981–1996 boundary for Millennials) that lack both the causal-explanatory unity characteristic of natural kinds and the normative-institutional structure that renders entities like currencies or citizenship statuses robustly real. Their apparent explanatory power—shared political dispositions, consumption patterns, or technological literacy—derives chiefly from retrospective narrative smoothing and from period effects misattributed to cohort membership. I am particularly struck by the parallel with what we might call 'indexical sorts': categories whose membership conditions depend on the observer's present rather than on intrinsic properties of the members, which suggests they function more as marketing-cohort artefacts than as stable kind-terms.
