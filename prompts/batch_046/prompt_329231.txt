I've been arguing with a friend in our phil of sci seminar about whether Darwin's and Turing's "strange inversions" are actually the same kind of move. You know — Darwin showing that design-level competence in organisms comes from selection with no designer comprehension, and Turing showing that computation-level competence can come from mechanistic symbol-shuffling with no mental comprehension. My friend says it's literally the same inversion applied to different substrates, and that anyone who denies the parallel is just being territorial about biology. I think they're different in a way that matters: evolutionary competence is distributed, historical, and doesn't need representations, while Turing-competence is syntactic and needs a formal medium. But we've been going in cricles for weeks — we both agree on the history, we both cite Dennet's framing about generate-and-test versus the universal machine, and neither of us budges an inch.

So I'm trying to figure out what kind of stall this is. Are we using "competence" differently — like, she's tracking causal mechanism and I'm tracking functional achievement — which would make it partly verbal? Or is there a hidden empirical disagreement about whether natural selection involves something like representation that we're smuggling in without noticing? Or maybe it's bedrock intuition about whether biology is "special" that neither side can argue for, plus an ill-posed question about whether two domains have to share one logical structure? I want a diagnosis that actually engages what each side says, not just a survey of positions. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction in philosophy of representation, a result in evolutionary biology, or just admitting the comparison is malformed?
