I just concluded a long and somewhat heated argument with a friend who serves with me on our hospital's Clinical Ethics Advisory Panel, and I need to circulate a formal memo before Thursday's session. We are reviewing the case of a 78-year-old patient, Ms. R., who executed a psychiatric advance directive four years ago stating that if she developed advanced dementia and lost sustained narrative memory, she should not receive artificial nutrition, hydration, or antibiotics for intercurrent infections. She now meets those clinical criteria; she is calm, responsive to sensory comfort, and shows no evident distress. Her two adult children—who practice Theravada Buddhism—have petitioned the panel to disregard the directive. Their claim is not a request for religious accommodation but a metaphysical objection drawn from anatta: they argue that the directive was authored by a prior psycho-physical aggregate that bears no substantial identity to the present patient, so enforcing it does not honor autonomy but imposes the preference of a now-nonexistent self upon a living being whose experience consists only of present-moment aggregates. The attending physician responds that anatta actually dissolves the notion of a "future self" being harmed by treatment, and that the directive should be read as protecting the continuity of experiential welfare rather than a metaphysical self. The panel must issue a concrete recommendation—honor the directive and initiate withdrawal within a defined timeframe, honor it only if observable suffering emerges, or suspend it in favor of a best-interest evaluation—together with reasoning that takes the children's anatta argument seriously rather than reducing it to a category error about medical law, and that addresses the physician's counter-argument without collapsing the issue into standard capacity analysis. Please draft the memo I will circulate.
