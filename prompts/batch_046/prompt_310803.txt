I have recently been appointed to advise a working group on the design of reflective reasoning systems, and I find myself preoccupied with a specific conceptual problem: how can a bounded agent coherently maintain self-trust without falling into the Löbian obstacle? By this I mean the difficulty that arises when an agent can represent its own proof conditions—Löb's theorem suggests that unconditional self-endorsement of consistency becomes derivable in ways that undermine genuine epistemic authority. I want the full desiderata that any adequate theory of rational self-trust must satisfy in this context.

Set out the requirements precisely: the formal constraints it must respect (for instance, blocking derivability of global self-endorsement while permitting local confidence), the distinctions it must preserve (such as that between object-level inference and meta-level licensing, or between logical omniscience and bounded rationality), the reflective failure cases it must accommodate rather than rule out by fiat, and the results from modal epistemic logic it must remain consistent with. For each, provide the motivating consideration or case. Then indicate which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are open to relaxation, and state which single requirement does the most damage to existing proposals—whether Cartesian, Bayesian, or coherence-theoretic.

Please respond in British English, and do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or citations. The analysis should stand on its own reasoning.
