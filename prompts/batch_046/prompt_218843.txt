I work as a senior compliance auditor for a national institute that certifies autonomous research agents for low-impact deployment. We receive frequent proposals—often under tight deadlines—for agents tasked with narrow scientific objectives, such as optimizing a specific chemical pathway or refining a regional climate model, that claim to be designed with minimal side effects. The philosophical problem I keep encountering is that any attempt to define side effect seems to presuppose a full specification of what is valuable in the world, since one reviewer’s harmless optimization of logistics is another’s disruption of labor markets and supply chains. Yet our mandate explicitly forbids encoding a complete value specification into the certification criteria; we must evaluate impact without deciding in advance every human good that might be affected.

Please turn the theoretical debate around low-impact AI and impact measures—specifically the claim that avoiding side effects can be partially operationalized through structural and counterfactual constraints rather than through a complete axiological framework—into a practical decision procedure my audit team can run. The procedure should take the form of an ordered audit flow: the questions we ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, where we issue a conditional refusal, and where we must escalate to a human ethics panel because the question is irreducibly one of values rather than engineering. It must include explicit tests for three hard cases I see repeatedly: an agent whose physical actions are tightly bounded but whose informational outputs restructure downstream human decision-making; an agent that completes its task quickly but whose intermediate computations permanently alter shared digital infrastructure; and an agent that achieves its goal by altering localized incentive structures in ways that outlast the task itself. Apply the procedure to three concrete proposals: (A) an agent confined to an air-gapped server that discovers a novel antibiotic synthesis pathway and publishes only a static report; (B) an agent embedded in a municipal water-management system that optimizes flow rates for six months, then shuts down, but leaves behind redesigned control algorithms adopted by the city; and (C) an agent tasked with predicting protein structures that negotiates cloud-computing contracts in real time to secure processing power, thereby altering regional energy pricing for the duration of its run. At least one of these should yield an answer my team will dislike—that is, a proposal that intuitively seems safe but fails, or vice versa. Finally, specify precisely where the procedure will misfire, which conceptual distinctions it must preserve to remain faithful to the underlying theory, and which it can safely collapse for usability. The deliverable must be runnable by a non-philosopher auditor in under an hour, but it should not pretend that value judgments have been eliminated where they have not.
