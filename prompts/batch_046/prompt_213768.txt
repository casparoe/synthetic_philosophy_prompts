I work for a small museum digitizing old signage and graffiti, and we keep hitting glyphs that are clearly meant to be letters but are distorted, merged, or drawn in styles that break every geometric template. I need a decision procedre my team can actually run on each case — we get maybe five minutes per glyph, the call has to be signed off by me or the senior curator, and none of us have philosophy training. Write it as ordered questions, what counts as enough, where it stops with a hard no, and where we just have to admit it's a values call about style vs essence. The principle is that being an 'a' isn't about matching an ideal shape; it's about morpholigcal continuity within a script family — inheriting the bowl-stem-counter relation from some recognized tradition — plus being readable as part of a phonetic word pattern, not just in isolation. But graffiti and glitch art break both conditions, so the procedure has to handle that without just saying "use your gut". Apply it to three cases: (1) a medieval manuscript glyph where the bowl is open at the top like a u but the stem is on the right, (2) a graffiti symbol that's basically a triangle with a tail that everyone reads as a in context, and (3) a perfect digital replica of a Times New Roman a placed in a chemical diagram where it's clearly meant to be the symbol for alpha. Make sure one of these gives an answer my curator will hate. Flag exactly where human judgment can't be eliminated and where the procedure is likely to misfire on future cases.
