I'm trying to formalize an argument that biography lacks a distinctive moral-pedagogical function, and I worry it proves too much about the tradition running from Plutarch to hagiography. Here is the chain. (1) For a biography to educate morally, the reader must be able to relate the subject's choices to actions they themselves might face. (2) This relation must take one of two forms: direct emulation, which requires substantial similarity in circumstance, temperament, and social role; or abstract principle-extraction, which renders the narrative details dispensable. (3) If direct emulation requires substantial similarity, then the universalist ambition of moral biography collapses — Plutarch writing for a broad audience about Roman and Greek lives, or hagiographers presenting ascetic saints as models for all Christians — because readers who differ in gender, status, culture, or epoch cannot apply the model. (4) If the mechanism is abstract extraction, then biography is redundant with ethical philosophy and its narrative form is merely ornamental. (5) No intermediate mechanism survives scrutiny: admiration or awe at extreme virtue produces affect rather than specific moral knowledge; negative exemplarity, learning from the subject's failures, still requires either prior moral vocabulary (making the biography redundant) or sufficient contextual similarity (reinstating the problem in (3)); and proleptic identification — treating the life as constructing a future self rather than mirroring the present one — either smuggles in abstract norms or assumes the reader already knows which future self to become. (6) Therefore biography either fails to reach diverse audiences or collapses into philosophy; it has no distinctive pedagogical role. (7) Consequently, the claim that lives are uniquely suited to moral formation — whether through Plutarch's mirror metaphor or through hagiographic exemplary literature — is mistaken.

This looks suspicious because the conclusion dismisses a long and varied practice. Where does the argument go wrong? Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am especially reluctant to abandon (1) or (2), since they seem to capture something true about how narrative works, but I also want to preserve the intuition that a modern reader can genuinely learn from the Life of Alcibiades or from a desert father's vita without being an Athenian general or a fourth-century ascetic. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) than (5), yet I want to know what I am paying either way.
