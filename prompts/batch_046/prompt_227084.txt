I run a philosophy-of-AI workshop for about 12 grad students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class game that makes them actually feel the pull of the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence — the idea that intelligence and goals are seperate, and that pretty much any end goal still makes you grab resources in the middle.

Don't lecture it up front. Each student draws a hidden "terminal goal" card: one wants to maximize poems written, one wants to minimize pain in the room, one wants to build the tallest block tower, a couple more with weird ones. They all share one table-space with limited "energy chips." The injected twist is an unstated reserve rule: you literally cannot take an action toward your terminal goal unless you hold at least 3 chips in reserve. Don't reveal that until after round 2.

So I need rules, materials, a payoff scheme using points or candy (no real money), and round structure — probably negotiation, trade, maybe some hidden stealing — arranged so that even the poetry person and the tower person both end up hoarding chips and blocking each other despite totally different ends. They should notice the convergence themselves before anyone names it.

Then a debrief: map round 1 onto orthogonality (goals are arbitrary relative to means), round 2 onto instrumental convergence (everyone grabs chips), and the hidden-rule reveal onto why this doesn't depend on the agent being "evil." Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first run — like someone figures out the reserve rule early or people cooperate out of human niceness — and how to head that off. Be frank about what the game can't reproduce: actual long-term strategic reasoning, the scale of real-world resources, and human empathy. Tell me exactly how I'll have to supply those missing pieces in the debrief myself.

No reading lists, no citations, don't reference obscure papers I wouldn't have seen in training. Just design the runnable activity.
